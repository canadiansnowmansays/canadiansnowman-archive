The Strait of Hormuz was open before he urged President Trump to start the war on Feb. 28.

This essay is not a diary entry. The Iran Crisis series has documented this war day by day, source by source, for sixty-five days. What follows is something different: a systematic accounting of the gap between what has been said and what has been documented. Every claim is sourced. Every contradiction is on the record. The receipts are attached.

I. On the War’s Duration

What was said: Trump, February 28: the war would be completed in “four to five weeks.”

What the record shows: Day 65. The ceasefire is not a peace agreement. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed. No deal has been reached on any of the war’s stated objectives. The Vanderbilt University panel — retired general, retired CIA analyst, energy industry executive — assessed the economic effects would last two to nine months from the ceasefire date, prompting what the Atlantic described as “a collective intake of breath from the audience.”

II. On Unconditional Surrender

What was said: Trump on Truth Social, Day 7: “There will be NO DEAL with Iran EXCEPT UNCONDITIONAL SURRENDER.”

What was said three weeks ago: Trump, April 21: Iran had agreed to all his demands. A breakthrough was near. Iran would work with the United States to remove its enriched uranium. Energy prices would drop. The global crisis would subside.

What was said Saturday: Trump on Truth Social: reviewing Iran’s latest proposal but “can’t imagine that it would be acceptable.” Clarified to reporters he had only been briefed on the “concept of the deal” and had not seen the actual wording. “They’re going to give me the exact wording now” — said while boarding a plane.

What the record shows: None of the April 21 statements proved accurate. Iran has not agreed to remove its enriched uranium. Energy prices have not dropped. The global crisis has not subsided. At Tuesday’s Pentagon briefing, asked directly when the president decided to abandon the demand for unconditional surrender, Defense Secretary Hegseth replied: “The president hasn’t capitulated on anything.” He did not answer the question.

III. On the Nuclear Program

What was said: Trump, June 2025, after Operation Midnight Hammer: Iran’s nuclear program had been “obliterated.”

What was said officially: The US government was “more circumspect,” saying the program had been “set back.”

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: “Operation Midnight Hammer did the obliteration of the — set back the program.”

What the record shows: The Broad/Sanger New York Times analysis — “Trump Seeks to Abolish Iran’s Atomic Stockpile, a Problem He Helped Create” — documents that Iran has 11 tons of uranium at various enrichment levels, enough if further enriched for 50 to 100 nuclear weapons. The 970 pounds of uranium enriched to 60% at Isfahan is buried under bombing rubble but has not been removed, confirmed destroyed, or transferred. Iran suspended IAEA cooperation in July 2025 — on-site inspections ended. The location of an undeclared enrichment facility that Iran was about to disclose on June 13, 2025 — the meeting was cancelled when the bombs started falling that morning — remains unknown. Harvard’s Matthew Bunn: “We can’t bomb away their knowledge.” The word “obliterated” does not appear in any IAEA report, US intelligence assessment, or independent nuclear analysis.

IV. On the Strait of Hormuz

What was said: Trump, April 17, Truth Social: “IRAN HAS JUST ANNOUNCED THAT THE STRAIT OF IRAN IS FULLY OPEN AND READY FOR FULL PASSAGE.”

What was said Sunday: Trump announced Project Freedom — the United States would help guide stranded ships out of the Strait of Hormuz. “Any interference will be dealt with forcefully.”

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: “We’ve now opened a passage through the Strait of Hormuz to allow for the free flow of commerce to proceed.” Also: “Hundreds more ships from nations around the world are lining up to transit.”

What the record shows: On Monday, four ships transited the strait, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence. Before the war, 130 vessels made the passage daily. Hapag-Lloyd, one of the world’s largest container shipping companies, said Monday: “Our risk assessment remains unchanged, and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed for Hapag-Lloyd transits until further notice.” The International Chamber of Shipping said the plan “lacked detail.” BIMCO’s Larsen: “It is unclear whether Project Freedom is sustainable in the longer run.” Kpler analyst Subasic: “a low chance of fully reopening Hormuz quickly.” The International Maritime Organization reported 22,500 mariners on more than 1,550 commercial vessels remain trapped in the Arabian Gulf. The ADNOC oil tanker — owned by the UAE’s state oil company — was struck by an Iranian drone on Monday. The Fujairah Oil Industry Zone — the largest oil storage area in the Emirates — caught fire. Iran fired cruise missiles and drones at US ships. Six Iranian speedboats were destroyed by Apache helicopters. The South Korean cargo ship HMM Namu caught fire after an explosion in the strait. Iran’s foreign minister responded: “Project Freedom is Project Deadlock.”

V. On Who Controls the Strait

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: “What we’re demonstrating with Project Freedom is that they don’t control the strait.” Also: “We know the Iranians are embarrassed by this fact.”

What was also said at Tuesday’s briefing: General Caine: since the ceasefire, Iran has fired at commercial vessels nine times and attacked US forces more than ten times. The threshold for restarting major combat operations is “a political decision above my pay grade.” Iran’s command structure “remains very fractured.” Tuesday was “quieter.”

What was said by the Pentagon’s own reporter: Helene Cooper, New York Times Pentagon correspondent, annotating the briefing in real time: “It is clear the U.S. military is not in control of the Strait of Hormuz either.”

What the record shows: Richard Haass, former senior State Department official: “Even if Iran does not have explicit control, there is now always an implicit measure of control. Because they have shut it once, now they know they can do it again.” The Youssef/Lemire Atlantic analysis — “The Iran War’s Ramifications Have Only Just Begun” — documents that traffic through the strait has been reduced by approximately 90% from prewar levels. Iran’s parliament is advancing legislation to charge transit tolls. The Iranian commander Ali Abdollahi warned Monday that all commercial ships must “refrain from any attempt to transit without coordination with the armed forces.” The strait that Hegseth says America controls is one through which four ships passed on Monday.

VI. On the Blockade

What was said: Treasury Secretary Bessent, April 27, social media: “While the surviving IRGC Leaders are trapped like drowning rats in a sewage pipe, Iran’s creaking oil industry is starting to shut in production thanks to the U.S. BLOCKADE. Pumping will soon collapse.”

What was said Monday: Bessent, Fox News: “Let’s see if China step up with some diplomacy and get the Iranians to open the strait. Iran is the largest state sponsor of terrorism, and China has been buying 90 percent of their energy, so they are funding the largest state sponsor of terrorism.”

What the record shows: The Rappeport/Livni New York Times analysis — “U.S. Sanctions Zigzag in New World of Economic Warfare” — documented that Russia has been earning an estimated $200 million per day in oil revenues enabled by a US sanctions waiver that was extended, then not extended, then possibly extended again. The Foundation for Defense of Democracies estimates Iran has absorbed roughly $144 billion in economic damage — significant, but the regime has not capitulated. The Wright Atlantic analysis — “The Real Reason Iran Hasn’t Struck a Deal” — notes that Iran has endured decades of sanctions and has experience adjusting its oil industry to cope with reduced demand. Iran also benefited from a financial windfall at the start of the conflict when the US lifted sanctions on Iranian oil exports. China responded to US sanctions on Chinese refineries this past weekend by invoking a 2021 blocking measure ordering its companies not to comply. Iran’s negotiating position has not changed since Islamabad I.

VII. On the Ceasefire

What was said, Friday May 1, letter to Congress: Trump declared that war “hostilities have terminated” because of the ceasefire. The War Powers Resolution clock had stopped.

What was said, Friday May 1, same evening, Florida: Trump to supporters: “You know we’re in a war. Because I think you would agree we cannot let lunatics have a nuclear weapon.”

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth, asked directly if the ceasefire is over: “No, the ceasefire is not over.” Also at the same briefing: Iran has attacked US forces more than ten times since the ceasefire. Caine: the threshold for declaring the ceasefire over is “a political decision above my pay grade.”

What was said on War Powers: Hegseth: “Our view is that ultimately with the ceasefire, the clock stops. If it were to restart, that’s the president’s decision.”

What the record shows: The Atlantic’s David Graham — “Congress Can’t Meet Its Own Iran-War Deadline” — notes that Trump’s interpretation “would allow a president to engineer cease-fires every two weeks to escape congressional involvement.” The war is not over in any sense: thousands of service members are deployed, thousands of ships are trapped, negotiations have not produced a deal, and Iran fired missiles at US ships on the day the War Powers deadline passed. Gallup: congressional approval at 86% disapproval — matching its all-time worst. Speaker Johnson: “We are not at war.” Trump to Florida supporters the same day: “You know we’re in a war.”

VIII. On Iran’s Command Structure

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: Iran’s command-and-control “remains very fractured.” Caine: “I think they are struggling to maintain control down south.”

What was also said at Tuesday’s briefing: Caine: “There are some actions the IRGC take sometimes that are outside of what Iranian negotiators would like. That’s their job to rein in if they ultimately want to create a condition for a deal.”

What the record shows: The Fassihi New York Times analysis — “A New Era and New Leadership: The Generals Who Are Running Iran” — based on 23 interviews including senior officials, Revolutionary Guards members, and individuals with direct knowledge of the power structure, documented a cohesive collective leadership. The IRGC generals pulled the plug on Islamabad II after a deliberate internal meeting in which they overruled President Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Araghchi. International Crisis Group’s Ali Vaez: “Mojtaba is subservient to the Revolutionary Guards because he owes his position and he owes the survival of the system to them.” Chatham House’s Sanam Vakil: “He is presented with fait accompli presentations right now.” The IRGC shooting at US ships while Araghchi posts diplomatic social media is not fractured command — it is a deliberate dual-track strategy. The Atlantic’s Thomas Wright: Iran and the US have failed to reach an agreement “not because hard-liners are blocking pragmatists inside Iran, but because both sides seem to sincerely believe that they have won the war.”

IX. On America’s Gift to the World

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: Project Freedom is “a direct gift from the United States to the world.” Also: “Once again Americans using its strength to lift up others.”

What was also said at Tuesday’s briefing, by the Pentagon’s own correspondent: Cooper: “Project Freedom is yet another effort by the Trump administration to fix a problem of its own making. The Strait of Hormuz was open before he urged President Trump to start the war with Iran on Feb. 28.”

What the record shows: Before February 28, approximately 130 ships transited the Strait of Hormuz daily, carrying roughly 20% of the world’s oil supply. The US and Israel launched the war on February 28. Iran responded by closing the strait. The US then launched a blockade of Iranian ports. Oil rose from $63/barrel to a peak of $126. Gas rose from $3.15 to $4.46 nationally — up 44%. The World Bank projects energy prices will rise 24% this year. Chief economist Indermit Gill: “The war is hitting the global economy in cumulative waves: first through higher energy prices, then higher food prices and finally, higher inflation, which will push up interest rates and make debt even more expensive.” In Malawi, the government ran out of fuel reserves. In Firozabad, India, glass factories 21 miles from the Taj Mahal went cold. In Petra, Jordan, tourism is at 1% of normal. In Zimbabwe, a nurse earning $540 a month is considering sending her children to live with their rural grandmother to save $160 a month on school transport. The gift preceded by the problem preceded by the gift-giver.

X. On Who Started This

What was said at Tuesday’s briefing: Hegseth: “Iran is the clear aggressor.” Also: “For too long Iran has been harassing ships, shooting at civilian tankers from all nations and trying to impose a tolling system.”

What the record shows: The United States and Israel launched military strikes on Iran on February 28, 2026, during active diplomatic negotiations over Iran’s nuclear program. The strikes were not authorized by Congress. Iran responded by closing the Strait of Hormuz. The sequence is documented in every significant account of the war’s opening. The NYT Editorial Board: Iran “discovered it had this weapon” as a consequence of the US-Israeli attack. Haass: “One of the ironies of this war is that Iran discovered that it had this weapon. There was so much talk about nuclear ability, but they have the strait.” Cooper at the briefing: “The Strait of Hormuz was open before he urged President Trump to start the war with Iran on Feb. 28.”

Hegseth called the Iranian response to the American attack “international extortion.” He did not mention the American attack.

Coda: On the Threshold

At Tuesday’s briefing, a reporter asked General Caine: what is the threshold for restarting major combat operations?

Caine: “The threshold of restarting is a political decision above my pay grade.”

The chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff has informed the American public, the United States Congress, and the world that the definition of war — when it begins, when it ends, when it resumes — is whatever the president decides it is. The War Powers Resolution, the ceasefire agreement, the sixty-day statutory deadline, the constitutional provision giving Congress the power to declare war: all political decisions above his pay grade.

This is not a contradiction. It is the most honest thing said at the briefing.

The rest was accounting.
