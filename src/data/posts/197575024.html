In 1947, virtually every newly independent nation in Asia and Africa chose some form of authoritarianism. The reasons were understandable. Post-colonial states faced illiteracy rates above 80 percent, economies wrecked by extraction, borders drawn by departing Europeans, ethnic and religious tensions weaponized by decades of divide-and-rule, and populations with no experience of democratic governance. Strongmen offered order. Ideology offered direction. Democracy, with its messy arguments and slow compromises, looked like a luxury that countries fighting for survival could not afford.

China chose Mao. Indonesia chose Sukarno. Egypt chose Nasser. Pakistan — India’s twin, born the same night from the same wound — cycled through parliamentary dysfunction and into military dictatorship within a decade.

India chose democracy. And the man most responsible for that choice was Jawaharlal Nehru.

It was a gamble. Nothing in the precedents suggested it would work. A country of 350 million people, 85 percent of whom could not read, speaking over a thousand languages, divided by caste, religion, region, and the fresh trauma of Partition — this was not the textbook setting for democratic self-governance. The textbook said it was impossible.

Nehru bet on it anyway. And to understand why Indian democracy exists — why the world’s largest democratic experiment was attempted at all — you have to understand the man who placed the bet.

I. The Aristocrat Revolutionary

Nehru was not, by any reasonable measure, a man of the people. He was born in 1889 into one of the wealthiest Brahmin families in northern India. His father, Motilal Nehru, was a prominent lawyer who lived in a mansion called Anand Bhavan — the Abode of Bliss — in Allahabad, complete with a swimming pool, imported furniture, and English servants. The young Jawaharlal was educated at Harrow and Cambridge, trained as a barrister at the Inner Temple in London, and returned to India steeped in Fabian socialism, Western liberalism, and the self-assurance of a man who had been groomed for leadership since birth.

He joined the independence movement and became Gandhi’s chosen political heir — a relationship that was genuinely affectionate but also strategically convenient for both men. Gandhi needed a modern, secular, internationally credible figure who could translate the movement’s moral energy into political action. Nehru needed Gandhi’s connection to the masses that his own aristocratic background denied him. The partnership worked, though it was never without tension. Nehru was a modernist; Gandhi was, in many respects, a medievalist who idealized village life and distrusted industrialization. Nehru wanted to build a modern nation-state. Gandhi wanted to dissolve the concept of the state altogether.

Nehru spent a total of nine years in British prisons. He wrote books in his cell. He emerged each time more popular than when he went in. By 1947, he was the inevitable first Prime Minister of independent India — not because he had been elected, but because Gandhi had anointed him, the Congress machinery supported him, and no plausible alternative existed except Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, whose claims Nehru outmaneuvered with a combination of charm, international stature, and Gandhi’s backing.

II. Ambedkar’s Constitution

The decision to write a constitution was itself a political act of the first order. India could have governed itself through parliamentary convention, as Britain does, without a written constitutional document. Nehru and the Congress leadership chose otherwise — and the man they chose to lead the drafting committee was the most unlikely figure in Indian public life.

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar was a Dalit — an Untouchable, born in 1891 into a caste so despised that his childhood was defined by segregation as rigid as anything in the American South. He was not permitted to sit with upper-caste students in school. He could not drink from the common water pot. When he needed water, a peon had to pour it from a height into his cupped hands so that no Untouchable’s lips would contaminate the vessel. He sat on a gunny sack in the classroom, separated from his peers, and carried it home each day because no one else would touch it.

He became, through a combination of extraordinary intellect and the patronage of the Maharaja of Baroda, one of the most educated men in India — Columbia University, the London School of Economics, the bar at Gray’s Inn. He returned to India and dedicated his life to the annihilation of caste, which he understood not as a social custom to be reformed but as a system of graded inequality maintained by religious sanction that could only be destroyed by legal and constitutional means.

The constitution Ambedkar wrote — adopted on November 26, 1949, effective January 26, 1950 — is a remarkable document by any measure. At the time of its adoption, it was the longest constitution in the world. It established India as a sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic. It guaranteed fundamental rights including equality before the law, freedom of speech and religion, and the prohibition of discrimination on the basis of caste, religion, race, sex, or place of birth. It created an independent judiciary, a parliamentary system, universal adult suffrage, and — crucially — a system of affirmative action called “reservations” that set aside seats in legislatures, government jobs, and educational institutions for Scheduled Castes (Dalits) and Scheduled Tribes.

Universal adult suffrage. In 1950. For 350 million people, 85 percent illiterate. The United States, by contrast, did not achieve anything approaching universal suffrage until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — fifteen years after India. The Swiss did not grant women the vote until 1971. India did it at birth, not because its leaders were confident it would work, but because Ambedkar insisted that democracy without universal participation was not democracy at all.

The constitution was also a rejection of Gandhi. Ambedkar did not say this publicly, but the document’s architecture made it clear. Where Gandhi wanted decentralized village republics, Ambedkar built a strong central state. Where Gandhi romanticized village life, Ambedkar — who had experienced village life as a Dalit — called Indian villages “a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness, and communalism.” Where Gandhi wanted moral transformation, Ambedkar wanted legal rights enforceable in courts. The constitution was Ambedkar’s answer to everything Gandhi could not provide: a structure. A mechanism. A set of rules that did not depend on the goodwill of the powerful but could be demanded as a right by the powerless.

The Indian Constitution is, in my assessment, the most radical democratic document of the twentieth century. It was written by a man who had been denied water as a child, for a country that had been denied self-governance for two centuries, at a moment when the country was tearing itself apart. If that isn’t the definition of democracy as an act of faith, I don’t know what is.

III. Patel and the Princely States

While Ambedkar wrote the constitution, another man was building the country it would govern.

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel — the “Iron Man of India” — was given the task of integrating the 500-plus princely states into the Indian union. The princely states were not automatically part of independent India. The British had granted them nominal sovereignty under paramountcy; when the British left, paramountcy lapsed, and each state was technically free to accede to India, to Pakistan, or to declare independence.

Patel accomplished the integration through a combination of persuasion, pressure, and — when necessary — the threat or use of military force. The Nizam of Hyderabad, the richest man in the world and ruler of a Muslim-majority princely state in the heart of Hindu India, declared independence. Patel sent in the army in September 1948 in what was called “Operation Polo” — a euphemism for invasion. Hyderabad was absorbed within four days. The Nawab of Junagadh, whose Hindu-majority state had a Muslim ruler who acceded to Pakistan, was overrun by Indian forces after a popular revolt. And Kashmir — Hindu ruler, Muslim majority — became the intractable problem it remains today.

Patel’s achievement is staggering in its scale and largely invisible in the Western narrative. Without him, there would be no India — not as a geographic entity, not as a political unit, not as a democracy. There would be a subcontinent of competing statelets, a South Asian Europe without the European Union. Patel built the country. Ambedkar wrote its rules. Nehru gave it a direction.

IV. The Nehruvian State

The direction Nehru chose was a distinctive hybrid that reflected his own intellectual formation: parliamentary democracy, secularism, non-alignment in foreign policy, and state-directed economic development modelled loosely on the Soviet Union’s five-year plans but without the totalitarianism.

The economic model — sometimes called the “Nehruvian consensus” or, less charitably, the “licence raj” — was built on the premise that a post-colonial economy could not be left to the market. The market, in India’s experience, meant the East India Company. It meant the destruction of Indian industry to benefit British manufacturing. It meant famine while grain was exported. Nehru’s answer was state planning: government control of key industries, import substitution, centralized allocation of resources, and a labyrinthine system of licences, permits, and quotas that determined who could produce what, how much, and where.

The system produced results. Indian GDP grew. Industrial capacity expanded. The Indian Institutes of Technology, founded in the 1950s and 1960s, created a world-class engineering and scientific establishment. The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s — state-directed investment in agricultural technology — ended the spectre of mass famine that had haunted India since the British era. India built dams, steel plants, nuclear reactors, and space programs. Nehru called the great dams “the temples of modern India,” and he was not being ironic. He was a devout secularist who believed in progress with the same fervour that others believed in God.

The system also produced pathologies. The licence raj created a bureaucratic aristocracy that controlled access to economic opportunity and extracted rents at every level. Corruption became systemic — not because Indians were inherently corrupt, but because a system that required government permission for every economic decision created infinite opportunities for gatekeepers to demand payment. The private sector was stifled. Innovation was discouraged. India’s growth rate — derisively called “the Hindu rate of growth” — averaged 3.5 percent per year from the 1950s to the 1980s, a rate that barely kept pace with population growth and left hundreds of millions in poverty.

The tension between democracy and development — between the messy freedom of a democratic society and the efficient coercion of an authoritarian one — was the defining question of Nehru’s India. China, starting from a comparable base in 1949, chose coercion and grew faster. India chose freedom and grew slower. Whether that tradeoff was worth it depends on how you weight economic growth against human liberty, and the answer changes depending on whether you are a development economist in Washington or a farmer in Bihar.

V. Non-Alignment

Nehru’s foreign policy was as distinctive as his domestic program, and it infuriates the West to this day.

Non-alignment — the refusal to join either the American or Soviet bloc during the Cold War — was not neutrality. It was a positive ideology, rooted in Nehru’s conviction that newly independent nations had no obligation to subordinate their foreign policy to the superpower rivalry, and that the Cold War itself was a distraction from the real issues facing the post-colonial world: poverty, development, racial equality, and the dismantling of the imperial order.

Nehru, along with Nasser of Egypt, Tito of Yugoslavia, Sukarno of Indonesia, and Nkrumah of Ghana, founded the Non-Aligned Movement at the Bandung Conference of 1955. The movement was idealistic, often impractical, and occasionally hypocritical — several of its founding members were dictators. But its core premise was sound: the world did not belong to two superpowers, and the countries that constituted the majority of humanity had the right to chart their own course.

The United States never forgave India for this. Washington wanted allies, not philosophers. When India refused to join SEATO or CENTO — the Cold War alliance systems — and maintained friendly relations with the Soviet Union while also engaging with the United States, American policymakers regarded Nehru as naive, arrogant, or secretly pro-Soviet. The fact that India was genuinely non-aligned — that it criticized Soviet actions in Hungary and American actions in Vietnam with equal vigour — did not fit the binary framework of the Cold War, and so the Americans chose not to believe it.

The echoes are audible today. India’s refusal to condemn Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, its simultaneous deepening of ties with the United States through the Quad, its purchase of Russian oil while hosting American military exercises — all of this bewilders Western commentators who want India to pick a side. India’s answer, unchanged since Nehru, is: no. We are on India’s side. If that confuses you, the confusion is yours.

My great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside would have understood this. He spent his career building international institutions that respected the sovereignty and agency of developing nations. The Non-Aligned Movement was, in its aspirations if not always in its execution, exactly the kind of international order he worked toward — one in which newly independent nations were subjects, not objects, of global governance.

VI. The Price of the Gamble

Nehru died on May 27, 1964, exhausted and diminished by India’s humiliating defeat in the 1962 border war with China. The war shattered the illusion of Hindi-Chini bhai-bhai — Indians and Chinese are brothers — and exposed the limits of non-alignment as a security strategy. Nehru had trusted China. China invaded. The Indian army, underequipped and underprepared, was routed in the Himalayas. Nehru reportedly told Parliament, “We were getting out of touch with reality.”

He left behind a democracy that had survived its first seventeen years. This was not a small achievement. In those same seventeen years, democracy had collapsed in Pakistan, Burma, Egypt, Iraq, and a dozen other post-colonial states. India had held three general elections — in 1952, 1957, and 1962 — each one the largest democratic exercise in human history. Power had not changed hands, but elections had been held, courts had functioned, the press had remained free, and the military had stayed in its barracks. The gamble, so far, was paying off.

But Nehru also left behind a dynasty. His daughter, Indira, was already a powerful figure in the Congress Party. She would become Prime Minister within two years of his death. And she would test the democracy her father built to its absolute breaking point.
