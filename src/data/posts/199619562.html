There is a line this series has been tracking across twenty-three parts. It began with James Comey — a former FBI director who had investigated Trump. It moved to Letitia James — an attorney general who had prosecuted him. It reached Jerome Powell — a Fed chair who had declined to do what he wanted. It found Sarah Fitzpatrick — a reporter who had written about his FBI director’s drinking. At each step, a question: how far does the list reach?

We have a new answer today.

The Justice Department has opened a criminal investigation into E. Jean Carroll.

Carroll is 82 years old. She is a former magazine writer. She is not a politician, a prosecutor, a federal official, or a journalist. She is a woman who said Donald Trump sexually assaulted her in a Bergdorf Goodman dressing room in the mid-1990s. A federal jury believed her. Two unanimous federal appeals panels upheld the verdict. She was awarded $5 million in the first trial and $83.3 million in the second — the punitive damages specifically because the jury found Trump had acted with malice, and because Carroll had received death threats as a result of his public attacks on her.

The Justice Department’s theory, according to two people with direct knowledge of the situation: perjury. Carroll, the government suggests, may have lied in her civil lawsuits.

The jury that heard those lawsuits did not think so. The appeals courts that reviewed the verdicts did not think so. The three-judge Second Circuit panel that unanimously rejected Trump’s request for a new trial — finding he had “not demonstrated that the district court erred in any of the challenged rulings” — did not think so.

The Justice Department, under Todd Blanche, thinks so.

The Prosecutor They Found

Andrew S. Boutros

The investigation has been assigned to Andrew Boutros, the Trump-appointed U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of Illinois.

Carroll lives in New York. The alleged perjury occurred in New York. The trials took place in New York. Boutros is in Chicago.

The apparent Chicago connection is a nonprofit associated with Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn billionaire and Trump critic who provided financial support to Carroll’s legal case. Trump’s lawyers had raised the Hoffman funding during the first trial, arguing it raised “questions” about Carroll’s credibility. Carroll’s lawyers responded that the funding was irrelevant to her legal claims and that she had nothing to do with obtaining it. The court did not find this argument persuasive enough to disturb the verdict.

The jurisdictional logic is, to put it gently, strained. But jurisdiction, in Blanche’s Justice Department, is increasingly a formality rather than a constraint. The NYT reports that DOJ leadership has made extensive use of a provision allowing cases to be assigned to handpicked prosecutors across the country, regardless of where the alleged crimes occurred. The investigation into John Brennan, the former CIA director, was assigned to the U.S. attorney in Miami specifically because he was seen as more willing to pursue a case that other offices had declined as legally questionable.

You find the prosecutor who will take the case. You assign the case to that prosecutor. The geography is whatever you need it to be.

The Boutros Problem

There is an additional detail about Boutros that deserves to be prominently noted.

Last Thursday — four days ago — Boutros announced that misdemeanor charges against protesters outside a Chicago immigration detention facility were being dismissed. The reason: potential misconduct by prosecutors under his supervision during the grand jury process. Defense lawyers reported that prosecutors had held conversations with individual grand jurors outside the courtroom — a serious breach of grand jury rules that exists specifically to prevent prosecutors from improperly influencing proceedings.

The U.S. Attorney whose office just had a grand jury misconduct scandal is now opening a criminal investigation into the woman who beat Trump in two civil trials.

This is the prosecutor the Justice Department chose.

The Recusal That Isn’t

Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general, has recused himself from the Carroll investigation.

The reason: Blanche represented Trump in the Carroll litigation. He was the president’s personal defense lawyer during the very civil cases that Carroll won. He is now, in his capacity as the nation’s top law enforcement officer, overseeing a Justice Department that has opened a criminal investigation into the woman who beat his former client.

He has recused himself from the specific case. Officials from DOJ headquarters are involved anyway. The department he runs, the culture he has set, the message he has sent about what kinds of cases the administration wants pursued — none of that is subject to recusal.

The investigation will proceed in an environment entirely shaped by the man who argued against Carroll in court on Trump’s behalf, who has now been “unleashed,” in the president’s own word, to pursue Trump’s enemies.

What Two Juries and Two Appeals Courts Found

It is worth pausing to state plainly what the legal record shows, because the criminal investigation will generate headlines suggesting Carroll’s conduct is under legitimate scrutiny, and that suggestion deserves context.

In May 2023, a federal jury found Trump liable for sexually abusing Carroll and defaming her. The jury awarded $5 million. A three-judge Second Circuit panel unanimously upheld the verdict, finding Trump had “not demonstrated that the district court erred in any of the challenged rulings.”

In January 2024, a second jury awarded Carroll $83.3 million in a related defamation case — $65 million of that in punitive damages, specifically because the jury found Trump had acted with malice. The panel noted that Trump “never wavered or relented in his public attacks” on Carroll, and that she had been subjected to death threats and threats of physical violence as a result.

A second unanimous three-judge Second Circuit panel upheld that verdict as well.

That is the record: two juries, two unanimous appellate panels, $88.3 million in total verdicts, and a finding that the defendant acted with malice and caused a woman to receive death threats.

The Justice Department’s response is to open a criminal investigation into her for perjury.

The Pattern, Extended

Twenty-three parts. One through-line. But Part XXIV represents something the series has been building toward and has not until today reached fully.

Every previous target had some public role that could be framed, however disingenuously, as political opposition. Comey ran the FBI. James ran the New York AG’s office. Powell ran the Fed. Brennan ran the CIA. Fitzpatrick writes for a national magazine. Each of them had, in some professional capacity, done something Trump could point to.

Carroll sued him for what he did to her in a dressing room thirty years ago. She had no office. She wielded no institutional power. She was a private citizen who made an accusation that two juries found credible and two appellate courts refused to disturb.

The enemies list has now reached a woman whose only offense was telling the truth about what happened to her — and winning.

The series has documented the escalation across twenty-three parts: from political opponents to prosecutors to journalists to institutions to civil society organizations to comedians. But there was always, somewhere in the background, the implicit assumption that the target had done something in the public sphere that placed them in proximity to power.

Carroll did not seek proximity to power. Power came to her, in a Bergdorf Goodman dressing room, and did what two juries found it did. She reported it. She sued. She won.

And now the Justice Department, under the direction of the man who argued against her in court, is investigating whether she lied.

What Comes Next

The perjury theory is, on its face, legally implausible. The same courts that would have to evaluate a perjury claim have already evaluated Carroll’s testimony — twice — and found it credible enough to sustain verdicts totalling $88.3 million. Perjury prosecutions are difficult under the best of circumstances; perjury prosecutions involving testimony that multiple appellate courts have found unremarkable face an almost insuperable evidentiary burden.

But legal plausibility, as this series has now documented across twenty-four parts, is not the operative standard. The standard is whether the process serves the purpose. The purpose is punishment and deterrence: punishment of Carroll for winning, deterrence of anyone else who might consider bringing similar claims.

The investigation does not need to result in charges. It does not need to survive a motion to dismiss. It needs only to exist — to impose legal fees, reputational cloud, and the quiet suggestion that the woman who accused the president of sexual assault was herself a criminal. That suggestion, circulated through the apparatus of the Justice Department, is the point.

Kyle Boynton’s warning from Part XXII bears repeating one final time: “A Justice Department willing to stretch both the facts and the law to mislead a grand jury into returning an indictment could literally charge any American with a crime.”

Not any political enemy. Not any public official. Any American.

E. Jean Carroll is 82 years old. She told the truth. She won.

The Enemies List continues.
