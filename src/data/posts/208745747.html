The Introduction to this series left Zhang Qian at the moment of his return to the Han court: a diplomat who had failed at diplomacy, having spent roughly thirteen years away — a decade of it in Xiongnu captivity — only to come home with no alliance, no treaty, and no resolution to the problem he’d been sent to solve. What that account left out is what happened next, both to Zhang Qian personally and to the Han Dynasty’s foreign policy, and it’s worth dwelling on, because the actual mechanics of how the Silk Road went from “an interesting report filed by a returning diplomat” to “a functioning, sustained, multi-continental trade network” involved a second expedition, a series of brutal military campaigns, and a level of state investment in infrastructure that most people don’t associate with a period they mostly remember, if they remember it at all, for the Great Wall.

Zhang Qian did not retire quietly after his first mission. In 119 BCE, roughly two decades after his original departure, Emperor Wu sent him west again — this time not to seek a military alliance against the Xiongnu, but to establish direct diplomatic and trade relations with the Wusun, another Central Asian people, and by extension with the broader network of kingdoms Zhang Qian had described in his original report. This second mission traveled with a far larger retinue, reportedly numbering in the hundreds, and carried substantial gifts of silk and gold intended to demonstrate Han wealth and seriousness. Where the first mission had been an act of desperate improvisation, the second was a calculated extension of state power, and it worked in a way the first had not: Zhang Qian’s deputies fanned out from the Wusun court to Ferghana, Bactria, Parthia, and India, establishing the first documented direct contact between the Han court and the kingdoms of Central and South Asia. Some of these deputies didn’t return for years. Others never returned at all. But the relationships they established — trade relationships, mostly, dressed in the language of tribute and diplomacy that Chinese court records preferred — became the connective tissue of everything that followed.

None of this happened in a vacuum, and it’s worth understanding what else Emperor Wu was doing at the same time, because the diplomacy and the warfare were not separate projects; they were the same project pursued through different means. The tribute arrangement with the Xiongnu that had so offended Han pride was not simply going to end because a court official filed an interesting report about horses in Ferghana. It ended because Emperor Wu, having absorbed Zhang Qian’s intelligence about Xiongnu territory, logistics, and vulnerabilities, launched a series of major military campaigns against them beginning in 133 BCE and continuing intermittently for decades. Han generals — most notably Huo Qubing and Wei Qing — pushed Xiongnu forces out of the Hexi Corridor, the narrow, oasis-studded strip of land running through what is now Gansu province that forms the only practical land route between China proper and Central Asia. This is, geographically, the single most important fact about why the Silk Road runs where it runs: there is essentially one viable overland corridor between China and the West that doesn’t require crossing the Tibetan Plateau or the Gobi Desert at its widest, and it runs through the Hexi Corridor. Whoever controls that corridor controls whether trade happens at all, and in the 120s and 110s BCE, for the first time, that was the Han Dynasty rather than the Xiongnu.

Having taken the corridor, the Han did something that separates a genuine trade route from a merely possible one: they built infrastructure and administration to secure it. Emperor Wu established a chain of military garrisons, watchtowers, and eventually four commanderies — Wuwei, Zhangye, Jiuquan, and Dunhuang — running the length of the Hexi Corridor, each one fortified, staffed, and connected by an extension of the Great Wall’s watchtower system pushed further west than it had ever run before. These weren’t merely defensive; they were logistical. A caravan moving from the Han capital at Chang’an toward Central Asia could now expect, at intervals, fortified way stations with water, supplies, and armed protection against the raiding that had made the route lethal for independent travelers beforehand. This is the unglamorous truth behind the romantic image of the Silk Road caravan crossing open desert under starlight: for the parts of the journey within Han control, it was a heavily garrisoned supply corridor built and paid for by the same imperial treasury that had once been drained by Xiongnu tribute, now repurposed to make trade — and tax revenue from that trade — possible.

The Ferghana horses deserve their own moment here, because Emperor Wu’s fixation on them produced one of the more absurd military campaigns in Han history, and one that says something enduring about the gap between strategic necessity and the particular obsessions of the men who get to decide what counts as strategically necessary. Zhang Qian’s report had described the horses of Ferghana — the so-called “heavenly horses,” or in some translations “blood-sweating horses” — as vastly larger and stronger than the steppe ponies available to Han cavalry, and Emperor Wu became convinced that acquiring a breeding stock of these horses was essential to finally defeating the Xiongnu on their own cavalry-based terms. When the king of Ferghana refused to sell, Emperor Wu sent a military expedition in 104 BCE specifically to seize the horses by force. It failed, badly, undone by the logistics of marching an army across Central Asian desert with insufficient supply. A second, much larger expedition in 102 BCE succeeded, after a siege of the Ferghana capital, in extracting several thousand horses along with a new, more compliant king. Han China had, in effect, fought a war for livestock — a war that also, not incidentally, extended Han military reach and prestige deep into Central Asia in a way that made the trade routes considerably safer for merchants who had nothing to do with horses at all.

By the time these campaigns concluded, in the final years of the first century BCE, the Silk Road had gone from an improvised set of merchant paths following Zhang Qian’s intelligence to a state-secured, garrison-protected corridor connecting Han China to a chain of Central Asian kingdoms that were themselves connected, in turn, to Persia, India, and eventually the Mediterranean. Trade along this route was never a single continuous journey undertaken by any one merchant; the geography made that essentially impossible. Instead, goods moved in relays, passing through the hands of Sogdian, Bactrian, Parthian, and eventually Arab and Roman intermediaries, each group controlling and profiting from one segment of the journey without necessarily knowing much about what lay at either end. A bolt of silk that left a workshop near Chang’an might change hands eight or ten times, crossing several currencies and several languages, before it reached a Roman market three or four years later, and no single person involved in that chain had ever traveled its entire length or fully understood where the fabric had originated or where it was ultimately headed. The Silk Road, in this sense, was never one road at all. It was a relay of overlapping regional trade networks, stitched together over decades of Han military and diplomatic investment, that only looks like a single continuous “road” in retrospect, from the comfortable distance of two thousand years.

It’s worth pausing on the Dunhuang commandery specifically, because of all four garrisons Emperor Wu established along the Hexi Corridor, it would go on to have the longest and strangest afterlife. Founded around 111 BCE at the westernmost edge of Han territory, where the corridor opens out into the Taklamakan’s forbidding expanse and travelers had to choose between a punishing northern route skirting the Tian Shan mountains or an equally punishing southern route along the edge of the Tibetan Plateau, Dunhuang began as exactly what its sister garrisons were: a fortified administrative outpost meant to tax, supply, and monitor traffic passing through the last piece of secured territory before the desert took over. It had no particular reason, in 111 BCE, to expect the future that awaited it — centuries later, it would become one of the great repositories of Buddhist art and manuscript culture in the world, its cave temples preserving material found nowhere else on earth, a transformation entirely unimaginable to the garrison commanders who first built its walls to keep watch for Xiongnu raiding parties. That story belongs to a later installment in this series. What matters here is the more modest point that the administrative skeleton making Dunhuang’s later cultural flowering possible — the roads, the water stations, the basic fact that a traveler could reach that point alive — was laid down for reasons that had nothing to do with religion or art and everything to do with taxation and border security.

The commodities moving through this corridor deserve some attention too, because “silk road” is a nineteenth-century coinage — the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen popularized the term Seidenstraße in 1877, and no one living through the actual trade would have used it or thought of the routes as a single named entity — and the label somewhat oversells silk’s exclusivity as cargo even as it correctly identifies silk as the trade’s most consequential luxury good. Alongside silk, the eastbound traffic into China carried Ferghana’s horses, Central Asian jade and precious stones, glassware that Han elites prized as an exotic novelty since Chinese glassmaking lagged behind Roman and Persian techniques, and crops that would permanently alter Chinese agriculture and cuisine: grapes, alfalfa (grown initially to feed the newly acquired Ferghana horses, in one of history’s more practical footnotes), walnuts, and sesame all entered the Chinese diet via this corridor during the Han period. Westbound traffic carried, alongside silk, Chinese ceramics, lacquerware, and — though its export was nominally restricted, since silk manufacturing was a closely guarded state secret for centuries — occasionally the raw techniques of sericulture itself, smuggled out by travelers or married-off princesses in a pattern that would eventually break China’s monopoly entirely, a story this series will return to when it reaches the Byzantine Empire.

The financing and taxation of all this traffic reveals something about Han statecraft that’s easy to miss in a narrative focused on generals and horses: the Hexi Corridor garrisons functioned as customs stations as much as military ones, and the Han treasury derived real, ongoing revenue from taxing the caravans passing through commandery checkpoints. This means the popular image of the Silk Road as a loosely organized web of independent merchant caravans, while true of the stretches beyond Han administrative reach, understates how much of the route’s earliest and most secure segment operated as a state-managed toll road — one whose profitability gave the Han court every incentive to keep the corridor fortified and functioning long after the original Xiongnu threat that justified its construction had receded. Trade, in other words, didn’t just follow the flag in Han Central Asia. It paid for the flag’s upkeep.

Zhang Qian himself did not live to see the full flowering of what his original, failed mission had set in motion. He died in 113 BCE, a few years after returning from his second expedition, having been posthumously honored by the Han court with a title recognizing his role in “opening communications” with the western regions — a phrase that undersells, in the dry manner of official court language, what he had actually done. He had not opened communications so much as stumbled into them during a decade of captivity, reported back on a world nobody at the Han court had known existed, and then spent his final years helping to formalize contact with kingdoms whose distance from China had, until his first journey, made them essentially theoretical. Sima Qian, the great Han court historian and one of Zhang Qian’s contemporaries, recorded his exploits in the Records of the Grand Historian with an appropriate mixture of respect and mild bewilderment at how much had come from a mission that failed at its own stated purpose.

The route Zhang Qian’s intelligence opened did not remain continuously secure for long, and the pattern of its interruption is worth establishing early, because it recurs for the next fifteen hundred years. Han control over the western regions was contested repeatedly across the first and second centuries CE, lost during the interregnum of Wang Mang’s usurpation between 9 and 23 CE, and reasserted at the end of the first century by the general Ban Chao, whose campaigns across the Tarim Basin are among the more celebrated episodes in Chinese frontier history and were conducted, remarkably, with very small forces and a great deal of shrewd diplomacy among the oasis kingdoms — playing them against one another, against the Xiongnu, and against their own internal factions. Ban Chao spent roughly three decades in the western regions and is said to have dispatched an envoy, Gan Ying, westward in 97 CE with instructions to reach the Roman Empire; Gan Ying got as far as the Persian Gulf and turned back, reportedly after Parthian intermediaries described the onward sea voyage in terms alarming enough to deter him. Whether the Parthians were being helpful or were protecting the middleman position that Part II of this series will examine is not recorded, though the commercial incentives are not difficult to reconstruct.

When the Han collapsed in 220 CE, Chinese control over the corridor collapsed with it, and the route entered a long period of fragmentation under a succession of northern dynasties, many of them founded by steppe peoples who had settled inside the former Han frontier. This mattered less for trade than one might expect. The oasis kingdoms continued functioning, merchants continued moving, and the Sogdian commercial networks that would dominate Silk Road trade for the next several centuries expanded during precisely this period of Chinese political disunity — because the trade did not actually require an emperor. It required demand at both ends and a tolerable level of violence in the middle, and those conditions could be supplied by local kingdoms and merchant self-organization when no empire was available to supply them.

What Zhang Qian’s story offers this series, beyond a starting date, is a template that will recur throughout the centuries that follow: the Silk Road was never built by anyone who set out to build a trade network, a cultural exchange, or a highway for religion and disease. It was built by generals fighting for corridors, emperors chasing horses, merchants filling gaps between empires, and diplomats failing at their actual assignments and stumbling into something larger instead. The infrastructure — garrisons, watchtowers, oasis administration — came from state military necessity. The trade came from opportunists filling the space that infrastructure created. And the consequences, which would eventually include the spread of Buddhism into China, plague into Europe, and gunpowder into a world unprepared for it, came from nobody in particular, accumulating instead over centuries of individually small, individually rational decisions made by people who could not have seen, and mostly did not care, what they were collectively assembling.
