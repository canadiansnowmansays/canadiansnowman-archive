This ledger’s fourth part traced the political backlash building against AI infrastructure at the state and federal level — Bernie Sanders’ sovereign-wealth-fund proposal, Virginia’s data-centre energy tax, New York’s moratorium. Two pieces of reporting, both published on August 19, 2026, push that argument down to the level where it actually gets decided: the town council meeting, the property tax bill, the recall petition. One tells the story of a county that made the bargain work spectacularly. The other tells the story of a city where the same bargain collapsed within months of being signed. Read together, they suggest the difference was never really about the money. It was about whether anyone believed the number they were being told.

The County That Got It Right, By Its Own Account

As Cecilia Kang reported for the New York Times, Loudoun County, Virginia, now hosts more than 250 data centres — the highest concentration on Earth, built on a foundation laid by early-1990s federal fibre infrastructure and a pivotal 2000 zoning decision that classified data centres as ordinary office parks, sidestepping the regulatory scrutiny that later projects elsewhere would face. The county’s business personal property tax on computer equipment inside those facilities is projected to bring in roughly $1.3 billion next year alone, close to forty percent of Loudoun’s total tax revenue. The county has used that windfall to cut residential property taxes by about thirty percent over the past decade, in a jurisdiction that already has the highest median household income in the United States, while funding a $102 million recreation centre, a $22 million conversion of James Monroe’s estate into parkland, and new schools.

This is the case study every economic-development office in the country wants to replicate, and Loudoun’s staff know it — officials from Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Delaware have toured Data Center Alley seeking the formula. Buddy Rizer, the county’s economic development director, calls the current pushback “the great overreaction,” noting data centres occupy just three percent of Loudoun’s land while employing roughly 15,000 people and, by his account, saving the county from the fiscal condition many comparable jurisdictions now face.

The Cracks in the Model Everyone Wants to Copy

Even Loudoun’s success story carries its own dissent, and this series should resist the temptation to treat the county as an uncomplicated win. Juli Briskman, the Board of Supervisors member overseeing land and zoning policy, proposed a moratorium on new data centres in July 2026, framing the county’s fiscal position in the language of addiction rather than achievement: “We’ve become addicted to the data centers for their tax revenues, but at what cost?” That question has concrete answers in the experience of residents like Greg Pirio, who watched his Sterling neighbourhood transform after Vantage began construction nearby in 2023, and now lives roughly two hundred yards from diesel generators and gas turbines the company installed after the local utility, Dominion Energy, failed to deliver the grid access it had promised. Vicky Hu, an Ashburn homeowner who bought her property specifically for its adjoining protected greenbelt, was informed in 2026 that Dominion plans to erect a 185-foot high-voltage tower in her backyard to serve new data-centre demand. “There are no boundaries,” she told Kang — a sentence that reads as the precise inverse of Rizer’s confident “we struck the right balance.”

Loudoun’s own governance response is instructive. The Board of Supervisors voted in 2025 to end the two-decade-old office-park zoning classification that had let data centres bypass public review, meaning future projects will, for the first time, require the kind of hearings and approvals Independence, Missouri residents wish they’d had before their own deal was struck. Amazon’s July 2026 application to build four more centres in the county may be rejected outright by board members who supported the zoning change — a sign that even the model jurisdiction has concluded its own formula needs a ceiling.

The City Where the Bargain Broke

As David McCabe reported for the New York Times, roughly nine hundred miles west, in Independence, Missouri, the same fundamental trade — tax breaks in exchange for a data centre’s promised revenue — produced a councilman facing removal from office rather than a mayor touring conferences. In March 2026, Independence’s City Council voted five to two to award Nebius, a Dutch AI infrastructure firm, tax breaks totalling $6.26 billion over twenty years on a $150 billion data-centre project: a 90 percent break on personal property taxes and a 98 percent break on real property taxes, in a city whose entire general fund totalled roughly $85 million the year before. Councilman John Perkins, who cast one of the five votes, now faces a recall election on September 1, 2026, driven by more than 1,200 resident signatures and a level of public anger a June council meeting captured vividly: standing-room crowds, children holding protest signs, a mayor threatening to cut the microphone of anyone making “personal, impertinent or slanderous remarks.”

The contrast with Loudoun is not simply one of scale, though the numbers are instructive on their own — Independence’s twenty-year incentive package is nearly five times the size of its entire annual general fund, a leverage ratio nothing in Loudoun’s decades-long build-out resembles. The deeper contrast is procedural and reputational. Loudoun spent two decades compounding trust through delivered results — visible schools, cut property taxes, functioning roads — before residents were asked to accept the next expansion on faith. Independence asked residents to accept a $6.26 billion, twenty-year commitment on the promise of $35 million to $55 million in annual revenue that had not yet materialised, defended by a company lawyer’s warning that the project was “unlikely” to proceed at the same investment level without the breaks — the precise argument-from-scarcity this series flagged as a negotiating tactic, not a fact, when it appeared in Part IV’s account of state-level data-centre fights.

What the Recall Wave Actually Measures

McCabe’s reporting situates Independence within a genuinely national pattern, not an isolated local grievance. Festus, Missouri saw a successful recall-signature drive against officials who approved a $6 billion project, currently tangled in legal challenges. Mason, Michigan and Yukon, Oklahoma both have recall votes scheduled for November 2026 over data-centre-related council decisions. In 2025, two members of the Georgia Public Service Commission lost their seats over data-centre-driven electricity price concerns, and a Virginia state delegate lost his own race after his opponent’s campaign branded him “Data Center Geary” — proof this backlash cuts across conventional political alignment rather than sorting neatly into one party’s coalition. Bernie Sanders, who this series has already quoted in Part IV proposing a federal equity-stake mechanism, offered McCabe a more populist framing of the same underlying sentiment: officials who won’t stand up to a large corporation, he argued, shouldn’t remain in office. Notably, Amy Kremer of Humans First — a conservative-leaning group pushing for more AI regulation — described the same recall dynamic in nearly identical terms from the opposite end of the political spectrum, evidence that data-centre siting fights are becoming one of the rare issues capable of producing genuine left-right alignment on the ground, whatever the partisan framing looks like in Washington.

The Independence case also illustrates how quickly the political consequences can arrive once a vote is cast, which is itself a data point worth weighing against Loudoun’s decades-long timeline. In April 2026, barely a month after the tax-break vote, two council members who had supported the Nebius deal were on the ballot for re-election. Jared Fears, canvassing the day before his vote, described a resident who told him flatly she did not believe the project would benefit the city financially and expected it to raise her electricity costs; he lost his bid for an at-large seat to a challenger campaigning on greater accountability. Bridget McCandless, another council member who had voted for the incentive package, lost her own bid for mayor in the same cycle. Perkins was not up for re-election that spring, which is precisely why his opponents turned to a recall petition rather than waiting for the ballot box to do the work — a mechanism Missouri law makes available specifically for cases where residents feel a representative’s term has outlasted their confidence in a single, consequential vote.

What the recall wave actually measures, I’d argue, is not opposition to data centres as such — Independence’s own McKenna Cobb, who led the recall effort, did not campaign on shutting the project down, and Nebius’s local vice president insisted the company stays out of political races while simultaneously describing the council vote as being “for the future of Independence.” What the recall wave measures is the gap between a number a company or council promises and a number residents can verify for themselves before the concrete is poured. Loudoun’s low property taxes and new schools are visible, spendable, arrived. Independence’s promised $35 to $55 million in annual revenue is, as of the recall vote, still a projection attached to a facility not yet generating it, secured against a twenty-year tax break already locked into law regardless of whether the projection holds.

The TACO Pattern, Localised

This series’ TACO framework usually tracks how national or corporate power responds to pressure, but it maps cleanly onto the municipal level too, and the two stories together show every stage of it running in parallel at different jurisdictions. Loudoun’s Board of Supervisors, facing Briskman’s addiction framing and residents like Hu and Pirio, chose Accommodate: ending the office-park zoning loophole, opening future projects to public review, without reversing the underlying economic relationship that built the county’s current prosperity. Independence’s council, facing nearly identical resident concern before the vote, chose something closer to Obfuscate — Perkins’s repeated insistence at the March meeting that “the protections are there” and he felt “very comfortable,” delivered to a room that would spend the following months trying to remove him from office specifically because they did not share that comfort.

The company language sits inside its own familiar category. CloudHQ’s Hossein Fateh, quoted defending Loudoun’s centres, reached for the argument this series has heard from nearly every infrastructure company facing local resistance: you can’t have Netflix, Instagram, or Uber Eats without us, so the argument goes, therefore the argument about siting, incentives, and community cost is somehow settled by the existence of consumer convenience. It is the data-centre-era update of “you wouldn’t have electricity without utilities” — true as far as it goes, and entirely beside the point residents in Sterling, Ashburn, and Independence are actually raising, which is not whether the technology should exist but who bears its physical and fiscal cost, and whether that group had a genuine say before the decision was made.

The Question Answered at National Scale

Everything this essay has traced so far — Loudoun’s patient two-decade accumulation of trust, Independence’s collapse of it within a single vote — turns out to be a local instance of a pattern David Wallace-Wells described at the scale of the entire country in an Opinion piece for the New York Times published August 31, 2026. The numbers he cites are not a modest confirmation of the backlash this ledger has already documented. They describe something closer to a supermajority. A Heatmap Pro and Embold Research poll found three in four Americans opposed to a data centre being built in their own community, with opposition breaking above seventy percent in every region of the country and more than half the public now backing a national moratorium. A year earlier, on the same question, the public had been roughly split. Net approval swung sixty-two percentage points in twelve months — one of the fastest reversals of public opinion on any issue in recent memory, comparable in scale to the shifts this series has already flagged as historically unusual.

What makes Wallace-Wells’s reporting more than a bigger version of the Loudoun-Independence contrast is his insistence on ruling out the easy explanations, one by one, using exactly the kind of comparative evidence this ledger has lacked until now. Call it environmentalism, and you have to explain why coal plants poll better than data centres. Call it NIMBYism, and you have to explain why the same communities supported solar and wind farms by three to one only three years ago. Call it anti-corporate sentiment, and you have to explain why nearby Amazon distribution centres, semiconductor plants, and even nuclear power stations all poll better too. Data-centre opposition, in other words, is not a proxy for some other, more familiar grievance. It is its own thing, and the county-level fights this ledger has spent nine parts documenting are apparently a fair sample of a genuinely national mood rather than a scattering of unusually vocal local disputes.

That matters for the trust-based explanation this essay has already offered for the Loudoun-Independence divergence, because Wallace-Wells’s reporting confirms the mechanism runs the same way nationally as it does locally. Even the most generous version of the industry’s pitch — and he offers one, pointing to Quincy, Washington, where roughly thirty data centres now cover more than half the town’s property-tax base and have funded a $120 million high school, a $15 million aquatic centre, and a 143,000-square-foot sports complex, or to Richland Parish, Louisiana, where a Meta project delivered first-grade teachers a $51,000 bonus larger than most of their annual salaries — does not appear to be moving the national number. The sales pitch, as he puts it, looks on paper like it should sit in an unassailable political sweet spot: better public services and lower taxes, without any visible downside. And it is failing anyway, almost everywhere, which is the same asymmetry this essay found between Loudoun’s demonstrated results and Independence’s undelivered promise, just playing out even in places where the promise has already been kept.

Wallace-Wells’s own explanation for that failure is worth setting directly against the TACO framework this series has used throughout: people are not weighing the concentrated local benefit against the diffuse local cost so much as they have simply stopped believing the people making the pitch. The activist and podcaster Saul Levin, who tracks the backlash for a show called The Hum, gave him the sentence this ledger will keep returning to: “This is not a fight about technology. This is a fight about control. This is about who controls the future.” That is Perkins’s “the protections are there,” and Fateh’s “you can’t have Netflix without us,” restated as a diagnosis rather than a defence — evidence that residents in Sterling and Ashburn and Independence were never really evaluating a spreadsheet. They were evaluating whether the person holding the spreadsheet had earned the right to be believed.

Two further details from Wallace-Wells’s reporting sharpen the stakes of the recall wave this essay has already tracked. The Princeton researcher Arvind Narayanan calculated that a typical one-year state-level moratorium — the kind Loudoun’s Briskman proposed and dozens of other jurisdictions have since enacted — would slow the advance of AI capabilities by a matter of hours. And the EPA has moved to eliminate the federal requirement that states announce data-centre plans and solicit public feedback before they proceed, stripping away, at the federal level, precisely the review process Loudoun’s own Board of Supervisors decided in 2025 it could no longer do without. Both details suggest the same uncomfortable conclusion for anyone hoping September 1’s recall vote settles anything: even a wave of successful local revolts may do remarkably little to slow the underlying build-out, while a federal government moving in the opposite direction guarantees the trust deficit this essay has documented has no obvious mechanism left to close.

The Number That Actually Matters

If this ledger has a running thesis by its ninth part, it is that AI’s financial architecture keeps producing enormous numbers — $500 billion project costs, $662 billion in off-balance-sheet lease liabilities, $200 billion jury awards, $6.26 billion in twenty-year tax breaks — that arrive pre-packaged as settled facts and only later reveal themselves as negotiating positions, projections, or bets. Loudoun’s $1.3 billion in projected tax revenue is real because two decades of prior years already proved the model works at that county’s specific scale, with that county’s specific tax structure, under that county’s specific level of public trust in its own economic development office. Independence’s $35 to $55 million is not yet real by the same standard, and the twenty-year, $6.26 billion commitment securing it was made before residents had the years of delivered evidence Loudoun took two decades to accumulate. The recall vote on September 1 will not, strictly speaking, decide whether that number ever becomes real. It will decide whether the people asked to live next to the facility believe anyone who told them it would.
