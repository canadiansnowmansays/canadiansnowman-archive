Every monarchy examined so far in this series has had some theoretical limit built into it somewhere — Zhou China’s revocable Mandate, the Israelite prophet’s power to depose an anointed king, even Rome’s careful semantic dodge around the word itself. What none of them had, in any of the cases this series has covered, was a limit written down as an enforceable legal document, imposed on a reigning king by his own subjects, specifying in concrete and binding language exactly what the crown could and could not do. That document exists, it was forced into being in a field in England in June of 1215, and the king who signed it did everything in his power afterward to make sure it never actually took effect — which is, in its own way, the most instructive part of the whole story.

John, who became king of England in 1199 following the death of his brother Richard I, inherited a position that was already considerably weaker than it appeared on paper, and much of what went wrong for him over the following sixteen years traces back to decisions made before he ever took the throne. His father, Henry II, and his brother Richard had built and maintained a sprawling cross-Channel territory — the Angevin Empire this series touched on in Part V, assembled substantially through Eleanor of Aquitaine’s marriages — that required constant, expensive military attention to hold together against an increasingly assertive French crown under King Philip II Augustus. John lost most of it. Through a combination of military incompetence, questionable political judgment, and Philip’s own considerable skill as an opportunist, John forfeited Normandy, Anjou, Maine, and Touraine to the French crown by 1204, a catastrophic reduction in Angevin territory that earned him, even among contemporaries who had no reason to be generous to him, the nickname “Softsword.”

The loss of these territories was not simply an embarrassment. It was a direct financial catastrophe, because the English crown’s revenues from continental holdings had substantially subsidized John’s father’s and brother’s expensive wars and court expenditures. Determined to reclaim what he had lost, John spent the following decade extracting money from his English barons through mechanisms that were technically legal under feudal custom but were pushed to a degree of severity that made the technicality feel almost beside the point. Scutage — a payment barons could make in place of providing military service directly — was levied eleven times during John’s reign, far more frequently than under his predecessors, and at rates his barons increasingly experienced as extortionate. Feudal reliefs, the payments an heir owed the crown to formally inherit his father’s lands and title, were set at wildly inconsistent and often ruinous amounts depending on how much John’s advisors calculated a particular baron could be squeezed for. Wardship, the crown’s right to manage and profit from the estates of minor heirs until they came of age, was exploited aggressively, with John’s officials frequently stripping wealthy estates of resources during the period they controlled them. And the royal forest laws, which reserved enormous tracts of land for the king’s exclusive hunting rights and criminalized ordinary use of that land by the people who actually lived near it, were enforced with a severity that generated constant, grinding resentment across the country.

One episode from earlier in his reign is worth including here because it shows the same pattern of impulsive overreach that would define his later conduct with the barons. In 1200, John married Isabella of Angoulême, a marriage that secured him a strategically valuable French county — except that Isabella had already been betrothed to Hugh de Lusignan, a powerful nobleman from a family whose loyalty John’s continental territories depended on. Hugh, understandably furious at having his intended bride simply taken by his own king, appealed to Philip Augustus of France, who was, as John’s feudal overlord for his continental possessions, entitled to hear exactly this kind of grievance. When John refused to appear before the French court to answer the complaint, Philip declared John’s continental territories forfeit, providing the legal pretext for the very campaign of conquest that stripped John of Normandy and the other Angevin lands within the following four years. A marriage taken impulsively, without regard for the political debt it would leave unpaid, cost John a substantial share of his inheritance — a smaller-scale, faster-moving version of the same lesson Part V drew from the Habsburgs, that a crown’s marriages are never merely personal, and the bill for treating them carelessly eventually arrives.

John compounded all of this with a catastrophic dispute with the Papacy over the appointment of the Archbishop of Canterbury, refusing to accept Pope Innocent III’s chosen candidate, Stephen Langton, which resulted in England being placed under interdict in 1208 — suspending most religious sacraments across the entire kingdom for six years — and John himself being formally excommunicated in 1209. He eventually capitulated in 1213, going so far as to surrender his kingdom to the Pope and receive it back as a papal fief in a humiliating display of submission that secured him, at least, the Pope’s political backing going forward — a backing that would matter enormously for what happened after the barons finally rebelled.

That rebellion had been building for years, and it crystallized in 1215 after John’s latest and most disastrous continental military campaign, an attempt to reclaim his lost French territories that ended in decisive defeat at the Battle of Bouvines in July of 1214. Returning to England having spent enormous sums of extracted baronial money on a campaign that produced nothing but further humiliation, John faced a coalition of barons who had simply run out of patience with a king who took their money, their military service, and their inheritance rights, and delivered nothing in exchange but further extraction and now outright military failure abroad. In the spring of 1215, this coalition of barons, with London’s support, presented John with a set of demands. Facing armed rebellion and the loss of London itself, John agreed to negotiate, and on the fifteenth of June, 1215, at Runnymede, a meadow beside the Thames roughly twenty miles from London, he affixed his royal seal to the document that would become known as Magna Carta — the Great Charter.

The document itself, running to sixty-three clauses in its original 1215 form, is considerably more medieval and situational than its later mythology suggests — a substantial portion of it addresses extremely specific feudal grievances about fish weirs on the Thames, the width of cloth sold in markets, and the precise conditions under which the crown could seize a debtor’s chattels, none of which have survived into modern constitutional memory for good reason. But embedded within that thicket of thirteenth-century feudal housekeeping are a handful of clauses that did survive, because they articulated something genuinely new: that the king’s power was not, in fact, absolute, and that specific and enumerable limits on it could be written down and made binding. Clause 12 held that no scutage or aid — the very taxation mechanisms John had exploited so aggressively — could be levied without the consent of the kingdom’s common council, an early ancestor of the principle of no taxation without representation that would echo, centuries later, in a rather different rebellion across the Atlantic. Clause 39 promised that no free man would be imprisoned, dispossessed, outlawed, or exiled except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land — the root of what would eventually become habeas corpus and due process protections across the English-speaking world. Clause 40 stated simply and directly that the crown would not sell, deny, or delay justice to anyone — a rebuke aimed squarely at a king who had, by all contemporary accounts, treated access to royal justice as a revenue stream to be priced according to what a petitioner could afford rather than a right owed equally to all.

What makes John’s story genuinely instructive, rather than simply a tale of a bad king brought to heel by good barons, is what he did immediately after sealing the document. He had no intention of honoring it, and he moved with real speed to escape its terms. Within weeks of Runnymede, John appealed directly to Pope Innocent III — the same pope to whom he had surrendered his kingdom as a fief two years earlier, a relationship he had cultivated for exactly this kind of leverage — arguing that the charter had been extracted from him under duress and was therefore invalid. Innocent agreed, issuing a papal bull in August of 1215 that annulled Magna Carta entirely, describing it as “illegal, unjust, harmful to royal rights and shameful to the English people,” and threatening excommunication against anyone who attempted to enforce it. John, backed by papal authority, prepared to reassert his power by force, and the barons, having watched their negotiated settlement dissolved by exactly the kind of unaccountable authority they had tried to constrain, rose in open rebellion in what became known as the First Barons’ War, going so far as to invite Prince Louis of France — the same Philip Augustus’s son who had already benefited enormously from John’s earlier territorial losses — to cross the Channel and claim the English throne for himself.

John died in October of 1216, in the midst of this ongoing civil war, reportedly of dysentery contracted after a disastrous crossing of the Wash estuary in which he lost a substantial part of his baggage train and treasury to the tide — a genuinely undignified final chapter for a king whose entire reign had been one long argument about money he did not have and had extracted too aggressively from people who eventually stopped tolerating it. His death, rather than his signature at Runnymede, is arguably what actually saved Magna Carta’s legacy: his nine-year-old son, crowned as Henry III, inherited a civil war rather than a settled kingdom, and Henry’s regency council, desperate to undercut support for the French pretender Louis and rally the barons back to the young king’s side, reissued Magna Carta in 1216 and again in 1217 as a gesture of good faith — not because anyone had suddenly decided the document was sacred, but because it was, in that specific moment, useful political leverage for a regency that needed the barons’ loyalty more than John ever had. It was reissued yet again in 1225, in a version that would become the template cited in English law for the following several centuries, and its survival owes less to any king’s principled acceptance of its terms than to a long chain of desperate political circumstances in which reaffirming it happened to serve whoever currently held power.

The document’s more famous modern legacy owes almost everything to a much later revival, and it’s worth tracing briefly, because the gap between 1225 and that revival is itself instructive. For roughly three centuries following its final confirmed reissue, Magna Carta functioned mainly as an obscure piece of feudal law cited occasionally in specific property and procedural disputes, not as any kind of celebrated constitutional touchstone. That changed dramatically in the early seventeenth century, when Sir Edward Coke, an English judge and legal scholar locked in a running constitutional battle with King James I and later Charles I over the scope of royal prerogative, revived Magna Carta and specifically Clause 39 as a foundational precedent for the rule of law and the principle that even the crown was subject to it. Coke’s interpretation was, by most modern historians’ assessment, considerably more expansive than what the original 1215 barons had actually intended — they were protecting their own specific feudal privileges as landholding nobles, not articulating a universal theory of individual liberty — but Coke’s version is the one that stuck, precisely because it was useful to the political fight he was actually having with the Stuart monarchy of his own era. That reinterpreted, Coke-flavored Magna Carta crossed the Atlantic with English colonists who considered themselves entitled to the same protections against arbitrary taxation and imprisonment that the document, in its now-mythologized form, seemed to guarantee every English subject. It surfaces explicitly in the language of colonial charters, in the specific grievances the Declaration of Independence would later level at George III, and in the structure of the Fifth Amendment’s due process clause in the United States Constitution — a fairly direct rhetorical descendant of Clause 39’s promise that no free man would be deprived of liberty or property except by lawful judgment. A document John signed only to spend his final year fighting to escape had, four centuries later, become the founding vocabulary for an entirely new country’s argument against a different king altogether.

That accident of survival is worth sitting with, because it undercuts the more triumphant version of this story often told in retrospect. Magna Carta did not immediately create constitutional government, did not immediately protect ordinary people who were not free landholding barons, and was violated, reinterpreted, and selectively enforced by English kings for centuries after 1215. What it did was establish, as a matter of documented legal precedent rather than abstract political philosophy, the specific and durable idea that a king’s power over taxation and over a subject’s liberty could be written down as an enumerated limit rather than left entirely to the crown’s own discretion — an idea that later English constitutional crises, the English Civil War this series will examine directly in the next part, and eventually the American colonists rebelling against a king they explicitly accused of violating exactly this kind of settled right, would all return to and build upon.

I’ve written elsewhere, in an earlier essay on this same anniversary, about the resemblance between John’s temperament and that of a rather more contemporary figure — a leader who treats every institutional check on his authority, whether a court, a legislature, or a negotiated agreement he himself signed, as an illegitimate imposition to be escaped through whatever leverage is available, be it a friendly pope in 1215 or a friendly judge eight centuries later. I won’t belabor that comparison again here; the history stands on its own, and readers who want to draw the line forward to the present are welcome to draw it themselves. What matters for this series is the mechanism, not the modern echo of it: John proved that a king could be forced, under sufficient pressure, to sign away a piece of his own unaccountable power. He also proved, in the very same year, that signing is not the same thing as accepting, and that every subsequent generation which wanted that signature to actually mean something would have to keep fighting, reissuing, and reinterpreting the same sixty-three clauses to make it stick.
