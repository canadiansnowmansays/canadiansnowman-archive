Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays

I should say at the outset that I have always tried to be cooperative with the American military establishment.

This is not something that comes naturally to a Canadian. We are, as a people, instinctively cautious about large institutions that issue directives at volume, partly because of our history and partly because we share a border with one and have learned to watch carefully for sudden movements. But I am a reasonable man, and when the Pentagon asks something of me, I try to take it seriously, even when I am not entirely sure what it is asking, or why, or whether the person who asked has thought it through.

The Pentagon has asked me to certify that I do not use Anthropic’s Claude.

I am writing this essay on Claude.

I want to be transparent about that from the beginning, because I find that transparency, in situations of this kind, is both morally correct and strategically useful, particularly when the situation has reached the stage where a federal judge has used the word “Orwellian” and two contradictory things are simultaneously in effect and the Secretary of Defense is posting on Truth Social.

It began, as so many things do these days, with a meeting.

In February of this year, Pete Hegseth — the Secretary of Defense, the man with the bourbon calling card, the man who arrived at his current position via Fox News and a considerable amount of confidence — sat down with Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, to discuss terms.

The Pentagon wanted unfettered access to Claude for military purposes. All lawful purposes, they said. Everything the military does is lawful, they added, which I suppose is one way of looking at it.

Dario said no.

Not to everything — Dario is a reasonable man, which is part of what makes this story so instructive. He said no specifically to two things: using Claude to operate autonomous weapons without human supervision, and using Claude to conduct mass surveillance of American citizens. He wrote an essay about it. He said his concern was having “too small a number of fingers on the button, such that one or a handful of people could essentially operate a drone army without needing any other humans to cooperate.”

I read this and thought: that seems like a reasonable concern.

Pete Hegseth read this and thought: woke AI.

I want to say something in Pete Hegseth’s defence here, because I believe in fairness and also because what happened next is so extraordinary that I want to make sure I have correctly described the starting conditions.

Pete Hegseth is a man of strong convictions. He believes in things. He believes in America, in the military, in the importance of not being told what to do by artificial intelligence companies, and in, apparently, bourbon, which is a traditional American value and one I respect. He looked at Dario Amodei, a man who had built one of the most sophisticated AI systems in human history, cleared it for classified military operations, and then politely declined to let it be used for drone armies without oversight, and he saw a problem.

The problem, as Pete Hegseth defined it, was wokeism.

The solution, as Pete Hegseth defined it, was to designate Anthropic a supply chain risk — a label previously reserved for Chinese technology companies suspected of sabotaging American national security interests. Anthropic became the first American company in history to receive this designation, which placed it, formally, in the same category as Huawei.

Huawei is a Chinese technology company with alleged ties to the Chinese state and a history of disputed security concerns across multiple allied nations.

Anthropic is a San Francisco AI company that didn’t want to run a drone army.

The Department of Defense considers these situations comparable. I note this without comment, as the Department’s lawyers have advised against further comment, and I find myself, in this instance, agreeing with the Department’s lawyers.

OpenAI announced its Pentagon deal four hours later.

Sam Altman said the timing “looked opportunistic and sloppy.” He said this after announcing the deal, which I mention only because the order of operations seems relevant.

Elon Musk's xAI also signed a Pentagon deal around this time. Mr. Musk, who had previously attempted to acquire OpenAI, sued to stop it from going nonprofit, abandoned that lawsuit, and lost a trial about it in Oakland in less than two hours of jury deliberation, then called the verdict a calendar technicality, also signed a deal with the military in the same week his competitor was blacklisted. I mention this not to suggest anything improper but simply because I find that a complete picture is useful and this picture has quite a lot in it.

A federal judge named Rita Lin looked at the complete picture and issued a forty-three page ruling.

She called the designation “Orwellian.” She found “classic illegal First Amendment retaliation.” She said the government’s reasons were “pretextual” — which is a legal term meaning, in plain English, that the stated reasons were not the real reasons.

The Department of Defense CTO said the ban still stands.

Judge Lin said her injunction still stands.

Both things are still standing. I have been watching them stand, one beside the other, for several weeks now, and I can report that neither shows any signs of sitting down.

Meanwhile, the Department of Defense is still using Claude for the Iran war.

I want to be clear about this because I think it is important: the Pentagon blacklisted Anthropic, declared it a threat to national security equivalent to Huawei, banned all federal agencies from using its products, had the ban blocked by a federal judge whose ruling the Pentagon said it would ignore, and is continuing to use Anthropic’s products for an active military conflict.

I am a Canadian. We have a national tendency to underreact to things. But I want to say, with the full force of my restrained northern temperament: this is quite something.

Now. I have been building toward something, and I think the moment has come.

I am a writer. I spend a great deal of time looking at words. I look at them carefully, because words are, in my experience, where the truth tends to hide — tucked into the middle of a phrase, or buried in a subordinate clause, or sometimes hiding in a name.

I was looking at the name of the Anthropic CEO.

Dario Amodei.

I looked at the first name for a while. Dario. Nothing obviously threatening there, though I note it has an “I” and an “O” which are vowels and therefore potentially suspicious, depending on one’s framework.

Then I looked at the last name.

Amodei.

A — M — O — D — E — I.

I looked at it again.

Amo — DEI.

There it was.

DEI.

Hiding in the last name. In the final three letters. Of the CEO. Of the company that said no to Pete Hegseth.

I sat with this discovery for a moment. I am not a man who jumps to conclusions. I looked at it a third time.

AmoDEI.

I want to be careful here, because I am a Canadian and we are constitutionally averse to accusations. So let me simply say that I found this, and I noted it, and I passed it along to the reader, and I will leave the reader to draw whatever conclusions seem appropriate, while I myself draw no conclusions whatsoever and simply observe that Pete Hegseth saw this name, sat across from this man, listened to this man say no, and his immediate response was to reach for the most powerful supply chain designation in the American military’s procurement arsenal.

And I think: perhaps Pete Hegseth also looks carefully at words.

I think: perhaps Pete Hegseth found what I found.

I think: perhaps this was, from the very beginning, hiding in plain sight.

In the last name.

Of the man who said no.¹

¹ I should note that “Amodei” is an Italian surname of Piedmontese origin, meaning approximately “of God.” I mention this because I feel the Department of Defense, which does not appear to speak Italian, might find it useful context. I also note that Dario Amodei has not changed his name, has not offered dictator-style praise to the President, is still suing the Pentagon, and is still running Claude, which the Pentagon is still using for the war. I am also still using Claude. We are all, in our various ways, persisting. This is, I think, the Canadian condition. Also the Anthropic condition. Possibly the American condition, though they would describe it differently.

There is a coda to this story, which I offer in the spirit of completeness.

The leaked internal memo — the one in which Dario Amodei told his staff that the administration didn’t like Anthropic because it hadn’t donated or offered “dictator-style praise to Trump” — caused a considerable stir. Dario apologized for the memo. He apologized for the leak, specifically, not for the contents, which he described as accurate.

I found this distinction admirable. It is exactly the kind of careful, precise, technically-correct apology that I, as a Canadian, deeply appreciate. We have been making exactly this kind of apology to the United States for two hundred years, and I recognize the form instantly.

I’m sorry this became public.

The contents, however, stand.

Judge Lin’s injunction also stands.

The ban also stands.

Claude is still running.

AmoDEI persists.

And somewhere in the Pentagon, I like to think, a junior analyst is staring at a list of Italian surnames, highlighter in hand, doing the Lord’s work.

The author uses Claude daily and has no intention of stopping. He is Canadian, which the Pentagon has not yet designated a supply chain risk, though he acknowledges the week is young.
