94 year old Raúl Castro – The Indicted Man : credit Ismael Francisco/Associated Press

On May 20, 1902, the United States ended its military occupation of Cuba. One hundred and twenty-four years later, to the day, the United States indicted the man who has led Cuba for most of the last half-century and moved an aircraft carrier into the southern Caribbean.

The timing was not accidental. Nothing about this is accidental.

The Charges

On Wednesday, May 20, 2026 — Cuban Independence Day — the Department of Justice unsealed a federal indictment against Raúl Modesto Castro Ruz, the 94-year-old former president of Cuba, former defense minister, brother of Fidel, and the man who, despite holding no official title, remains perhaps the most powerful figure on the island.

The charges: murder and conspiracy to kill American citizens, stemming from the Cuban military’s downing of two civilian aircraft over the waters near Cuba on February 24, 1996. Four people died that day — three American citizens and one legal permanent resident — all associated with Brothers to the Rescue, a Miami-based Cuban exile group whose planes had been making flights over Cuban waters to assist refugees fleeing by raft. Cuba said the planes had violated its airspace. International aviation authorities disputed the claim.

The indictment was not new. It was secretly returned by a federal grand jury on April 23, 2026 — four weeks before it was unsealed, and thirteen days after the April 10 diplomatic talks in Havana that this series covered in Part 17. Which means: while American officials were sitting across from Cuban officials in Havana discussing the terms of a possible deal, a federal grand jury in Miami had already indicted one of the men those officials answer to. The administration was negotiating and indicting simultaneously. This is not a contradiction. It is the method.

Five other defendants were named: five fighter pilots involved in the attack. One of them, Luis Raúl González-Pardo Rodríguez, was discovered living in Florida last year and arrested in November on immigration fraud charges — he had applied for permanent residency without disclosing his thirty years of service in the Cuban Air Force as a MiG pilot. His arrest appears to have provided the renewed momentum for the broader case.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche announced the charges at a news conference held at the Freedom Tower in Miami — the building where Cuban refugees fleeing the revolution were once processed and received aid. The venue was chosen for its symbolism. The audience was chosen for its politics: mayors, local Cuban American politicians, former prosecutors who had worked on earlier, unsuccessful efforts to bring exactly these charges. The presence of public officials at a criminal indictment announcement was, as the Times noted, unusual — highlighting the political nature of the exercise.

Blanche’s message was direct: “The United States and President Trump does not — and will not — forget its citizens.” He added, in a formulation that bears examination: he expected Castro to appear in the United States, whether “by his own will or another way.”

By his own will or another way. The phrase is doing a great deal of work.

The Playbook

It cannot be lost on anyone in the Cuban government — and it was not lost on the Cuban ambassador who responded to it within hours — that the Trump administration used a federal indictment against Nicolás Maduro as the pretext for a military raid that seized Venezuela’s leader in January 2026.

The sequence was: indict, then extract. The indictment provided the legal architecture. The Special Operations raid provided the physical one. Whether the same sequence is planned for Cuba is, as of Wednesday evening, an open question. Trump, asked directly whether he would use the military to extract Castro, said: “I don’t want to say that.” He then added, in what he apparently intended as reassurance: “No, there won’t be escalation. I don’t think there needs to be.” He then added: “Look, the place is falling apart, it’s a mess.”

The place is falling apart. That is true. It is falling apart in significant part because the United States has made it fall apart — through an oil blockade that has left Cuba with no fuel reserves, twenty-two-hour blackouts in the capital, and citizens buying food in daily increments because they cannot run a refrigerator long enough to keep meat from spoiling. Whether “it’s falling apart” is an observation or a boast depends on where you are standing. The view from a rooftop in Havana, where people sleep to escape the heat because the power has been off for twenty-two hours, is different from the view aboard Air Force One.

The USS Nimitz and its escort warships entered the southern Caribbean on Wednesday. Southern Command confirmed it. The carrier is expected to remain for at least several days.

Nobody in Havana is confused about what this means.

USS Nimitz and escorts in the Caribbean. Because nothing says “happy Independence Day” quite like parking an aircraft carrier strike group off someone’s coast.

The Video

On the same day — the anniversary of the end of America’s previous military occupation of Cuba — Secretary of State Marco Rubio released a video address directed to the Cuban people. It was in Spanish, with English subtitles, posted on the State Department’s YouTube channel. It was, by the assessment of multiple outlets, the first time Rubio had made a direct appeal to the people of Cuba in this manner.

Rubio named GAESA — the military-run conglomerate that controls an estimated 40 to 70 percent of the Cuban economy, whose structure the New York Times detailed in an extraordinary visual investigation published May 15. He told the Cuban people that GAESA, not the American blockade, was responsible for their suffering. “The real reason you don’t have electricity, fuel or food is because those who control your country have plundered billions of dollars, but nothing has been used to help the people,” Rubio said. “President Trump is offering a new relationship between the US and Cuba. But it must be directly with you, the Cuban people, not with GAESA.”

Trump Tower Havana: satire, for now. Image credit: Baby News Network (@BabyNetworkNews on X).

The framing is strategic and it is not entirely wrong. GAESA is a problem — this series will examine it in detail in a forthcoming installment. A military conglomerate that refuses government audits, hoards profits from the central bank, registers shell companies in Panama to evade sanctions, and builds five-star luxury hotels while education spending drops 26 percent is not a defensible institution. The government’s own comptroller was fired for asking to see its books.

But Rubio’s framing asks the Cuban people to accept a premise that requires extraordinary credulity: that the country which cut off Cuba’s oil supply, barred all foreign tankers from delivering fuel, seized Cuba’s primary oil benefactor in a military raid, moved an aircraft carrier into Cuban waters, indicted Cuba’s de facto leader, and maintains the longest trade embargo in modern history is not responsible for the fact that Cubans have no electricity. It is GAESA’s fault, he says. Not the blockade. The blockade is irrelevant. The man who has dedicated his career to tightening the blockade would like you to know that the blockade has nothing to do with it.

This is the same Rubio who said in March: “The reason why they don’t have oil is because they don’t have any money to pay for oil.” The reason they don’t have money is the embargo. The reason they can’t get oil from Venezuela is that the United States seized Venezuela. The reason no other country will ship them oil is that the United States has threatened those countries with sanctions if they do. The causal chain is not complicated. Rubio knows it. The video is not aimed at people who will follow the chain. It is aimed at people who will not.

The Interview

Ambassador Ernesto Soberón Guzmán, Permanent Representative of Cuba to United Nations

On the same day, Ernesto Soberón Guzmán, Cuba’s ambassador to the United Nations, sat down with the New York Times for what was described as the first on-the-record interview a sitting Cuban government official had granted the paper in years. The decision to break that silence on this day, in this context, was itself a message.

Soberón Guzmán said what the Cuban government has been saying through back channels for months, but now said it on the record and for American public consumption: “Cuba is willing to talk about everything with the United States. There is no taboo subject in our conversations — on the basis of reciprocity and equality.”

He said what this series has documented since Part 11: that the warmongering rhetoric undermines any negotiating climate. “Obviously it does not help a climate of dialogue and trust that every other day there are statements like, ‘We are ready to take over Cuba.’ Warmongering rhetoric does not help. Building different pretexts for military aggression against Cuba, which is what they are building, does not help.”

He called the indictment “a circus — a circus they are now mounting as one more action to justify military aggression against Cuba.”

He made the argument that American analysts have largely ignored: Brothers to the Rescue had violated Cuban airspace twenty-five times before the Cuban military shot down its planes, and Cuban officials had repeatedly pleaded with American authorities to stop the flights, including in a letter from Fidel Castro to President Clinton. “How many deliberate and serious violations of U.S. airspace would any U.S. government allow before taking action?” he asked. The question is not rhetorical. It has an answer. The answer is: very few.

He called the $100 million in American food and medicine aid — offered on the condition it be distributed through the Catholic Church, not the Cuban government — “an insult.” He also said Cuba planned to accept it. That is what negotiating from the position of no fuel, no food, and no leverage looks like.

When asked about potential changes to Cuba’s political system — a system with one party and no free press — Soberón Guzmán declined to offer specifics. He instead noted the American electoral college, redistricting, and the influence of wealthy donors. “Is that the democracy they want for Cuba?” he asked. “It doesn’t interest us.”

He added the point that this series has made repeatedly: Washington maintains positive relations with numerous nations that lack democratic systems. “Thus democracy in Cuba is not the reason why the United States is applying pressure.” He is correct. Democracy has never been the reason. The reason is ninety miles, six decades of refusal, and the enduring political power of the Cuban American exile community in a state that decides presidential elections.

What Was Said and What Was Done

It is worth pausing to note what happened on a single day — May 20, 2026, the 124th anniversary of Cuban independence from American military occupation:

The Department of Justice indicted Cuba’s former president on murder charges. The acting attorney general announced the charges at the Freedom Tower in Miami, in front of an audience of Cuban American politicians, and said the defendant would appear in the United States “by his own will or another way.” The Secretary of State released a video addressed directly to the Cuban people, in Spanish, telling them their government — not the American blockade — was responsible for their suffering, and urging them to align with the Trump administration. The president of the United States said there would be “no escalation” and then declined to say whether he would use the military to extract the indicted man. An aircraft carrier entered the southern Caribbean. And Cuba’s ambassador gave his first on-the-record interview in years, calling the indictment a circus designed to justify military aggression.

All of this while the country has no oil. While blackouts last twenty-two to twenty-four hours per day. While people are buying five eggs one day and a pound of pork the next because they cannot keep food cold. While a woman in Havana whose name the Times did not publish, because she feared retribution from the police, said the power came on for one hour on Monday and two hours on Tuesday.

Raúl Castro is ninety-four years old. He is frail. He has poor hearing and difficulty speaking. He has not made public remarks in some time. He appeared at a May Day rally in Havana earlier this month, in his olive uniform with the medals and the epaulettes, looking like what he is — an old man who led a revolution and outlived most of the people who wanted to stop him.

The optics of sending an elite military team to seize him are, by any reasonable assessment, poor. But the optics of seizing Maduro were also poor, and they did it anyway. Whether this administration regards the optics as a constraint or as content for the next rally is an open question.

Maggie Khuly’s Thirty Years

The indictment has human weight beyond its geopolitical function, and it would be dishonest not to acknowledge it.

A Brothers to the Rescue aircraft, similar to the ones shot down on February 24, 1996. Four people died. Thirty years later, the United States remembered — on May 20.

Maggie Khuly is eighty years old. Her brother was one of the four people killed when the Cuban Air Force shot down the Brothers to the Rescue planes on February 24, 1996. She has waited thirty years for criminal charges to be brought against the man she holds responsible for his death. On Wednesday, she called the indictment “bittersweet.”

“It’s good in that justice in our case seems to be progressing,” she said. “It’s bitter in the sense that it’s taking 30 years and you know no good justice would ever be achieved unless we have our dead people back, which is impossible.”

Sylvia Iriondo, eighty-five, was in the third plane — the one that survived. She recalled José Basulto, the pilot, turning to her and saying: “We’re next and they’re going to shoot at us.” She pulled out a finger rosary. She took her husband’s hand. They made it to American airspace.

These are real people with real losses, and whatever the administration’s motives for bringing charges now — and the motives are transparently political — the underlying crime was real. Four people were killed. The Castro government took responsibility. The families have waited three decades. Their grief is not a pretext, even if the timing of the indictment is.

The question is whether justice for the families of the 1996 shootdown and the geopolitical use of their case as a lever to justify regime change or military action can coexist in the same legal instrument without the latter consuming the former. The evidence from Venezuela suggests they cannot. The Maduro indictment was also built on real charges — drug trafficking, in his case. It was also used as the legal architecture for a military raid. Justice and statecraft occupied the same document, and statecraft won.

The Core Deadlock — Again

Cuba says it is willing to negotiate everything. The United States has not explicitly stated what it wants. Rubio has at various points suggested that broad economic reforms might suffice — and, in his next interview, declared that nothing can change as long as the current regime holds power. On Wednesday night on Fox News, days ago, he said he doubted it was possible “to change the trajectory of Cuba as long as these people are in charge in that regime. I hope I’m wrong. We’ll give them a chance. But I don’t think it’s going to happen.”

That is the Secretary of State saying, on the record, that he believes the diplomatic track will fail — while simultaneously conducting the diplomatic track. The charitable interpretation is that this is negotiating pressure. The uncharitable interpretation is that it is the construction of a record of failure that will justify what comes next. This series noted in Part 17 that The Atlantic had reported administration officials describing the Venezuela negotiations as a “dry run” and noting that talks could function as “a form of subterfuge” — a way to build the case for more forceful action.

Soberón Guzmán sees it. “Building different pretexts for military aggression against Cuba, which is what they are building, does not help.”

William LeoGrande, a professor at American University, offered the assessment that this series has been making for eighteen parts: “The Cubans are not good at backing down.”

No. They are not. They have not backed down for five hundred and thirty years. They did not back down when Spain colonized them for four centuries. They did not back down when the United States replaced Spain and installed Batista. They did not back down during the Bay of Pigs. They did not back down during the missile crisis. They did not back down during the Special Period, when the Soviet Union collapsed and the economy contracted by 35 percent. They are not going to back down now — not because of ideology, though ideology plays a role, but because the Cuban government cannot negotiate its own dissolution and survive, and because the Cuban people, whatever they think of their government, do not want to be occupied by the country that has been strangling them for sixty-seven years.

The carrier is in the Caribbean. The indictment is on the docket. The lights are off in Havana. Baby Alejandro is in his incubator — if it’s still running, if the power came back for its hour or two today, if the voltage surge didn’t fry the circuits this time.

The island that has refused to kneel is still refusing. It is doing so in the dark, with an arrest warrant out for the man who built the revolution’s last standing wall, while the country that wrote the warrant sails a nuclear-powered aircraft carrier past its coast and wonders, out loud and on camera, why they haven’t surrendered yet.

This series will continue to follow events as they develop.
