The Three-Path Strategy

American healthcare reform can follow three possible paths, depending on political conditions. (The likelihoods below are my own rough judgments, not anyone’s forecast.)

Path A: Full Single-Payer (Medicare for All)

  • Requires: Democratic trifecta + elimination of filibuster OR 60+ Democratic senators

  • Likelihood (2025-2034): 15-20%

  • Timeline if passed: 4-6 years implementation

Path B: Regulated Multi-Payer (Swiss-German Model)

  • Requires: Bipartisan coalition of 10-15 senators + House majority

  • Likelihood (2025-2034): 30-35%

  • Timeline if passed: 6-8 years implementation

Path C: Incremental Expansion (Public Option + Reforms)

  • Requires: Reconciliation (50 votes) OR narrow bipartisan support

  • Likelihood (2025-2034): 50-60%

  • Timeline if passed: 8-10 years implementation

This plan focuses on Path C — the most achievable route — while laying groundwork for Path B or A if political conditions shift.

Phase 1: Immediate Executive Actions (Year 1)

Most of this a President can do through HHS on day one. Where an item needs Congress, I say so.

Action 1.1: Expand ACA Enrollment

Authority: Section 1311 of the ACA, which establishes the exchanges and the navigator program, gives HHS room to make enrollment easier

Implementation:

  • Streamlined or automatic enrollment in subsidized plans for people already receiving unemployment benefits, SNAP, or other federal assistance

  • Pre-populate ACA applications with federal data (opt-out instead of opt-in). Note: broader use of IRS taxpayer data is restricted by federal tax-privacy law and would need Congress.

  • Lengthen open enrollment and widen special enrollment periods (HealthCare.gov’s open enrollment currently runs from November 1 to January 15)

  • Increase funding for navigators and community enrollment assistance

Impact:

  • Reach eligible people who are uninsured today simply because no one signed them up

Action 1.2: Expand Medicaid Through Federal Incentives

Authority: Section 1115 waivers allow HHS to approve state innovations. But the expansion match rate itself is set in statute, so a richer match needs Congress.

Implementation:

  • (Requires Congress) Offer holdout states a 95% federal match (instead of the statutory 90%) for the first 3 years of expansion

  • (Requires appropriations) Provide $500 million in rural hospital stabilization grants contingent on expansion

  • (Executive) Use Section 1115 waivers to give states design flexibility, while prohibiting barriers that reduce coverage. Note: the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act now requires work requirements for expansion adults beginning in 2027, which limits what HHS can waive.

Impact:

  • Potentially flip 3-5 holdout states (Kansas, Wyoming, Wisconsin most likely)

  • Extend coverage to adults stuck in the “coverage gap” (see Part 8)

  • Stabilize rural hospitals that depend on Medicaid revenue

Action 1.3: Expand Prescription Drug Negotiation

Authority: The Inflation Reduction Act lets HHS negotiate Medicare drug prices. The number of drugs and the schedule are fixed in the statute, so expanding the count needs Congress.

What current law already does: 10 drugs with negotiated prices from January 2026, 15 more from 2027, 15 more from 2028, then up to 20 a year (Medicare Drug Price Negotiation, CMS). Medicare insulin is already capped at $35 a month, and Part D out-of-pocket costs are capped at $2,000 a year (indexed).

Implementation:

  • (Executive) Pick the highest-spend drugs the law allows, negotiate hard, and enforce the results

  • (Requires Congress) Raise the number of drugs per year and accelerate the schedule

  • (Requires Congress) Extend negotiated prices beyond Medicare to commercial insurance

Impact:

  • CMS estimated the first 10 negotiated prices would have saved Medicare about $6 billion (22%) had they applied in 2023, and cut enrollees’ out-of-pocket costs by about $1.5 billion in 2026 (Fierce Healthcare, “CMS: Negotiated drug prices would have saved Medicare $6B last year”, August 15, 2024)

  • For the second round of 15 drugs, CMS estimated net savings of about $12 billion, or 44% (CMS, “CMS Delivers Savings for Seniors on 15 Major Drugs for Cancer and Chronic Disease”, November 25, 2025)

  • More drugs means more of that

Action 1.4: Ban Surprise Medical Billing (Full Implementation)

Authority: The No Surprises Act (enacted December 2020, in effect since January 2022) and its implementing regulations

Implementation:

  • (Executive) Aggressive enforcement of existing law. Federal penalties of up to $10,000 per violation already exist.

  • (Executive) Tighten enforcement on out-of-network ancillary services — anesthesiology, radiology, pathology and lab work — which the Act already covers at in-network facilities

  • (Requires Congress) Cover ground ambulances, which the Act exempts by statute

  • (Executive) Speed up the Act’s existing federal arbitration process (independent dispute resolution), which is badly backlogged

Impact:

  • Fewer families blindsided by bills they had no way to avoid

Action 1.5: Require Hospital Price Transparency (Enforced)

Authority: Hospital Price Transparency Rule (2021) exists but is poorly enforced

Implementation:

  • Increase penalties for non-compliance from the current maximum of about $5,500/day for large hospitals (since 2022) to $10,000/day

  • Require machine-readable price files with no exemptions

  • Create a public website comparing hospital prices for common procedures

  • Mandate that hospitals show prices to patients before non-emergency procedures

Impact:

  • Enable actual price competition

Total Year 1 Impact:

  • Coverage: more eligible people enrolled; the Medicaid incentives depend on Congress and on state uptake

  • Cost reduction: drug-negotiation savings already scheduled under current law, plus enforcement gains

  • Legislative votes required: None for the core executive actions; the 95% match, rural grants, faster drug negotiation and ambulance coverage need Congress

Phase 2: Reconciliation Legislation (Year 2)

Requires: 50 Senate votes + House majority. Uses budget reconciliation to bypass filibuster.

Bill Title: “American Health Security Enhancement Act of 2026”

  • Legislative vehicle: Budget reconciliation (bypasses 60-vote threshold)

  • Senate Committees: Finance (taxes, Medicare, Medicaid) and HELP (insurance-market rules); the Budget Committee packages reconciliation

  • House Committees: Ways and Means Committee + Energy and Commerce Committee

Caution: reconciliation is limited by the Senate’s Byrd rule. Provisions whose main effect is to regulate private insurers, rather than change federal spending or revenue, risk being struck.

Provision 2.1: Make ACA Subsidies Permanent

Current status: Enhanced ACA subsidies expire December 2025

Reconciliation change:

  • Make enhanced subsidies permanent

  • Eliminate the subsidy cliff (which returns at 400% FPL when the enhanced subsidies lapse)

  • Extend subsidies to households earning up to 600% FPL (~$94,000 for individuals)

Impact:

  • Keep premiums affordable for middle-class families

  • The Congressional Budget Office estimates a permanent extension would increase the number of insured people by 3.8 million in 2035 (CBO, “The Estimated Effects of Enacting Selected Health Coverage Policies on the Federal Budget and on the Number of People With Health Insurance”, September 18, 2025)

Cost: About $350 billion over 2026-2035 for a permanent extension, per the same CBO estimate (roughly $35 billion a year); extending to 600% FPL would add to that

Reconciliation compliance: Directly affects federal budget (spending increase)

Provision 2.2: Create Public Option

Implementation:

  • Allow anyone to buy into Medicare (called “Medicare Part E” for Everyone)

  • Premium set at actuarial cost (no subsidy beyond existing ACA subsidies)

  • Same provider networks as traditional Medicare

  • Same benefits as Medicare

  • Administered by CMS (existing infrastructure)

Phase-in:

  • Year 1: Available to individuals 50-64

  • Year 2: Available to individuals 40-49

  • Year 3: Available to everyone

Impact:

  • Provide genuine competition to private insurance

  • Put downward pressure on premiums by paying closer to Medicare rates

Cost to federal government:

  • An initial subsidy for market stabilization

  • Designed to be funded by premiums over time; it would need a CBO score

Reconciliation compliance: Creates new revenue stream (premiums) and affects Medicare spending

Provision 2.3: Lower Medicare Eligibility to Age 60

Implementation:

  • Year 1: Eligibility at age 60 (voluntary buy-in)

  • Premium: Actuarially determined

  • Subsidies available based on income

Impact:

  • Give uninsured and under-insured 60-64-year-olds a stable option (most people in this age group already have coverage, so the gain is concentrated among early retirees and the self-employed)

  • Reduce emergency room costs for the 60-64 age group

Cost: Partly offset by premiums; it would need a CBO score

Reconciliation compliance: Affects Medicare spending and revenue

Provision 2.4: Expand Drug Price Negotiation to All Medicare Drugs

Implementation:

  • Medicare negotiates prices for all Part D drugs (currently 10 drugs in 2026, rising to 25 in 2027)

  • Use international reference pricing (average of Canadian, German, French, UK prices)

  • Implementation timeline: 3 years

Impact:

  • The first two rounds alone are estimated at about $6 billion and $12 billion in savings respectively (CMS figures, above). Applying negotiation to every Part D drug would multiply that.

Reconciliation compliance: Directly reduces federal spending

Provision 2.5: Cap Out-of-Pocket Costs

Implementation:

  • Federal maximum out-of-pocket cap: $2,000/year per person, $4,000/year per family

  • Applies to all ACA plans, Medicare, and public option (Part D already has a $2,000 drug cap; this extends the idea to all care)

  • Includes deductibles, co-pays, and co-insurance

Impact:

  • Sharply reduce medical debt and medical bankruptcy

  • Relieve the families hit hardest by today’s high deductibles

Cost: Substantial; government subsidizes the gap. It would need a CBO score.

Reconciliation compliance: Affects federal health spending

Total Cost of Reconciliation Bill:

  • Gross cost: hundreds of billions over ten years, led by the permanent subsidy extension (about $350 billion on its own, per CBO)

  • Savings: drug negotiation

  • Net cost: to be determined by a CBO score

Offsets (options, not yet scored):

  • Restore the top marginal tax rate to 39.6%

  • Tax carried interest as ordinary income (CBO put this at roughly $14 billion over ten years: “Tax Carried Interest as Ordinary Income”, CBO Options for Reducing the Deficit)

  • Further revenue on very high incomes

Net budget impact: The goal is deficit-neutrality. The revised figures are worked through in the Code Blue Ledger.

Phase 3: Comprehensive Reform (Years 3-5)

Requires: Bipartisan support (60 votes) OR narrow Democratic majority + filibuster reform

Bill Title: “American Universal Healthcare Act of 2028”

Model: Swiss-German hybrid (see Parts 4 and 5)

Provision 3.1: Mandate Universal Coverage

Implementation:

  • Every American must have health insurance

  • Options: Employer coverage, ACA exchange plan, Public option (Medicare Part E), Traditional Medicare (65+)

  • Individual mandate penalty: $850/year or 2.5% of income (whichever is higher)

  • Exemptions: Financial hardship, religious objections

Impact:

  • Cover the remaining uninsured (about 8% of Americans, or roughly 27 million people, in 2024, per the Census Bureau’s “Health Insurance Coverage in the United States: 2024”, September 2025)

  • Stabilize insurance markets (healthy people in pool)

Provision 3.2: Standardize Coverage Requirements

Implementation:

  • Federal government defines a “Basic Health Plan” that all insurers must offer

  • Covers: Hospital, physician, prescription drugs, preventive care, mental health, dental, vision

  • No annual or lifetime limits

  • No denial for pre-existing conditions (already law, but reinforced)

  • No coverage exclusions based on health status

Based on: Swiss KVG (Federal Health Insurance Act, adopted 1994, in force 1996)

Provision 3.3: Implement Price Controls

Implementation:

Federal government negotiates maximum prices for:

  • All hospital procedures (using Medicare rates as baseline)

  • All physician services (using Medicare physician fee schedule)

  • All prescription drugs (using international reference pricing)

  • All medical devices (using German pricing as baseline)

Hospitals and providers must accept these prices from all insurers

Based on: German SGB V (Social Code Book V, notably §87 on the uniform physician fee schedule and §130b on negotiated drug prices)

Impact:

  • Bring U.S. prices closer to those paid in other wealthy countries — the single biggest driver of the spending gap (Part 3)

  • Lower premiums

  • Eliminate price variation between insurers (all pay same rates)

Provision 3.4: Subsidize Premiums for Affordability

Implementation:

Premium subsidies based on income:

  • 0-138% FPL: Free coverage (Medicaid or public option)

  • 138-300% FPL: Premium capped at 4% of income

  • 300-400% FPL: Premium capped at 6% of income

  • 400-500% FPL: Premium capped at 8% of income

  • 500-600% FPL: Premium capped at 10% of income

  • Above 600% FPL: No subsidy (but still must buy coverage)

Based on: Swiss premium reductions (about 2.45 million people, or 27.5% of insured persons, received them in 2023, per Swiss Federal Statistical Office data reported by allnews.ch, “Primes maladie: 2,45 millions de bénéficiaires de réductions de primes en 2023”)

Cost: Large; it would need a CBO score

Provision 3.5: Simplify Administration

Implementation:

  • Single billing code system (eliminate proprietary insurer coding)

  • Standardized claim forms (one form for all insurers)

  • Electronic claims processing (required within 48 hours)

  • Eliminate prior authorization for evidence-based care

  • Require prior authorization for experimental treatments only

Impact:

  • Cut administrative costs toward the lower levels seen in simpler systems

  • Free physicians to practice medicine instead of fighting insurance companies

Total Cost of Comprehensive Reform:

Gross cost: large, driven by premium subsidies

Savings: price controls and administrative simplification

Net: unknown until scored. The best evidence is encouraging: a 2020 review in PLOS Medicine found that 19 of 22 economic analyses of U.S. single-payer proposals projected net savings in the first year, averaging 3.5% of health spending (Cai et al., “Projected costs of single-payer healthcare financing in the United States: A systematic review of economic analyses”, January 15, 2020). This hybrid plan has not been scored by anyone.

Yes, savings are possible. Universal coverage can cost LESS than the current system. Whether this version does is a question for the scorekeepers.

Phase 4: Optimization (Years 6-10)

Objective 4.1: Fine-Tune Subsidy Levels

  • Monitor premium affordability

  • Adjust subsidy schedules based on real-world data

  • Ensure no one pays more than 10% of income for basic coverage

Objective 4.2: Adjust Provider Payment Rates

  • Monitor access to care (wait times, provider availability)

  • Adjust payment rates if shortages develop

  • Ensure adequate physician supply through medical school funding

Objective 4.3: Expand Coverage Scope

  • Add dental, vision, hearing to basic coverage (if not included in Phase 3)

  • Add long-term care coverage (for elderly and disabled)

  • Strengthen mental health parity (full coverage, no limitations)

Objective 4.4: Address Medical Education Costs

Problem: Many American doctors graduate with six-figure debt

Solution:

  • Federal medical school loan forgiveness ($100,000 over 10 years of practice)

  • Expand medical school slots by 25% (increase supply, reduce costs)

  • Expand the existing National Health Service Corps (which offers scholarships and loan repayment for service in underserved areas) into free medical school in exchange for 5 years of rural/underserved practice

Impact:

  • Reduce physician debt burden

  • Increase primary care physician supply

  • Improve rural healthcare access

Cost: It would need a CBO score

Savings: Lower debt makes it easier, over time, to bring physician pay in line with international norms

Objective 4.5: Monitor and Enforce

  • Annual reporting on coverage rates, costs, outcomes

  • Enforcement of price controls (penalties for overcharging)

  • Insurance company oversight (medical loss ratio, claim denial rates)

  • Public transparency dashboard (all data publicly available)

The Copy-Paste Strategy

Why reinvent the wheel? Borrow proven legal frameworks — not word for word, but the architecture is there to adapt.

From Switzerland (KVG, the Federal Act on Health Insurance of 18 March 1994, in force 1 January 1996):

  • Article 3: Compulsory insurance. Everyone resident in Switzerland must take out basic health insurance within three months of taking up residence or of birth. The Federal Council can grant exceptions.

  • Articles 24-33: The benefits package. Article 25 lists what basic insurance covers (physicians’ treatment, medicines, general-ward hospital care and more); Article 32 requires every covered service to be effective, appropriate and economical.

  • Article 61: Premiums. Sets the principles for premiums. Basic insurance is community-rated: an insurer charges its members the same premium whatever their health, with variations allowed by region and lower premiums for children and young adults.

  • Article 65: Premium reductions. The cantons must reduce premiums for insured people of modest means, with a federal contribution under Article 66.

American version: “All persons residing in the United States must obtain health insurance. The Secretary of Health and Human Services shall determine minimum coverage standards. States shall provide premium subsidies on a sliding scale based on income, with federal government providing 90% of subsidy costs.”

From Germany (SGB V, Social Code Book V):

  • Section 5: Compulsory insurance. Employees earning below an income threshold must join statutory health insurance; higher earners may choose private cover.

  • Section 27: Medical treatment. Insured people are entitled to the treatment needed to detect, cure or ease an illness.

  • Section 87: Physician fee schedule. Doctors’ and sickness funds’ national associations agree a uniform fee schedule (the EBM) through a joint committee, giving each billable service a points value.

  • Section 130b: Drug price agreements. The sickness funds’ national association negotiates a new drug’s price with the manufacturer, based on the Federal Joint Committee’s assessment of its added benefit (Section 35a); arbitration decides if they fail to agree.

American version: “All insured persons are entitled to comprehensive medical care including preventive, diagnostic, therapeutic, and rehabilitative services. The Secretary of Health and Human Services shall establish uniform fee schedules for all healthcare services. The Secretary shall negotiate maximum prices for all prescription drugs using international reference pricing.”

It isn’t literally that simple. But the blueprints exist. Adapt them to the American federal-state structure. Pass it.

The Political Reality Check

This plan requires:

  • 1 year of executive action (achievable with any president, plus a few items Congress must pass)

  • 1 reconciliation bill (achievable with 50 votes)

  • 1 comprehensive reform bill (requires 60 votes OR filibuster reform)

  • 10 years of implementation

This plan aims to provide:

  • Universal coverage

  • Lower costs

  • Better outcomes

  • Administrative simplification

  • Preservation of private insurance (Swiss model)

  • Market competition

This plan threatens:

  • Insurance company profits on basic coverage

  • Hospital price gouging

  • Pharmaceutical company monopoly pricing

  • Medical device manufacturer markups

  • PBM middleman profits

Which is why it won’t pass without massive public pressure.

But it’s achievable. It’s tested. It’s working in Switzerland and Germany.

The question isn’t whether it can be done.

The question is whether Americans will demand it.

December 17, 2025 Update:

As I write this, Washington is in chaos.

Last week (Dec 11): The Senate rejected both parties’ healthcare bills. The Democrats’ three-year extension and the Republican alternative each failed 51-48, short of 60. Senator Josh Hawley was one of four Republicans, with Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Dan Sullivan, who crossed party lines to vote for extending subsidies — choosing people over politics. (NPR, “Competing health care plans fail in the Senate as ACA premium hikes loom”, December 11, 2025.)

Yesterday (Dec 16): Speaker Mike Johnson refused to let his own members vote on subsidy extensions. The House Rules Committee — which leadership controls — rejected every amendment from GOP centrists that would have extended subsidies with reforms. (Axios, “GOP centrists help Dems defy Mike Johnson on ACA in stunning revolt”, December 17, 2025.)

The centrists had spent days altering their proposals to meet leadership demands. They found ways to pay for it. They added income limits. They compromised on everything.

Johnson rejected it anyway.

The scene in Washington:

The frustration boiled over at a heated Tuesday luncheon between centrists and leadership (Colorado Politics, “Republican frustration on healthcare hits boiling point”, December 17, 2025). Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY) called the refusal to hold a vote “idiotic” and “political malpractice.” (Axios, “’It’s idiotic’: House moderates fume at Johnson over inaction on ACA”, December 16, 2025.)

Only days left in the House’s 2025 session. No path to a vote this year.

Subsidies WILL expire. This is no longer uncertain. On January 1, 2026 — 15 days from today — the roughly 22 million Americans who receive enhanced subsidies (CBS News, “Why ACA tax credits for 22 million Americans are at the center of the government shutdown drama”, October 2025) will see them shrink or vanish. KFF estimates subsidized enrollees’ premium payments will more than double, rising 114% on average (KFF, “ACA Marketplace Premium Payments Would More than Double on Average Next Year if Enhanced Premium Tax Credits Expire”, September 30, 2025). The Congressional Budget Office has projected 2.2 million more uninsured people in 2026 alone (as reported by Fierce Healthcare, “CBO: Premiums could increase substantially if ACA subsidies expire”, December 10, 2024).

Today, GOP moderates went further and defied their own Speaker. Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick (R-PA) signed the Democrats’ discharge petition for a clean three-year extension, followed by Reps. Mike Lawler, Rob Bresnahan and Ryan Mackenzie, bringing it to the 218 signatures needed to force a vote, likely in January. Fitzpatrick’s argument: the only thing worse than a clean extension without reforms is letting the subsidies expire with no bridge at all.

Electoral panic spreading: Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE) warned that Democrats will use the issue “like a sledgehammer” on the campaign trail (as reported by AP, “Speaker Johnson was ready to move on from ACA subsidies. But his members had other plans”, December 18, 2025).

Both parties still blaming each other:

  • Speaker Johnson: Democrats “broke the system” when they created the ACA 15 years ago

  • Rep. Chip Roy (R-TX): called his own party’s health bill “milquetoast garbage” at the Rules Committee

  • President Trump: wants to send the money to the people instead of “big, fat, bloated insurance companies” (aboard Air Force One; Mediaite, “Reporter Presses Trump on Logic of Healthcare Idea: ‘Won’t Americans Buy It From Insurance Companies?’”, November 14, 2025)

The House votes this week on a GOP bill that doesn’t extend subsidies. Even if it passes the House, the Senate won’t act before the deadline. The subsidies expire regardless.

Fifteen years since the ACA. Both parties admitting failure. Johnson blocks votes. Roy trashes his own party’s bill. Trump demands direct payments. Centrists revolt.

15 days until the enhanced subsidies disappear.

Meanwhile, the roadmap to fix this is in this essay. The legal frameworks exist. The international evidence exists.

What doesn’t exist is political courage.

And apparently, what also doesn’t exist is a Speaker willing to let his own members vote.
