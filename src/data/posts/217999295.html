In February 1847, the Illustrated London News sent a Cork artist named James Mahony west to Skibbereen to find out whether the stories coming out of the district were exaggerated. He reported that they were not. On the coach out of Cork he watched a woman at Clonakilty begging the passengers for money to buy a coffin for the dead child in her arms. In Skibbereen itself he drew a boy and a girl scratching in a field for potatoes that were not there, and a village where the living were too weak to bury the dead. His sketches ran in London over two weeks, and his readers, many of them for the first time, saw what was happening a short sea crossing away, inside their own country.

Ireland in 1847 was not a colony in the legal sense. Since the Act of Union took effect on 1 January 1801, it had been part of the United Kingdom, with its own MPs at Westminster and a Lord Lieutenant in Dublin Castle. Skibbereen was, on paper, as much a part of the realm as Surrey. That is the fact that makes this chapter the starkest in the British section, and the reason the Introduction called it the oldest case in the series.

Starting closest to home

Ireland’s history with England long predates the Famine, and a full account would begin with the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169. For the purposes of the rubric, three earlier moments matter most, because they built the structure the Famine fell on.

The first is the Cromwellian conquest of 1649 to 1653, which ended with the massacres at Drogheda and Wexford and an Act of Settlement that transferred most of the land still in Catholic hands to English soldiers, speculators and Protestant settlers. By the end of the seventeenth century, Catholics, the great majority of the population, owned a small fraction of the land.

The second is the Penal Laws of the decades after 1691, which barred Catholics from Parliament, the professions, the army, and from buying land or inheriting it undivided. They were eased over the eighteenth century, but they left behind a country where most of the people worked land owned by someone else, often someone who lived in England and knew his estates through an agent.

The third is the rising of 1798, suppressed with tens of thousands dead, which led directly to the Union: Ireland’s own Parliament voted itself out of existence in 1800, after a campaign of patronage and peerages that contemporaries and historians alike have described as bribery on an industrial scale.

By the 1840s, the result was a country of roughly eight and a half million people, perhaps three million of whom lived almost entirely on potatoes grown on small plots rented from landlords, paying for the plot with labour or with cash raised by selling other crops. The grain, the livestock and the butter those same people raised were largely sold to pay the rent. The potato was what they ate because it was the only crop that could feed a family on the patch of ground the arrangement left them.

The blight, and the choice

Phytophthora infestans arrived in September 1845. It destroyed perhaps a third of the potato crop that year, most of it in 1846, and returned in 1848 and later years. No government caused it, and no government of the day understood it. What governments did control was the response.

Sir Robert Peel’s Conservative government did respond, within the limits of its thinking. In late 1845 it secretly bought about £100,000 of Indian corn from America to release onto the market and hold down prices, and it set up public works. Peel then spent his political career repealing the Corn Laws in 1846, partly on the argument that Ireland’s crisis made cheap imported grain essential, and his party split under him.

Lord John Russell’s Whig government, which replaced Peel’s in the summer of 1846, took a harder line, and the man who shaped its approach more than anyone was Charles Trevelyan, Assistant Secretary to the Treasury. Trevelyan believed, as a matter of sincere principle, that the market must not be disturbed, that relief which undercut private traders would destroy the incentive to import food, and that Irish poverty had moral as well as economic causes. In a letter in October 1846 he wrote that the greatest evil to be dealt with was not the physical evil of the famine but the moral evil of what he called the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people. In a book published in 1848, he described the Famine as a stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence that had laid bare the deep and inveterate root of social evil.

That doctrine governed what came next. Government food depots were limited so as not to compete with merchants. Public works paid wages that often could not buy enough food at famine prices, and required starving men to break stones in winter. When the works failed, the government switched in early 1847 to soup kitchens, which at their peak in the summer of 1847 were feeding about three million people a day, and which worked. They were then wound down by September 1847, on the principle that Irish property must support Irish poverty, and the burden moved to the local Poor Law unions and their workhouses, funded by rates on the very landlords and tenants the Famine was bankrupting.

In June 1847, Parliament added what became known as the Gregory clause: anyone holding more than a quarter of an acre of land could not receive public relief unless they gave the land up. Families were forced to choose between food now and any chance of feeding themselves later. Landlords, facing ruinous rates on the smallholders living on their estates, had every incentive to clear them, and the clearances were enormous. Evictions ran into the hundreds of thousands of people across the Famine years.

The grain ships

This is the image the Introduction pointed to, and it needs stating carefully, because it has been both underplayed and overplayed.

Throughout the Famine, Ireland continued to export food to Britain: oats, wheat, barley, cattle, pigs, butter and eggs, shipped out of Irish ports under military escort where crowds tried to stop them. The historian Christine Kinealy, working from customs and shipping records, has shown how substantial those exports remained. In the worst year, 1847, Ireland imported more grain, mostly Indian corn, than it exported, so it is not accurate to say that the island was a net food exporter throughout. But the imports arrived late, were sold at prices the poor could not pay, and did not reach the people starving in Skibbereen or Mayo, while the exports were the rent. The food left because it belonged, legally, to someone who needed to sell it, and a government that could have closed the ports, as governments had done in earlier Irish food crises, chose not to on principle.

That is the precise sense in which this case belongs in the series. The blight was nature. The decision that the food produced in a starving country should continue to leave it, because the market said so, was policy.

What it cost

Somewhere around one million people died between 1845 and 1852, from starvation and, far more often, from the typhus, dysentery and relapsing fever that famine brings. About as many again emigrated in the same years, and the flood continued for decades. Ireland’s population, about 8.2 million in the census of 1841, was 6.6 million by 1851 and kept falling for most of the next century. It has never recovered to its pre-Famine level.

Canada saw a share of that cost directly. In 1847, the “fever ships” brought tens of thousands of Irish emigrants up the St. Lawrence. At the quarantine station on Grosse Île, below Quebec City, thousands died that summer and are buried on the island. In Toronto, a city of about twenty thousand people, more than thirty-eight thousand Irish migrants arrived in a single season, and fever sheds were built near the waterfront. More than a thousand of them died. So did some of the people caring for them, including Toronto’s first Catholic bishop, Michael Power, who caught typhus ministering in the sheds and died on 1 October 1847 at the age of forty-two. Ireland Park, on the waterfront at Bathurst Quay, marks their arrival.

Resistance, and what happened to it

In July 1848, with the Famine still running, a small group of Young Irelanders led by William Smith O’Brien attempted a rising in County Tipperary. It amounted to a stand-off with police at a farmhouse near Ballingarry, mocked ever since as the battle of Widow McCormack’s cabbage patch. It was militarily trivial. The response was not. Parliament had already passed the Treason Felony Act that spring. John Mitchel, whose newspaper had urged resistance to the grain exports, was convicted by a packed jury and transported for fourteen years. Smith O’Brien and his companions were sentenced to be hanged, drawn and quartered, commuted to transportation to Van Diemen’s Land. Habeas corpus was suspended in Ireland. The message to a starving population was that political resistance would be met with the full weight of the state, even as relief was being scaled back.

Scoring Ireland against the rubric

This is the case where the rubric has to be applied most carefully, because the argument over the Famine has been fierce for a century and a half, and some of it has turned on the word genocide. This series does not need to settle that argument, and does not make that claim. The rubric does not require an intent to kill. It asks whether the pattern was present.

Overwhelming asymmetry. Ireland had no army, no navy and, after 1800, no Parliament. Its people were governed from London by a Parliament in which Irish members were a minority, and where the Catholic majority had only been able to sit at all since 1829. Satisfied.

Coercion as first resort. The whole structure rested on it: conquest, confiscation, the Penal Laws, a Union secured by patronage after a rising was crushed, and a garrison that escorted the grain ships through hungry towns. There was no negotiation over whether food would leave. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Rent, and the food sold to pay it, flowed from Irish land to landlords, many of them absentee, and to British markets. The stated justification for keeping the flow going (sound economics, the protection of trade, the need to avoid dependence) diverged sharply from the operative result, which was that the property relationship survived intact while the people living under it did not. The Encumbered Estates Act of 1849 then eased the transfer of bankrupt estates to new owners, cleared of the tenants who had died or been evicted. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Political economy and Providence. Trevelyan’s language is the Irish equivalent of Tacitus’s bathhouses, with one difference: Tacitus knew he was describing a cover story. Trevelyan appears to have believed his. Satisfied.

Resistance gets punished. The response to 1848 was far out of proportion to a rising that barely happened, and designed to be seen. Satisfied.

Five for five, with an important qualification. Ireland is the case in this series where the bully’s own conscience was most visibly engaged. British Quakers ran some of the most effective relief in the country. The British Relief Association, founded in January 1847, raised hundreds of thousands of pounds, and Queen Victoria herself contributed. Mahony’s drawings moved London. None of it changed the doctrine, because the doctrine was not held by bad people. It was held by serious, educated, often devout people who were certain that interfering with the market would make things worse. That, more than any single act of cruelty, is why this chapter belongs here.

What Ireland tells us

Rome showed the bully’s methods in their purest form. Ireland shows something less comfortable: that the pattern does not need a Scipio or a Calgacus. It can run on principle, through ordinary administration, in a country that is legally part of the bully’s own state, with a free press reporting every stage of it. The grain ships were not a secret. They were simply thought to be the right policy.

Part III moves from the edge of the United Kingdom to the far side of the world, where Britain’s navy and Britain’s merchants were about to teach the Qing Empire the same lesson Ireland had learned about who decides what trade flows where, only this time with guns.
