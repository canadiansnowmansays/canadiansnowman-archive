In the first two essays I wrote about Europe — the heat that broke the records, and the physics and geography that keep aiming it at France. I did so because that is where the news was, and news is news. But there is a sentence in the original Times coverage that I underlined and have not stopped thinking about, because it quietly relocates the whole story to my own back yard.

Europe, the paper noted, is warming faster than any other continent on Earth.

That is true, and it is alarming, and it is also — read carefully — a statement about continents. Continents are large. They contain cold parts and warm parts and they average out. The fastest-warming place is not a continent at all. It is the top of the world, and Canada is sitting on it.

The numbers here are not subtle, and they are not contested by anyone serious. The Arctic is warming more than twice as fast as the planet as a whole — and along Canada’s Arctic coast, the longest Arctic coastline in the world, the rate over the past half-century has run two to three times the global average, according to Natural Resources Canada’s own scientists; some northern jurisdictions are warming nearly four times faster than the planet entire. The 2025 Arctic Report Card, the twentieth such annual reckoning, assembled by 112 scientists from 13 countries, found that surface air temperatures across the Arctic from October 2024 through September 2025 were the warmest since records began in 1900. The last ten years are the ten warmest on record up there. June snow cover across the Arctic is now roughly half what it was sixty years ago. These are not projections with error bars stretching to 2100. They are measurements, taken, written down.

I want to dwell on what this means in a way the European story, for all its drama, does not quite force you to confront. A heat wave in Paris is terrible, and it kills people, and it is a warning. But Paris will cool down. The buildings, however ill-suited, will still be standing in October. The heat wave is an event — a violent, deadly, increasingly frequent event, but an event, with a beginning and an end.

What is happening in the Canadian North is not an event. It is a foundation giving way — in some places literally.

Consider permafrost, which is one of those words that sounds permanent because the name says so, and is turning out not to be. Permafrost is simply ground that has stayed frozen year-round for at least two consecutive years; in much of the Canadian North it has stayed frozen for thousands of them, and entire landscapes — and everything built upon them — rest on the assumption that it always will. Across the Canadian and Alaskan Arctic, that ground is now thawing, and as it thaws it does several things at once, all of them bad and one of them genuinely strange.

It buckles. The thing to understand is that frozen ground is hard as rock and stable as rock, and thawed ground, especially the ice-rich ground common across the Canadian North, turns to something between mud and soup. A building that stood for decades on solid frozen earth finds its foundation pilings sinking on one side and heaving up on the other, until the walls crack and the floors tilt and, in the worst cases, the structure has to be condemned. In Inuvik, buildings have been torn down for exactly this reason. Roads ripple and collapse — stretches of the Dempster Highway, the great gravel artery of the western Arctic, slump and crack where the permafrost beneath the embankment gives way. Airstrips, which like roads are long thin things that must cross whatever ground lies in their path, are among the most vulnerable infrastructure of all, and in the North an airstrip is not a convenience but a lifeline, the only way in or out of fourteen of the Northwest Territories’ thirty-three communities through the long months when the winter roads have melted.

The price of all this is not abstract. The Northwest Territories Association of Communities has estimated that thawing permafrost causes roughly fifty-one million dollars in damage to public infrastructure in the territory every single year — and over a seventy-five-year horizon, some 1.3 billion dollars, in a territory of forty-five thousand people. A landmark international study published in Nature Reviews Earth & Environment projected that thirty to fifty per cent of critical circumpolar infrastructure could be at high risk by 2050, with repair costs running into the tens of billions. These are the bills for a house whose foundation was, without anyone deciding it, quietly placed on a thirty-year clock.

And it falls hardest on the people least responsible and least equipped to pay. Northern Arctic communities — many of them Indigenous, many of them already underserved by a country that has never quite known what to do with its own North — are watching the ground itself become unreliable beneath the infrastructure of daily life. In Tuktoyaktuk, on the Beaufort coast, thawing permafrost and storm waves are collapsing the shoreline by as much as a metre a year, forcing some families to move their homes and threatening others; the community has had to find a new site just to relocate its morgue. A homeowner in Iqaluit, quoted in a Canadian Climate Institute study, described the slow ruin with a resignation that has stayed with me: the house shifting, the drywall cracking around the windows — and the insurance, he noted, does not cover it, so he simply makes the repairs himself. Hold onto that detail. The insurance does not cover it. We will return, several essays from now, to what happens when the institution whose entire job is to price risk decides that certain houses, in certain places, are no longer worth insuring at all. The North has been living in that future for years already.

The strange part — the part I promised — is the rivers. As the permafrost thaws, it releases iron and other metals long locked in the frozen soil, and across more than two hundred watersheds in Arctic Alaska the streams have literally turned orange — “rusting rivers,” the scientists are calling them, a phrase that belongs in a fantasy novel and is instead in a federal report. The water becomes more acidic, the toxic metals climb, the drinking water degrades, the fish that subsistence communities depend on are imperiled. The land is, in a precise and chemical sense, bleeding rust into the water as it warms. There is no clearer image in all of climate science of a system unmaking itself: ground that has held its breath for ten thousand years, exhaling at last into the rivers, and turning them the colour of decay.

This is the house built for winter, but written at the scale of a region rather than a row of terraces. The entire Canadian North — its ecology, its hydrology, its built infrastructure, the cultures and economies layered on top of all three — was built, by nature and by people, for a climate of reliable cold. The cold was the load-bearing assumption, in the most literal possible sense: it was the thing holding the buildings up. Remove it and the structure does not adapt; it fails, in the specific ways structures fail when the thing holding them up goes away.

And here is the part that ought to keep a Canadian up at night, the part that makes this our story and not a documentary about somewhere else. The Arctic is not just a victim of warming. It is an engine of it. The permafrost holds an enormous quantity of carbon — the frozen remains of plants and animals that have been in cold storage for millennia, very possibly more carbon than is currently in the entire atmosphere. As it thaws, it releases that carbon, as carbon dioxide and as methane, into an atmosphere that is warming the permafrost that releases the carbon that warms the atmosphere. The technical term is a feedback loop. The plain term is a fire that helps light itself. And it is the kind of process that, once well underway, does not wait for human permission to stop; it runs on its own logic, indifferent to summits and pledges and the careful targets of the accountability essays still to come.

The boreal forest, which drapes across the Canadian middle like a green sash, is supposed to be the counterweight — one of the great carbon sinks of the planet, breathing in carbon and locking it into wood and soil. Increasingly, it is doing the opposite. Summer 2025 marked the fourth consecutive year of above-median wildfire across northern North America, with thousands of square kilometres burning in Canada’s Northwest Territories alone. A forest that burns does not store carbon. It exhales it, in a single grey breath, into the sky. The sink becomes a source. The counterweight joins the load on the wrong side of the scale — and the smoke from that burning, as the next essay will describe, does not stay politely within our borders.

I do not write any of this to frighten you, or at least not only to frighten you. I write it because of a strange asymmetry in how we talk about climate change in this country. We discuss it, when we discuss it at all, as a global problem — something happening to the planet, to the future, to islands in the Pacific and cities in the south. We are very good at this register. It lets us feel concerned and implicated and a little helpless, all at once, which is a comfortable place to stand, because it asks nothing of us except sentiment.

What we are less good at is the recognition that the most dramatic warming on the entire planet is happening inside our own borders, to our own land, to our own citizens, faster than almost anywhere else on Earth. Canada is not watching the climate crisis on television. Canada is one of its primary locations. The crew is filming in the Northwest Territories, and the actors are Canadian, and the foundations cracking under the houses are foundations our own governments are responsible for and cannot afford to fix.

Dior, my long-suffering husky, descends from dogs bred for exactly the climate that is now disappearing fastest. Her whole magnificent design — the double coat, the snow-built paws, the indifference to cold that makes her useless in July — is an artifact of a North that is melting. She is, you might say, a refugee from a country that is closing. So, increasingly, are a great many beings with better claims to the place than a Toronto pet: the caribou whose migration routes are dissolving into bog, the polar bears that have become the overexposed mascots of all this, and above all the people whose ancestors learned to live on that ice over a span of time that makes my own family’s century and a half in Canada look like a weekend visit. They built a civilization on the permafrost, in the most demanding conditions humans have ever permanently inhabited, and it worked — for millennia — because the cold could be relied upon. The cold can no longer be relied upon. That is not a small thing to take from a people. It may be the largest thing there is.

The continent warming fastest, the headline said, is Europe. It is a good headline and a true one. But the place warming fastest is north of here, and it is ours, and it is not a heat wave that will pass by October. It is the foundation, going.

Next, the smoke — which is where our melting North stops being a problem we can keep to ourselves.

Degrees

1900 — the year Arctic temperature records begin, and the benchmark against which the twelve months ending September 2025 were the warmest ever measured up there. My great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside was born in 1898. The Arctic has not had a year as warm as the last one in the entire span of his life and mine combined, and I am seventy-four.
