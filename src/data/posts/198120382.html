Louisiana polling stations on May 16, 2026. Voters arrived expecting a full ballot with both Senate and House primaries — only to find the House races postponed, creating confusion and depressed turnout in the Senate contest.

On April 29, 2026, the Supreme Court struck down Louisiana’s congressional map. On May 16, Louisiana held its Senate primary anyway — under conditions the ruling had transformed.

The connection between those two events is the subject of this instalment. Not the map itself — Part XXVIII covered what Louisiana’s legislature is doing with the maps. This is about what happened to the voters.

The Split

Louisiana’s House primaries had been scheduled for May 16, alongside the Senate race. After Callais, Governor Jeff Landry postponed the House primaries to allow the legislature to draw new congressional maps. He kept the Senate primary on schedule.

The result was an election that had been split in half. Voters who had planned to cast ballots in two races — their House primary and their Senate primary — were now being asked to show up for one. Some did not know the Senate race was still happening. John Fleming’s campaign sent texts and emails reminding voters that the Senate primary was still a go. Bill Cassidy publicly criticised the decision to split the dates.

A voting machine in Louisiana on primary day. Forty-five thousand absentee ballots already cast for the postponed House races were discarded. The split election turned a routine primary into a procedural obstacle course.

Forty-five thousand absentee ballots had already been cast for the House primaries. They were discarded. Those voters would have to vote again in November, Landry said on 60 Minutes, as though this were a minor administrative adjustment. “It’s not my fault,” he said. “If anybody has a grievance, take it to the United States Supreme Court.”

The Supreme Court that created the problem. Take your grievance there.

The Closed Door

The Senate primary was also the first under Louisiana’s new closed-primary system — the first since the 1970s. For decades, Louisiana had held open primaries where candidates and voters of all parties competed on a single ballot. The new system required voters to participate in their own party’s primary.

For Cassidy, this was a structural blow. His coalition included moderate Republicans, independents, and unaffiliated voters who valued his record on healthcare and disaster relief. Under the old system, those voters would have diluted the MAGA electorate. Under the new system, unaffiliated voters had to fill out additional paperwork to participate in the Republican primary. The playing field had narrowed — and it had narrowed toward the voters most likely to punish Cassidy for his 2021 impeachment vote.

Senator Bill Cassidy finished third in Louisiana’s Republican Senate primary on May 16, 2026. The combination of voter confusion, discarded ballots, and the new closed-primary system narrowed the electorate against the incumbent.

The combination was devastating. A split election that confused voters about whether to show up. A closed primary that filtered out the voters most sympathetic to the incumbent. And a Supreme Court ruling that had caused both.

The Result

Cassidy finished third. Julia Letlow, Trump’s endorsed candidate, led with 44.8 percent. Fleming finished second at 28.3 percent. Cassidy took 24.8 percent. More than 73 percent of Republican primary voters chose someone other than their sitting senator.

The Loyalty Trials — the companion series tracking the primary challenges against Republicans who crossed Trump — covers the political dynamics of Cassidy’s defeat in detail. What this series is concerned with is the machinery.

The machinery worked like this:

The Supreme Court struck down Louisiana’s map. The governor postponed the House primaries but not the Senate primary. The voter confusion depressed turnout among casual voters — the voters Cassidy needed. The closed-primary system filtered out independents and unaffiliated voters — the voters Cassidy needed. The result was a smaller, more conservative, more MAGA-aligned electorate than the one that would have existed under the previous rules, voting on a day when half the ballot had been removed.

Cassidy would likely have lost under any set of rules. The polling was clear. But the margin of his defeat and the fact of his third-place elimination — behind a candidate who did not even have Trump’s endorsement — were shaped by mechanics that flowed directly from the Supreme Court’s redistricting decision.

Callais was decided as a map case. Its first electoral consequence was not a map. It was a Senate primary.

The Parallel

Part XXVIII of this series documented what Louisiana’s legislature is doing to Black political representation: eliminating Calvin Duncan’s elected office, redrawing congressional maps to eliminate majority-Black districts, discarding the Voting Rights Act’s protections with the Supreme Court’s blessing.

The Senate primary adds a different dimension. The Callais ruling did not only change the maps. It changed the conditions under which all Louisiana voters — of every race — participated in democracy. The split election, the discarded ballots, the voter confusion, the closed-primary filter: these affected the entire electorate.

The new closed-primary system and split election date changed the composition of the electorate itself. A procedural cascade turned a Supreme Court map ruling into a different Senate primary outcome.

This is how structural decisions cascade. A redistricting ruling becomes a ballot-discarding decision becomes a primary-date split becomes voter confusion becomes a smaller electorate becomes a different result. Each step is procedural. Each step is defensible in isolation. The cumulative effect is that the electorate that showed up on May 16 was not the electorate that would have shown up under the rules that existed six months earlier.

The committee adjourned without a vote on the maps. The redistricting continues. But the redistricting’s reach now extends beyond the maps themselves — into the mechanics of every other election conducted in its shadow.

Shane Massey’s Question

In Part XXVIII, South Carolina Senate leader Shane Massey asked the question that frames this entire series: “Too many people in power just want to do whatever it takes to stay in power. They’ll do whatever it takes to keep it, but I ask — to what end? What do you do with it when you’ve attained it?”

Louisiana answered that question on May 16. You use it to eliminate the office a Black man won. You use it to redraw the districts Black voters elected their representatives from. You use it to split an election, discard 45,000 ballots, close a primary, and create the conditions for a smaller, angrier, more compliant electorate.

That is what you do with it when you’ve attained it.
