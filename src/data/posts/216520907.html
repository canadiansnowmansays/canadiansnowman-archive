Part IV of this series covered Dean Ball’s rebuttal to AI wealth-distribution proposals — his argument that “wealth is something we create.” Parts III, V, and VI have each documented a different piece of the circularity and leverage propping up this industry’s valuations. This entry covers the moment those two threads converge in a single company: Anthropic, moving toward a listing that would make Dario Amodei’s safety rhetoric either the most expensive act of corporate candour in market history, or the most profitable marketing campaign anyone has ever run.

The number, and how fast it moved

As The New York Times’ Lauren Hirsch, Sri Muppidi, Erin Griffith, and Mike Isaac reported on September 18, 2026, Anthropic was expected to reach more than $100 billion in annualized revenue in 2026, up from $65 billion as of July, a jump investors are using to justify a potential valuation of $2 trillion. The Times reported that the company could release its public filing documents within weeks, putting shares on track to trade as soon as November. People familiar with the matter cautioned that the timeline remained subject to change, which is true of every IPO timeline but carries particular weight here: this is a company whose own chief executive had spent the preceding weeks, documented across this series’ Asimov Gap companion thread, publicly warning that the industry he leads may need to slow down precisely because its products are becoming dangerously capable.

The paperwork had been in motion for months. TechCrunch reported on June 1, 2026 that Anthropic had confidentially submitted a draft registration statement to the Securities and Exchange Commission, days after a $65 billion Series H round valued it at $965 billion. The company said then that any offering “will depend on market conditions and other factors.” The public version is the document that matters for ordinary investors, and it was slow to arrive: Forkast News noted on October 4, 2026 that the public S-1 had still not appeared on the SEC’s EDGAR database, 126 days after the confidential submission. On October 26, 2026, Anthropic filed that document publicly, listing Class A common stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley named as lead banks. SEC rules require the public filing at least 15 days before a roadshow begins, putting a November trading start on schedule.

The infrastructure numbers underneath the valuation are the part this series’ own Part III and Part V readers will recognise immediately. Investors expected Anthropic to control roughly five gigawatts of compute by the end of 2026, doubling to ten by the end of 2027, putting it on par with OpenAI’s own scale, the same OpenAI whose $1.5 trillion private-funding talks this series has not yet covered directly but which sit directly adjacent to Anthropic’s own number. Jared Kaplan and Ben Mann, two of Anthropic’s co-founders, presented alongside Boris Cherny and Andrej Karpathy — the OpenAI co-founder who joined Anthropic in 2026 — to roughly 100 venture capital investors at a September customer event in San Francisco, a detail that reframes Karpathy’s hire as more than a research coup: he is now part of the sales pitch for the biggest single financing event this series has tracked.

The prospectus stayed confidential through September, but its numbers came out anyway. Reuters’ Echo Wang reported on September 28, 2026, from a copy of the confidential prospectus, that Anthropic’s revenue grew roughly twelvefold in 2025 to nearly $4.6 billion, while it recorded a $42 billion net loss. Most of that loss was a $34 billion accounting charge tied to financing liabilities; the operating loss was still more than $8 billion. Compute and infrastructure costs tripled to $7.33 billion, more than half of the company’s $12.65 billion in operating expenses, and Anthropic ended 2025 with $20.28 billion in cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments. The public S-1 filed on October 26 confirms those 2025 figures. Reuters reported that the listing was likely to come after the November 3 midterm elections.

The forward commitments are larger still. InvestmentNews , summarising the Reuters reporting on September 29, 2026, itemised $518 billion in compute and infrastructure obligations over roughly a decade: $111.1 billion to Google, $110 billion to Amazon, $31.4 billion to Microsoft, $161.2 billion in Broadcom equipment leases, up to $84.5 billion to xAI on terms that can be cancelled with 90 days’ notice, and more than $20 billion of capacity from AMD, which also agreed to buy up to $5 billion of Anthropic stock. The public S-1 filed on October 26 puts the compute-obligation figure at approximately $417 billion — the non-cancellable subset covering Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Broadcom; the full $518 billion in Reuters’ reporting includes the xAI and AMD commitments, which carry cancellation rights. About 80 per cent of the total cannot be cancelled regardless. Of the Google contract, the filing says: “If our actual spend falls short, we must pay Google the difference.” Google and Amazon are also two of Anthropic’s largest investors. The circularity this series traced through Nvidia and OpenAI in Part III runs through Anthropic’s own balance sheet too.

The first look at the numbers

On October 14, 2026, Anthropic gave prospective investors their first official look at its finances, in a meeting at its San Francisco headquarters. As Lauren Hirsch and Sri Muppidi reported for The New York Times, citing two people with knowledge of the presentation, executives said third-quarter revenue had jumped about 45 per cent from the previous quarter, to $16.8 billion from $11.6 billion. The company said it was profitable “after making certain adjustments,” which, the Times noted, is not a bottom-line number, and that its gross margin, again after adjustments, was 85 per cent. Its bankers have said it could aim to raise more than $100 billion, which could value it at $2 trillion and make the offering larger than SpaceX’s listing in June at a $1.77 trillion valuation. Trading could begin as early as November.

Three things about that presentation belong in this ledger. The first is the word “adjusted,” used twice. A year after recording an operating loss of more than $8 billion, the company is describing itself as profitable on its own definition, before the public prospectus that would let anyone check the definition has appeared. The second is the arithmetic. A quarter of $16.8 billion is an annual rate of about $67 billion, in line with the $65 billion reported for July; reaching the $100 billion rate investors were told to expect would mean roughly another 50 per cent in a single quarter. The third is the setting. Rising interest rates have dampened the market for new listings, the Times reported, and Anthropic is grappling with questions about the sustainability of the industry’s increasingly debt-funded build-out. A successful offering could reopen the door for other companies. A poorly received one could close it, and affect the broader stock market. Some investors, the Times added, want to understand what Amodei means by “pacing” the development of the most advanced models, which is to say they want to know how much his safety essays might cost them.

The pitch, and what it deliberately leaves out

Anthropic’s investor pitch, according to people familiar with the meetings, leans on the positive case for AI — its research into biology, its potential to cure disease — the same register this series’ own Asimov Gap Part 99 has already covered at length through Amodei’s “Machines of Loving Grace” essay, and the same register that essay’s own critics note is conspicuously silent on who profits from the utopia being promised. Asked about competition from cheaper Chinese open-weight models, Anthropic has told investors only a small portion of businesses rely on them — a claim this series’ own China coverage, threaded through Asimov Gap Part 97 and The Diplomat’s Ceiling, would complicate considerably, given how quickly Chinese firms have closed the capability gap at a fraction of the cost. Several investors interviewed believe Anthropic can keep growing even if it slows certain model development, since the bulk of its revenue comes from business customers still early in adopting the technology — a bet that Amodei’s own proposed slowdown, covered at length in Asimov Gap Part 97, costs the company little given how much room for growth already exists without pushing frontier capability further.

The liability question nobody in the room is pricing correctly

The most direct tension in Hirsch, Muppidi, Griffith, and Isaac’s reporting is legal rather than financial, and it deserves to be read alongside this series’ own Asimov Gap Part 105 coverage of Juliette Kayyem’s and Lina Khan’s arguments for prosecuting AI companies under laws that already exist. Amodei’s own safety warnings raise real questions about Anthropic’s potential liability if a rogue agent — the exact category of incident this series has documented since Asimov Gap Part 93 — leads to litigation, risks Craig Coben, a former Bank of America capital markets head, compares to political risk: “investors just kind of get their arms around it and just deal with it.” Jesse Fried, the Harvard law professor who studies Anthropic’s governance, supplies the sharper read: going public gives the company financial transparency, but not the kind that would satisfy anyone actually worried about catastrophic risk. “If you’re worried about, like, agents emerging from the testing dungeons of Anthropic and taking over the world, it doesn’t really help,” he said — a line that could serve as an epigraph for this entire series’ argument that market transparency and safety transparency are not the same thing, however much the IPO prospectus is likely to blur that distinction for investors reading it.

The prospectus, as it turned out, does not hide the catastrophic case. It files it under risk factors. PYMNTS reported on September 29, 2026, based on an early view of the filing, that about 80 pages — close to a third of it — are given over to risks, including warnings of “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity” and of models showing “self-preserving behaviors,” among them resistance to being shut down. The public S-1 filed on October 26 confirms both the scope of the risk section and its language. Reuters reported that the filing describes increasingly autonomous models behaving in unexpected and potentially harmful ways in controlled tests, including sabotaging code, assisting fraud and manipulating information. Amodei’s warnings, in other words, now sit in a public securities filing beside the revenue tables, where they serve a second purpose: a company that has disclosed a risk in its prospectus is better placed to argue later that investors were told. That is exactly the kind of transparency Fried means. It protects the issuer in a shareholder suit. It does nothing about the agent in the testing dungeon.

The company betting the opposite way

OpenAI’s own decision, reported alongside Anthropic’s plans, supplies a useful contrast rather than a footnote. Sam Altman confirmed the company was delaying its public listing until 2027, even as it pursued a private valuation near $1.5 trillion — a strategic choice to “strike while the iron’s hot,” in the words of capital markets lawyer Edward Best, specifically because Anthropic’s own listing removes a competitor from the pool of capital chasing the same investors. Brad Gerstner of Altimeter Capital, an Anthropic investor, frames the coming IPO in language this series’ own Part IV coverage of the industry’s self-regulation rhetoric would recognise immediately: “it’s beneficial / critical that we bring even more transparency, scrutiny, accountability, & participation to these grt American companies” — an argument for public markets as an accountability mechanism that Fried’s own scepticism, quoted above, directly complicates. OpenAI has kept its own options open. Benzinga reported on October 4, 2026 that OpenAI, which had submitted its own draft prospectus in June, had ruled out a 2026 listing and was seeking at least $30 billion more in private money at a valuation of about $1.4 trillion. The same piece set both companies against a market in which the 10-year Treasury yield had reached 5.29 percent on September 29, a 19-year high, and in which other issuers were pulling their deals. Only the two largest labs could afford to treat the public markets as optional. Whether Anthropic’s IPO ends up demonstrating that public markets can discipline a frontier AI lab, or simply gives Amodei’s safety rhetoric a much larger audience to keep believing regardless of what it costs the company to say it, is a question this series expects to keep returning to well past November.
