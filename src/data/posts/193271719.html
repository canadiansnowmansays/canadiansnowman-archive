Wayne launched the Elsinore Literary River Tour on a Saturday morning in July, at nine o’clock sharp, from the municipal dock on the Avon River — two hundred metres downstream from the pedestrian bridge and, he had been careful to note in the permit application, six metres from the bank the swans use in April.

It was now July. The swans had finished with that bank for the season. Wayne had checked.

He had rented four paddle boats from a recreational equipment supplier in Guelph. He had laminated twelve copies of the route map, printed in colour, with numbered waypoints, thumbnail photographs of relevant landmarks, and a brief historical note at each stop combining, in roughly equal measure, the literary heritage of the Elsinore Festival and the biographical highlights of Elsinore’s most famous native son. He had prepared a playlist. The playlist opened with three minutes of instrumental Shakespeare — a recording from a 1987 Festival production now in the public domain — and transitioned, at Waypoint 3, to a selection of acoustic arrangements from the years 2009 to 2015, which Wayne felt struck the appropriate balance between reverence and accessibility.

Alistair had not been consulted about the playlist. Wayne had assessed the probable outcome of that consultation and made a professional decision.

Waypoint 3: The Avon Narrows. Poetic reflection. Gerald is checking the documentation. The Swan Warden is on her way.

Twelve participants arrived at the dock at nine o’clock.

There were three South Korean tourists who had found the tour through a travel forum dedicated to Canadian cultural destinations and had booked six weeks in advance. There were two retired schoolteachers from Guelph who had seen the listing on the Elsinore tourism website and thought it sounded lovely. There was a couple from Hamilton celebrating their anniversary, who had intended to book the winery tour in Niagara and had ended up, through a sequence of decisions they would later be unable to fully reconstruct, in Elsinore instead, which they were beginning to consider an improvement. There was a man from Orangeville who had understood from the listing that this was a duck boat tour and had brought binoculars. There was Gerald Fitch, who had come because it was a public event associated with Elsinore’s tourism portfolio and someone had to see how it went. And there was Carly Meadows, who was covering it for the Beacon with her notebook and her phone, which Norman had told her to do as though it were a minor assignment, which she had learned to treat as a signal that it might not be minor at all.

Wayne welcomed the group. He assigned boats. He explained the safety procedures with the calm authority of a man who has given safety briefings before, which he had, though not previously on water. He started the playlist. They pushed off.

The first two waypoints proceeded as planned.

Waypoint 1, the Elizabethan Gardens, produced a photograph from the South Korean tourists, a note in Gerald’s margin about the seasonal planting schedule, and a question from Carly to Wayne about how long the gardens had been maintained in their current form, which produced a four-minute answer she wrote down entirely and later condensed to one sentence, keeping the detail about the bench placement because it was specific and true.

Waypoint 2, the pedestrian bridge, produced a moment of genuine loveliness as the morning light came through the iron railings in a way that Wayne had not planned for but that the anniversary couple photographed with the focused gratitude of people who have stumbled into something better than they expected. The man from Orangeville looked carefully at the water for ducks and found none, but noted three mallards near the far bank and photographed them anyway with the philosophical equanimity of a man who has made peace with the difference between what he came for and what he found.

At Waypoint 3, the Avon Narrows, which the laminated route map described as an opportunity for poetic reflection, the playlist transitioned from Shakespeare to the acoustic arrangements just as one of the paddle boats grounded gently on the bank.

It was not a dramatic grounding. There was no lurch, no cry, no moment of obvious alarm. The boat simply ceased to move forward, in the way that boats do when they have encountered the bottom, and sat there with the quiet finality of something that has decided it has gone far enough.

The boat contained Gerald Fitch, one of the retired schoolteachers from Guelph, and the man from Orangeville.

Gerald looked at the route map. They were at Waypoint 3: the Avon Narrows. He read the historical note, which described the Narrows as a site of significant ecological interest and an important habitat transition zone. He reflected on this, poetically, as the map suggested.

The man from Orangeville looked at the bank. “Is that normal?” he said.

Gerald said he didn’t believe so, but that he was checking the documentation.

Wayne, in the lead boat, observed the situation and turned his boat around with the smoothness of a man pivoting from one section of a presentation to another. He continued his narration. He noted that the Avon was a dynamic waterway with variable depth characteristics and that this was, in fact, an ideal opportunity to appreciate the intimacy of the river environment. He said this in the same voice he used for everything, which was the voice of a man for whom things are proceeding according to plan, whether or not they are.

The playlist continued. An acoustic guitar rendered a melody that was recognisable to approximately eight of the twelve participants.

Carly, in the trailing boat, had been photographing steadily since Waypoint 1. She took several photographs of the grounding situation. Then she lowered the phone and looked at Gerald in the stranded boat — sitting upright, the laminated route map open in his lap, one hand resting on a paddle, the July light on the water around him, reading the habitat description with the focused composure of a man who has been given documentation relevant to his situation and intends to read it — and she took one more photograph.

She did not immediately know why this was the photograph. She knew it was.

The Swan Warden had been in the Elizabethan Gardens since eight that morning, conducting her quarterly habitat survey. She had observed the tour group’s departure from the dock and had noted it in her log. She was at her bench near the water’s edge when the grounding occurred.

She watched for a moment. She stood. She walked to the nearest bank, looked at the boat, looked at the angle of approach, assessed the current.

“Push off from the left,” she said.

Gerald found a paddle. He pushed off from the left.

The boat released immediately and moved back into the main channel with the ease of something that had simply been waiting to be asked correctly.

The Swan Warden walked back to her bench. She noted the incident in her habitat log as a minor navigational event with no environmental impact. She resumed her survey.

Alistair was crossing the pedestrian bridge on his way to a production meeting at ten past nine when he observed the tour group below. He stopped. He watched Wayne navigating the lead boat with professional composure, narrating steadily. He watched Gerald’s boat release from the bank and rejoin the group. He watched the South Korean tourists photographing the whole proceeding with the thorough coverage of people for whom documentation is a form of respect.

He stood on the bridge for a moment longer than he had intended to.

Then he walked on.

He has denied, since, that he smiled. Carly, who had looked up at the bridge at that moment and seen him there, has not been asked for her recollection. She is keeping it.

Maggie heard about the tour from Jim, who had walked past the dock at eight forty-five with the vizsla and the corgi on their Saturday combined walk, and had observed Wayne’s preparations: the laminated maps laid out on the dock in a row, the playlist equipment in a waterproof case, the safety vest folded precisely.

He had assessed the preparations with the attention of a man who reads situations for a living.

“I’d give it four out of five stars,” he told Maggie. “Wayne’ll fix the depth thing.”

He was correct. Wayne consulted the Avon River navigation records that Gerald had on file, identified two additional shallow points, and adjusted the route for the following week’s tour without announcement. He simply incorporated it. The subsequent tours were uneventful.

That evening, Carly posted the photograph to the Beacon’s Instagram account. She wrote the caption in three drafts. The first was explanatory. The second was too clever. The third read: “Waypoint 3: Poetic Reflection. Results pending.”

Norman texted her at eight forty-seven: Good.

She stared at the text for a moment. In eight months, Norman had texted her Good twice. She filed this information.

The photograph was shared forty times that evening. By Sunday morning it had been shared two hundred times. By the following Friday it had been shared, from a Seoul address whose Instagram biography described its owner as a Canadian literature enthusiast with a particular interest in small-town Ontario, three hundred and forty times to an audience Carly could not fully identify but that Wayne was already researching with the focused attention of a man who has found a new market.

Gerald was shown the photograph on Wayne’s phone the following Monday. He looked at it for a moment. He said the route map was very clear, which was the point of laminating it. He handed the phone back.

Wayne framed the photograph. He hung it in the Bieber Heritage Lounge, between the signed hockey photograph and the Tim Hortons commemorative cup. It became, over the following months, the most commented-on object in the Lounge, which Wayne noted in his quarterly tourism figures and which Gerald filed.

A Note for the Record

Canada has approximately thirty-one thousand rivers. The Avon River in Perth County is not among the longest or the widest or the most historically significant. It runs through Elsinore because rivers run where the land allows them to run, which is not a decision the river makes so much as a fact the river inhabits.

Wayne’s Literary River Tour offers a ninety-minute excursion combining the theatrical heritage of a seventy-year-old festival and the biographical landmarks of a thirty-two-year-old pop star, narrated by a man who has found his niche and occupied it with complete professional conviction. It is not a world-historical event.

It is a paddle boat tour on a river in Ontario on a Saturday morning in July.

Three hundred and forty people in Seoul have seen Gerald reading the laminated route map in the grounded boat. Some of them have booked tickets for next summer. The Swan Warden’s navigational advice has received, at last count, twelve comments describing it as “iconic,” which is a word she has not used and would not use and which the river itself, if asked, would regard as an overstatement.

Push off from the left.

Sometimes this is all the instruction you need.
