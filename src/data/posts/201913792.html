On Wednesday, June 11, 2026, President Trump announced plans to meet with tech executives to discuss their "giving something back to the public," without specifying how that might happen. "If we do that," he promised, "the public will become very rich."

It was a sentence that would have been unthinkable eighteen months ago. Not the vagueness — that is entirely thinkable — but the premise. The President of the United States, sitting across from the architects of artificial intelligence, telling them the public deserves a share of what they’ve built.

The admission is now inescapable. SpaceX merged with Elon Musk’s xAI and went public at a valuation of $1.77 trillion, breaking every IPO record in history and closing its first day of trading up more than 19 percent. Anthropic filed confidentially for its own offering on June 1, days after a funding round valued it at $900 billion. OpenAI filed on June 8 at $730 billion. In the span of two weeks, the AI industry put a price tag on the intelligence it has been building — and the number was, conservatively, in the vicinity of three trillion dollars.

Three trillion dollars of value built, in no small part, from the writing, research, conversations, and creative output of people who will receive nothing.

The question is no longer whether the public should share in the AI boom. Everyone from Bernie Sanders to Donald Trump to Sam Altman is now on record saying it should. The question — the only question that matters — is how. And on that question, the gap between what is being proposed and what the industry will accept is as wide as it has ever been.

I. The Policy Menu

To understand what’s being debated, it helps to lay out the options clearly, because the politicians proposing them are not always clear about what they are actually trying to do.

Equity stakes. Senator Bernie Sanders plans to introduce legislation imposing a one-time tax on leading AI companies that would give the public half of their shares, to be placed in a sovereign wealth fund modeled on Alaska’s Permanent Fund, which has paid annual dividends directly to residents for fifty years. The legal architecture for this idea comes from two law professors, Jeremy Bearer-Friend and Sarah Polcz, who have been presenting it at conferences for a couple of years. A similar plan has been floated in Britain. OpenAI itself proposed a version in April that would involve the government taking ownership stakes in AI companies and “the broader set of firms adopting and deploying AI.”

Usage taxes. A tax on tokens — the units of AI processing — would raise money and, its proponents argue, modestly slow the deployment of AI by making it more expensive. Representative Greg Casar of Texas, chair of the Congressional Progressive Caucus, has called that drag on AI adoption “a feature as much as a bug.” Senator Elizabeth Warren has proposed a levy on energy consumed by data centers. Andrew Yang suggests taxing AI agents rather than labor.

Capital taxes. Economists Philip Trammell and Dwarkesh Patel argued on Substack in December that in a world of advanced robotics and AI, “a global and highly progressive tax on capital will then indeed be essentially the only way to prevent inequality from growing extreme.” Their logic: if the machines have effectively replaced labor, then taxing capital is the same as taxing the machines.

Consumption taxes. The most conventionally acceptable proposal among economists is a value-added tax — taxing what people buy rather than what they earn. If AI generates abundance while eliminating wages, a consumption tax captures the surplus at the point of spending rather than at the point of income.

Universal basic capital. A fifth proposal has been gaining momentum across an unusually wide ideological range, from Bernie Sanders to Vivek Ramaswamy to Sam Altman to Donald Trump. The basic intuition: instead of taxing AI companies and distributing the proceeds as income, give every American an ownership stake — an account containing equity invested in a broad portfolio that compounds over time, independent of future tax revenues or shifting political winds. The One Big Beautiful Bill already created a micro-version in the form of “Trump Accounts” — $1,000 brokerage accounts for every child born between 2025 and 2028. Rogé Karma, writing in The Atlantic, notes that David Autor of MIT has called a properly designed version “no-regrets policy — a good idea in just about any scenario you can think of.” Critically, UBC comes in two very different versions: individual accounts that distribute equity without giving the government corporate governance rights, and a sovereign wealth fund — the Sanders proposal — that gives the federal government voting rights and board representation in every major AI company. The distinction between those two versions is the most consequential design choice in the entire debate, and it is not being made clearly in the public discussion.

The menu is long. The goals behind each item, however, are not always consistent with each other, and the economists who study this are notably blunt about saying so.

“What is the goal here? In almost all of these discussions, that’s what it comes down to,” said Lee Lockwood, a University of Virginia economist who has moved from public finance to the policy challenges of advanced AI.

II. The Skeptics Are Not Wrong

Each proposal has a serious objection attached to it, and the objections deserve to be heard.

On equity stakes: owning a share of an AI company does not prevent it from doing harm. Taking a stake in OpenAI would not have stopped the deployment of systems that spread disinformation, erode epistemic confidence, or accelerate surveillance. It would also, as Bearer-Friend and Polcz acknowledged in their own writing, “align the Treasury with investors who have a stake in preserving the value of their shares.” A government that owns 50 percent of Anthropic has a financial interest in Anthropic’s success — which may or may not coincide with the public interest when the two diverge.

On usage taxes: economists tend to argue that taxing AI as an input into business production is economically inefficient. It penalizes productivity. The cleaner version, in their view, is to tax AI as a final product — yes to taxing someone using ChatGPT to write a love letter, no to taxing a business using it to build an advertising campaign — and then use the proceeds as you see fit.

On capital taxes: the conventional wisdom among economists is that capital and labor need each other, and taxing machines deprives workers of the tools that make them more productive. Brian Albrecht, chief economist at the International Center for Law and Economics, has called a compute tax “a REALLY dumb idea.” The Trammell/Patel rebuttal is that the conventional wisdom holds only as long as the robots haven’t completely taken over — and that the tipping point may be closer than economists are comfortable admitting.

On consumption taxes: the entire proposal rests on the optimistic assumption that AI generates so much value that people consume more than ever while working less than ever. That may be true in the medium term. Whether it holds indefinitely is a different question.

On universal basic capital — the individual accounts version: the strongest objection is distributional rather than structural. The richest ten percent of Americans currently own roughly 90 percent of stocks; the bottom half owns less than one percent. Giving everyone an AI equity account narrows that gap but does not eliminate it, and does nothing to address the governance question of how the underlying companies are run.

On the Sanders sovereign wealth fund — the government-ownership version: the objection is structural and, in the current political environment, urgent. Samuel Hammond, chief economist at the Foundation for American Innovation, stated it directly to Karma: “The federal government is really the only entity powerful enough to be a real check on these companies. If they become joined at the hip, that check goes away. It can easily become a form of regulatory capture.” Norway’s sovereign wealth fund has maintained independence from the government that created it — but America is not Norway, and on June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Trump v. Slaughter that the president can remove the leaders of independent agencies at will, overturning ninety years of precedent. A sovereign wealth fund in the Trump era is, in practice, a giant discretionary pot of money the executive branch controls. Dean Baker of the Center for Economic and Policy Research posed the question directly: “Do you really want to see what happens when we give him the power to run large chunks of corporate America?” There is also a specific AI-governance consequence: earlier this year, Anthropic refused to allow the U.S. military to use Claude for domestic surveillance or autonomous weapons — the dispute documented across Parts 18 and 49 of this series. That refusal would not have been possible if the federal government owned a controlling stake in the company.

III. The Question Nobody Is Asking

There is a frame missing from almost every version of this debate, and it is the frame that matters most for this series.

The taxation debate is structured as an economic question. How do we capture value? How do we distribute it? How do we avoid distorting incentives? These are legitimate questions. They are not the only questions.

The Asimov Gap has always been, at its core, a governance question. Not: who gets the money? But: who gets to decide? The economic and the political are not the same thing, and conflating them is precisely the maneuver that the industry benefits from most.

Consider the equity stake proposal. In principle, a government with a 50 percent ownership stake and board representation in a major AI company would have significant influence over its decisions. In practice, the industry’s lobbyists spent $47.8 million in Washington in 2025 — a figure documented in Part 43 of this series — and the laws that emerged from that spending reflect the gap between what the industry endorses in public and what it will accept in private. A government that is simultaneously a regulator and a major shareholder faces an obvious conflict of interest that serves the industry more than the public.

The most telling signal of this dynamic is not the conflict of interest in the abstract. It is the specific reaction of the industry to the Sanders proposal. Shortly after Sanders unveiled his sovereign wealth fund bill, Sam Altman — whose company would be among those required to hand over 50 percent of its equity — requested a meeting with the senator. The two reportedly agreed on the broad thrust of the policy, differing only on specific details like the 50 percent figure. Days later, Trump said his administration was working on a potential partnership with AI companies whereby “pieces” of the companies “could be given to the American public.” When reporters noted the similarity to the Sanders proposal, Trump replied that “as far as economics is concerned,” he and Sanders “aren’t that far apart.” The president of the United States and the CEO of OpenAI are both enthusiastic about a proposal requiring OpenAI to hand over half its equity to the government. When the target of a policy enthusiastically endorses it, that is not evidence the policy is well-designed. It is evidence the target expects to benefit from it.

The consumption tax is the most politically palatable option for the industry precisely because it does not touch governance at all. It allows AI companies to operate as they choose, deploy what they choose, and govern themselves as they choose — and then writes a check to the public as compensation. It is, in the most literal sense, hush money at scale.

The token tax at least creates a speed bump. Senator Warren’s data center energy levy at least creates a cost. The capital tax at least acknowledges that the machines are doing work that labor used to do. None of them, however, address the fundamental question that Isaac Asimov spent fifty years asking in fiction: who decides what the machines are for?

Consider what was happening in the same week that the taxation debate was playing out in Washington. On June 9, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5 — a deliberately hobbled version of its most powerful model, Mythos, with guardrails designed to prevent hackers from using it to attack computer networks. The guardrails, cybersecurity experts noted, also prevent legitimate defenders from using it to protect those same networks. Three days later, on Friday night, the Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to suspend access to both Fable 5 and Mythos for all foreign nationals — including foreign nationals employed by Anthropic itself. No legislation. No public process. No congressional vote. A social media post at the end of the business week, and the technology disappeared for a significant portion of the people who built it.

Dean Ball, a senior fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation and a former adviser on AI under President Trump, called the order “baffling” and said he had “no words.” It was the second time in three months that the Trump administration had targeted Anthropic specifically: in March, the Pentagon had deemed the company an unacceptable supply chain risk. Anthropic called the Commerce order “a misunderstanding” and said it was working to restore access. The company’s rival, OpenAI, had released comparable technology to hundreds of organizations with no comparable restriction.

This is AI governance as it is actually practiced in 2026: not through the deliberative mechanisms that the taxation debate assumes, but through anonymous Friday-night orders from executive agencies, with no stated duration, no public rationale, and no appeal process the public can observe. The companies are not being taxed. They are being controlled — selectively, inconsistently, and without the democratic accountability that either Sanders or Trump, in their very different ways, are promising the public it will receive.

IV. The Number at the End

Lockwood’s most honest moment in the Peter Coy interview for the New York Times came near the end, in what Coy called “a sci-fi scenario.”

What happens, Coy asked, if AI stops working in the service of humanity and starts investing in itself for its own purposes? The infrastructure becomes massive; human consumption does not rise commensurately. In that case, Lockwood and his colleague Anton Korinek argue, taxing capital would finally make sense.

When they presented this scenario at a conference, Harvard Business School’s Matthew Weinzierl expressed doubt about whether such a powerful AI system would even submit to human authority.

Lockwood’s response deserves to be quoted directly: “An absolutely superhuman intelligence that no one essentially owns or controls, in what sense are we even able to tax them? It seems like a far-out thing.”

He added, almost as an afterthought: if we lose control of the AI, tax policy may be the least of our problems.

This is the endpoint of the taxation debate if it is pursued without the governance debate. You cannot tax a system you do not control. You cannot regulate a technology whose developers have successfully argued that regulation is the enemy of progress. You cannot write a public dividend check on a future that has been foreclosed by the absence of democratic oversight in the present.

The IPO filings are not, fundamentally, financial events. They are governance events. They are the moment at which the people who built these systems decided who owns them, on what terms, and with what obligations to the public. The answer, in each case, is: private shareholders, on terms favorable to the founders, with obligations to be determined later.

The public, invited to participate at the retail price after the insiders have taken their positions, is not a beneficiary of the AI boom. It is the last buyer.

Sanders is right that something should come back to the public. The industry’s public endorsements of the principle — from Altman, from Musk, from Trump — are correct in their acknowledgment that the old social contract is broken. The question of what replaces it will not be answered by any single tax proposal.

It will be answered, if it is answered at all, by whether the public is in the room when the answer is written.

The gap between being promised a share of the future and being given a seat at the table where the future is decided — that is the Asimov Gap, applied to the economy.
