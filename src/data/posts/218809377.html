Stephen Gordon · October 6, 2026

There is an old Texas rule, older than the Republican majority and probably older than the Alamo gift shop: you don’t talk about a man’s religion at the dinner table. The Republican Party of Texas has suspended it for the duration.

At the midterm convention in Dallas last month, Robert Jeffress, senior pastor of First Baptist Dallas, stepped to the microphone and delivered something closer to an excommunication than a campaign speech. “The beliefs of James Talarico are certainly wrong. But they’re more than wrong. The beliefs of James Talarico are blasphemous. They are evil, and they are not beliefs that any true Christian could support. Ever.”

The president has his own version, shorter and less theological: “Talafreako.” And, as David French reported in “The ‘God Gap’ Is Narrowing in Texas” (New York Times, October 4, 2026), the right-wing podcast circuit has been mentioning Trump less and Talarico more, a shift the Wall Street Journal documented last month.

Tonight, October 6, a debate stage is set in Dallas; as of this writing, Paxton has not said whether he will be on it. When we last talked Texas, the subject was the man at the top of the Republican ticket (”The Drag”). This instalment is about the thing Texans are supposed not to discuss at dinner, and which has quietly become the central argument of what French calls arguably the most consequential Senate race in the country.

A Seminarian on the Ballot

Talarico is, in the words of his critics, the worst kind of Democrat: a believing one. He is a Presbyterian, a former middle-school teacher, and a seminarian on leave from Austin Presbyterian Theological Seminary. He quotes scripture without notes and without apology. He is part of a small wave of faith-forward Democrats this cycle, alongside Adam Hamilton, the Methodist pastor running for the Senate in Kansas, and Rob Sand in Iowa. The Rev. Raphael Warnock has already shown in Georgia, twice, that the model can win in the South.

The trouble, for his opponents, is that this makes Talarico hard to attack in the usual way. Texas Republicans have spent forty years telling voters that Democrats are hostile to faith. That line doesn’t work against a man who can out-quote them on Matthew. So the attack has shifted from whether Talarico is a Christian to whether he is the right kind.

Jeffress, speaking with French on Zoom, laid out his three objections: that Talarico has said there is “a spectrum of genders,” that his spiritual mentor has expressed discomfort with words like God and Father, and that he has “very recently indicated that all religions were basically different paths that led to the same truth.” Jeffress was careful to add that he endorses no one except for president, and that Talarico’s beliefs “certainly don’t disqualify him from being a senator. They do disqualify him from being a follower of Christ.”

French, an evangelical himself and a conservative by any pre-2016 measure, called that “thin gruel” on which to condemn someone to hell. The Paxton campaign’s advertising, which runs to more than $100 million, has been less nuanced: it quotes Talarico’s since-renounced remarks that “God is nonbinary,” calls him “radical” and “weird,” and has questioned his manhood along with his faith.

The Pass

Here is where the argument turns on itself. For a decade, many of the same leaders now examining Talarico’s soul told evangelical voters that character was beside the point. You are electing a president, not a pastor, went the line in 2016. Personal conduct belongs to God; policy belongs to the ballot.

In Texas this year, that logic has been inverted, but only in one direction. The Democrat is being judged like a pastor. The Republican is being judged like a president. “Paxton?” French writes. “He gets a pass. Just like Trump.”

It is a considerable pass. Paxton was impeached by a Republican Texas House on articles including bribery and obstruction of justice, and acquitted by the Senate in a vote his own wife was barred from casting. That wife, State Senator Angela Paxton, has since filed for divorce on what she called “biblical grounds.” He has reportedly been seen overseas, mid-campaign, with a woman who appears to be his current girlfriend.

And on October 1, the Associated Press published an account of his calendar. Brian Slodysko and Jesse Badayn found roughly 730 blank workdays on his official schedule since he took office: 90 in 2021, 112 in 2024. They counted more than 275 out-of-state trips: California wine country, the Caribbean, Israel, India, Taiwan, South Africa, three trips to Europe, and five visits to Park City, Utah. Texas taxpayers have paid at least $1.9 million for the security detail that goes with him. His office’s response was that a blank calendar doesn’t capture calls, negotiations and case strategy.

French’s summary of the man is hard to improve upon: Paxton “possesses all of Donald Trump’s corruption and none of Trump’s charisma.”

The Resolution of 1998

The Southern Baptist Convention once had a view on all this. In 1998, at the height of the Clinton–Lewinsky scandal, it passed a resolution on the moral character of public officials, which warned that “tolerance of serious wrong by leaders sears the conscience of the culture, spawns unrestrained immorality and lawlessness in the society, and surely results in God’s judgment.”

French asked Jeffress about it. Jeffress’s answer was disarmingly candid: “What was going on in 1998? Monica Lewinsky and Bill Clinton… I think it could have been partisan, very much a partisan reaction to that.”

Which is to say, the most prominent evangelical voice in Texas politics has conceded what critics have argued for a quarter-century: the standard was never about character. It was about which party the character belonged to. Asked what he looks for instead, Jeffress cited Romans on government’s role in protecting society, and then put it more plainly: “I don’t want somebody with a demeanor of Jesus necessarily — be nice if they did — but I want the meanest, toughest S.O.B. I can find.”

As a Canadian, I’ll note that we have had our own moments of mixing the pulpit with the polls. Stockwell Day’s views on creation became an issue in the 2000 federal campaign, and the voters made their judgment. But I can’t recall a Canadian pastor of national standing announcing from a party convention stage that a candidate’s faith was evil, while explaining that the other candidate’s conduct was immaterial because the job called for an S.O.B. Even in Texas, that is a big hat to wear.

The God Gap, by the Numbers

None of this would matter much if the religious vote weren’t so lopsided, and so large. French lays out the arithmetic. In 2024, white evangelicals voted for Trump 82 to 17, a 65-point margin; Trump lost everyone else by 18. In Texas, evangelicals make up 27 percent of adults and Catholics 22 percent, and nationally Catholics swung 13 points toward Trump.

The more unsettling finding for Democrats comes from the political scientist Ryan Burge, who looked at 22 Protestant denominations and found that all but four moved right between 2020 and 2024. The predominantly Black National Baptist Convention went from 10 percent for Trump to 29 percent, nearly a tripling. That is the “God gap” Democrats have been losing on since Reagan, and it got wider in the last election, not narrower.

French offers two readings. The pessimistic one is that the religious vote is sliding away from Democrats for good. The hopeful one is that the slide is recent, and therefore reversible: that believers weren’t converting to Republicanism so much as punishing Democrats for a secular, socially radical left. If that’s right, a candidate who speaks fluent scripture and campaigns on decency is exactly the kind of correction they were waiting for.

Permission Structures

The most useful phrase in French’s piece comes from an unexpected source: Matt Mackowiak, a former senior Cornyn campaign official. Paxton’s supporters, he told French, are “trying to exterminate RINOs, but they’ll turn the state blue.” His point was that Paxton’s unpopularity can only push voters so far. Talarico has to give them somewhere to land, a “permission structure” that lets a lifelong Republican vote for a Democrat without feeling like a traitor.

The evidence is that he has built one, and that it has very little to do with theology. Voters aren’t interested in joining Talarico’s church. What they respond to is how he conducts himself. French, characteristically, found the verse for it: “By their fruit you will recognize them.” The head of an Austin-area evangelical nonprofit told him Talarico “seems like a genuinely good guy… I’d like to have a coffee with him.” A former Republican official said, “I would want him as my neighbor.”

Neither would give a name. “There is a massive social barrier” to saying you’d vote for a Democrat, one told French, and “evangelical cancel culture” is real. That may be the most important detail in the whole story. The polls have Talarico ahead, and the people moving his way can’t say so out loud. That is not a recipe for overstating his support.

It also helps explain what happened in the primary. Karl Rove said at the Texas Tribune Festival that tracking polls had Republicans breaking toward John Cornyn until Trump endorsed Paxton, after which the trend reversed. Paxton won the May runoff by nearly 28 points. By Mackowiak’s count, Cornyn had voted with Trump 99 percent of the time. His offences were the other one percent: the gun-safety compromise after Uvalde, and voting to certify the 2020 election. Paxton’s credential was the opposite, the December 2020 lawsuit to block four states’ electoral votes, a suit so flimsy that his own hand-picked solicitor general, Kyle Hawkins, refused to sign it.

Beaumont

French did the thing most columnists don’t: he drove to Beaumont and walked a working-class neighbourhood with Talarico canvassers. Samantha Bartley of the Hardin County Democrats captured the mood: “I’m praying we aren’t like the Dallas Cowboys, OK? We can beat some booty until the fourth quarter, we got two minutes left, and we can drop the ball and lose every time.”

Shannon Heath, a precinct chair, said local membership had doubled. She wasn’t seeing movement among “Mega MAGA,” she said, but “Republicans who are still Republicans, a lot of them have changed their minds.”

Two doors tell the story. Sharon, an older woman who leans Republican, listened politely and said she’d supported Trump: “rude and crude, but things had to change.” Then, unprompted: “I hate Ken Paxton.” She didn’t commit to Talarico. But the difference was plain. Trump was rude and crude and had his uses. Paxton, to Sharon, had none.

The last house of the day belonged to Brady, who answered the door shirtless and in a kilt, excused himself to put on a shirt, and then described himself as a Marxist-Leninist who “chronically doomscrolls.” He votes Democratic, and doubts anyone else is changing: “Everyone’s kind of dug in.”

Sharon and Brady are, as French put it, the two farthest poles of the Talarico tent. He needs both, and everyone between.

Grace as a Political Weapon

The French piece ends somewhere I didn’t expect a political column to go, which is grace.

Paxton’s movement, he argues, runs on fear and fury. That is what lets its members overlook or even cheer the corruption: there are no lines he won’t cross against the people they hate. Jeffress’s “mean S.O.B.” is not a bug; it is the product. But what happens to that product when the hatred starts to fade?

In Beaumont, French couldn’t find any. Julie Stephenson, pastor of First Christian Church, a Disciples of Christ congregation with red and blue members, said, “There’s no animosity. It’s not red against blue in our congregation.” When French asked Heath whether she was angry at MAGA supporters, she looked at him with something like incredulity: “They are our neighbors.”

Even Jeffress, for all his talk of blasphemy, offered a door left ajar. Asked what he’d say to a parishioner whose conscience pointed to Talarico, he said: “Follow what you feel like God’s leading you to do. Period. I’d say, ‘Fine, I’ll see you in church next Sunday.’”

It is worth noticing what has happened here. The Republican campaign in Texas has spent $100 million and a convention keynote arguing that Talarico is the wrong kind of Christian. And the voters drifting toward him, in coffee shops in Austin and on doorsteps in Beaumont, seem to have concluded that the question was never which kind of Christian a candidate is. It was whether he behaves like one.

One evangelical, asked why he’d vote for a man he admired but disagreed with, gave French the most honest answer of the campaign: “It’s an experiment. Let’s see what happens.”

Texas has run the other experiment for thirty-two years. It may be ready to try this one.
