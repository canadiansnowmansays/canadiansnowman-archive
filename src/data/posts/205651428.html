When Xi Jinping announced the Belt and Road Initiative in September 2013 — the Silk Road Economic Belt in a speech in Kazakhstan, and the Maritime Silk Road in a speech in Indonesia a month later — he framed it in the language of historical connection and mutual benefit. The ancient Silk Road, along which Chinese silk and porcelain had moved westward and spices and glassware had moved eastward for two millennia, was being revived for the 21st century. China would invest in the infrastructure that its partners needed — the roads, railways, ports, power plants, and communications networks that the developing world lacked — and both sides would prosper from the connection.

The framing was not wrong. The infrastructure deficits in Central Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa are real. The Asian Development Bank estimated in 2017 that Asia alone needed $26 trillion in infrastructure investment by 2030 to sustain its growth trajectory. The Western financial institutions that might have financed this infrastructure — the World Bank, the IMF, the regional development banks — had neither the capital nor the institutional appetite to move at the speed and scale that developing countries wanted. China, which had accumulated $4 trillion in foreign exchange reserves and a state banking system capable of mobilising enormous sums rapidly, did have both.

The initiative grew faster than almost anyone anticipated. By 2023, more than 150 countries had signed onto Belt and Road agreements, representing about 75 percent of the world’s population and roughly 40 percent of global GDP. Chinese state banks — the China Development Bank and the China Export-Import Bank — had disbursed more than $800 billion in loans and investments across the network. The physical infrastructure being built included the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, a $62 billion collection of roads, railways, and power plants connecting China’s Xinjiang region to Pakistan’s Gwadar port on the Arabian Sea; the standard-gauge railway from Mombasa to Nairobi in Kenya; the Piraeus port in Greece, acquired by COSCO Shipping in 2016 and expanded into China’s first significant European logistics hub; and dozens of other projects across three continents.

The Western criticism of the Belt and Road — that it represents “debt trap diplomacy,” deliberately entrapping borrowing countries in debt they cannot repay in order to seize strategic assets — is both partially true and significantly overstated.

The debt trap narrative is built primarily on the case of Hambantota port in Sri Lanka, which the Sri Lankan government leased to a Chinese state company for 99 years in 2017 after defaulting on loans used to build the port. The case is real and the strategic implications are significant — a Chinese-operated port on a major Indian Ocean shipping lane is not a neutral commercial facility. But it is not representative of the Belt and Road’s typical mechanics. Most Belt and Road loans have not resulted in asset seizures. Most borrowing countries have renegotiated terms, received debt relief, or delayed repayment without losing sovereignty over financed assets. The initiative has produced genuine infrastructure that borrowing countries wanted.

It has also produced genuine problems: some projects were overpriced relative to competitive alternatives, some featured opaque contracting that favoured Chinese companies and Chinese workers over local ones, some financed projects with questionable economic rationale that happened to serve Chinese strategic interests, and some generated unsustainable debt loads for countries that lacked the capacity to evaluate the terms they were accepting. Ecuador, Zambia, Pakistan, and Ethiopia have all experienced significant debt stress from Belt and Road borrowing, requiring renegotiation with Chinese lenders that has generally produced extended repayment timelines rather than asset seizures but that has created political leverage for Chinese interests in each country.

The Africa dimension of the Belt and Road is the one that most dramatically illustrates both the genuine development contribution and the strategic calculation. Chinese investment in African infrastructure — ports, railways, roads, telecommunications networks, and power generation — has been transformative in scale. The Addis Ababa to Djibouti railway, completed in 2016, was the first electrified railway in sub-Saharan Africa and the first cross-border railway on the continent in decades. The Mombasa to Nairobi standard-gauge railway in Kenya, completed in 2017, reduced freight journey times from twenty hours to eight. The power generation investment — Chinese-built hydroelectric dams, coal plants, and solar installations — has brought electricity to regions that had none.

The strategic calculation is equally clear. Africa holds roughly sixty percent of the world’s uncultivated arable land, thirty percent of the world’s mineral reserves including the cobalt, lithium, and rare earth elements that the 141 pages require, and a population that will reach 2.5 billion by 2050 — the world’s largest consumer market of the second half of the 21st century. China is building the infrastructure relationships, the diplomatic ties, and the commercial presence now that will shape its access to these resources and markets for decades. The development is real. The strategic purpose is equally real. The two are not in contradiction, which is precisely what makes the Belt and Road difficult for Western governments to counter: they are competing with a programme that is simultaneously genuinely helpful and explicitly strategic, and they lack the state financing capacity to offer an alternative at comparable scale.

The G7’s Build Back Better World initiative — rebranded as the Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment in 2022 — has pledged $600 billion in infrastructure investment by 2027. Whether these pledges translate into disbursed capital at the required speed, through institutions capable of moving as fast as Chinese state banks, remains to be demonstrated.

The tributary system that China’s imperial dynasties used to organize their international relationships is the most useful historical frame for understanding the Belt and Road, and Chinese scholars have not been shy about drawing the comparison.

The tributary system worked as follows: peripheral states sent envoys to the Chinese court bearing tribute — symbolic recognition of the emperor’s suzerainty — and received in return imperial recognition of their rulers’ legitimacy, access to Chinese trade, and the protection that association with the Middle Kingdom implied. The relationship was asymmetric — China at the centre, tributaries at the periphery — but it was not simply exploitative. Tributary states received genuine benefits: trading rights, diplomatic protection, and the prestige of imperial recognition. What they gave up was a degree of sovereign equality and an acknowledgement, however formal, that their authority derived in some sense from Chinese recognition.

The Belt and Road does not require tributary acknowledgement in the traditional sense. It does tend to produce voting alignment at the United Nations, reduced criticism of Chinese policies on Xinjiang, Taiwan, and the South China Sea, and access for Chinese companies to markets and resources in partner countries. The benefits flow from China to partners. The recognition flows from partners to China. The asymmetry is structural and intentional.

This is not unique to China. American foreign policy has operated through comparable mechanisms for decades — aid, military alliances, and trade relationships that provide genuine benefits while producing political alignment with American positions. The difference is that the American system is built around explicit values commitments — democracy, human rights, rule of law — that create friction with authoritarian partners and provide a basis for criticism when those values are violated. The Belt and Road asks for none of this. It asks only for access and alignment, which is both more flexible and more honest about what infrastructure finance actually requires.

The strategic geography of the Belt and Road is most clearly legible in its maritime dimension.

The Maritime Silk Road traces a chain of port investments from China’s eastern seaboard through the South China Sea, across the Indian Ocean, through the Gulf of Aden, and into the Mediterranean. The ports that China has invested in along this route — Gwadar in Pakistan, Hambantota in Sri Lanka, Chittagong in Bangladesh, Kyaukpyu in Myanmar, Djibouti in the Horn of Africa, Piraeus in Greece — form a network that military analysts have called the “string of pearls”: a series of logistics and potential naval facilities that could support Chinese naval power projection across the Indo-Pacific and beyond.

The Digital Silk Road, a less-discussed component of the Belt and Road, may ultimately prove more consequential than the physical infrastructure. Huawei, ZTE, and other Chinese technology companies have built telecommunications networks — including 5G infrastructure, submarine cables, and data centres — across Belt and Road partner countries. The strategic implications are significant: a country whose telecommunications infrastructure is built and operated by Chinese companies is a country whose digital traffic passes through systems that China can monitor, and whose infrastructure can theoretically be disrupted by the same companies that built it. Huawei alone has built telecommunications infrastructure in more than 170 countries. The 5G networks it has installed in developing countries are, in many cases, the only 5G those countries will have for a generation.

Western governments have responded by restricting Huawei’s access to their own markets and pressuring allies to do the same. The UK, Australia, Canada, and several other countries have moved to exclude Huawei from their 5G networks. The United States has gone further, restricting the export of American semiconductors to Huawei, which forced the company to develop its own chip design capabilities — which it has done, successfully enough to produce the Kirin 9000S processor that appeared in the Mate 60 Pro in 2023, demonstrating that American export controls, while damaging, had not achieved their stated goal of preventing Chinese high-end chip development. The 141 pages were partly written in response to that assessment.

China’s only overseas military base — in Djibouti, opened in 2017 — sits at the entrance to the Red Sea on a plot adjacent to the American base at Camp Lemonnier. The proximity is not accidental and is not comfortable for either side. It is a physical demonstration of what the Belt and Road has produced at its most strategic: Chinese military infrastructure at a chokepoint that commands the shipping lanes connecting Europe, the Gulf, and Asia. Zheng He’s treasure fleets sailed these waters. The People’s Liberation Army Navy now patrols them.

The Long Return, as a geographic project, is this: the reassertion of Chinese presence across the maritime routes that connected the Middle Kingdom to the known world before the Century of Humiliation interrupted the connection. The Belt and Road is not simply commercial infrastructure. It is the physical architecture of restored centrality — the 21st-century equivalent of the tribute system, built not with imperial edicts but with construction loans and port agreements.

Whether it achieves its strategic purpose depends on what happens in the places the next two parts of this series address: the territories China claims and cannot yet fully control, and the technology competition that will determine whether the restored centrality is real or merely structural.

A Canadian note is appropriate here, because Canada’s relationship with the Belt and Road has been distinctly uncomfortable and distinctly instructive.

Canada formally declined to join the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank — the multilateral development bank that China established in 2015 as the Belt and Road’s financing partner — in the face of American pressure, making it one of the few major developed economies to do so. Canada subsequently experienced a sustained deterioration of its relationship with China following the arrest of Huawei CFO Meng Wanzhou in Vancouver in December 2018, at American request, under a US extradition warrant related to sanctions evasion. China responded by arresting two Canadian citizens — Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor — in what was widely characterised as hostage diplomacy. The two Michaels spent nearly three years in Chinese detention before being released simultaneously with Meng’s return to China in September 2021.

The episode illustrated something the Belt and Road also illustrates: that Canada, like every mid-sized Western country, sits in a position where its relationship with China is substantially determined by decisions made in Washington and Beijing rather than in Ottawa. The Belt and Road is not primarily about Canada. Canada is not primarily in the Belt and Road. But the strategic competition that the Belt and Road represents shapes every Canadian export sector, every Canadian university research partnership, every Canadian pension fund that holds Chinese equities, and every Canadian company that sources from Chinese manufacturers. The Middle Kingdom’s Long Return is happening whether Canada is watching or not. This series, written from Toronto, is an attempt to watch.
