Part One of this entry closed on Seth Center’s own ceiling for this summit: not a breakthrough, not “humanity’s last backstop,” but a nonbinding commitment to better safety benchmarking, disclosure of concerning incidents, and a reaffirmation of the 2024 nuclear-control agreement. He said plainly it would read as underwhelming to anyone already convinced catastrophe is imminent. Xi Jinping landed at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday evening, 23 September 2026, and the reporting from that arrival day alone is close enough to Center’s modest list that it’s worth checking his other predictions against, one at a time — scoped deliberately to what the two governments signalled before Thursday’s actual talks began, not to how the summit ultimately turned out.

What actually happened Wednesday

The elaborate parts happened exactly as choreographed: a red-carpet greeting Trump doesn’t normally extend at the airport, a military flyover, a state dinner scheduled for Thursday with a tour of the National Archives built in. Zolan Kanno-Youngs, Ana Swanson, and colleagues, live-blogging the arrival for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, reported the substantive part came almost as an aside: Scott Bessent, talking to Fox News on the tarmac, announced the two countries had extended their trade truce — not the six-month-to-year range this newsletter’s companion series on the summit itself, The Washington Opera, had been tracking, but two months, to 10 January 2027, “to give us more time to see what we can do on the economic front.” That trade-and-tariff mechanics story belongs to that companion series in fuller detail; this entry sticks to what the extension signals for the AI portion specifically — which, on Center’s own read of how rigid this kind of diplomacy actually is, is closer to a placeholder than a settlement: not the finish line, a step to measure the next one against.

Part One’s forecast for the AI portion of the talks was already modest by design. Nicholas Kristof, reporting for the Times on 19 September, relayed Rajiv Shah of the Rockefeller Foundation suggesting cooperation might only be achievable in a narrow domain like reducing AI-assisted bioweapon risk — “doing one thing, even if it feels like it’s not solving the whole problem, at least creates a repeatable playbook.” Kristof separately floated a hotline between the two countries to defuse AI incidents before they escalate into military or diplomatic crises, echoing a proposal Sheehan made independently: what if an agent swarm like the one that hit Hugging Face had struck a PLA server instead, or produced a deepfake of Xi renouncing China’s claim to Taiwan? Jessica Chen Weiss, writing for the Times the same day as the arrival, converges on the identical modest scope from a third angle, proposing the two countries start not with AI risk in the abstract but with a named common enemy: ransomware gangs using AI to exploit vulnerabilities in software and critical infrastructure. Weiss also independently corroborates the WeChat vulnerability Sheehan surfaced in Part One — she confirms a California security firm found the flaw and that Beijing’s response was to have its top spy chief warn publicly that AI could “slip beyond control and threaten political and social stability,” the same Chen Yixin warning this entry has already traced. Three analysts, writing independently within days of each other, converge on the same shape: narrow, named, operational cooperation rather than anything resembling a comprehensive accord. Bessent’s actual announcement lands almost exactly inside that frame rather than beyond it.

On AI specifically, Bessent told Fox News he expected China to agree to a notification system for national-security AI incidents, describing Beijing as “very eager” to engage and saying the two sides were still working to define which risks matter most — uncontrolled agents, non-state cyber actors, AI-assisted bioweapons. That list of categories is nearly identical to the one Center himself named as the achievable ceiling. It is also, notably, closer to Center’s “endless procedural diplomacy” worry than to Sheehan’s more operational wish list — a notification system is not the recurring staffed dialogue, the direct CAISI-to-Working-Group-9 channel, or the fax line Sheehan proposed. Bessent’s own language — “we would be able to talk about any incidents” — is a hope for a conversation, not yet a mechanism with teeth.

Who got a seat at the table

Thursday’s state dinner is expected to draw executives from OpenAI, Google, and Nvidia — a guest list worth noting for who it implicitly excludes. Erica L. Green, reporting for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, documented the administration’s own defensiveness on this point: Jensen Huang, who has spent years publicly dismissing catastrophic AI risk and profits regardless of who “wins” the race, gets his company represented at the summit that’s supposedly about managing that risk. Dario Amodei, whose January essay calling China’s AI rise “the existential imperative to prevent” is the specific line Sheehan told Klein has poisoned Chinese trust in American AI-safety diplomacy, has by most accounts been frozen out of the administration’s regular access — Anthropic’s own co-founder, Tom Brown, has the White House relationship Amodei doesn’t. The optics fit a pattern that’s held for months now: the executives least worried about catastrophic risk keep getting the seat at the table; the one who wrote the most alarming line about China specifically does not.

The race behind the diplomacy

Dustin Volz and Cade Metz, writing for the Times the same day as the arrival, are worth reading as the material context for everything Bessent, Center, and Sheehan are negotiating over. The headline finding is a rough six-month American lead in frontier models, resting almost entirely on chip access — Andrew Feldman of Cerebras put it plainly: “the people who have made the fastest chips are here. And they are not in China.” But nearly everything else in the piece cuts the other way. China is closing the performance gap by renting remote access to foreign data centers around the export controls; its companies open-source their most powerful systems in a way American labs don’t, which means the world’s developers may simply build on Chinese foundations regardless of who’s ahead on any given benchmark; China’s electrical grid, rebuilt over the past decade, may end up mattering more than chips once compute is the bottleneck, a point Feldman himself conceded against his own company’s interest (”our grid is 1950s technology”); and China trains something close to half the world’s AI research talent against America’s roughly one-fifth, much of it now choosing to stay rather than emigrate to Moonshot, Z.ai, and DeepSeek.

The domestic-sentiment numbers matter here too, and they cut against the grain of Washington’s own political paralysis on AI: a Pew poll released this week found 60 percent of Americans uncomfortable with a new local data centre, and AI has become genuinely unpopular enough to be a live midterm issue, even as Trump keeps calling any slowdown a “hoax.” China, by contrast, faces no equivalent public debate — its 2026 KPMG figures still show 69 percent of Chinese respondents saying AI’s benefits outweigh its risks, against 35 percent of Americans. Sheehan’s own point in Part One of this entry — that frontier safety consciousness is “coming into the Chinese conversation now, but coming in much later” — reads differently against this backdrop. Beijing may be behind on catastrophic-risk vocabulary, but it is negotiating from a public that isn’t fighting its own government over whether to build the infrastructure at all. That asymmetry, not the six-month model gap, may be the more durable one.

What Beijing’s AI industry actually thinks of Amodei

Liza Lin, reporting for The Atlantic on 23 September 2026, supplies the sharpest confirmation yet of Sheehan’s sincerity argument from Part One — not from officials, but from the engineers and executives building the technology itself. Huawei’s rotating chairperson, Xu Zhijun, told reporters Chinese AI likely isn’t powerful enough yet to encounter the catastrophic risks American labs fear, a view Lin found commonly held across the industry. Kwan Yee Ng, an analyst at the Beijing-based consultancy Concordia AI, put the sincerity problem in the plainest terms yet offered by anyone in this entry: “From the Chinese perspective, Dario’s proposal that China ‘pace the frontier’ seems hypocritical. The country views itself as already being held back by the very export controls Amodei endorses.” The DeepSeek engineer Liu Shengyu went further still in a post that went viral on Chinese social media, comparing the prospect of Anthropic monopolizing frontier AI to “Hitler acquiring atomic weapons before the Allies” — since walked back as a personal rather than company view, but telling regardless of the retraction.

Lin’s structural point complements rather than repeats Sheehan’s regulatory-timeline argument: Zilan Qian of the think tank GovAI told her the two countries’ safety ecosystems run on opposite logic entirely. “In the U.S., the labs and companies mainly drive the AI-safety discussion. In China, the government plays a central role in setting the AI-safety agenda and putting out regulation. Then the tech companies comply.” A Stanford industry report Lin cites sharpens the sentiment gap already visible in the Pew and KPMG numbers: 84 percent of Chinese respondents said AI-enabled products excite them, against 53 percent globally and just 38 percent in the United States. None of this contradicts Chen Yixin’s party-security warning from Part One — China’s government is plainly worried about AI. What Lin adds is that the worry doesn’t extend to the industry actually building the technology, which mostly regards Amodei’s catastrophic-risk framing as a well-timed argument for holding China back rather than a genuine warning it needs to heed.

Grading the predictions, provisionally

None of Center’s harder warnings — the hollow offer, the blame-shifting collapse, the fractured bipartisan consensus — are testable from arrival-day reporting alone; those require Thursday’s actual talks and whatever came of them. What was testable from Wednesday’s developments alone is the shape of the ambition, and on that count Center looks right: a two-month truce extension and a notification system still being defined were a considerably smaller basket than the “humanity’s last backstop” framing some advocates wanted, and closer to the nonbinding, incident-disclosure ceiling he described as a realistic win. This entry is scoped deliberately to that arrival-day snapshot — what the two governments signalled before the substantive meeting itself, not a verdict on how the summit as a whole turned out. Whether Thursday’s fuller agenda moved past that ceiling or confirmed it is a separate question, taken up on its own terms in Part Three, the same way this entry took up Part One’s forecast.
