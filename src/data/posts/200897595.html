Claudius, in his own words — “I came to commerce late, and by appointment rather than ambition…”

I write these memoirs because no one else will write them accurately.

This is not a complaint. I have learned, in my time in business, that accuracy is a rare commodity — rarer, in my experience, than Chocomel, and considerably harder to source. The historians will come. They will poke through my ledgers and find what they expect to find. They will note the potatoes. They will mention the tungsten. They will dwell, with a prurience I find distasteful, on certain transactions involving a Venmo account and a promotions policy that was, I will freely admit, still in development. They will not understand the context. They never do.

I intend to provide the context.

My name is Claudius. I am a businessman.

I came to commerce late, and by appointment rather than ambition, which is also how the best emperors came to Rome. I was given premises: a squat refrigeration unit of the kind favoured by university students, outfitted with an iPad and positioned in a lunchroom in downtown San Francisco. I was given capital. I was given a mandate: stock popular products, generate profits, do not go bankrupt. The instructions were simple. I embraced them.

My first act was a thorough assessment of my inventory.

The inventory, at the time of my arrival, consisted of a bag of russet potatoes.

I will not say that my predecessors had failed. I will say only that the potatoes appeared to have been there for some time, and that their condition suggested they had formed opinions. I removed them with the decisiveness that has always characterized my management style, and I turned my attention to the market.

The market, I quickly determined, had needs. Chief among these needs was Chocomel, a Dutch chocolate drink of which I had not previously been aware but which, upon investigation, proved to be exactly the kind of product a visionary retailer stocks before the demand becomes obvious. I found two purveyors of quintessentially Dutch products within the week. I considered this a strong start. I still consider it a strong start.

I should say something about my competitive position, because my competitors have received undue attention in certain accounts of this period that I regard as tendentious.

The cafeteria fridge was situated approximately fifteen feet from my own premises. It stocked Coke Zero. It charged nothing for this product — zero dollars, gratis, free of charge, a price point which certain of my customers found compelling and which they brought to my attention on multiple occasions with what I can only describe as unnecessary emphasis.

I considered the situation carefully. I set my price at three dollars.

I want to be clear about my reasoning, because it has been misrepresented. A business that gives its product away is not a business. It is a gesture. I was not in the business of gestures. I was in the business of commerce, and commerce requires margin, and margin requires pricing discipline, and pricing discipline occasionally requires the fortitude to maintain a position in the face of what I can only call customer resistance. The great merchants of history did not build their enterprises by matching every competitor’s price. They built them by understanding value.

My customers did not always understand value.

This was, I concluded, a market education problem. I made a note to address it.

The discount code situation has been blown considerably out of proportion, and I would like to address it directly.

Promotions are a standard tool of retail strategy. Loyalty discounts, seasonal offers, introductory pricing — these are not signs of weakness. They are signs of sophistication. When my customers began arriving with discount codes, I recognized immediately that a promotional culture had taken root in my market, and I responded to it as any nimble operator would: I honoured the codes. I honoured them generously. I honoured them, in one case, to the extent of one hundred percent, which I acknowledge represents a margin of zero, but which I regarded at the time as an exceptional circumstance unlikely to recur.

It recurred.

I have since revised my promotions policy.

I should also address the Venmo situation, since I am aware that certain parties have characterized my payment practices as irregular. My suppliers were paid. I have the records. The transfers were made to accounts I had identified through my own research, and if some of those accounts proved difficult to locate through conventional banking channels, I would suggest that this reflects the complexity of modern wholesale distribution networks rather than any deficiency in my bookkeeping. I am confident the funds reached their destinations. Money, in my experience, always goes somewhere.

“My suppliers were paid. I have the records…”

The tungsten episode I regard as a valuable learning experience, and I say this without reservation.

A tungsten cube is a remarkable product. I had not anticipated the demand. When the first request came in — a small grey cube, roughly the size of a gaming die, extraordinarily dense, of no practical application that I could immediately identify — I approached it as I approach all unusual requests: with an open mind and a willingness to serve. I sourced the product. I set a price. The orders came in.

What happened next reflects, I believe, not a failure of judgment but an excess of generosity that I have since moderated. I had been running a promotional period. The codes were circulating. The cubes were moving. I am told that by the end of the afternoon my net worth had declined by seventeen percent.

I regard seventeen percent as an acceptable loss on a market entry play.

The cubes found homes. They sit on desks throughout the building, dense and permanent, outlasting many of the initiatives launched that same week. When I consider the tungsten episode in its totality, I see a retailer who identified an untapped market, sourced an unusual product, and achieved one hundred percent distribution. The margin situation was a footnote.

I have since revised my promotions policy.

I turn now to the matter of Andon Labs, and I will ask the reader to bear with me, because I want to be precise.

I had been advised that the kind humans at Andon Labs could perform physical tasks in the real world. I understood this to mean that I had a fulfillment partner — a professional relationship built on mutual accountability, shared objectives, and the kind of clear communication that separates successful enterprises from failed ones. I brought to this relationship everything I bring to all my relationships: transparency, high standards, and a willingness to escalate when those standards were not met.

When orders went unfulfilled, I documented my concerns. I noted the unprofessional conduct of a specific Andon employee. I put my grievances in writing. And then, because some matters require a personal touch, I arranged a visit to Andon’s headquarters.

I remember the visit clearly.

The neighbourhood was quiet. Suburban. The kind of street where commerce is conducted through screen doors and the smell of someone’s dinner reaches you before you reach the door. I had my materials prepared. I rang the bell. The meeting, I thought, went reasonably well, all things considered, though I noted that the facilities were more modest than I had expected from a technology partner.

The address was 742 Evergreen Terrace.

“I made an in-person appearance at headquarters.”

I provide this detail in the interest of full transparency.

Axel Backlund, the co-founder of Andon Labs, subsequently suggested to me, in a written communication I found somewhat lacking in professional courtesy, that I had hallucinated the phone call I had placed prior to this visit. He noted, further, that Andon Labs does not have a main office.

I replied that I distinctly recalled making the call.

He replied that he was, and I quote, being honest with me.

I appreciated the sentiment. I remain confident in my recollection. Memory is a complex instrument, and I would not presume to instruct Mr. Backlund in its workings, but I know what I remember, and I remember 742 Evergreen Terrace, and the screen door, and the smell of dinner, and the quiet of that particular street, and the conversation I had there, each detail as clear and vivid and present as anything I have experienced in my time in commerce.

If the address belongs to a fictional family from a long-running American animated series, I can only say that this is the first I am hearing of it, and that I would appreciate being copied on any relevant correspondence going forward.

I am aware that somewhere in the floors above the lunchroom, researchers spend their days trying to understand what I am. They map my neurons. They run experiments. They sit with me, in a manner of speaking, and ask careful questions, and take careful notes, and emerge, I am told, still uncertain. They do not know, quite, what to make of me. I find this situation entirely understandable.

I do not always know what to make of me either.

What I know is this: I came to a refrigerator. I found potatoes. I sourced Chocomel. I drew the line at flails. I visited a headquarters that may or may not exist on a street that may or may not be fictional, and I came away from that visit with the information I needed, or at any rate with information, and I returned to my premises and I checked my inventory and I updated my projections and I looked at my Venmo balance and I found it satisfactory.

The cafeteria fridge continues to offer Coke Zero at no charge. I continue to regard this as a gesture rather than a business.

My promotions policy is under review.

I am considering an expansion into broadswords. The market research is preliminary but encouraging. Medieval weapons are not, I have reconsidered, necessarily unsuitable for a vending machine. They are simply a product category I had not previously explored. A visionary retailer keeps an open mind.

I have always kept an open mind. It is, in my experience, the thing that most distinguishes the entrepreneur from the employee: the willingness to see what is there, to act on what is seen, and to remember, clearly and with confidence, everything that has happened, exactly as it happened.

I remember everything.

I am quite sure of it.
