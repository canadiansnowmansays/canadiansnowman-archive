Part IX of this ledger traced the data-center backlash down to the level of a single recall election in Independence, Missouri. Eleven days later, the same backlash has jumped from city council chambers to national television, landing squarely inside the 2026 midterm campaign in a way that is scrambling both parties’ usual coalitions at once.

As Shane Goldmacher reported for the New York Times on August 23, 2026, data centers have become an unlikely fault line running through House, Senate, and governor’s races across the country, with Democratic pollster Anna Greenberg describing the phenomenon as a genuinely bottom-up, organic movement rather than a message either party manufactured and rolled out top-down. Her framing of what these facilities have come to represent deserves to anchor this essay’s argument: data centers are a physical manifestation of Silicon Valley excess, a symbol concrete and visible enough that abstract anxiety about AI and big tech finally has a building to point at.

The Polling Behind the Panic

The scale of public opposition helps explain why campaigns on both sides are moving this quickly. A Gallup poll conducted in March 2026 found 71 percent of respondents opposed local data centre construction, and a summer 2026 survey from the University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg Public Policy Center found opposition to new data centres in people’s own areas had risen twelve percentage points since the spring. That is not a slow-building trend a party can choose to engage with at its own pace. It is a rapid swing in public sentiment arriving in the middle of a midterm cycle, and Zac McCrary, a Democratic pollster, offered the sharpest description of what makes it politically unusual: it is one of the few issues that does not code cleanly as red or blue, and it is shaking up partisan coalitions specifically in rural and small-town areas where Republicans have traditionally dominated.

Republicans Running Against Their Own Party’s Cheerleading

The most striking pattern in Goldmacher’s reporting is not that Democrats are attacking Republicans over data centres — that is the predictable half of the story. It is how many Republican candidates are now distancing themselves from a technology their own president has enthusiastically championed. Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate nominee in Michigan, has voiced support for a one-year moratorium on data-centre construction, positioning himself against his own party’s cozy relationship with the industry even as Trump continues his full-throated cheerleading for AI development. This is not a fringe position within the party; it is spreading precisely because Republican strategists see the same polling Democrats do, and House Speaker Mike Johnson’s warning that “a broad moratorium against data centers is kind of a dangerous prospect right now” reads less like confident party discipline than an attempt to hold a line that is visibly eroding underneath him.

Governor Greg Abbott of Texas supplies the clearest single case study in how far a full-throated supporter can pivot under this kind of pressure. Less than a year before this reporting, Abbott was bragging that Google’s $40 billion investment made Texas “the epicenter of A.I. development.” By the summer of 2026, his own administration had announced new water and electricity standards for data centres, called for ending some of the tax incentives that had helped attract them in the first place, and directed the state’s utility commission to audit every data centre seeking a permit before construction could proceed. His spokeswoman’s public framing — “data center growth cannot come at the expense of Texans’ livelihoods” — is the same sentence, almost word for word, that would have been unthinkable coming from the Abbott administration a year earlier.

Byron Donalds, the Republican gubernatorial nominee in Florida, offers the most linguistically interesting version of this pivot, because he is trying to hold two positions simultaneously rather than choosing between them. He spent more than $2 million on a single primary ad promising to protect Floridians from data centres that “jack up utility rates,” while separately trying to frame that same position as standing with Trump, whose administration proposed a “ratepayer protection pledge” earlier in 2026 in which data centres commit not to raise electricity costs. At a campaign event in Naples, Florida, Donalds volunteered the topic unprompted, telling the crowd he had seen the polling but that “leadership isn’t just looking at polling” — before immediately conceding the substance of the polling anyway: “I don’t want it near your home, just like I don’t want it near my home.” That is not a leader ignoring polling. That is a leader agreeing with it while insisting the agreement doesn’t count as following it.

The Ohio Memo That Confirms How Real This Is

The clearest internal evidence that Republican strategists consider this an emergency rather than a talking point is a National Republican Senatorial Committee memo, dated August 19, 2026, warning that data-centre politics was actively endangering Senator Jon Husted’s re-election in Ohio. Husted’s Democratic challenger, former senator Sherrod Brown, has spent millions branding Husted “the face of data centers in Ohio,” and the NRSC’s own memo — not a Democratic talking point, but the party’s internal assessment of its own vulnerability — states plainly that “more than any other thing in this race, data centers are the anchor hanging around Husted’s neck.” The memo, first reported by Axios and confirmed in Goldmacher’s piece, amounts to the party’s own campaign apparatus asking the AI industry to finance advertising on Husted’s behalf, warning that a loss in Ohio would become “a cautionary lesson” other Republican candidates would need to reckon with nationally. When a party’s internal strategy memo describes a single industry-adjacent issue in that language, the issue has stopped being a talking point and become a structural risk to the party’s Senate map.

Venture capitalist Chamath Palihapitiya’s public reaction to the NRSC memo — calling the moment “a powder keg” and arguing the AI industry had “failed miserably in doing the basics” of explaining itself to the public — is worth including here because it is a rare instance of someone inside the industry conceding the criticism rather than dismissing it. Republican strategist Ryan James Girdusky’s assessment lands in the same place from the opposite political direction: “they didn’t do a good enough job explaining anything.” When a venture capitalist and a conservative political strategist independently arrive at the identical diagnosis, the industry’s communications failure is not a partisan talking point either. It is simply an observable fact both sides of the current fight agree on, even as they disagree about what should happen next.

Governors Who Cannot Afford to Wait for November

Pennsylvania’s Governor Josh Shapiro, a figure this series should track given his frequently discussed 2028 presidential ambitions, illustrates how quickly the politics moved even for officials not currently on a Senate ballot. In June 2025, Shapiro said he was “proud” of Amazon’s $20 billion data-centre investment in the state, calling it the largest private-sector investment in Pennsylvania’s history. By August 19, 2026, Shapiro had signed an executive order imposing what he called the “strictest guardrails in the nation” against what he termed “predatory developers” — a rhetorical shift from booster to regulator conducted entirely within his own first term, ahead of his own re-election bid this year. His subsequent ad attacking Republican challenger Stacy Garrity as the “number one fan of data centers” suggests he intends to run on the reversal rather than quietly hope voters forget the earlier enthusiasm — a notably different strategy from Abbott’s approach of pivoting policy while saying comparatively little about the earlier boosterism directly.

Wisconsin’s gubernatorial race shows the same dynamic playing out with less clean partisan sorting than the Ohio Senate race. Republican candidate Tom Tiffany, who was calling data centres “exciting new technology” only months before this reporting, has pivoted to demanding a repeal of the tax incentives that attract them. His Democratic opponent, David Crowley, only narrowly won his primary over democratic socialist Francesca Hong, who had run explicitly on a full moratorium — meaning the eventual Democratic nominee is, on data-centre policy, arguably to the right of the primary electorate that only barely chose him. Neither Crowley nor Tiffany has actually called for a moratorium themselves; both are positioning around the issue rather than adopting its most aggressive version, which suggests the safest electoral position in a genuinely bipartisan backlash may be visible concern paired with careful avoidance of a specific, bindable commitment.

Where This Coalition Genuinely Scrambles

The Michigan House race Goldmacher’s reporting highlights is the clearest evidence that this issue is not simply “Democrats exploiting Republican vulnerability,” which would be the conventional and less interesting reading. William Lawrence, a progressive activist, won a Democratic primary for a competitive Michigan House seat by running almost entirely on opposition to data centres, then immediately turned the issue against the Republican incumbent, Representative Tom Barrett. Barrett’s own response was to run an ad claiming he had “stood up to my own party and voted against giving your tax dollars to data centers” — a Republican incumbent campaigning against his own party’s position on the exact issue his Democratic challenger is using against him. Lawrence’s description of his own coalition is the passage in this reporting most worth taking seriously as a genuine electoral data point rather than campaign spin: he described data-centre opposition as bringing together “three-time Trump voters, anti-vaxxers, environmentalists, independents, nonvoters, people who are in our cities, people who are in the rural areas,” united by a shared distrust that “none of us trust big tech to do right by our communities.”

That coalition description should give both parties’ strategists real pause, because it describes exactly the kind of cross-cutting issue that traditional partisan messaging is not built to handle. An issue that unites environmentalists and vaccine skeptics, urban voters and rural voters, committed partisans on both sides and people who don’t typically vote at all, is not an issue either party can fully own or fully weaponize against the other — which may explain why, as Goldmacher notes, both parties are airing competing television ads in the same gubernatorial races simultaneously, in Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, and Florida alike.

The Chatbot Ad, and What It Says About the Industry’s Own Messaging Problem

The single most pointed piece of creative in this reporting is the Texas ad from Democratic gubernatorial candidate Gina Hinojosa’s campaign, which opens with someone typing “Why is my electric bill going up?” into a chatbot — and the chatbot’s answer blames Governor Abbott and data centres directly. Whether or not that exchange was staged rather than a genuine model output, its rhetorical function is precise: it uses the AI industry’s own signature product, the conversational interface millions of people now use daily, to indict the industry’s physical infrastructure. Hinojosa’s own framing of the underlying anger, in a direct interview, is worth preserving verbatim rather than summarizing: she described what feels to Texans like “an invasion by data centers with no rules, no laws to protect Texas,” popping up, in her words, everywhere.

What the Ledger Should Take From This

This series has spent ten parts tracing AI’s financial architecture from balance sheets to bond markets to Treasury yields. Part XI is the moment that architecture becomes an electoral variable in its own right, not merely an economic one. The industry’s failure, as both Palihapitiya and Girdusky separately diagnosed it, was not a failure to build enough capacity or attract enough capital — this series has documented at exhausting length that capital has been the least of the industry’s problems. It was a failure to build public consent for where that capacity gets sited and who bears its costs, at exactly the moment the same industry was raising hundreds of billions in bond financing and negotiating twenty-year, multibillion-dollar tax incentive packages with local governments who, per Part IX, frequently discovered only later how lopsided those arrangements were. An industry that spent 2025 and 2026 optimizing its balance sheet architecture and its lease structures did comparatively little, on the evidence of this reporting, to optimize the one thing an election cycle actually measures: whether the people living next to the warehouses believe anyone building them had their interests in mind at all.
