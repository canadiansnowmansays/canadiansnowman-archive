The structure was described as temporary. It was also described, by its promoter, as so impressive you might never want to take it down. The green had hosted the Canada Day picnic since 1955.

Confederation Park has a green.

It is not a large green. It is a flat rectangle of grass between the bandshell and the Elizabethan Gardens, bordered on one side by the path that runs down to the municipal dock and on the other by a row of maples planted in 1955 by the Elsinore Horticultural Society, which kept minutes of the planting that Gerald has on file. The green has hosted, in the seventy years since the maples went in, the Canada Day picnic, the Festival opening-weekend reception, the Horticultural Society plant sale, the Legion’s Remembrance Day gathering, and an uncountable number of afternoons on which nothing was scheduled and people simply used it, which is the highest function a green can perform and the one least likely to appear in a committee report.

The green has never had a corporate logo on it.

This was not a policy. It was simply a fact, in the way that the absence of certain things is a fact before anyone thinks to make it a policy, and remains a fact only until someone proposes to change it, at which point it becomes a debate, and the debate reveals that the fact had been a kind of agreement all along, held without being spoken, which is the most fragile kind of agreement there is.

Bradley Fenn proposed to change it in July.

He had slides.

The proposal was for a ticketed combat-sports exhibition, to be held on the green over a single weekend, in partnership with a promotional company from Mississauga that specialised in what the slides described as “premium live spectacle activations.” The event would feature, the slides explained, a regulation competition cage erected on the green, tiered seating for two thousand spectators, a concert stage, and corporate sponsorship signage at a scale Bradley described as “tasteful and revenue-positive.”

The event would be held, the slides noted, over the Canada Day weekend — the long weekend at the start of July when Elsinore has held its Canada Day picnic on the Confederation Park green every year since 1955, the picnic the maples were planted to shade, the one with the Legion’s barbecue and the Horticultural Society’s lemonade table and the children’s races that Gerald has timed and recorded since 2004 because someone should keep the records and no one else volunteered.

Bradley’s proposal did not cancel the picnic, exactly. It absorbed it. The picnic would become, in the language of the slides, “a heritage programming component” of the larger ticketed event — a small free zone at the edge of the green, beside the two thousand seats and the competition cage and the sponsorship signage, where the families who had come for the picnic for seventy years could continue to come, in a reduced area, while the main event proceeded beside them.

Bradley had also checked the Canada Day weekend against another date, which the slides did not mention but which Norman Stalker, covering the meeting from the back row, established within an hour through a records search he conducts with the efficiency he brings to things that smell wrong. The other date was Reg Plimpton’s sixtieth birthday, which fell on the same weekend. Reg had been planning to mark it quietly. Bradley’s proposal would mark it not quietly at all, and would do so under cover of Canada Day, so that the celebration of the nation and the celebration of the Deputy Reeve and the promotion of a Mississauga events company would all occur simultaneously on the same green, indistinguishable in the aerial footage, which is precisely the condition under which a personal celebration prefers to occur: wrapped in a national one, where no one can say for certain which birthday the cake is for.

The combat cage required a foundation.

This was the detail that Gerald flagged, in his measured way, at the committee meeting. He noted that a regulation competition cage with tiered seating for two thousand spectators was a significant structure, that significant structures placed on grass required ground preparation, and that ground preparation on the Confederation Park green would affect the green, which had been grass since 1955 and which the Horticultural Society had maintained, at its own expense, for seventy years.

Bradley said the green would be restored after the event.

Gerald asked how long restoration would take.

Bradley said the promotional company had assured him the restoration was straightforward.

Gerald asked whether the promotional company had restored a seventy-year-old maintained green before.

Bradley said the company had extensive experience in venue restoration.

Gerald noted this. He wrote down the word restoration. He underlined it once, which Gerald does when he intends to check a claim against reality at a later date, and which he did, later, when the reality became available.

The promotional company’s representative described the structure, at a subsequent meeting, as potentially permanent.

He did not mean it would stay. He meant it as praise — the structure was so impressive, so well-engineered, so much more substantial than the temporary arrangements one might expect, that one could almost imagine leaving it up. He said this in the manner of a man who believes that the highest compliment you can pay a temporary thing is to suggest it deserves to be permanent.

He said: “Honestly, you put something like this up, you might never want to take it down.”

There was a silence in the committee room.

Constance Hay, who attended the meeting because she attended meetings that concerned the green, looked at the representative for a moment.

She said: “The green has been there for seventy years. The structure has been described, this evening, as worth keeping. I would ask the committee to consider which of these two things actually belongs to Elsinore, and which is being sold to it.”

She did not say anything further. She had identified the question. The question did its work without her assistance.

The event went ahead.

The committee approved it by a narrow margin, with Constance voting against, Gerald abstaining in the particular way that Gerald abstains — which is to say recording his abstention along with a note for the minutes that the restoration provisions had not been independently verified — and Reg voting in favour, his arm extending toward Bradley with the warmth of a man who had decided that two thousand ticketed spectators and a concert were, on balance, the kind of energy Elsinore could use, and who had not mentioned, because no one had raised it, that the weekend was also his birthday.

The cage went up. The seating went up. The sponsorship signage went up — not, in the end, tasteful, but at the scale that revenue-positive signage is always actually deployed at once the revenue becomes the point. The concert was loud. The Mississauga promotional company filmed the event for its own promotional purposes, and the footage opened with aerial shots of Elsinore — the Avon, the Festival Theatre, the Elizabethan Gardens — over which were superimposed the logos of the event’s sponsors, so that the town itself became the backdrop to the advertising, which is a specific thing to do to a town and which the town noticed.

The swans relocated for the weekend. The Swan Warden documented this. Gertrude, who runs the east bank, moved her operation upstream and conducted it from there with visible irritation, returning to the green’s edge on the Monday to find it changed.

The green was changed.

The swans returned on the Monday to find the green changed. Gertrude inspected the damage. She was not impressed. The Horticultural Society would spend two years restoring what the event had taken one weekend to undo.

The restoration was not straightforward.

The cage’s foundation had compacted the soil. The seating’s footings had left a grid of bare rectangles. The concert crowd had worn the grass to dirt across the central third of the green, and the dirt, exposed to a July rain three days after the event, had become mud, and the mud had been tracked across the path to the dock, and the maples planted in 1955 had a tier of seating footings driven in among their roots that the Horticultural Society’s chair examined on the Monday with an expression that Gerald, who was present, declined to describe in his notes beyond the word concerned.

The promotional company sent a crew. The crew reseeded the worn third. The reseeding did not take, because reseeding in late July in southwestern Ontario does not take, which the Horticultural Society could have told anyone who asked, and which no one had asked.

The green, by August, was a rectangle of struggling new grass, bare patches, and the faint permanent grid of where the footings had been. The Horticultural Society undertook the real restoration itself, at its own expense, over the following two years, because that is who actually restores greens, which is to say the people who maintained them before anyone proposed putting a cage on them.

Bradley described the event, in his report to the committee, as “a successful activation that raised Elsinore’s profile.” He noted the ticket revenue. He did not note the restoration cost, which had not appeared in the revenue calculation because it had been absorbed by the Horticultural Society, which is to say by the volunteers, which is to say by the people.

Gerald checked the word restoration against reality. Reality did not match the claim. He filed a note to that effect under Confederation Park — Green — Combat Event — Restoration — Not As Represented.

He had underlined the word in July. He had been right to.

A Note for the Record

The green is a particular kind of thing. It is not anyone’s property in the sense that generates a deed. It is everyone’s property in the sense that generates a picnic. It exists because a community decided, at some point, that a flat rectangle of maintained grass between a bandshell and a garden was worth having for its own sake, available to everyone, scheduled for nothing in particular, capable of holding a Canada Day picnic or a Remembrance gathering or an empty Tuesday afternoon with equal grace.

To the south, the most famous green in the country — the South Lawn of the White House — was recently fitted with an eighty-foot steel structure for a combat-sports event, held to mark the nation’s two hundred and fiftieth anniversary, on a date that also happened to be the president’s eightieth birthday, a coincidence that both the president and the event’s promoter insisted was a coincidence. The structure was compared, by the president, to the Eiffel Tower. He suggested it might never come down. The event was sponsored; advertisements for the president’s own branded products played during the broadcast. The lawn, afterward, was ruined — compacted, worn, requiring resodding, the historically pristine grass reduced to mats and bare dirt in aerial photographs.

The two anniversaries sit three days apart on the calendar. Canada marks its founding on the first of July, quietly, with picnics and small-town parades and the Legion barbecue and the children’s races that someone has volunteered to time. The republic to the south marks its founding on the fourth, and this year marked it with a cage, a concert, a structure compared to the Eiffel Tower, and a man’s birthday wrapped inside the nation’s so thoroughly that the question of which was being celebrated answered itself. Both are birthdays. They are not, this year, the same kind of birthday. One is a community gathering on a green that belongs to everyone. The other is a spectacle on a lawn that used to.

The lawn belonged to the public. The structure belonged to the spectacle. The anniversary belonged to the nation, and also, on the same weekend, to one man, and the question of which of these the event actually celebrated was answered by the signage, the sponsorship, and the date.

In Elsinore, the green is being restored by the Horticultural Society, at its own expense, over two years. The volunteers are doing it because they are the ones who do it. The maples planted in 1955 will survive, the Society believes, though the footing damage to the roots will not be fully known for several seasons.

The swans have returned to the green’s edge.

Gertrude inspected the restoration. She was not impressed. She has resumed her operation from the east bank regardless, because the green is hers in the way it is everyone’s, and because swans do not require a structure to be impressive in order to decline to be impressed by it.

The green has been there for seventy years.

It will be there when the grid of footings has finally grown over, which the Horticultural Society estimates will take until approximately 2031.

They are keeping the record. They always have.
