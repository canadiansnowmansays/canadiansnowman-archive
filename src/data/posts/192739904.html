Often misidentified as the U.S. Embassy roof, this photo captured the chaotic final hours of American withdrawal as North Vietnamese forces closed in.

On April 30, 1975, a North Vietnamese tank crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon. The last American helicopters had lifted off from the roof of the US Embassy the previous day, carrying the final evacuees — diplomats, CIA officers, South Vietnamese employees who had worked for the Americans and now faced uncertain fates — in images that were broadcast around the world and have never been forgotten.

The fall of Saigon was not just a military defeat. It was the moment the American century first looked at itself in the mirror and did not recognize what it saw.

North Vietnamese tank crashing through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon, April 30, 1975 — the symbolic end of the Vietnam War and the fall of South Vietnam.

The war in Vietnam cost 58,220 American lives, somewhere between one and three million Vietnamese lives — the count varies depending on whether you include civilians, North Vietnamese soldiers, and the casualties of the secret wars in Laos and Cambodia — and approximately $843 billion in 2023 dollars. It produced no strategic gain. It ended with the unification of Vietnam under communist rule — exactly the outcome it had been fought to prevent. And it left wounds in American society, American institutions, and American credibility that shaped everything that came after, including the wars of the twenty-first century and the political fractures that produced Donald Trump.

To understand how the reluctant empire built in the 1940s became the rogue superpower of 2026, you must understand Vietnam. Not just as a military failure but as a systemic failure — a failure of the decision-making architecture, the credibility frameworks, and the domestic political consensus that had sustained American leadership since 1945.

Vietnam was not the beginning of the end. But it was the first crack in the foundation.

How It Started: The Logic That Made Sense

Vietnam did not begin as a mistake. It began as the application of a principle that had worked — the principle, demonstrated in Korea, that the United States would use military force to resist communist aggression and uphold the post-war collective security order.

The logic was internally consistent. If the United States had demonstrated in Korea that it would fight to resist aggression, then failing to resist aggression in Vietnam would undermine that demonstration. Allies who had aligned with the Western order on the basis of American credibility would have reason to doubt that credibility. Adversaries who had been deterred by American willingness to fight would have reason to test it further.

This was the domino theory — the argument that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would follow, and the entire architecture of the post-war order would unravel. It was the same reasoning Truman had applied to Korea. It was not obviously wrong. In the context of the early Cold War, when Soviet power was expanding and the post-war order was fragile and new, the instinct to hold every piece of ground was understandable.

The problem was not the logic. The problem was the country.

Korea was a peninsula, with clear geography, a definable front line, and an opponent whose external sponsor — the Soviet Union — had reason to avoid direct confrontation with the United States. South Korea was a functioning state, however imperfect, with a government capable of organizing its own defense with American support.

Vietnam was a different kind of war in a different kind of country. It was a civil war as much as an external aggression — a conflict with deep roots in Vietnamese nationalism, anti-colonial history, and the legitimate grievances of a population that had spent decades under French imperial rule. The government the United States was defending — the regime in Saigon — was corrupt, unstable, and incapable of generating the popular legitimacy that effective governance requires. The enemy was not a conventional army that could be defeated in set-piece battles but a guerrilla movement embedded in the civilian population, supplied through a supply network — the Ho Chi Minh Trail — that ran through the jungle and could not be interdicted from the air.

None of this was secret. The intelligence was clear, the analysis was available, and the warnings were given. Senator Mike Mansfield told President Kennedy in 1962 that American involvement in Vietnam risked becoming “an anti-communist crusade” that would “drain away American resources and manpower.” Undersecretary of State George Ball told President Johnson in 1965 that the war was unwinnable and would cost the United States 500,000 troops. Ball’s prediction was accurate to within 50,000.

The warnings were ignored. Not because the decision-makers were stupid — Kennedy, Johnson, McNamara, Bundy, Rusk were among the most intellectually accomplished people ever to hold high office in Washington. They were ignored because of something more insidious than stupidity: the trap of credibility.

The Credibility Trap

The credibility trap is one of the most dangerous mechanisms in the management of great power. It works like this.

A nation makes a commitment — to defend an ally, to uphold a principle, to resist an adversary. The commitment is made in good faith and for genuine strategic reasons. But the commitment is harder to fulfill than anticipated. The costs are higher, the obstacles are greater, and the chance of success is lower than the original calculations suggested.

At this point, the rational choice might be to reassess the commitment and, if the costs outweigh the benefits, to find an exit. But the logic of credibility intervenes. If the commitment is abandoned, the nation’s other commitments will be doubted. Allies will wonder whether American guarantees are reliable. Adversaries will wonder whether American threats are credible. The entire architecture of deterrence and alliance — built on the belief that American commitments mean something — will be undermined.

So the commitment is doubled down. More troops are sent. More bombs are dropped. More money is spent. Not because the strategic situation has improved but because the price of failure has increased. Every escalation makes the next potential failure more catastrophic to American credibility, which justifies the next escalation.

Robert McNamara in later years. In his 1995 memoir In Retrospect, the former Secretary of Defense admitted: “We were wrong, terribly wrong.” He had recognized by 1966 that the war was unwinnable but continued due to the logic of credibility.

This is the logic Robert McNamara, in his extraordinary memoir “In Retrospect,” identified as the mechanism of American failure in Vietnam. He had access to the intelligence. He understood, by 1966 at the latest, that the war was not winnable on American terms. And he stayed. He stayed because the logic of credibility — the fear of what abandonment would do to American standing in the world — was more powerful than the evidence of failure in front of him.

“We were wrong, terribly wrong,” McNamara wrote, three decades after the war ended. It is one of the most belated admissions in the history of American statecraft.

The credibility trap has a further dimension that makes it particularly dangerous: it reverses the relationship between means and ends. In rational strategic thinking, ends determine means — you define your objective and then allocate the resources necessary to achieve it. In the credibility trap, means determine ends — the accumulation of resources already committed to an objective creates pressure to redefine the objective to justify the expenditure. The war in Vietnam was not fought to achieve a strategic goal. By 1967 it was being fought to justify the 500,000 troops already there.

The order’s architects understood this danger, at least in principle. The post-war framework they built was designed to distribute American power through multilateral institutions precisely to prevent any single commitment from becoming so large that abandoning it would be unthinkable. The UN authorization for Korea, the NATO framework for European defense, the entire multilateral scaffolding of the post-war settlement — all of these were mechanisms for sharing the burden of commitments so that no single failure would be catastrophic.

Vietnam was fought outside this architecture. There was no UN authorization. There was no genuine multilateral coalition — the “many flags” program that brought token contributions from Australia, South Korea, and a few other countries was a political exercise rather than a genuine alliance effort. The war was America’s alone, which meant its failure was America’s alone, which meant its cost to American credibility was unmediated by any institutional framework.

Lester B. Pearson (left) with Lyndon B. Johnson. In 1965, Pearson publicly called for a pause in U.S. bombing — prompting Johnson to physically grab him by the lapels in anger during a private meeting. The incident highlighted both Canadian independence and the limits of allied dissent.

Canada had a particular window onto this failure that Americans lacked. Canadian diplomats served on the International Control Commission — the body established under the 1954 Geneva Accords to monitor the ceasefire between North and South Vietnam — alongside representatives from India and Poland. For years, Canadian officials reported directly to Ottawa from inside Vietnam on conditions that bore no resemblance to the optimistic assessments coming from Washington. In April 1965, Prime Minister Lester Pearson used a speech at Temple University in Philadelphia — on American soil, to an American audience — to call for a pause in the US bombing campaign. President Johnson, furious, summoned Pearson to his Texas ranch and, in a scene witnesses described as shocking in its aggression, physically grabbed the Prime Minister by the lapels and berated him for criticizing American policy on American soil. Pearson held his position. The bombing continued. The episode illustrates both the Canadian vantage — close enough to see clearly, independent enough to say so — and the limits of that vantage when the superpower next door has made up its mind.

The Home Front: The Domestic Fracture

The credibility trap explains the strategic failure. But Vietnam’s most lasting damage was not strategic. It was domestic.

The war divided American society in ways that had no precedent in the post-war period and that have never been fully healed. The division was not simply between supporters and opponents of the war — although that division was real and bitter. It was a division about the nature of American power itself: about whether the United States had the right to use military force in the service of strategic abstractions, about who bore the costs of that force, and about whether the institutions of American democracy were capable of holding power accountable.

The draft was the mechanism that made Vietnam’s domestic damage uniquely severe. Unlike Korea, which had also used conscription but for a shorter and more sharply defined conflict, Vietnam required years of draft calls that reached deep into American society — or rather, that reached into American society selectively, in ways that exposed the class dimensions of military service with uncomfortable clarity.

The Vietnam Veterans Memorial in Washington, D.C. — Maya Lin’s black granite wall inscribed with 58,220 names. Dedicated in 1982, it stands as a quiet, unflinching mirror to the human cost of the war and the fractures it left in American society

College deferments, National Guard placements, medical exemptions — the mechanisms for avoiding Vietnam were available to those with money, connections, and education. The burden of actual combat service fell disproportionately on working-class men, on men from rural communities, on Black Americans who faced discrimination in the assignment of both service and deferment. Muhammad Ali’s refusal to be inducted — “I ain’t got no quarrel with them Viet Cong” — was the most famous individual expression of a broader reality: that the costs of American credibility were not distributed equally.

This inequality had a radicalizing effect on American politics that persisted long after the war ended. The generation that came of age during Vietnam — the baby boomers, born between 1946 and 1964 — was permanently shaped by the experience of watching their government lie to them, systematically and at the highest levels, about the progress and purpose of the war.

The Pentagon Papers, published by the New York Times in 1971, documented what many had suspected: that successive administrations had misled Congress and the public about the war’s progress, its costs, and its prospects. The papers revealed that the Johnson administration had made the decision to escalate massively in Vietnam while publicly claiming to be pursuing a limited commitment. They revealed that military commanders had consistently provided optimistic assessments to their civilian superiors that bore no relationship to the operational reality on the ground.

The credibility gap — the term journalists used to describe the distance between official statements and observable reality — became one of the defining features of American political culture. A generation learned that their government lied to them about matters of life and death, and that lesson proved more durable than any specific policy dispute.

Donald Trump did not invent anti-establishment politics. He harvested a crop that Vietnam planted fifty years earlier.

The Military’s Reckoning

Vietnam’s damage to the American military was as profound as its damage to American society, and in some ways more consequential.

The professional military that emerged from Vietnam was an institution in crisis. Morale had collapsed. Drug use was rampant. Fragging — the murder of unpopular officers by their own troops — was a documented phenomenon. The officer corps had been damaged by a system that rewarded political reliability over military competence, that rotated commanders through Vietnam on one-year tours optimized for career advancement rather than operational continuity, and that evaluated success through body counts — a metric that bore no relationship to strategic progress and created powerful incentives for falsification.

The military’s response to Vietnam was, in many respects, admirable. The Goldwater-Nichols Act of 1986 reformed the command structure. The volunteer army replaced the draft, creating a professional force with higher morale and greater unit cohesion. The Powell Doctrine — named for General Colin Powell, who had served in Vietnam and drawn his own lessons — articulated a framework for the use of military force that insisted on clear objectives, overwhelming force, a defined exit strategy, and genuine public support before committing to military action.

General Colin Powell at a briefing during Operation Desert Storm, 1991. The Gulf War exemplified the Powell Doctrine — clear objectives, overwhelming force, defined exit strategy, and public support — the military’s direct institutional response to the failures of Vietnam.

The Powell Doctrine was, in essence, the military’s answer to the credibility trap: a set of institutional constraints designed to prevent the kind of open-ended commitment that Vietnam had become. It was also, implicitly, a critique of the civilian leadership that had sent the military into Vietnam without those constraints.

The doctrine worked. The Gulf War of 1991 — fought under Powell’s framework, with clear objectives, overwhelming force, a genuine multinational coalition, and an exit when the objectives were achieved — was everything Vietnam was not. It was quick, decisive, and strategically coherent. It restored American credibility in a way that Vietnam had damaged.

But it left an unresolved tension at the heart of American strategic culture. The Powell Doctrine was conservative — it said that military force should be used only when you could win quickly and get out. It was not designed for the complex, ambiguous conflicts that would define the post-Cold War world. And the civilian leaders who followed Powell — particularly in the administration that took office in January 2001 — would eventually override its constraints with consequences that this series will examine in Part VII.

The Strategic Legacy: What Vietnam Changed

Vietnam changed four things about American power that matter for understanding everything that came after.

The first was the relationship between the public and military force. Before Vietnam, Americans broadly trusted their government’s judgment about when and why to use military power. The experience of the Second World War — a genuinely popular conflict against genuinely monstrous enemies — had created a presumption of legitimacy that extended to subsequent military commitments. After Vietnam, that presumption was gone. Military force required justification in ways it had not before, and the justifications were subject to skepticism that previous generations had not applied.

This was, in some respects, healthy. Democratic accountability for military force is a genuine value, and the Vietnam generation’s insistence on it was a correction to an excess of deference. But it also created a political landscape in which building and sustaining public support for military commitments became genuinely difficult — a difficulty that would have consequences in conflicts where sustained commitment was essential to success.

The second was the relationship between American power and international legitimacy. Vietnam was fought without UN authorization, without a genuine multilateral coalition, and in violation of the norms of the post-war order that the United States had done so much to build. The hypocrisy was not lost on the rest of the world. The country that had insisted on collective security and international law in Korea was, a decade later, conducting a war of choice in Southeast Asia that violated both.

The damage to American moral authority was real and lasting. The United States could no longer claim unambiguous leadership of a rules-based international order while simultaneously conducting a war that violated those rules. The gap between American rhetoric and American practice — always present, but previously manageable — became a permanent feature of the international landscape.

The third was the relationship between American power and domestic consensus. The Cold War had been sustained by a bipartisan consensus — the “vital center,” as the historian Arthur Schlesinger called it — that united Democrats and Republicans around a shared understanding of American global responsibility. Vietnam shattered that consensus. The Democratic Party moved left, incorporating the anti-war movement’s critique of American imperialism. The Republican Party moved right, incorporating the military’s resentment of civilian interference and the working class’s anger at the cultural elites who had avoided service.

The bipartisan foreign policy consensus was never fully restored. It persisted in attenuated form through the Reagan years and the first post-Cold War decade, but the fractures were always there — and they widened with each subsequent crisis, until the Iran war of 2026 found a Republican secretary of defense unable to complete a sentence about his own political base’s discomfort with American military intervention.

The fourth legacy was the most personal and in some ways the most politically potent: the figure of the Vietnam veteran.

The men who served in Vietnam — and it was almost entirely men — came home to a country that did not know what to do with them. The welcome-home parades of the Second World War were not repeated. Some veterans reported being called baby killers. Many came home with physical and psychological wounds that the Veterans Administration was not equipped to treat. Post-traumatic stress disorder was not yet a recognized diagnosis; the men who experienced it were told to get over it.

The treatment of Vietnam veterans became, over the following decades, one of the central grievances of American working-class conservatism — the sense that the men who had done their duty had been abandoned by the same elites who had avoided service and then condemned those who served. This grievance was real, and it was legitimate. It was also weaponized — by politicians who had not served and had no intention of serving but who understood that veteran symbolism was politically powerful.

Donald Trump received five deferments from Vietnam, including one for bone spurs. He spent the war building real estate in New York. He has claimed, repeatedly and falsely, that he “always” supported the Vietnam veterans. He called a prisoner of war a loser for being captured. And he was elected, twice, on platforms that incorporated the deepest grievances of the Vietnam generation.

The crack that Vietnam opened in the foundation of American democracy ran deep enough, it turned out, to eventually swallow the house.

What Korea Built and Vietnam Destroyed

Part III of this series described Korea as the first test of the post-war collective security architecture — the demonstration that the principle of collective security had teeth, that the United States would fight for the order even when direct national interests were not at stake.

Vietnam was the counter-demonstration. It showed that the United States would also fight outside the order, without authorization, for objectives that were never clearly defined, at costs that were never honestly presented, through methods that violated the humanitarian norms the order was meant to embody.

The contrast between the two wars is the contrast between American power at its most principled and American power at its most self-defeating. Korea was imperfect — costly, incomplete, and ultimately inconclusive — but it was fought for a coherent principle, within a legitimate framework, with honest accounting of its costs and limitations. Vietnam was fought for a principle — resisting communist expansion — that was legitimate in the abstract but applied without the institutional discipline, the multilateral legitimacy, or the honest assessment of feasibility that coherent strategy requires.

The lesson that should have been drawn from Vietnam was the lesson of institutional constraint: that American power was most effective, and most durable, when it operated within the frameworks of multilateral legitimacy that the post-war order had created. When it operated outside those frameworks — in pursuit of unilateral objectives, without international authorization, without honest public accounting — it consumed its own foundations.

That lesson was partially absorbed. The Powell Doctrine was an institutional expression of it. The Gulf War coalition of 1991 was a practical demonstration of it. But it was never fully absorbed, never deeply enough embedded in American strategic culture to survive the temptations of the unipolar moment that the Cold War’s end would create.

In 2003, a different administration, with different advisers, facing a different enemy, would walk straight into the same trap. We will come to that story in Part VII.

For now, it is enough to note what Vietnam established as a permanent feature of American power: the possibility of catastrophic self-inflicted failure. The post-war order had been built on the assumption that American power, properly channeled through multilateral institutions, was broadly benign. Vietnam demonstrated that American power, improperly channeled, was capable of producing outcomes that damaged American interests, American credibility, and American society more severely than any external adversary had managed to do.

The reluctant empire had discovered that it was capable of defeating itself. That discovery would prove to be the most consequential strategic lesson of the twentieth century — and the one most consistently ignored by the leaders who followed.

The Last Helicopter

The image of the last American helicopter leaving the roof of the US Embassy in Saigon on April 29, 1975 — photographer Hubert van Es captured it for UPI, and it became one of the defining images of the twentieth century — has been interpreted in many ways over the intervening half-century.

Some see it as a symbol of American defeat — the most powerful nation in the world, humiliated by a small Asian country that simply refused to stop fighting.

Some see it as a symbol of American wisdom — the recognition, finally, that a war that could not be won should be ended, and that no strategic objective was worth an indefinite commitment of American lives and resources.

Some see it as a symbol of American betrayal — the abandonment of the South Vietnamese who had relied on American commitments and were now left to face the consequences of American withdrawal.

All three interpretations contain truth. And all three point toward the same underlying reality: that American power, for all its magnitude, was not unlimited, and that the illusion of its limitlessness — the belief that sufficient American will and resources could overcome any obstacle — was itself one of the most dangerous products of the post-war period.

The men and women who built the post-war framework had never believed in unlimited American power. They had constructed international institutions precisely because they understood that American power had limits, and that those limits were best managed by embedding American commitments in structures that distributed their costs and constrained their excesses. Keenleyside had watched from the Canadian side of the table as that architecture was assembled. He had watched from the sidelines — as all Canadians did — as Vietnam dismantled it piece by piece.

The men who prosecuted the Vietnam War — the best and brightest, as David Halberstam ironically called them — believed otherwise. They believed that American power, properly applied, could achieve any objective. They were wrong. And the price of their wrongness was paid, in the most direct sense, by 58,220 Americans whose names are inscribed on a black granite wall in Washington, and by the millions of Vietnamese, Cambodian, and Laotian civilians whose names are inscribed nowhere.

The crack Vietnam opened in the foundation of American power was not fatal. The house did not fall immediately. But it never quite stood straight again.

And fifty years later, in the spring of 2026, you can trace a direct line from the last helicopter over Saigon to the B-52s over Tehran — from the first great failure of American power to its most recent one. The line runs through the decisions that were made and the lessons that were ignored, through the domestic fractures that were opened and never healed, through the credibility traps that were sprung and escaped and sprung again.

The reluctant empire, it turns out, was capable of learning. It just was not always capable of remembering what it had learned.
