Part X of this series left Starship in a strange but oddly promising position: Flight 13 had finally reached orbit and splashed down successfully on July 24, 2026, after two further scrubs, even though its booster was lost during the landing burn when only ten of thirteen engines relit. I noted at the time that the summer’s margins had been thin — a rocket surviving on ten of thirteen engines is not a rocket with room to spare — and that Flight 14 would need to attempt something considerably harder: an actual orbital catch of the ship by the Mechazilla tower arms. That instalment ended on a note of cautious, thin-margin optimism, paired with a look at the Trump administration’s “Golden Age of Space Transportation” policy, which envisions more than a thousand launches and re-entries a year on American soil by 2030.

I did not expect the next major development in this series to be a spaceport. But on August 25, 2026, SpaceX and Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry announced exactly that: Starbase Louisiana, a second Starship launch site on the Gulf Coast, described by the company as a $100 billion investment and, if built as planned, the largest launch facility on Earth. It is worth walking through this announcement with the same scepticism this series has applied to every previous Starship milestone, because the numbers involved are large enough that scepticism is not optional. It is also worth acknowledging, up front, that at least one piece of the engineering logic behind this announcement is genuinely sound, which is more than can be said for a great many megaproject press releases.

Where, and how big

The site sits in Vermilion Parish, on Louisiana’s Gulf Coast, on land SpaceX describes as the product of a seven-year search for a location capable of supporting Starship launches at genuinely industrial cadence — the company’s stated ambition is more than thirty flights a day from this one site once fully built.

I want to flag something before going further, because it is the kind of detail a diligent reader should catch rather than one I want to quietly smooth over: circulating figures for the site’s acreage disagree substantially, with one account putting it at roughly 14,000 acres and another, matching the figure in Louisiana’s own economic development materials, at roughly 125,000 acres, described elsewhere as some 195 square miles. That is nearly a ninefold discrepancy, and it is not a rounding error — it is the difference between a large industrial site and a parcel of land approaching the physical footprint of a mid-sized American city. I have not been able to resolve which figure is correct from public sources, and I would rather print that uncertainty plainly than pick the more dramatic number because it makes for a better paragraph. Whichever figure is accurate, the physical description is consistent across sources: ten launch pads arranged in five paired sites roughly 1.5 kilometres apart, a production complex spanning more than two kilometres, and a “Star Factory” building with floor space exceeding 7.6 million square feet — genuinely larger, by a factor of roughly four, than the existing Starbase Texas assembly building.

The propellant argument, which actually holds up

The most substantively interesting piece of this announcement, and the part I am most willing to defend against the charge of simply repeating a press release, is the propellant logistics problem, because someone plainly did the arithmetic before committing to a site. At a launch cadence approaching a thousand flights a year, supplying propellant by truck would require, on one circulating estimate, something in the range of a thousand truckloads a day — a tanker arriving roughly every eighty-six seconds, continuously, forever. That is not a supply chain; it is a logistical impossibility dressed up as a spreadsheet line item. SpaceX’s answer is to manufacture propellant on site, with oxygen production and pipelines built directly adjacent to the natural gas infrastructure that has served Gulf Coast oil and gas operations since the 1950s — infrastructure, and workforce, that Vermilion Parish already has in abundance from seven decades of offshore energy work. Musk’s own response to a public breakdown of this calculation was a terse “mostly correct,” which is about as close to an on-the-record confirmation of a specific technical claim as this series has seen from him on any Starship-related figure. Whatever else is uncertain about this announcement, the reason Louisiana specifically was chosen over other Gulf Coast alternatives appears to be a genuine engineering constraint rather than simply the deal Governor Landry’s office was willing to offer.

The deal itself

Speaking of that deal: Louisiana’s state release describes SpaceX paying the state $25 million a year for twenty-five years, with an escalator clause, plus a $20 million upfront payment and a separate $25 million donation to the Community Foundation of Acadiana. In exchange, the state’s own materials cite 3,000 direct jobs over a ten-year window, at an average salary the state pegs at $92,600 — a figure it describes as 192 percent above the Vermilion Parish average, which is either a remarkable economic uplift for a historically underserved region or a sign of how low the existing regional wage baseline actually sits, and probably some of both.

Musk, separately, told SpaceX employees on site that the real jobs figure would end up considerably higher than the state’s number — a claim that appeared alongside a third figure, 8,100 direct jobs, circulating in adjacent coverage without clear reconciliation against either of the other two. Readers who have followed this series’ sister project, The Musk Stack, will recognise this pattern from that series’ own treatment of the same announcement: three different jobs numbers from the same news cycle, none formally reconciled, all serving the same announcement.

The timeline, and what “2029” is actually worth

Construction is slated to begin in 2026, with first launch targeted for 2029 — though Louisiana’s own economic development office describes “initial operations” arriving closer to 2030, a small but real gap between the company’s public framing and the state’s own paperwork. Readers of this series know better than most how much weight to put on a Musk-attached launch date. Starbase Texas took roughly five years to go from bare ground to orbital launch capability, a timeline SpaceX itself has cited as the basis for its confidence that it knows, this time, exactly what a second site of this kind actually costs and how long it actually takes. That is a fair point, and one this series should credit: an organisation building its second version of something it has already built once, on a similar coastal site with similar logistical requirements, is in a genuinely different position than an organisation attempting something for the first time. It is also true that “we have done this before, so we know how long it takes” has not, historically, prevented this specific company’s timelines from slipping — Flight 13’s own saga, chronicled across three instalments of this series, involved multiple scrubs before it ever left the pad. Confidence born of experience is worth something. It is not worth nothing going wrong.

The Rio Grande Valley precedent

SpaceX’s own promotional material for the Louisiana announcement leaned heavily on a specific, checkable claim about Starbase Texas: that roughly 70 percent of its workforce is drawn locally from the Rio Grande Valley, a historically low-income region of South Texas, and that the company intends to replicate that pattern in Vermilion Parish at an even larger scale.

It is worth taking that claim seriously rather than dismissing it as boilerplate, because if broadly accurate it represents a genuine and unusual counterexample to the more common pattern of large technology infrastructure projects importing skilled labour and leaving the surrounding local economy comparatively untouched. SpaceX’s own framing of the jobs on offer — welders, technicians, tube benders, the unglamorous trades that actually turn raw material into finished rocket hardware, rather than the software engineers most outside observers assume dominate the payroll — matches the skill set already concentrated in a region built on seven decades of offshore oil and gas construction. Whether Vermilion Parish sees anything like a 70 percent local hiring rate in practice is a claim this series intends to revisit once construction is actually underway, rather than simply accepting the company’s own precedent as a guarantee.

The parts of this that are still speculation

Beyond the confirmed ten pads and the Star Factory building, SpaceX and Musk have floated a considerably larger long-term vision for the site: offshore launch platforms extending capacity beyond the ten confirmed pads, and even an airport to bring visitors and workers to what would otherwise be, by every account, a genuinely remote stretch of coastal marsh reachable only by a multi-hour drive off the main interstate corridor. None of this is confirmed construction, and readers should treat it accordingly — as the kind of expansive, additive vision Musk tends to attach to every one of his major infrastructure announcements, some fraction of which eventually materialises and a meaningful fraction of which does not. The combined pad count across all of SpaceX’s Starship sites, including Louisiana, is reported at seventeen, with a theoretical combined annual launch capacity cited in some coverage at roughly 15,000 launches a year system-wide — a figure that, if it ever proved accurate, would exceed the total number of orbital launches conducted by every country on Earth combined, in every year of spaceflight history, several times over. I mention that comparison not to endorse it but because it is the scale at which this company is now asking to be evaluated, and a series that has spent ten instalments tracking thin margins and scrubbed launches owes its readers at least that much perspective on how far the ambition has now outrun the track record.

What actually needs 30 flights a day

The genuinely important question this announcement raises, and the one this series’ companion piece on Capex Ledger will take up in more financial detail, is why a single site needs to sustain more than thirty Starship launches daily at all. The public answer, offered by Musk and by SpaceX’s own materials, is that this capacity is not primarily for satellite deployment or crewed missions in the conventional sense, but for the company’s own cargo: an emerging orbital AI compute venture, developed jointly with Nvidia, alongside the continued build-out of Starlink’s direct-to-cell service following SpaceX’s roughly $19.5 billion acquisition of EchoStar spectrum. Starship, on this reading, is not being built to fly people or conventional satellites at unprecedented cadence. It is being built as the delivery mechanism for an entirely new industry SpaceX intends to build in orbit, one this series will examine on its own terms in the next instalment of Capex Ledger. For now, the honest summary is this: the spaceport’s engineering logic holds up better than its headline numbers do, the jobs figures need reconciling before anyone should cite a specific one with confidence, and the acreage discrepancy sitting unresolved in public reporting is exactly the kind of detail worth someone at a Louisiana newsroom actually confirming before the concrete starts pouring.
