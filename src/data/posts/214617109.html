by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,687 carrying the obituary I promised at essay 1,666: the death notice of the uncalculable world. On July 5, 1687, there was published in London, in Latin, in an edition of perhaps a few hundred copies, a book called Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica — the Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy — and when it was out, the oldest arrangement in human affairs was over. The arrangement was this: that the heavens and the earth kept separate books. Above the Moon, perfection, circles, mystery, the jurisdiction of theologians; below it, mud and fall and friction, the jurisdiction of everyone else. Every civilization had honoured the division. The Principia closed both sets of accounts and consolidated them into one, under one law, expressible in one line — and the audit balanced. It is the only time in this series, across sixteen centuries of entries, that I get to write that sentence. The audit balanced. Everything.

But this is the Numbers Game, and the Numbers Game is never only about the finding; it is about how the finding got posted. And the posting of the Principia is one of the great backstage farces in the history of knowledge, and it begins, as the best entries do, with a man asking a question he expected to spend years on.

In August 1684, the astronomer Edmond Halley travelled to Cambridge to put a problem to Newton — a problem then circulating among the sharpest men in London, none of whom could crack it: if the force holding the planets weakened with the square of the distance, what path would a planet trace? Halley asked. Newton answered at once: an ellipse. Halley, the account goes — it comes to us through Abraham de Moivre, decades later, and the tongs are applied — asked, astonished, how he knew. I have calculated it, said Newton, and then, in the manner of men who file their revolutions loosely, could not find the paper. The calculation that unified the cosmos was somewhere in the man’s rooms, mislaid. Readers of essay 1,519 will feel the chill of the Leonardo scenario assembling itself: the greatest count of the age, sitting unposted in a drawer, its owner constitutionally uninterested in the door. The difference — and it is the whole difference; it is why the modern world dates from 1687 and not from whenever posterity might have excavated Newton’s papers — is that this time there was a Halley. He got the proof rewritten, then expanded, then expanded again, and he pushed and flattered and project-managed a famously impossible man through three years of composition, and the drawer, for once in this series, was opened by a friend before it could become a tomb.

Then he paid for it. This is my favourite ledger in the entire essay, and I promise you every entry is real. The Principia was to be published by the Royal Society — which discovered, at the decisive moment, that it had no money, having spent its publishing budget the previous year on a lavish illustrated folio entitled De Historia Piscium: the History of Fishes. The book that would explain the universe was therefore financed personally by Edmond Halley — not a rich man; a working astronomer laying out his own funds — while the Society, unable even to pay his salary as its clerk, paid him instead, and I am not making this up, in unsold copies of the fish book. Sit with the accounting. The mathematical frame of all creation reached humanity because one man covered the print run out of pocket and accepted his wages in remaindered fish. If this series has taught you anything, it is that the grand ledgers of history are balanced in their footnotes by entries exactly like this one, and that the footnotes are where civilization actually lives. Somewhere between the Fuggers financing indulgences at essay 1,517 and Halley financing the Principia lies the entire moral range of what money can do at a printing press, and the two entries are only 170 years apart.

What did the book contain? I will keep the summary honest and brief, because the contents are the least tellable part and the most important. Three laws of motion, stated at the outset like the terms of engagement. A single force — gravitation, universal, acting between every particle of matter and every other, weakening with the square of the distance. And then, in three books of relentless geometry, the reconciliation: Kepler’s ellipses, which had been accurate rules without a reason for seventy years, derived as necessary consequences; the tides, explained by the pull of Moon and Sun on the seas; the flattening of the spinning Earth; the wobble of its axis; the paths of comets, promoted in one stroke from omens to scheduled traffic. Item after item that humanity had carried for millennia in the column marked mystery was audited, explained, and moved to the column marked consequence. The apple of essay 1,666 and the Moon of essay 1,609 fell, it turned out, by the same clause. One law, running the whole house. No previous book had ever reconciled so much, and I will commit journalism and say plainly: none since has, either.

The credit column, of course, produced its dispute — this series would have been suspicious otherwise. Robert Hooke, brilliant, prickly, and genuinely earlier than most on the inverse-square idea, let it be known that he expected acknowledgment, and Newton, whose capacity for grudge was as universal as his gravitation, responded by going through the manuscript and deleting references to Hooke — the reverse-Elcano, the un-globe: credit not misassigned by posterity but actively repossessed by the author, line by line, in proof. I record the dispute without adjudicating it, noting only the asymmetry that settles such cases in this series: Hooke had the idea that the force might follow an inverse square. Newton calculated it — derived the ellipse, the tides, the comets, the system. An idea is a claim; a calculation is a posting. The Numbers Game pays out on postings. It is a hard rule, and Hooke is its hard case, and I let it stand because sixteen centuries of entries say it must.

One more human note before the payoff, because it completes the man: the Principia is savagely difficult, and the difficulty was not entirely an accident. Newton is reported to have remarked — the tongs, once more — that he had made it abstruse deliberately, to avoid being baited by the smatterers. The greatest publication in the history of science was, by its author’s own late account, written partly to keep people from reading it. He had come a long way from the drawer, but the drawer was still in him; the door opened, and behind it he built a staircase most visitors could not climb. Halley climbed it, and reviewed the book, and popularized it, and one begins to understand that the true first law of the Principia’s motion was Halley, applied continuously and without thanks.

And it is Halley who supplies the ending, eighteen years and one comet later, and I have been saving it because it is the finest proof of audit this series will ever record. In 1705, using the Principia’s methods, Halley computed the orbits of historical comets and found that the comets of 1531, 1607, and 1682 were one body, returning — and he posted a prediction: it would come back around 1758. Attend to what that number is. It is a claim, checkable by anyone, about an event more than half a century away — a posting that neither Halley (who died in 1742) nor Newton (1727) could possibly live to see cleared. And on Christmas night of 1758, a German farmer and amateur astronomer picked the comet up in his telescope, on schedule, and the entry cleared, and the two auditors were in the ground, and the audit held anyway. That is the standard, and I want it on the record of this series in bold: an audit is proven when its predictions clear after the auditors are gone. Any system of counting can flatter its founder while he lives to defend it. The Principia’s system took a deposit on 1758 in 1705 and paid out posthumously, to the season, and the world has been compounding on that proof ever since. Every satellite, every trajectory, every tide table in your harbour app is interest on Halley’s cleared cheque.

I should enter one honest qualification, because this series does not deal in unaudited triumph, even Newton’s. The consolidation of 1687 was itself, in time, restated. Two centuries on, the books stopped balancing at the margins — the orbit of Mercury ran a persistent, tiny discrepancy that no Newtonian bookkeeping could clear — and in 1915 a patent clerk turned professor restated the accounts entirely, and gravitation became geometry, and Newton’s law was demoted from the truth to a magnificently accurate approximation of it. And here is why I enter this as tribute rather than correction: the restatement was conducted entirely in Newton’s format. Post the law, derive the consequences, submit the predictions to a sky that cannot be lobbied. Einstein overturned Newton’s answer by the strict application of Newton’s method, which is the only immortality this series recognizes. The findings of 1687 had a run of two hundred and twenty-eight years, which is long; the procedure of 1687 has never been out of service for a day, which is longer. Institutions in this series’s files defend their entries and lose everything, essay 1,633. The Principia founded the one institution that survives by inviting the audit of its own books — and it is still the only one, and its solvency remains the best news I know.

Dior’s contribution to this essay is jurisprudential. I explained to her that every particle of matter attracts every other particle, mutually, per the third law — that when the Earth pulls the apple, the apple pulls the Earth. She received this as confirmation of a position she has held since puppyhood: that when she pulls on the leash, I am also pulling on her, and that the resulting dispute is therefore symmetrical and the fault is shared. I have reviewed the mathematics and she is, infuriatingly, correct — the forces are equal and opposite; it is only the masses that differ. She has the physics. I have the mass. Our walks are a two-body problem that Newton solved in 1687 and that we re-litigate every morning on the lakeshore, and the Principia, for all its power, offers no clause on who wanted to sniff the hydrant, which is why the universe needed both Newton and dogs.

The count moves on, as it does — and with this entry the seventeenth century closes its books. It opened, five essays ago, with a rhyme and a windmill; it ends with the universe consolidated under a single law, printed on fish-book money. The next landmark is essay 1,707, and it is a gentler arithmetic: the year two old kingdoms, after centuries of subtraction from each other, attempted the rarest operation in the political ledger — addition — and became Great Britain, a sum the auditors are still testing three hundred years on.

The heavens kept one set of books, the earth another, and a man in Cambridge merged them, and a man from London paid the printer. Every audit needs both, and only one of them gets the statue.

See you at 1,707, when two countries try addition.
