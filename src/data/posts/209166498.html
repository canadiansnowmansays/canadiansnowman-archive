If Anthony Fauci’s career ended the day before Christmas, 2001 — his sixty-first birthday, and roughly two decades before anyone outside his field had cause to know his name — he would still belong in any serious accounting of consequential twentieth-century American public servants, on the strength of a single program most Americans have never fully understood the scale of. The President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, universally known by its acronym, PEPFAR, is by most independent estimates the most successful global health initiative any government has ever run, and Fauci was one of its principal architects, working behind the scenes on the technical and clinical design of a program that a Republican president would eventually announce to the world as his own.

The problem PEPFAR was built to solve, by the early 2000s, was staggering in a way that is difficult to convey in retrospect precisely because the program worked. Across sub-Saharan Africa, HIV/AIDS was killing millions of people a year, orphaning tens of millions of children, and threatening to erase decades of development gains across entire nations. Antiretroviral therapy already existed — the same combination drug regimens that had turned AIDS from a death sentence into a chronic condition for patients in wealthy countries by the late 1990s — but it existed almost nowhere in the countries where the epidemic was killing the most people, at prices and through supply chains that made it functionally unavailable to the overwhelming majority of the people who needed it. The scientific problem had been solved. The delivery problem had not.

Fauci, whose NIAID had funded and helped run much of the clinical research establishing how those antiretroviral regimens worked and could be simplified for use in resource-poor settings, became one of the small group of officials who persuaded the Bush administration that a large-scale American intervention was not just morally necessary but logistically achievable. President George W. Bush announced PEPFAR in his 2003 State of the Union address, committing an initial $15 billion over five years — at the time, the largest financial commitment any nation had ever made to address a single disease internationally — and Congress, in a display of the kind of bipartisan consensus that would become almost unrecognizable within Fauci’s own lifetime, funded it with votes from both parties. As Katherine J. Wu reported for The Atlantic, Fauci was one of PEPFAR’s chief architects, and it was this work — not his later television appearances — that earned him the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Bush.

The results are not really in dispute, which is itself unusual for anything touching American foreign policy over the past twenty-five years. Independent public health researchers estimate that PEPFAR has prevented something on the order of 25 million deaths since its launch, primarily across sub-Saharan Africa, through a combination of antiretroviral treatment access, prevention-of-mother-to-child-transmission programs, and testing infrastructure that did not previously exist in most of the countries it now covers. Millions of children who would otherwise have been born HIV-positive were not. Entire generations of parents who would have died in their thirties and forties, leaving behind the kind of orphan crisis that was already devastating parts of southern and eastern Africa by the early 2000s, instead lived to raise their own children. Life expectancy in some of the hardest-hit countries — Botswana, Zambia, parts of Kenya and Uganda — rose by more than a decade within the program’s first ten years of full operation, a shift that public health researchers rarely get to attribute this cleanly to a single intervention.

The mechanics of how PEPFAR actually worked are worth dwelling on, because the scale of the accomplishment is easy to state in the abstract and much harder to picture concretely. The program did not simply write checks to foreign governments and hope for the best. It built, largely from scratch, supply chains capable of delivering antiretroviral drugs to rural clinics that had never before reliably stocked basic medications of any kind; it trained and paid tens of thousands of local health workers, often for the first time giving countries like Zambia and Mozambique the kind of clinical workforce needed to run a chronic-disease treatment program at national scale; and it built testing and monitoring infrastructure that, as a byproduct, strengthened these countries’ capacity to respond to entirely unrelated public health threats years later, including, in more than a few cases, the response to Covid-19 itself. Fauci’s specific contribution ran through NIAID’s clinical research programs, which had spent years establishing which drug combinations could be simplified into once-daily regimens robust enough to work in settings without reliable refrigeration or laboratory monitoring — unglamorous science, entirely necessary, and almost never mentioned in any account of Fauci’s career that begins with Covid.

None of this is to suggest PEPFAR was without genuine controversy of its own, and a fair accounting owes it that complexity too. Early versions of the program, shaped by the political coalition that got it through a Republican Congress in 2003, included a requirement that a fixed share of prevention funding go toward abstinence-and-fidelity programs rather than condom distribution — a provision that public health researchers, including some within Fauci’s own orbit, argued undercut the program’s effectiveness in the populations most at risk, particularly sex workers and, in some countries, gay men, whom the abstinence framework was poorly suited to reach at all. Congress eventually loosened that requirement in 2008, after evidence accumulated that it was costing lives rather than saving them, but the fact that PEPFAR’s design bent to a domestic culture-war compromise in its earliest years is part of the honest record too, not a detail this series should smooth over simply because the program’s overall results turned out to be extraordinary.

It is worth pausing on what kind of accomplishment this actually represents, in spite of that early flaw, because the instinct with foreign aid programs — even wildly successful ones — is to treat them as abstractions, numbers on a ledger that never quite register the way a domestic crisis does. Twenty-five million is larger than the population of Florida. It is a number that, stated plainly, should be the single fact most Americans associate with Anthony Fauci’s career, and instead is very often a fact most Americans encountering his name for the first time during Covid never learned at all. This is not an accident of memory. It is, in part, a consequence of exactly the trait this series has spent two essays complicating rather than simply praising: Fauci’s decades-long insistence on staying out of the spotlight on anything that might look partisan, paired with his genuine preference for working the technical and clinical machinery of a program rather than standing in front of the cameras claiming credit for it. PEPFAR’s public face, by design and by Bush’s own choice, was the presidency, not the NIAID director who helped build its clinical backbone. Fauci got the medal. He did not get the myth.

That asymmetry matters for understanding what happened to his reputation two decades later, because it means that when Fauci did finally become the public face of a health crisis — Covid, starting in early 2020 — a very large number of Americans were encountering him for the first time with no context whatsoever for the preceding thirty years of his career. They had no memory of the AIDS-era reversal, no awareness of PEPFAR, no sense that the man now appearing nightly on their televisions had spent decades being trusted specifically because he avoided this kind of visibility. What they saw instead was a seventy-nine-year-old government scientist suddenly everywhere at once, and without the biographical context PEPFAR alone should have supplied, it was far easier for his critics to cast that visibility as an ambition rather than an emergency assignment thrust on a man who had spent his entire career trying to avoid exactly this kind of exposure.

The bitter irony, then, is that the program most likely to secure Fauci’s reputation as a serious, consequential public servant — the one least entangled in the partisan arguments over lockdowns, masks, and gain-of-function research that now dominate every conversation about him — has also been the most direct casualty of the administration currently investigating him. Since January 2025, the Trump administration has substantially dismantled large parts of PEPFAR’s funding and operational structure, a decision current and former public health officials have warned puts millions of the program’s existing patients at risk of losing access to the antiretroviral therapy keeping them alive. As The Atlantic’s Katherine J. Wu has reported, Fauci’s former colleagues have described watching that dismantlement with something close to despair, not merely as a policy reversal but as a targeted unwinding of the specific accomplishment least available for the caricature Fauci’s critics have built around Covid, Wuhan, and a diary full of celebrity phone calls. You cannot easily accuse a man of self-serving ego through a program that spent two decades explicitly refusing to put his own name on anything.

There is a version of this essay that simply ends there, with PEPFAR standing as the uncomplicated rebuttal to everything the current Senate hearings are trying to establish about Fauci’s character. I don’t think that would be honest either. The same instincts that made Fauci effective at building PEPFAR quietly — the comfort operating behind the scenes of a Republican president’s signature achievement, the willingness to let political credit flow entirely elsewhere in exchange for getting the program built at all — are inseparable from the broader pattern of accommodation this series keeps returning to: a man whose survival strategy across five decades depended on being useful enough to every administration that no administration ever had reason to look too closely at how much authority he had personally accumulated in the process. PEPFAR is a genuine, extraordinary accomplishment. It is also, not incidentally, a case study in how completely Fauci had mastered the art of making himself indispensable without ever making himself the story — a skill set that would collapse entirely, and almost overnight, the first time circumstances forced him to become the story whether he wanted to or not.

The dismantlement itself has proceeded in pieces rather than through a single dramatic announcement, which has made it easier for the program’s collapse to escape the kind of sustained coverage a program of PEPFAR’s scale might otherwise warrant. Funding freezes early in 2025, driven substantially by the Department of Government Efficiency under Elon Musk’s direction as it moved to dismantle much of USAID’s operating structure, disrupted drug distribution in several countries before court challenges partially restored some disbursements; waivers meant to protect “life-saving” treatment programs have, according to public health officials tracking the rollout, been applied inconsistently enough that clinics in multiple countries have reported running short of both medication and the staff needed to distribute it. Separately, and by multiple contemporaneous news accounts, tons of already-purchased food aid intended for famine-affected populations were left to spoil in warehouses after the funding freeze halted the logistics contracts needed to distribute it — a specific, documented failure that critics of the DOGE-era cuts, including several former USAID officials, have pointed to directly. The particular cruelty of this timeline, for a series concerned with fairness to its subject, is that Fauci himself has had no operational role in PEPFAR for years and no ability whatsoever to defend it — he is, at the moment his signature achievement is being unwound, occupied instead with pleading the Fifth Amendment before the same government dismantling it.

That collapse is where the next essay in this series picks up: seven presidents, one unbroken record of nonpartisan trust, and the single administration that finally broke it.
