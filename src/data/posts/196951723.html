Let’s reconstruct what happened in Virginia, because the sequence matters.

Democrats wanted to redraw Virginia’s congressional map. State law required them to amend the state constitution. Amending the constitution required the General Assembly to pass the amendment twice, with a legislative election occurring between the two votes. It required a statewide referendum. It required a majority of Virginia voters to approve it directly.

They did all of it. The General Assembly voted. Virginians went to the polls on April 21st. Fifty point seven percent voted yes. The margin was forty thousand votes. Democrats spent $70 million on the campaign. They won.

Seventeen days later, the Virginia Supreme Court struck it down.

The problem, in the court’s 4-to-3 ruling, was this: the first General Assembly vote on the amendment occurred on October 31st. Early voting in the November 2025 legislative elections had begun days earlier. More than 1.3 million Virginians had already cast their ballots before the legislature voted to begin the amendment process. Therefore, those early voters had unknowingly “forfeited their constitutionally protected opportunity” to weigh in on which legislators would vote on the amendment.

Four days. The margin between a valid democratic exercise and a nullified one is four days of early voting.

Donald Trump posted: “Huge win for the Republican Party, and America, in Virginia.”

The Asymmetry

I want to be precise about what the Virginia ruling reveals, because it illuminates something that has been present throughout this series but has now arrived in its starkest form.

Democrats installed an independent redistricting commission in Virginia in 2020 because they believed good-government principles mattered. They created a process intentionally insulated from partisan control. When they later needed to override that process to respond to Republican gerrymandering, the complexity of the constitutional amendment procedure — the two-vote requirement, the election-between-votes requirement, the precise sequencing — became the mechanism through which their democratic victory was nullified.

The good-government structure they built became the trap.

Republicans, meanwhile, kept map-drawing power in their state legislatures. In Texas, Florida, North Carolina, Missouri, Tennessee, Louisiana, and Alabama, Republican-controlled legislatures draw maps, amend maps, and redraw maps with no comparable procedural complexity. They do not need to pass amendments. They do not need referenda. They do not need to navigate two-vote sequences with elections occurring between them. They need a majority in a chamber they already control.

When Texas redrew its map at Trump’s request last summer, no procedural requirement compelled them to ask voters. When Florida passed a map as the Callais ruling was being handed down, no constitutional amendment process stood between the legislature and the map. When Tennessee carved Memphis into three pieces this week, the only procedural hurdle was repealing the state’s own ban on mid-decade redistricting — which the Republican supermajority did, in the same session, before drawing the map.

Democrats built the rules. Republicans kept the power. The rules are now being used against the people who built them.

The old expression is that you cannot have your cake and eat it too. This week, the Republican Party had the cake, ate it, and watched a court take Virginia’s cake off the table on a technicality while they helped themselves to Tennessee.

What the Virginia Court Actually Said

The majority’s reasoning deserves examination, because it is not frivolous — and that is precisely what makes it so effective as a weapon.

The Virginia Constitution requires that before voters weigh in on an amendment, the General Assembly must approve it twice, with a legislative election occurring between the two votes. The purpose of this requirement is democratic: it ensures that voters have the opportunity to elect representatives knowing how they will vote on constitutional changes.

The question before the court was whether “election” means Election Day or the beginning of early voting. The court said early voting. When the General Assembly voted on October 31st, more than 1.3 million Virginians had already cast ballots. Those voters, the court reasoned, had not had the opportunity to factor the amendment vote into their choice of legislators.

This is a defensible reading. It is also a reading that was applied with extraordinary consequences — the nullification of a statewide referendum that 1.385 million Virginians approved — on a 4-to-3 split, in a ruling that came seventeen days after the election.

The court acknowledged the awkwardness. “It is fair to ask whether we could have or should have reviewed the constitutionality of the proposed amendment prior to it being presented to the voters,” the majority wrote. It then noted that Democrats themselves had insisted the court could not lawfully decide the case before the referendum. Which is true. And which means Democrats argued for the process to proceed — spent $70 million on it — and then had the process invalidated after they won it.

Legal expert Maureen Edobor of Washington and Lee was direct about what happens next: “I don’t think it looks good for the Democrats.” The Virginia Attorney General has signaled a potential appeal to the federal courts. The conservative U.S. Supreme Court awaits. The court that upheld the Texas map and handed down Callais is the court Virginia Democrats are now looking to for relief.

Tennessee, Meanwhile

While Virginia’s ruling was being digested Friday, the Tennessee General Assembly had already passed its new map on Thursday — and the lawsuits were already filed overnight.

A coalition of voters and Democratic candidates sued in federal court, arguing that implementing new district lines this close to the August primary was unconstitutional under the Purcell principle — a legal doctrine discouraging changes to voting rules close to elections. The plaintiffs include Representative Steve Cohen, State Representative Justin Pearson, and two other candidates whose campaigns have been scrambled by the new map.

The NAACP Tennessee State Conference filed a separate lawsuit in state court Thursday afternoon.

The federal lawsuit makes a specific argument worth noting: the sudden shift of congressional districts just months before the primary “will wreak chaos on the electorate, will cause significant voter confusion” and will affect election officials’ ability to administer the election. Candidates who have spent months campaigning in their districts now face districts that no longer exist. Voters who thought they knew their representative face a completely new map. The filing deadline for candidates is May 15th — one week away.

The Purcell principle has been used by courts, including the Supreme Court, to block voting changes close to elections. It has also been used selectively, in ways that have tended to benefit incumbents and existing maps. Whether it will be applied here, at the speed the Tennessee situation requires, is the immediate legal question.

Tennessee was the first state to pass a map directly in response to Callais. It will also be the first test of whether the courts can move fast enough to stop a post-Callais gerrymander before it takes effect.

The Structural Verdict

We are now in a position to assess the redistricting war’s structural outcome as it stands on May 8th, seventeen days after Virginia voted and one week after Callais.

What Democrats gained:

  • California: five seats (enacted, upheld)

  • Utah: one seat (court-ordered)

  • Virginia: four seats (passed by referendum, struck down seventeen days later)

What Republicans gained:

  • Texas: five seats (enacted, judicially upheld)

  • North Carolina: one seat (enacted)

  • Missouri: one seat (enacted)

  • Ohio: one to two seats (enacted)

  • Florida: four seats (enacted)

  • Tennessee: one seat (enacted, under legal challenge)

  • Louisiana: one seat (pending new map)

  • Alabama: potentially one seat (special session underway)

  • South Carolina: potentially one seat (under consideration)

The rough draw of April 21st is no longer a draw. With Virginia’s four seats gone and the Southern cascade adding seats to the Republican column, Democrats are looking at a structural deficit of somewhere between six and ten seats before a single campaign dollar is spent in November.

In a House where the majority is measured in single digits, that is not a rounding error. That is, depending on the political environment, the majority itself.

The one saving grace — and it is real — is that the political environment is not neutral. Trump’s approval ratings are below forty percent. The Iran war is unpopular. Gas prices are rising. Democrats have been overperforming their 2024 baselines in special elections across the country by margins large enough to overcome structural disadvantages. In a wave election, maps are less decisive.

But the redistricting war was never primarily about 2026. It was about the baseline — the floor beneath which Democrats cannot fall even if the wave breaks their way, and the ceiling above which Republicans cannot be pushed even if the environment turns against them. The structural advantage being built now will outlast this election cycle. It will be the starting position for 2028.

The Good-Government Trap

There is a painful lesson embedded in the Virginia ruling that the Democratic Party will spend years processing.

For decades, good-government reformers — largely aligned with Democrats — pushed for independent redistricting commissions, nonpartisan map-drawing processes, and constitutional safeguards against partisan gerrymandering. Virginia’s 2020 commission was a model of this movement. Colorado, Michigan, Arizona all followed similar paths. The argument was that if you removed politics from the process, you would get fairer maps and a healthier democracy.

Republicans, by and large, declined to participate in this project. They kept their legislatures. They kept their map-drawing power. They watched Democrats disarm.

Now the commissions are constraints on Democratic retaliation. The constitutional safeguards are procedural traps. The good-government architecture is the mechanism through which Democratic victories are being reversed — in Virginia, because the amendment process was too complex; in Colorado, because the constitutional threshold is 55 percent; in Maryland, because the senate president followed the rules when everyone else was breaking them.

The people who built the rules are losing to the people who never followed them.

This is not an argument against good-government reform. Independent commissions and nonpartisan processes are genuinely better for democracy than the alternative. The Virginia commission produced a fair map. The problem is not the principle. The problem is unilateral disarmament — the adoption of constraints by one party in a system where the other party has rejected them.

Representative Hakeem Jeffries called the Virginia ruling “an unprecedented and undemocratic action that cannot stand.” He added that Democrats were “exploring all options.” Governor Spanberger said she was “disappointed” but focused on ensuring voters had information for November.

These are the words of people absorbing a blow they did not fully anticipate. The blow is real. The absorption is necessary. What comes after the absorption — whether it is a new legal strategy, a constitutional appeal, a more aggressive approach to the states where Democrats still hold legislative power — will define whether this series has more instalments to write about democratic resilience, or whether it pivots to something harder.

The View From Here

In Part I of this series, I asked whether American democracy had the institutional resilience to fight back against a coordinated assault on the structures of electoral fairness.

Twenty-five parts later, the answer is more complicated than it was when I started.

The immune system worked — in the courts that blocked executive overreach, in the voters who turned out in April for a ballot question about congressional lines, in the attorney generals who filed lawsuits, in the Spencer Deerys who gave the speech and held on by a coin flip, in the Raumesh Akbaris who spoke the record into existence in a chamber that had stopped listening.

The immune system is also losing ground — in the Supreme Court that handed down Callais, in the Virginia ruling that nullified a democratic referendum on a procedural technicality seventeen days after it passed, in the Tennessee legislature that carved up Memphis in a single week, in the structural deficit that is accumulating district by district across the South.

Democracy is fighting back. It is fighting back with the tools available to it — commissions, referenda, lawsuits, votes. The other side is fighting back with legislatures that answer only to themselves, a Supreme Court that has removed the last structural guardrail, and a president who calls a ruling that eliminates majority-Black districts “a huge win for America.”

The rules only apply to one side. That is not a partisan observation. It is a description of what happened this week.

Virginia voted. Virginia won. Virginia lost seventeen days later to four days of early voting.

The redistricting war continues. The playing field is no longer level. It may never have been.
