In early 2025, J.D. Vance visited Les Invalides in Paris and was invited to hold the sword of the Marquis de Lafayette, hero of the American Revolution. The next day, addressing an AI summit, he drew the comparison directly: the sword and artificial intelligence were both, he said, “weapons that are dangerous in the wrong hands but are incredible tools for liberty and prosperity in the right hands.”

It is a good line. It is also, on the historical record, an incomplete one — and the part Vance left out turns out to be the more useful guide to where his AI doctrine is actually heading.

This series has tracked Vance’s positions in fragments: the Air Force Academy speech invoking Pope Leo’s encyclical in Part 41, the Gillibrand legislation he voiced support for in Part 43. Vivian Salama’s profile in The Atlantic, published last month, assembles those fragments into something coherent for the first time — a genuine doctrine, not just a collection of quotes. It is worth taking seriously on its own terms before returning to what it leaves out.

I. The Doctrine, Stated Plainly

Vance’s position synthesizes two constituencies that do not naturally agree with each other, and he has built his political career on holding them together. The first is Silicon Valley — the venture capital world that funded his Senate run, anchored by Peter Thiel and David Sacks. The second is the working-class, anti-institutional populism Vance built his public identity on, the audience that views both government and big business with suspicion and fears, specifically and personally, that AI is coming for their jobs.

The doctrine that emerges from holding both constituencies simultaneously has five identifiable pillars. Innovation should not be slowed by premature regulation — Vance’s stated nightmare, articulated in his Paris speech, is that overregulation of the kind he attributes to Europe will calcify the position of today’s dominant firms and choke off the startups that might unseat them. AI is a matter of national power, comparable to oil, steel, and nuclear reactors in prior eras — a frame that treats the technology as geopolitical infrastructure first and commercial product second. Workers should be protected, through a labor framework Vance has not yet specified beyond invoking it. Concentrated corporate power is itself a danger, a position that puts him at odds with the traditional Republican coalition’s skepticism of antitrust enforcement. And on matters of life, death, and military judgment, human responsibility cannot be delegated to machines — the one place where Vance’s deregulatory instinct stops cold.

That fifth pillar is the one this series has already documented taking on legislative weight. The fourth is the one most likely to surprise readers who assume MAGA economic policy and Silicon Valley economic policy are now simply the same thing. They are not, in Vance’s framing — though whether his anti-concentration rhetoric ever produces anti-concentration policy is, as of this writing, untested.

II. The Race Vance Says America Cannot Afford to Lose

Vance’s second pillar — AI as national power, comparable to oil and steel — is not abstract positioning. It is being tested in real time, and the results as of this week complicate his framing considerably.

China reclaimed the world’s fastest supercomputer ranking for the first time since 2017. As noted by Don Clark in the New York Times, LineShine a system in Shenzhen, posted results more than 20 percent faster than the American machine it displaced, and did so using standard microprocessors rather than the specialized chips, called GPUs, that most high-end systems rely on. Jack Dongarra, an organizer of the Top500 supercomputer rankings who inspected the machine directly, called the underlying design “impressive” precisely because it points to a way of blending AI with traditional scientific computing that does not depend on the chip architecture the United States has tried to restrict China’s access to. Jimmy Goodrich of the University of California’s Institute on Global Conflict and Cooperation called the loophole exactly that — a loophole — and said export controls on the underlying processor type, rather than just GPUs, may now be necessary.

The pattern extends well beyond supercomputing. The New York Times’s DealBook newsletter reported that six of the ten most popular AI models on OpenRouter, a major model marketplace, are now Chinese — built by DeepSeek, Tencent, and Xiaomi. Microsoft is reportedly weighing whether to make DeepSeek’s V4 model available through its Copilot Cowork product as a lower-cost alternative to Anthropic’s or OpenAI’s offerings, fine-tuning it to reduce bias because the model is open-source. The Chinese lab Z.ai released GLM-5.2, a model the American developer-tools executive Guillermo Rauch — CEO of Vercel — described himself as “genuinely impressed, almost shocked” by, adding: “This changes things.”

This is the empirical test of Vance’s framing, and it is not going Vance’s way on the timeline he has implied. Dario Amodei warned this spring that Chinese frontier models were roughly six to twelve months behind their American counterparts. Jie Tang, a founder of Z.ai, responded directly on X to a separate prediction by Elon Musk that Chinese models could approach Anthropic’s capability: “won’t take that long.” The administration’s own enforcement record undercuts the urgency of its own rhetoric — Reuters reported that the administration held off on formally blacklisting DeepSeek as a national security risk, even as it imposed the export control directive on Anthropic’s Fable 5 and Mythos 5 chronicled in Parts 49 through 51.

The contradiction is worth stating plainly, because it cuts against the entire logic of Vance’s national-power framing. If AI is genuinely “going to make us more productive, more prosperous, and more free,” and if losing the AI race genuinely threatens American economic and military primacy in the way Vance argues at every opportunity, the administration’s posture toward the company most central to that race — restricting Anthropic’s most capable models through an opaque export control while declining to take equivalent action against the Chinese labs actually closing the gap — is not a national-power strategy. It is a strategy for losing the race to the company you have sidelined while the company you are racing keeps shipping.

III. The Sword Cuts Both Ways

Vance’s preferred analogy for AI’s effect on labor is the ATM. “What actually happens,” he told the Interesting Times podcast in May 2025, “is we have more bank tellers today than we did when the ATM was created, but they’re doing slightly different work. More productive. They have pretty good wages relative to other folks in the economy.”

James Bessen, the economist who has written extensively on the ATM’s actual labor history, told The Atlantic the claim does not hold up. ATMs initially reduced teller counts at individual branches. What grew teller employment afterward was 1990s bank deregulation, which made new branches cheap to open — more branches, each needing tellers, even as each branch needed fewer of them. That growth phase ended after 2010, when online banking spread and teller employment fell sharply and has not recovered.

Bessen’s better analogy is nineteenth-century textile automation: machines made cloth cheaper, demand surged, and both productivity and employment grew together — for a while. By the mid-twentieth century, the surge in demand had already been captured. Productivity kept climbing. Demand plateaued. Jobs disappeared. The lesson Bessen draws is not that automation always destroys jobs, nor that it always creates them. It is that the relationship has a lifecycle, and the comfortable early phase does not predict the difficult later one. “What is clear,” he said, “is that people across the workforce will need to acquire new skills if they want to keep working” — a sentence with no comparable promise embedded in it.

Vance’s response, given directly to Salama, was not engagement with the textile comparison. It was a flat denial of the premise: “I don’t buy the premise. I have not yet seen the evidence that you’re going to see widespread job destruction because of AI.” He points to flat labor productivity numbers as proof the robots have not yet come for anyone’s job — reasoning that inverts the warning of Part 47 of this series. Productivity may be flatlining not because automation isn’t happening, but because the apprenticeship pipeline that used to convert entry-level grunt work into skilled judgment has already been quietly hollowed out, with the damage not yet visible in aggregate statistics because it has not finished compounding.

Vance is right to worry that AI’s gains will concentrate upward, “as has happened in previous industrial revolutions.” He said so explicitly: “I do worry about that. I think it’s a serious concern.” That concern and his ATM analogy cannot both be correct. An industrial revolution whose gains concentrate upward by historical pattern is, definitionally, one in which displaced workers do not automatically land in equally good new roles — which is precisely the dynamic Bessen’s textile comparison describes and Vance’s ATM comparison denies.

IV. The Fight in Vance’s Own Office

The clearest evidence that the Republican coalition has not resolved its internal AI contradiction took place, by Salama’s reporting, in Vance’s own office.

Seated across from each other: David Sacks, who had served as the administration’s AI czar before stepping down in March (he continues to advise the White House), and Mike Davis, a conservative attorney who was trying to block language Sacks wanted inserted into legislation — language that would have limited congressional oversight of AI and eliminated state jurisdiction over it entirely. The exchange, according to officials familiar with it, grew heated. Vance, close to both men, tried to broker common ground.

He did not fully succeed. Weeks later, in December, Trump signed an executive order seeking to curb states’ ability to pass their own AI regulations and directing the administration to work with Congress on a national framework. Republican governors from seventeen states pushed back, calling on Congress to strip the state-preemption language from a budget reconciliation bill. Florida’s Ron DeSantis went further, arguing the White House cannot legally strip states of their regulatory authority through executive order alone — and followed through by making Florida the first state to sue OpenAI and Sam Altman, alleging ChatGPT prioritizes profit over the safety of minors.

This is the federalism question this series flagged as unresolved in Part 43, now visible as an open fracture inside the governing party itself, not just between Washington and the states. Vance’s own preferred resolution — “one standard,” applied nationally, specifically to prevent what he calls “far-left California” from “dominating the entire AI regulatory map” — has not actually been achieved by the executive order that was supposed to deliver it. The order asserted federal primacy. Seventeen Republican governors and a Republican state attorney general’s office have since acted as though it did not.

V. The Anthropic Question, Restated by Hegseth

Vance’s fifth pillar — human responsibility cannot be delegated to machines — runs directly into the dispute this series chronicled across Parts 18, 48, 49, and 50. Salama’s reporting adds a detail that sharpens Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s position considerably beyond what was available when Part 49 went to press.

Hegseth has argued the Pentagon’s AI initiatives should operate “without ideological constraints that limit lawful military applications,” adding that the military is “building war-ready weapons and systems, not chatbots for an Ivy League faculty lounge.” He has issued a directive requiring a handful of AI defense contractors to permit “lawful operational use” without exceptions — the same “all lawful use” demand that, per the original dispute documented in Part 18, Anthropic refused, triggering the supply chain risk designation that is still in litigation.

Vance’s position, stated at the Air Force Academy and reaffirmed to Salama, is the opposite of Hegseth’s framing: “Decisions over life and death must be made by humans and not machines.” The vice president is, on the public record, aligned with the company his own administration has blacklisted twice and is currently restricting via export control — and at odds with his own defense secretary’s stated doctrine. Salama’s report adds one further data point: Trump himself said last week that talks with Anthropic to resolve the ongoing dispute were “going fine,” a notably milder register than Hegseth’s “every passing day proves blacklisting was the right move.”

Three senior figures in the same administration — the vice president, the defense secretary, and the president — are not narrating the same story about the same company in the same week. That is not a contradiction this series invented. It is the administration’s own internal incoherence, restated by three different officials in three different registers.

VI. What Lafayette Actually Teaches

Salama’s closing move deserves to be the closing move of this essay too, because it is sharper than anything this series could construct independently.

On July 14, 1789, Parisian insurgents seized weapons from Les Invalides — the same building where Vance would later hold Lafayette’s sword — and used them to storm the Bastille that same day. Lafayette, then head of the French National Guard, tried to steer a middle path between the monarchy and the revolutionaries who had just armed themselves from his own armory. He failed. He was eventually forced to flee France.

Vance’s invocation of Lafayette was meant to illustrate a tool that is dangerous in the wrong hands and liberating in the right ones. The actual history of that sword’s building illustrates something else: that a moderate trying to hold the center between two forces that are each gathering real power can be overrun by both, not because the middle path was wrong in principle, but because moderation has no army of its own. Lafayette had a title and a uniform. He did not have the through-the-streets support of either side, and when calculation turned to action, that absence mattered more than his intentions did.

Vance is attempting something structurally similar: a position that takes Silicon Valley’s deregulatory instinct and labor’s anti-concentration anxiety and tries to hold both, plus a national-security carve-out for human judgment in matters of life and death. Whether that position survives contact with the actual deployment of AI across the American economy and the American military is the open question this series has been asking in different language since Part 1 — and Lafayette’s own history is not an encouraging precedent for the man who borrowed his sword as a metaphor.

The Pentagon is already operating on Hegseth’s doctrine, not Vance’s. Anthropic is still blacklisted. Seventeen Republican governors have not accepted the executive order Vance’s own administration issued to settle the federalism question in his preferred direction. The middle path Vance is describing in speeches has not yet been built in policy. Whether it can be — or whether, like Lafayette, the moderate position gets run over by the forces it tried to balance — is a question this series will keep asking for as long as the gap remains open.
