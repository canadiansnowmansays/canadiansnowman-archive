LES MISÉRABLES: THE 2026 NATIONAL TOUR

Venues: approximately 1,027 lycées across France, plus selected bus shelters

Opened: 21 September 2026, Lycée Saint-Exupéry, Créteil

Running time: ongoing; no interval announced

Rating: ★★★★☆ (one star withheld for the ending, which everyone already knows)

It is not every season that a French production of Les Misérables opens without a producer, a director, a rehearsal period or a single ticket sold, and then expands to more than a thousand venues in under a fortnight. Rival producers would weep. Cameron Mackintosh, who has spent four decades keeping this property running in the West End, might reasonably ask for royalties.

Yet here it is. The 2026 National Tour began, as the most interesting productions do, in the suburbs. As Amnesty International France records it, the first blockade went up on 21 September 2026 at the Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil, and by Tuesday 29 September some 410 actions had been counted in schools across the country. By Thursday 1 October, according to Interior Ministry figures reported by Sud Radio and AFP, 1,027 of France’s roughly 3,700 lycées had been touched by the movement, 222 of them blockaded outright.

For a touring company, that is extraordinary market penetration. For a Ministry of Education, it is something else.

The book

Purists will want to know whether the revival is faithful to the source, and the answer is: more faithful than the source’s admirers might like.

The barricade in Victor Hugo’s novel is not, as tourists tend to assume, 1789. Nobody storms the Bastille. The barricade belongs to the June Rebellion of 1832, a small and doomed rising that flared in Paris on 5 June after the funeral of General Lamarque and was finished by the army the next day. Its leaders were, in large part, students. Hugo, who was thirty and in the city at the time, gave them names and speeches and a red flag, and then he did the honest thing and let them die. The barricade at the Rue de la Chanvrerie falls. Enjolras is shot against a wall. Gavroche, a street child of perhaps eleven or twelve, is killed in the open while gathering cartridges from the dead.

The musical, which opened in Paris in 1980 and reached London in 1985, kept all of that. What audiences remember, however, is the anthem: a rousing first-act number in which the students ask whether you can hear the people singing. It is the kind of song that makes a theatre full of accountants feel briefly revolutionary on the way to the car park. It is also, if you listen to the plot rather than the orchestra, a song sung by young people moments before the state shoots them.

The 2026 revival has restored this original emphasis. The young people are back at the centre. And the state, as we shall see, has been cast with unusual commitment.

Set design

Hugo’s insurgents built with paving stones, omnibuses, wine barrels and the furniture of whoever was unlucky enough to live on the corner. The 2026 company works in a more contemporary idiom. Its principal material is the green wheelie bin of the City of Paris, supplemented by steel crowd barriers, wooden pallets, a stray armchair and the occasional municipal construction hoarding.

One such hoarding, visible in the photograph above, carries a cheerful official notice announcing new street furniture from the City of Paris, with the period of works neatly written in by hand. It is perhaps the single most eloquent piece of set dressing in the production. The city can schedule the replacement of street furniture to the day. The students are on the other side of it because nobody can tell them when the classroom ceiling will be fixed.

Critics of an earlier generation would call this found art. The Préfecture calls it obstruction of the public highway. Both are correct.

Lighting and effects

No expense has been spared on effects, chiefly because they are being paid for by two different departments. The students supply the flares, the bin fires and, in the less admirable scenes, the arson. As 75 Secondes reported, the entrance hall of the Lycée Nelson-Mandela in Nantes was set alight from a bin fire, and the façade of the Lycée Lumière in Lyon was scorched. These are not small things. Teachers and head teachers were jostled. A school is a building that children need, and setting fire to one in the name of better schools is the kind of plot hole that would get a librettist sacked.

The state, for its part, supplies the smoke. The tear gas in this production is technically superb: dense, white, beautifully backlit on a sunny afternoon, and drifting across tram lines like dry ice at the Barbican. The lighting designer, whoever he is, understands that nothing photographs better than a lone teenager standing in a cloud of something meant to make her cry.

Casting

This is, by some distance, the youngest company ever to mount the show. The original Gavroche was one child. The 2026 tour appears to have cast several thousand.

The figures here are not satire, and I set them down plainly. On Thursday 1 October alone, according to the Interior Ministry, police made 1,949 arrests in connection with the school blockades. The Justice Ministry counted 1,793 people taken into police custody since the start of that week, and, as 75 Secondes noted, 90 per cent of those held were minors. A press review from Sud Radio put the injured at close to 300.

A national government has, in other words, spent a week arresting its own schoolchildren at a rate of roughly two thousand a day. The charges, the ministry said, were chiefly assaults on officers, violence and unlawful assembly. Some of that will be true. Some of the teenagers in these photographs are throwing things, and one widely shared image shows a group of them hauling down a set of traffic lights with the methodical teamwork their physics teachers can only dream of. But a policing operation in which nine in ten detainees are children is not a public-order story with some young people in it. It is a story about young people, with some public order in it.

In Hugo’s novel, Gavroche dies singing a mocking little ditty at the soldiers. It is the bravest and most childish thing in the book. The novelist understood what the state, in the moment, did not: that a child on a barricade is not proof of a dangerous revolution. He is proof that the adults have failed somewhere further back.

The chorus

The ensemble numbers are where this production truly sings. The marches are enormous, good-humoured, and armed principally with cardboard and marker pens. The signs are the libretto, and they are better written than most of what the government has said this month.

There is a running bilingual pun that no English adaptation will ever capture. Le bac, masculine, is the baccalauréat, the school-leaving exam that every French teenager is working towards. La BAC, feminine, is the Brigade anti-criminalité, the plain-clothes anti-crime police. The students’ signs say they asked for one and were sent the other. A student in Lille, as Euronews showed, carried the English version, wanting the exam and not the brigade. It is, I think, the best line in the show.

Other banners reach for the family album. Several invoke May 1968, the last time French students closed the schools and frightened a government. One suspects their grandparents, who were there, have been helping with the lettering.

And what do the lyrics actually demand? This is the point at which a critic is supposed to complain that the revolutionaries’ motives are vague. They are not vague in the slightest. Amnesty France lists them: dilapidated buildings, classes with no replacement teacher, timetables that crush, the pressure of Parcoursup, the national university-admissions platform. One example has become the movement’s refrain. A classroom at 39 degrees, thirty-seven students, one fan.

No librettist would dare write that line. It is too on the nose. That is how you know it is true.

Javert

Every production of Les Misérables lives or dies by its Javert, and the 2026 company has made a bold choice: Javert is played not by one actor but by the entire apparatus of the Interior Ministry, with prefects in supporting roles.

It is a committed, unshowy performance. The Paris police prefect, Patrice Faure, banned all gatherings near thirteen closed Paris lycées by decree, listing each school by name. Riot units have been deployed outside schools in numbers usually reserved for football derbies. The national arrest tally, as Reuters reported on 3 October, had passed two thousand, most of them teenagers.

Like the original, this Javert believes absolutely in the law and has no interest whatsoever in why it was broken. Unlike the original, he has not yet had his crisis of conscience on the bridge. Critics are divided on whether that number has been cut from this production or merely postponed to the second act.

A surprise appearance by the Thénardiers

The single most daring piece of recasting comes late in the week. Gérald Darmanin, speaking on RTL radio, said the parents of students who damage property should be made to pay reparations.

Fans of the musical will recognise the move instantly. It does not belong to Javert at all. It belongs to the Thénardiers, the innkeepers who itemise everything, and whose big comic number, “Master of the House,” is essentially a song about invoicing. Somewhere in a ministry, an official is now drawing up a bill for a green wheelie bin, a traffic light and a scorched lycée façade, addressed to the parents of a sixteen-year-old in a room that reached 39 degrees.

One does not want to be glib. Property was destroyed, and someone will pay for it. But one notices that the government has found a mechanism for charging families for the damage done to schools during one week of protest, and has not yet found one for repairing the damage done to them during the previous decade of neglect.

The question of the producer

Who is behind all this? The government thinks it knows. Ministers and their allies, according to Public Sénat, have accused La France Insoumise of orchestrating and spreading the movement, and of manipulating the students. The Education Minister said the party had chosen to crystallise it from the outset. LFI’s leaders, for their part, accused the government of politicising the protests ahead of the election.

This is the oldest argument in theatre: when a show is a hit, everyone claims to have produced it, and when it goes wrong, everyone blames the producer. It also happens to be the oldest argument of the French state, which in 1832 blamed republican secret societies, in 1968 blamed agitators, and in 2026 blames a parliamentary party. It is never the leaking roof. It is always the impresario.

The election, it should be said, is seven months away. As AFP drily observed, in such an electric climate a youth mobilisation is more than sensitive. That is French journalistic understatement for everyone is now auditioning.

The audience

The most quietly devastating element of this production, as of the novel, is the audience.

In Hugo, the students on the barricade wait for Paris to rise with them. Paris does not. The shutters stay closed. The people they are singing for listen from behind their windows and do not come down.

Look closely at the photograph above. On the first-floor balconies, residents are standing in the open, watching the charge as one might watch a parade. They are not hostile. They are not joining in. They are spectators, which in this particular show is the most important role of all, because the story depends entirely on whether they stay upstairs.

The second act

On the steps of the Lycée Turgot in Paris, under a stone bust of the Republic in her helmet, a student has propped a sign asking Marianne whether she has forgotten her children.

It is the kind of line a lazy critic calls melodramatic. But that is the whole genre. Les Misérables is a melodrama about a nation that keeps forgetting its children: Fantine’s daughter worked like a scullery maid by the Thénardiers, Gavroche sleeping inside the plaster elephant at the Bastille, the students left to die while their city sleeps. Hugo was not subtle because he did not think the subject deserved subtlety.

The Education Minister, Édouard Geffray, announced that roughly 400 schools would close as a precaution and that some would teach remotely, according to Ecuador’s Primicias, which carried the wire coverage. The government says it will receive representatives of the young people. Teachers’ unions have called strikes. This is the point in the second act where the music changes key, and nobody in the stalls yet knows which way it will go.

Verdict

As a piece of theatre, the 2026 National Tour is uneven. It is overlong, under-rehearsed, and in places genuinely destructive in a way that harms the very people it claims to represent. A burnt school is a burnt school. The violence on both sides will be argued about for months, and it should be.

But the production has done something the West End version, for all its splendour, cannot. It has reminded its audience what the song is actually about.

For forty years, theatregoers in London, New York and Toronto have stood up at the end of the first act and felt something stirring. They have bought the T-shirt. They have sung along in the car. And then they have gone home and voted for whatever was cheapest. The anthem has become a sort of national karaoke of conscience, a way for comfortable adults to feel solidarity with young people on barricades on the strict condition that the young people are fictional and the barricades are made of plywood.

France in October 2026 has removed the condition. The young people are real. The barricades are made of municipal bins. The heat in the classroom is 39 degrees. The state has met them with gas, batons, two thousand arrests a day, and a proposed invoice to their parents.

The photograph above is the curtain call. The three words of the Republic sit over a door with its glass smashed, the scorched remains of rental scooters piled against the step, and a woman in the foreground gesturing at the damage as if asking whose turn it is to clean up. Liberty, equality, fraternity. All three are still there on the wall. The door beneath them is broken.

Hugo knew how this story ends. The barricade falls. The army wins. The students are buried, and the city goes back to work. He wrote it anyway, as a warning, in the hope that a later Paris might choose differently.

Four stars. One withheld for the ending. Not because it is badly written, but because, unlike the anthem, it has not changed in nearly two hundred years.
