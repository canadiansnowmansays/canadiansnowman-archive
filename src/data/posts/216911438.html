By the time anyone sat down to design a successor to the League of Nations, the design flaw was not a matter of academic debate. It had a body count. The men — and at San Francisco, in 1945, they were overwhelmingly men — who drafted the Charter of the United Nations had watched Manchuria, Abyssinia, and the Rhineland unfold in real time, and most of them had spent the previous six years living through the war those three failures had failed to prevent. Whatever else divided them, they agreed on the diagnosis: an organisation that required unanimous consent from every member state, great power and minor state alike, before it could act, would produce exactly the paralysis Geneva had produced. The cure they settled on was not to abandon the idea of collective security. It was to stop being embarrassed about the inequality already present in practice, and write it into the founding document instead.

From Atlantic Charter to Dumbarton Oaks

The intellectual path to San Francisco ran through several earlier wartime agreements, each adding a piece to what would eventually become the UN Charter. In August 1941, four months before the United States formally entered the war, Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill met off the coast of Newfoundland and issued the Atlantic Charter, a statement of shared postwar principles that gestured toward “a wider and permanent system of general security” without yet specifying what that system would look like. On 1 January 1942, in the wake of Pearl Harbor, representatives of twenty-six Allied governments signed the Declaration by United Nations, formally adopting the name Roosevelt had coined for the wartime coalition and committing each signatory to the Atlantic Charter’s principles — the first time the phrase “United Nations” was used to describe anything other than the alliance fighting the war itself. Roosevelt reportedly settled on the name after considering and rejecting several alternatives, wheeling himself into Churchill’s room at the White House to propose it directly; Churchill, who liked the phrase enough to quote Byron’s “Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage” back at him in response, agreed on the spot. It is a detail worth pausing on only because it is easy to forget, eighty years and hundreds of Security Council meetings later, that the organisation’s very name began as one man’s improvisation in a wartime bedroom rather than a carefully workshopped brand.

The more concrete architectural work happened later, and largely out of public view. From August to October 1944, representatives of the United States, Britain, the Soviet Union, and China met at Dumbarton Oaks, an estate in Washington, D.C., to hammer out proposals for the new organisation’s structure: a General Assembly in which every member state would have a voice, a Security Council with primary responsibility for maintaining peace, and an International Court of Justice to handle legal disputes. China’s presence at that table was itself a small preview of the trade-offs the whole enterprise would run on: the Nationalist government in Chongqing was, in 1944, fighting for its survival against Japanese occupation and a rising Communist insurgency, and would lose control of the mainland entirely within five years of the Charter’s signing — yet it sat, and its successor government still sits, as one of five permanent, veto-wielding members of the body built to police the peace of the entire planet. Rooseveltian strategy treated China’s inclusion as an investment in a future great power rather than a reflection of the power China actually held in 1944, a bet the war’s end would only partially vindicate.

Even the sequencing of the Dumbarton Oaks talks reflected the era’s remaining diplomatic sensitivities: because the Soviet Union was not yet at war with Japan and wished to avoid provoking a two-front conflict before it was ready, the conversations were held in two separate phases — one covering the US, UK, and USSR, the other bringing in Nationalist China separately — rather than as a single four-power negotiation from the start. The Council itself was sketched at Dumbarton Oaks as a body of eleven members — five permanent, six elected by the Assembly for rotating terms, later expanded to fifteen total in 1965 as UN membership itself grew — deliberately smaller and more nimble than the League’s Council had been, on the theory that a compact body might actually be able to convene and act quickly in a genuine crisis rather than waiting for a full plenary session. What Dumbarton Oaks did not resolve was the single question that would determine whether any of the rest of it mattered: how, exactly, the Council should vote, and whether any state’s vote should count for more than any other’s.

Yalta and the voting formula

That question was settled in February 1945, at Yalta, in the Crimea, where Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met for what would be their final wartime summit together. The arrangement they agreed on — since known simply as the Yalta voting formula — gave each of the five permanent members of the proposed Security Council (the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, France, and China) the power to block any substantive Council decision with a single negative vote, regardless of how the other members present had voted. It was, in essence, the League’s unanimity requirement reborn in a deliberately narrowed form: rather than requiring every member state to agree, the Charter would require only the five most powerful states to agree, while leaving the Assembly’s much larger membership able to debate and recommend but not to bind anyone. The logic, as Roosevelt and his advisers understood it, was blunt and largely unapologetic. No great power — least of all a United States whose Senate had just spent 1919 and 1920 demonstrating exactly what happened when an international commitment felt like it might override domestic sovereignty — would agree to join an organisation that could vote to send its own soldiers to war without its consent. Better, on this reasoning, to build an organisation the great powers could actually be persuaded to join and fund, even at the cost of paralysis whenever those same great powers disagreed among themselves, than to build one more principled on paper that ended up, like its predecessor, missing its most important member entirely.

Roosevelt did not live to see the plan through. He died on 12 April 1945, thirteen days before the San Francisco Conference opened, leaving Harry Truman — a vice president who had been in office barely three months, had met with Roosevelt alone only a handful of times, and had not been closely briefed on the wartime diplomacy that produced the veto formula — to preside over the Charter’s completion. Truman would later describe learning of the full scope of Roosevelt’s foreign-policy commitments, including the still-secret development of the atomic bomb, only after being sworn in; the man who opened the San Francisco Conference under Roosevelt’s name was, in a very literal sense, catching up on his predecessor’s plans in real time even as delegates from fifty nations expected the American delegation to speak with settled authority. That the conference proceeded largely on rails already laid by Roosevelt’s advisers — men like Secretary of State Edward Stettinius, who chaired the US delegation throughout — says something about how much of the Charter’s substance had already been locked in before anyone in San Francisco cast a single vote.

San Francisco: the smaller states object, and lose

The United Nations Conference on International Organization convened at San Francisco’s War Memorial Opera House on 25 April 1945 and ran for nine weeks, closing on 26 June. Fifty states sent delegations; Poland, unable to send one in time because of the unresolved question of which Polish government was legitimate, signed later and was counted as the fifty-first original member. The conference divided its work among four commissions, covering general provisions, the Assembly, the Security Council, and judicial matters, and for most of the nine weeks the broad architecture inherited from Dumbarton Oaks and Yalta moved through largely as the great powers had already agreed it would.

The veto did not move through unopposed. Smaller and middle-sized states — Australia’s delegation, led by the combative External Affairs Minister H.V. Evatt, was especially vocal — pushed hard against a provision that gave five states the power to block action even in disputes to which they were themselves a party, arguing that it inverted the entire principle of “sovereign equality” the Charter’s own second article was simultaneously being drafted to promise every member state. The tension between Article 2’s declaration that the organisation “is based on the principle of the sovereign equality of all its Members” and Article 27’s grant of a blocking power to only five of them was not resolved so much as left standing, unremarked, a few pages apart in the same founding document. The sponsoring powers were unmoved by Evatt’s objections, and for a specific, non-negotiable reason they said openly rather than concealed: without the veto, none of the “Big Three” — the United States, Britain, or the Soviet Union — would ratify the Charter at all, and an organisation without American or Soviet membership was, by 1945, not an organisation worth building. Smaller states could object to the veto, debate it at length, and vote to record their objection. They could not remove it, because removing it meant losing the great powers the entire structure existed to bind, and everyone in the room understood the trade being made.

There was, in fact, a further wrinkle to the veto that the sponsoring powers clarified only reluctantly, in a joint statement issued during the conference’s final weeks: the question of which Council decisions counted as merely “procedural,” and therefore exempt from the veto altogether under the Charter’s own text, versus “substantive,” and therefore subject to it, was itself a substantive question — meaning any of the five permanent members could veto even the attempt to classify a matter as procedural in the first place. This became known, half-affectionately and half-despairingly, among diplomats and legal scholars in the years that followed as the “double veto,” and it meant that the supposedly narrow, well-defined exception smaller states had been promised — routine, housekeeping matters that no single power could block — turned out to have no fixed boundary at all. Where that boundary sat, in any given case, was itself something a permanent member could refuse to agree to.

“We the peoples,” and what the phrase concealed

The Charter that emerged from San Francisco opens with a famous line — “We the peoples of the United Nations, determined to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war” — deliberately echoing the opening of the United States Constitution, and deliberately projecting an authorship broader than the one that actually produced the document. The peoples of the world did not draft the Charter’s Article 27, the clause establishing the veto; five governments did, in private negotiations at Yalta that most of those peoples only learned about after the fact. The preamble’s soaring language is generally credited to Jan Smuts, the South African statesman and field marshal who had also drafted language for the League’s own founding Covenant a quarter-century earlier — an authorship detail that carries its own quiet irony, since Smuts would spend much of the following decade defending South Africa’s early apartheid legislation against exactly the kind of General Assembly condemnation the Charter’s human-rights language he helped write would eventually be used to justify. This is not a criticism unique to the UN — the League’s Covenant made no comparable claim to popular authorship in its opening line, if anything a more honest position — but it is worth noting as the pattern this series will keep returning to: an institution whose legitimating language gestures toward universal participation, while its actual decision-making architecture concentrates real power in a small number of hands that insisted on keeping it there as the price of showing up.

That gap between the Charter’s stated authorship and its actual drafting process was not, however, absolute. San Francisco was also where a coalition of American civil-society organisations — women’s groups, labour unions, religious bodies, and the NAACP among them, attached to the US delegation as official consultants at the insistence of Secretary of State Stettinius — successfully pushed for human rights language and the creation of the Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC) to be written into the Charter over the initial objections of some of the sponsoring governments, who had not planned to include either in any prominent way. It was a genuine, if narrow, instance of non-governmental input shaping a founding document otherwise negotiated almost entirely between great powers, and it established the precedent — expanded considerably in the decades since — for NGOs to hold formal consultative status at the UN under what became Article 71. The Charter’s most democratic-sounding line may have been written by one man in Smuts’s own hand, but the institutional space for actual outside voices, however modest at the time, was carved out in San Francisco’s committee rooms by people who were not delegates at all.

The Charter was signed by all fifty of the original delegations present on 26 June 1945 — a Poland-shaped gap in the room, filled a few weeks later — and entered into force on 24 October 1945, once China, France, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, the United States, and a majority of the other signatory states had deposited their instruments of ratification. That date is still marked annually as United Nations Day. Unlike 1920, when the League came into being missing the government that had insisted on designing it, the UN’s most powerful founding members were, this time, actually inside the building when the doors opened — not because the design had become more egalitarian, but because it had become, deliberately, less so.

Whether that trade was worth making — an organisation with the great powers inside it but structurally unable to act against any of them, versus the League’s more principled paralysis, which at least applied its unanimity requirement without favour — is the argument this series exists to trace across the eight decades since.
