Kim Jong-un had been watching the jangmadang generation with alarm long before the pandemic gave him the tools to act. He had understood, from almost the moment he took power in 2011, that the informal economy his father had tolerated and the foreign media his father had failed to stop represented not merely ideological embarrassments but structural threats — slow-acting corrosives dissolving the foundational premise on which the Kim dynasty’s legitimacy rested. That premise was simple: the people needed the state. The markets had spent twenty years proving otherwise.

What he needed was an excuse to shut the markets, seal the border, and re-impose the state’s monopoly on survival without provoking the kind of mass resistance that could destabilize the regime. He needed, in other words, a public health emergency.

In January 2020, COVID-19 gave him one.

The Most Restrictive Border in the World

North Korea sealed its border with China on January 21, 2020 — earlier than virtually any other country, before the World Health Organization had declared a pandemic, before the virus had been fully characterized, before most governments had begun to take it seriously. The official rationale was public health. The operational reality was something considerably more comprehensive.

The Korean People’s Army deployed additional units to the border. Guards were issued shoot-to-kill orders for anyone attempting to cross in either direction. In August 2020, the government formalized what it called “buffer zones” extending one to two kilometres inside the Chinese border, with instructions to shoot on sight any person or animal entering without permission — the animal provision added, with the particular bureaucratic absurdity of totalitarian states, because the regime had decreed that COVID-19 could be spread through wildlife as well as people. At least fourteen people were shot and killed in the buffer zones between September 2020 and July 2022. Defector numbers collapsed: from 1,047 people reaching South Korea in 2019, to 63 in 2021. The Underground Railroad, that network of brokers and missionaries and Korean-Chinese intermediaries that had sustained a fragile flow of people and information across the border for two decades, effectively ceased to function.

The border closure was devastating in its practical effects. North Korea’s trade with China — which had been the primary source of imported goods, medicine, and fuel — dropped by approximately eighty percent in 2020. Food prices spiked. Medicine became scarce. The North Korean economy, already crippled by international sanctions, contracted by an estimated 4.5 percent between 2020 and 2021. The UN estimated that approximately twelve million North Koreans — nearly half the population — faced food insecurity. Kim Jong-un made a rare public admission at a 2021 Workers’ Party meeting that the “people’s food situation is now getting tense,” the North Korean equivalent of an emergency declaration.

And yet he did not reopen the border. He kept it closed for three years.

The question of why — why a leader who acknowledged his people were hungry chose to maintain a closure that he knew was causing that hunger — is answered not by public health logic but by political logic. The border closure was not primarily a COVID response. It was the COVID response’s cover.

The Market Destruction

Simultaneously with the border closure, the regime moved against the jangmadang with a thoroughness that the pandemic restrictions made politically possible in ways that ordinary peacetime governance would not have permitted.

Approximately ninety percent of North Korea’s markets were ordered closed in the early pandemic period. The public health justification was straightforward — markets were gathering places where people could spread the virus. The political benefit was equally straightforward: markets were gathering places where people exchanged goods, information, foreign media, and the accumulated social knowledge that the state did not control. Closing the markets in the name of COVID was simultaneously closing them in the name of everything else Kim needed to close them for.

The crackdown on the informal economy was reinforced by legal architecture that had no connection to public health whatsoever. The Law on Rejecting Reactionary Ideology and Culture, enacted in 2020, criminalized the possession, distribution, and consumption of media from “hostile countries” — a category defined broadly enough to include any South Korean drama, any K-pop song, any film or television programme from the United States, Japan, or anywhere else the regime deemed ideologically contaminating. The penalties were calibrated to the offence: up to ten years of hard labour for possession, death for distribution. The death penalty provision was not theoretical.

A twenty-two-year-old farm worker in South Hwanghae Province was publicly executed in 2022. The charge: listening to seventy South Korean songs and three South Korean films, and sharing them with seven other people. He was dressed in a long white sack-like robe, hooded, and shot by three gunmen who each fired five rounds. Hundreds of residents were required to watch. In another case, two teenagers — aged sixteen and seventeen — were shot by firing squad on an airfield in the city of Hyesan, on the border with China, for watching and distributing South Korean films. A third teenager was executed alongside them for an unrelated crime. The assembled residents of Hyesan were brought to watch that too.

The executions were not only punishments. They were pedagogy.

The Information War

The Law on Rejecting Reactionary Ideology and Culture was supplemented in 2021 by the Youth Education Guarantee Act, which targeted the jangmadang generation specifically — North Koreans in their teens and twenties who had grown up watching South Korean dramas under blankets and trading USB drives at school, and who represented the most significant long-term threat to the regime’s ideological monopoly. The act required comprehensive ideological education programs aimed at eliminating “anti-socialist and non-socialist thought” among young people and binding them to total loyalty to Kim Jong-un personally.

In 2023, the regime added the Pyongyang Cultural Language Protection Act, which criminalized the use of South Korean speech patterns, slang, abbreviations, and nicknames — targeting not just the consumption of foreign content but its linguistic residue. North Koreans caught using South Korean expressions in their everyday speech faced ideological re-education. The grammar of the Korean language had become a political battleground.

The enforcement was systematic and pervasive. Defectors described random stops on the street in which young people’s mobile phones were inspected for South Korean content, South Korean language patterns in their text messages, and contacts whose names were stored in South Korean nickname conventions. If a phone’s keyboard showed evidence of South Korean input methods, the owner was detained. If a search history contained evidence of attempted foreign media access, the owner was detained. The physical surveillance of bodies — checking hairstyles for South Korean-influenced cuts, checking clothing for tight jeans or printed t-shirts associated with South Korean fashion, checking wedding ceremonies for the use of white dresses or the groom-carrying-the-bride customs that had spread from South Korean dramas — extended the state’s reach into the most intimate domains of daily life.

The HRW report that documented this crackdown gave it a precise and accurate name: “a sense of terror stronger than a bullet.” The metaphor is not rhetorical. When executions are public and random enough, when the targets are ordinary people caught doing ordinary things that millions of other ordinary people are also doing, the terror functions not as specific deterrence but as general atmosphere. You do not need to shoot everyone who has a USB drive. You need only shoot enough people in public that the fact of having a USB drive, for everyone who does, becomes a source of constant low-level dread. The terror is not the bullet. The terror is the awareness of the bullet — the knowledge that the bullet exists and that it can find you, carried in the body every day, reshaping behaviour at its most fundamental level.

The Apology and the Lie

In the summer of 2020, Kim Jong-un appeared on North Korean state television and wept. He apologized, with what appeared to be genuine emotion, for his failure to improve his people’s lives. “I am really sorry,” he said. “My efforts and sincerity have not been sufficient enough to rid our people of the difficulties in their life.”

The speech was widely analyzed in the West as a sign of vulnerability — a leader acknowledging weakness in a system built on the premise of infallibility. Some analysts saw it as evidence of genuine pressure within the regime. Others viewed it as a calculated performance, designed to pre-empt criticism by absorbing it within the leader’s own narrative.

Both readings missed something. The apology was real and the crackdown was real, and they were not in contradiction. Kim Jong-un was genuinely sorrowful about the material conditions his people faced — the food shortages, the medicine scarcity, the economic contraction. He was not sorrowful enough about those conditions to reopen the border that was causing them. The calculus was not cynical. It was clear-eyed. The short-term suffering caused by the border closure and the market crackdown was, in his assessment, a price worth paying for the long-term consolidation of control that those measures enabled.

Consider the analogy of a doctor who puts a patient through genuinely painful treatment. The pain is real. The doctor’s regret about the pain is real. Neither the pain nor the regret changes the doctor’s conviction that the treatment is necessary, or the decision to continue it. Kim Jong-un occupies an analogous position — one in which the suffering he observes and acknowledges does not alter the policy framework from which that suffering flows, because he has concluded, with what appears to be genuine conviction, that the alternative is worse. The alternative, in his frame, is a North Korea that loses ideological coherence, fragments under the pressure of foreign cultural influence, and becomes vulnerable to the kind of external subjugation that Korean history has repeatedly delivered. Against that outcome, the crackdown’s costs are bearable.

A state that had spent twenty-five years slowly losing its monopoly on its people’s economic and informational lives had found, in the pandemic, the conditions under which it could reassert that monopoly by force. The jangmadang generation had learned to survive without the state. Kim was teaching them, at considerable cost to both parties, that survival without the state’s permission was no longer possible.

This was not exclusively brutal calculation, even if calculation was present. Kim Jong-un is the product of a system that genuinely believes — or at least performs the belief with sufficient consistency that the distinction barely matters — that the Kim dynasty’s continued rule is identical with the Korean nation’s survival. In that framework, measures that appear to be cruelty are understood internally as necessity. The people suffer now so that the state endures. The state must endure because without it, the peninsula returns to the colonial vulnerability that the founding mythology exists to prevent. The wound that won’t close — the Japanese occupation, the American bombing, the frozen war — justifies everything, including the market closures, including the executions, including the three years of sealed borders and food shortages and people shot at the Yalu River for trying to find something to eat.

The ideology is not window dressing. It is the frame within which the cruelty becomes, in the minds of those who impose it, something other than cruelty.

What the Crackdown Achieved

By the time North Korea began cautiously reopening its border with China in late 2023, the crackdown had achieved several of its objectives.

The flow of defectors had been reduced to a trickle. The jangmadang were operating under substantially more state control than before the pandemic — prices were regulated, goods were monitored, the most egregious examples of private accumulation had been suppressed. The distribution of South Korean media, while not eliminated, had been driven deeper underground and made substantially more dangerous. The jangmadang generation, which had grown up with a casual relationship to foreign content, had been forcibly reminded that casualness was a luxury the state could revoke.

The mechanisms of reasserted control extended beyond the markets and the media. The pandemic restrictions gave the state a justification for reimposing internal travel restrictions that had been informally eroding for years — the system of travel permits required to move between provinces, which had been widely circumvented through bribery during the jangmadang era, was enforced again with genuine rigour. The bribery system itself was targeted: officials who had spent two decades collecting kickbacks from traders in exchange for looking the other way found that the pandemic enforcement culture made their traditional accommodations suddenly dangerous. A crackdown on corruption among local officials was not primarily about corruption. It was about closing the gap between the formal rules — which had always prohibited the markets and the foreign media — and the informal reality, in which those rules had been systematically purchased into irrelevance.

The North Korean won had depreciated sharply against the dollar — losing more than two-thirds of its value over the pandemic period — and rice prices had more than tripled. These were consequences of the border closure and market crackdown that represented real material hardship for real people: families who had depended on market income for most of their household budget found themselves without income and without the market to spend it in. The state’s reassertion of control had come at a price paid by the population, not the leadership.

But Kim Jong-un had also, in the years of the border closure, accomplished something outside North Korea’s borders that changed the strategic context entirely. While the pandemic gave him the tools to reimpose domestic control, the war in Ukraine was giving him something he had wanted for decades: a great power patron who needed him more than he needed it.

That story — the war dividend, the Russian partnership, the transformation of North Korea from isolated pariah to valued military supplier — is the subject of the next essay. The crackdown created the internal conditions for Kim’s ascent. Ukraine created the external ones.
