This morning Dior and I did our loop before most of the city had found its coffee. Nothing remarkable in that — we have been doing some version of it for three summers now, she tethered and trotting, I aboard a near-silent e-bike, the pair of us threading the same lakefront trails I wrote about last summer when my chief complaint was that nobody calls a pass anymore.

What is remarkable is the number on the screen. As of this Canada Day, Dior and I have ridden 7,698 kilometres together. I confess I sat on the porch and did the arithmetic twice, because it was telling me something I hadn’t quite believed until I saw it.

We have, the two of us, nearly ridden across Canada.

The number that stopped me

The Trans-Canada Highway, as the Canadian Encyclopedia will tell you, runs 7,821 kilometres from the Mile Zero monument at the foot of Douglas Street in Victoria to its eastern terminus in St. John’s, passing through all ten provinces on the way. It is, depending on how you count, the fourth-longest highway on earth and the second-longest national one. It was opened by John Diefenbaker at Rogers Pass in 1962, though — in a detail so Canadian it ought to be set to fiddle music — it wasn’t actually finished until 1971, and British Columbia’s premier boycotted the opening over money and held his own ceremony at the same spot a month early.

Seven thousand, eight hundred and twenty-one kilometres. I am a little over a hundred short of it. That leaves a handful of our morning loops — a week or so of porch-coffee arithmetic — and Dior and I will have run the distance from the Atlantic to the Pacific without once leaving the city of Toronto.

There is a delicious absurdity in this that I want to sit with, on the country’s birthday, for a moment.

The highway that skips us

Here is a fact that every Torontonian should be made to memorize, preferably while standing: Toronto is not on the Trans-Canada Highway. The planners of the 1950s, as the highway’s own historians cheerfully note, ran their great national ribbon of asphalt through Calgary and Regina and Winnipeg and Ottawa and Montreal — and routed it cleanly around the largest city in the country. We are connected to the nation’s spine the way a suburb is connected to a dinner party it wasn’t invited to.

So there is something fitting, on July the first, about completing that highway on a bicycle, on trails, in the one major city the highway declined to touch. Dior and I have traversed the Dominion by not going anywhere at all. We have driven across Canada, as the old Sun Country fellow once did in a Tesla Roadster from St. John’s to Victoria, except that we did it in a loop roughly seven hundred times, and my co-pilot has a tail.

Mile Zero to Mile Zero

The thing that gets me — the thing I keep turning over — is the symmetry of the endpoints. Victoria has its Mile Zero monument at Beacon Hill Park. St. John’s, not to be outdone, has claimed “Mile One” for its downtown stadium. Two small plaques at two edges of an enormous country, and between them very nearly eight thousand kilometres of rock and prairie and lake and the long winding misery of northern Ontario that long-haul truckers avoid by dipping into the United States.

Dior is five and a half now. This is our third summer. When we started she was a young dog learning the tether’s 180-degree swing — left of the bike, right of the bike, falling in behind when a stroller came the other way. She is, in highway terms, on Vancouver Island by now, the Malahat behind her and the Pacific almost in view. She does not know this. She knows that mornings are good, that the lake smells of itself, that I am slow and she is not, and that there is breakfast at the end. This is, I have come to think, the correct amount for a dog to know.

A word, on Canada Day, for the soon to be retired

I am 74 years old, and I want to say something to the people my age who are eyeing the door, or have already walked through it, and are quietly afraid of what waits on the other side.

The fear is always the same. What will I do with myself? Eighteen years I spent in the Office of the President at Rogers, until I got the boot — or the pass, perhaps; maybe that too was a Rogers Pass, my own small ceremony at the summit, no premier in attendance. A working life before that, and like everyone I wondered whether the calendar would simply go blank when the office was finally in the rear-view mirror. Whether retirement was a long grey afternoon you endured rather than a life you lived.

I am here to report, from the porch, that this fear is nonsense.

I have never in my life been busier. Dior and I run and ride the better part of two thousand kilometres a summer. I write — and I am not exaggerating for effect — something on the order of five essays a day, on phone bills and foreign wars and the slow strange unravelling of the republic next door, a furious daily output that would have been impossible when I had a job eating the daylight. There is a dog who needs the lake. There is a husband, a daughter, a son and a grandson, three boarders, a great-uncle’s diplomatic ghost wandering through my paragraphs. The days are not empty. The days are full — fuller, I think, than they have ever been.

This is the part nobody tells you, so I will tell you. The office was never the point. The office was the toll you paid to get here. Here is the morning loop and the empty page and the dog already at the door before you’ve found your shoes. Retirement is not the end of the useful life. For a great many of us it is the first time the useful life is entirely our own.

What the distance means

I did not set out three summers ago to ride across Canada. I set out to get the dog exercised and myself out of the house, and the kilometres accumulated the way kilometres do — fourteen here, twenty there, a number quietly climbing on a screen I rarely looked at. That is how most large things get done, I have noticed. Not in a grand departure from Mile Zero with the press assembled, but in a thousand unremarkable mornings, each one fourteen kilometres long.

There is a national lesson in that, this being Canada Day and my being constitutionally unable to leave a lesson unstated. The Trans-Canada itself was built that way — Saskatchewan first, in 1957, then the rest grumbling along province by province, a stretch of Newfoundland not paved until 1965, the whole thing not truly complete until 1971. Twenty-two years from the Act to the finish. We are not, as a country, a people of grand gestures. We are a people who get there eventually, one cold morning at a time, complaining mildly the whole way. It is not a bad way to build a highway. It is not a bad way to spend a retirement, either.

So Dior and I will reach Victoria sometime within the week or so — on the trail, on the lake, on the loop the highway never bothered to include. I’ll know the morning it happens because I’ll do the arithmetic on the porch and the number will tick past 7,821. I may pour something stronger than coffee. Dior will get an extra biscuit and no explanation.

And then we’ll turn around the next morning and start back, because westbound is just eastbound with the lake on the other side. There is always more country, and there are always more mornings, and the dog is always ready before I am.

And so today the household has two things to toast, and I intend to toast both. There is the country, 158 years old and wearing it well, give or take a constitutional crisis or two it has weathered before and will weather again. And there is a small private milestone that no one will mark but us: a man and his dog, three summers and 7,698 kilometres into a journey neither of them quite meant to take, now within a week of having run the whole width of the nation on a single loop of lakefront trail. One of these achievements will be celebrated with fireworks over the harbour. The other will be celebrated with a biscuit and a glass of something on the porch. I leave it to you to decide which of us earned it more — though I will note, for the record, that only one of us did the running.

Happy Canada Day. Get the dog. Get out there. The kilometres add up — and so, it turns out, do the years, in the very best way.
