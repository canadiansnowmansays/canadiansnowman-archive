On Wednesday, October 28, Mostafa Najafi, an Iranian analyst said to be close to the security chief Mohsen Rezaei, set a deadline in public. Washington wanted a deal before its midterm elections, he wrote, and wanted nuclear concessions as part of it, which Tehran would not give. “If no agreement is reached in the next two days, the atmosphere will likely shift toward confrontation in the first week after the elections.”

The two days ran out on Friday without a deal. On Saturday, October 31, Iran’s supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, posted that he had met his senior commanders and ordered them to answer “any kind or level of threat” immediately, to establish “maximum deterrence,” and to be ready to strike every kind of American and Israeli asset, as Yeganeh Torbati reported for the New York Times. Readers of this diary have followed his silence since Part XCVII. This is the first public order in his own voice that the diary has recorded, and it is about war.

In Part XCIX, five weeks ago, this diary wrote that the war had acquired an election calendar. In Part CIII, the American and Israeli militaries’ plans for the week after the vote came into view. Now Tehran has adopted the same timetable. Washington has two plans for that week: a letter proposing talks and a list of targets. The President is also asking his friends whether the men who promised him a short war should keep their jobs.

Americans vote tomorrow. Each side has said what it intends to do in the days after.

The Fall Guy

On Sunday, November 1, Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire of The Atlantic reported what the White House has started to accept. The President is “unlikely to pull out another remarkable political comeback in the midterms. The House appears gone, and the Senate may be too.” He believes the Iran war is at least partly to blame, and he is “searching for a scapegoat.” According to two outside advisers close to him, he has been asking friends about the performance of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and General Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs. They are the two advisers who were most optimistic about the war at the start, and neither has held a press conference since May.

“Hegseth is good at telling the president what he wants to hear,” one adviser told them. A Pentagon spokesman called any suggestion that Hegseth had not given the full picture “dead wrong.” The White House said the President has “full confidence in both.”

Hegseth’s answer has been to hunt for leakers. Last week he fired Lieutenant General Brett Sylvia, the Joint Staff’s director of planning, after what officials described as failed polygraph tests in an investigation into leaks, including leaks about the munitions shortage the war has caused. The Joint Staff first thanked Sylvia for “32 years of distinguished service.” Hours later it withdrew the statement, saying it “did not appropriately reflect the full circumstances.” Hegseth has now fired or forced out more than two dozen generals and admirals. Earlier this year, at Camp David, he reportedly blamed his own deputy for the munitions shortfall. Some Pentagon officials suspect the Sylvia firing was meant to weaken the Joint Staff while the President decides who is responsible for the war.

Last week Hegseth, Caine and Admiral Brad Cooper of Central Command brought the President and Vice-President Vance a set of options “designed to create the conditions for Trump to declare victory.” Strikes could last hours, days or several weeks. A long campaign could go back to Iran’s air defences and hit infrastructure, which the administration would describe as serving both civilian and military purposes. The reporters note that civilians would be at risk and that the strikes could breach international humanitarian law. Another option is a days-long campaign against the nuclear programme. The President could also keep the blockade in place and leave the threat unspoken, or he could walk away.

“If those strikes don’t work, you’ve now sort of exposed the limits of those strikes,” said Michael Cohen of the Stimson Center. “You keep hitting them and they don’t back down. So what are you going to keep doing? Keep escalating?” Senator Chris Murphy, who has been briefed on parts of the plans, called them “insane.”

Three details from the piece matter here. First, none of the options deals with the Houthis, although eliminating Iran’s proxies was one of the President’s stated war aims and the Houthis are now attacking Saudi Arabia almost daily. Second, one official said the President’s recent turn against Ukraine was partly meant to get more Russian energy onto world markets to offset the oil shock from any new strikes. Third, the reporters recall a precedent. After Republicans lost both houses in the 2006 midterms, largely over Iraq, President Bush fired Donald Rumsfeld. Two months later he ordered the surge.

The President has not given the order. If he does, the reporters note, he may keep it to himself until the last moment, as he did in February.

The Letter

The administration has also opened a second track.

Last week, as Farnaz Fassihi and Tyler Pager reported for the New York Times, Vice-President Vance wrote to Rezaei through Qatari mediators, and the letter was delivered on Sunday, October 25. The President read and approved it. It proposes direct talks, led by Vance, with Iran’s nuclear programme on the table from the start, possibly as soon as this week in Qatar or Turkey. Vance did not write to President Pezeshkian. He wrote to the former commander of the Revolutionary Guards, because American officials had concluded that only someone with influence over Iran’s hard-liners could deliver a deal. Vance is also, as The Atlantic reported last month, the senior official who privately opposed joining the war.

Tehran has responded positively. On Saturday, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the letter “a positive step.” A Qatari delegation arrived in Tehran on Friday, and Iranian diplomats were in Doha on Saturday. Araghchi said Iran was “reviewing proposals on its nuclear program” and was ready to carry out its own seven-day plan “in even less than a week.”

The two sides are still far apart. Najafi listed Iran’s conditions for Torbati: lift the blockade and the oil sanctions, end the war on every front including Lebanon, release frozen assets, and accept Iranian management of the Strait of Hormuz. All of these would come before any substantive talks. A week earlier, as Erika Solomon, Vivian Nereim and Adam Rasgon reported for the New York Times, Qatar had been negotiating a five-day sequence of concessions in which Iran would reopen the strait and America would lift the blockade. It stalled over who would move first, and over Rezaei’s promise that “not a single particle of enriched nuclear material” would leave Iran. Mehrzad Boroujerdi of Missouri University of Science and Technology expects Tehran to follow “a dual-track strategy, engaging in intermittent diplomatic negotiations while simultaneously preparing, to the best of its ability, for a possible resumption of hostilities.”

Washington is doing the same thing. The mediators are pressing for a meeting later this week, around the time The Atlantic expects the President to decide whether to strike. The talks and the strike plan point to the same days.

Fairness requires this diary to say that the letter is real diplomacy. A vice-president writing directly to the man in Tehran most able to deliver a deal is the most serious American offer in months. It may also be the last thing standing between the region and a third round of war. The diary hopes it works. It also notes that the President’s own advisers have described the strike options as ways to “declare victory,” and the letter, so far, as a way to apply pressure.

Maximum Deterrence

Tehran is preparing as well as negotiating. “They have been expecting a new round of war already for a few weeks, and they’ve been preparing accordingly,” Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group told Torbati. Readers will know Azizi from the toll-booth thesis this diary has used since the summer. He now describes a deeper change. “The Islamic Republic’s strategic thinking has undergone a significant shift, involving, first and foremost, a greater willingness to take risks and a move away from its long-established policy of strategic patience.” Iran, he says, could now answer even covert Israeli operations “through direct military action.”

Khamenei’s order on Saturday makes that the official position. It follows threats this diary recorded in Part CIII: against the region’s data centres, against seven named undersea cables in the Strait of Hormuz, and against Saudi refineries, chosen because higher oil prices “will hurt the American people.” Najafi does not expect any deal to hold. “Even if Iran were to agree to nuclear negotiations and make concessions, it is highly unlikely that this tangled web of conflict between the two nations will reach a meaningful resolution or result in a lasting agreement.”

The Caretaker

Israel will go into the week after the American vote with a caretaker prime minister who campaigned on fear of annihilation.

The final count, as the New York Times reported on Thursday, gave Netanyahu’s alliance 53 seats and Gadi Eisenkot’s bloc 48, with the Arab parties holding 19. Neither bloc can govern without crossing a line it has sworn not to cross. The Israel/Palestine series takes up that question this week. For this diary, what matters is that Netanyahu stays in office until a new government is sworn in, and no Israeli government has taken office less than 50 days after an election. His caretaker period will cover the whole of the American strike window.

His conduct before the vote is now on the record. Three senior Israeli defence officials and three other people briefed on the matter told David M. Halbfinger, Ronen Bergman and their colleagues at the New York Times that Netanyahu spent weeks pushing for a new strike on Iran before polling day. The military’s chief of staff, Lieutenant General Eyal Zamir, found “no immediate or urgent need.” Zamir then became concerned that Netanyahu was pressing for “a separate action against Iran, short of an overt strike,” which he judged could provoke an Iranian attack on Israel. The same officials warned that a war after the election could prevent the opposition from forming a government within the legal deadline, or push it into a unity government that keeps Netanyahu in office. Avigdor Lieberman has asked the attorney general to investigate the prime minister for treason.

European analysts reached the same conclusion, as Steven Erlanger reported for the New York Times. “He will have greater incentive to escalate, whether with Iran after the U.S. midterms on Nov. 3, or in the West Bank or Gaza,” said Beth Oppenheim of the European Council on Foreign Relations. Sanam Vakil of Chatham House said: “The ticking clock of a war with Iran is speeding up and seems all but inevitable. It won’t accomplish anything, but it could convince all the parties it’s time to make a deal.”

Tehran does not expect a change of prime minister to change much. “Iranian and Israeli policies toward each other have remained consistent regardless of which government has been in power in Israel since the 1980s,” Mohammad Saleh Sedghian of the Arab Center for Iranian Studies told Torbati. “Any changes would likely be temporary rather than strategic.”

Everyone Else

Part CIII described how everyone else in the region stopped waiting for Washington. This week the list grew to include a country that would have been unthinkable two years ago.

On Saturday, at the Manama Dialogue in Bahrain, Syria’s foreign minister, Asaad al-Shaibani, said “discussions are currently underway to evaluate Syria’s entry into the Mecca alliance,” as Euan Ward and Vivian Nereim reported for the New York Times. Under Bashar al-Assad, Syria was Iran’s closest Arab ally. Under Ahmed al-Sharaa, it is considering a mutual-defence pact with Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan against Iran’s proxy in Yemen. “Syria will stand beside all of the Gulf countries,” al-Shaibani said, against “militias that cross borders.” The pact’s members have agreed to prepare for the rapid deployment of forces to the kingdom. Egypt, which is not a member, formally authorised military aid this week. A week earlier the pact’s leaders had flown to Tehran together, the Saudi defence minister alongside Pakistan’s army chief and Turkey’s intelligence chief, to urge Iran to be flexible. The region is preparing for war and pressing for a deal at the same time, as Washington and Tehran are.

The fighting in Yemen continues. The World Health Organization now counts at least 1,677 people killed and more than 7,300 wounded since August. The United Nations says nearly 300,000 have been displaced. On Saturday the Houthi authorities said a Saudi airstrike on a grocery store in Taiz province wounded six people, three of them children. The same day, the UN announced that Mohammad el Shami, a Yemeni employee of its human rights office held by the Houthis since June 2024, had died in custody. Inside Saudi Arabia, the Saudi-led coalition counts at least 19 people killed by Houthi strikes. The Times reported on October 22 that at least 22 civilians had been killed. The figures come from different sources, and this diary gives both.

None of the President’s options, The Atlantic reports, deals with any of this.

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude passed $112 on Thursday, October 22, its highest since mid-May, as reports of the strike plans spread. Every new option the President is considering would push it higher, which is why, according to an official quoted by The Atlantic, his policy toward Ukraine is now being shaped partly by the need for Russian oil. The Pentagon’s last public estimate of the war’s cost was $43.6 billion through September 3. Munitions stocks were “dangerously low” at the height of the fighting, and any new campaign “would further deplete” them. A New York Times/Siena poll finds 65 per cent of American voters disapprove of the President’s handling of the war. The Congress elected tomorrow will inherit the bill.

The Numbers

  • One: day to the American midterm elections.

  • Two: days in Najafi’s deadline for a deal, which expired on Friday.

  • Hours, days or weeks: the lengths of the strike options presented to the President last week.

  • Seven: Iran’s conditions for substantive talks.

  • More than two dozen: generals and admirals Hegseth has fired or forced out.

  • 32: years of service in the Joint Staff’s first statement on General Sylvia, withdrawn hours later.

  • 53, 48 and 19: seats won by Netanyahu’s alliance, Eisenkot’s bloc and the Arab parties.

  • 50: the fewest days after an election in which an Israeli government has ever been sworn in.

  • 1,677: people killed in Yemen since August, by the WHO’s count.

  • Nearly 300,000: Yemenis displaced.

  • 65 per cent: of American voters who disapprove of the war.

  • 2006: the year a president lost the midterms over a war, fired his defence secretary, and then sent more troops.
