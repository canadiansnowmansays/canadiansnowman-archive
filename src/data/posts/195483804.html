Red Square, Moscow. Russia possesses more land than any nation on Earth, vast natural resources, and one of history’s richest scientific and literary traditions. A nation that had everything — and chose to squander it

Underperformer #2

Russia possesses more land than any nation on Earth — 17.1 million square kilometres spanning eleven time zones from the Baltic to the Pacific. It has enormous reserves of natural gas, oil, minerals, timber, and fresh water. Its scientific tradition produced Mendeleev, Pavlov, Landau, Sakharov, and the teams that launched humanity into space. Its literary tradition produced Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Chekhov, and Solzhenitsyn. Its mathematical and engineering talent remains world-class — Russian programmers, physicists, and mathematicians are recruited by the world’s leading technology companies and research institutions.

This is a nation that had everything. Everything.

In 2025, Russia became the least peaceful nation on Earth.

The wasted inheritance: a nation that had everything it needed to be extraordinary, and chose to burn it.

Russia’s extraordinary scientific and cultural inheritance — from space exploration to world literature — stands in stark contrast to its current trajectory.

The Scorecard

The Analysis

Human Development: 19 out of 50

Life expectancy in Russia has been declining — a phenomenon virtually unique among nations not experiencing active famine or epidemic. Russian male life expectancy is approximately 67 years, more than a decade lower than the EU average. The healthcare system has deteriorated, particularly outside Moscow and St. Petersburg, with rural facilities understaffed, underfunded, and increasingly unable to provide basic care. Alcoholism and its consequences remain a public health catastrophe — contributing to rates of liver disease, cardiovascular failure, and accidental death that would be classified as a national emergency in any nation whose government was paying attention. The war in Ukraine has accelerated the health crisis, diverting medical resources, producing tens of thousands of casualties that strain an already fragile system, and driving a brain drain of medical professionals who have fled the country.

The war in Ukraine has accelerated Russia’s decline — draining resources, lives, and the country’s remaining human capital.

Education scores a 6 — the highest score on Russia’s human development card, reflecting a genuine legacy. Russian mathematical, scientific, and engineering education has historically been world-class, and the institutional infrastructure — while degraded — still produces talent that is sought globally. But academic freedom has been gutted. Universities that challenge state narratives face funding cuts and political pressure. Researchers who speak against the war face dismissal or prosecution. The talent pipeline that Russia’s educational system once fed into its own institutions now flows overwhelmingly outward — to Europe, North America, and Israel — as the regime drives its most capable citizens away.

Democratic governance earns a 1. Russia is a personalist autocracy dressed in the costume of democratic institutions. Elections are theatrical exercises in which the outcome is predetermined, opposition candidates are imprisoned or barred from running, and independent media has been effectively eliminated. Alexei Navalny, the most prominent opposition leader, died in a prison colony in 2024 under circumstances that the international community has not accepted as natural. Independent civil society has been systematically dismantled through “foreign agent” legislation that effectively criminalises any organisation receiving international funding or expressing views at odds with the state.

A personalist autocracy and kleptocratic system where power and wealth are deeply entangled, and opposition is systematically eliminated.

Gender equality and minority rights earn a 3. Domestic violence was partially decriminalized in 2017. LGBTQ+ rights are under sustained assault, with “propaganda” laws effectively criminalizing any public expression of non-heterosexual identity. Ethnic minorities in Chechnya and other regions face repression. The war in Ukraine has been prosecuted in part through the disproportionate conscription of ethnic minority populations from Dagestan, Buryatia, and other peripheral regions — a pattern that echoes colonial-era practices of sending subject peoples to die in the metropole’s wars.

Environmental Stewardship: 20 out of 50

Russia’s economy runs on fossil fuel exports — oil and natural gas constitute the overwhelming majority of export revenues and a significant share of government income. Almost no meaningful transition to renewable energy is underway. The renewable energy share earns a 3 — wind and solar combined represent a negligible fraction of Russia’s energy mix, and the war economy has eliminated whatever minimal momentum existed toward diversification.

Emissions per capita earn a 3. Russian per capita emissions are high — roughly 12 metric tons — driven by an energy-intensive industrial base, vast transportation distances, cold climate, and an economy that has no incentive to decarbonise because its revenue model depends on selling the carbon.

Russia’s Siberian forests represent enormous natural capital and act as a major global carbon sink — preserved more by geographic vastness than by deliberate stewardship

Biodiversity and habitat protection earn a 5 — and this score deserves honest explanation. Russia’s vast Siberian forests, tundra, and freshwater ecosystems represent some of the most significant natural capital on Earth. These ecosystems are largely intact not because of environmental policy but because of the sheer scale of Russian territory and the impossibility of exploiting it all. The forests sequester enormous amounts of carbon. The lakes and rivers contain a significant portion of the world’s freshwater. These are genuine environmental assets. They are also, in the context of Russian governance, largely unintentional — the product of geographic vastness rather than stewardship.

Water and air quality earn a 4. Russian industrial cities suffer from chronic pollution. Legacy contamination from Soviet-era industrial and nuclear activities persists across vast areas. The Norilsk nickel smelting complex, one of the most polluted industrial sites on Earth, represents in miniature what Russian industrial policy has produced across the country: extraction without remediation, profit without accountability, and environmental damage that will persist for generations.

Systemic Behaviour: 8 out of 50

The lowest systemic behaviour score in the entire series.

Arms exports and conflict contribution earn a 1. Russia invaded Ukraine — a sovereign European nation with which it shares a border and a deeply intertwined history — in February 2022, beginning the largest land war in Europe since 1945. The invasion has killed hundreds of thousands — soldiers and civilians on both sides — displaced millions, destroyed cities, and produced a humanitarian crisis that reverberates across Europe and beyond. Russia has deployed forces in Syria, where its air campaign has been documented as deliberately targeting hospitals and civilian infrastructure. It has projected military power across Africa through the Wagner Group (now rebranded), offering mercenary services to authoritarian regimes in exchange for mining concessions and political influence. Its arms exports, while declining due to sanctions and the demands of the Ukraine war, have historically fuelled conflicts worldwide with no regard for human rights or humanitarian law.

Adherence to international law earns a 1. The invasion of Ukraine alone would merit this score — it is the most flagrant violation of the UN Charter’s prohibition on aggressive war since the Charter was written. Add the annexation of Crimea in 2014, the violation of the Budapest Memorandum (under which Russia guaranteed Ukraine’s territorial integrity in exchange for nuclear disarmament), the use of chemical weapons on foreign soil (the Skripal poisoning in Salisbury, England), the shooting down of Malaysia Airlines Flight 17 over eastern Ukraine with a Russian-supplied missile, and the systematic undermining of every arms control treaty the Soviet Union and Russia had signed — and the score is not merely a 1. It is a 1 that strains against the floor.

Tax haven and corruption facilitation earn a 2. Russia is not merely a corrupt state — it is a kleptocracy that has turned corruption into a system of governance. The oligarchic wealth that flowed out of Russia in the 1990s and 2000s — laundered through London, Cyprus, Dubai, and Swiss banks — represents one of the largest transfers of national wealth into private hands in history. Russia exports corruption as much as it exports gas.

Refugee and humanitarian contribution earn a 2. Russia has produced millions of refugees through its military actions — from Syria to Ukraine — and accepted negligible numbers from crises it did not create. Exploitation earns a 2. Russia’s engagement with developing nations, particularly in Africa, is overwhelmingly extractive — Wagner Group mercenaries in exchange for mining rights is the operating model.

Before the invasion of Ukraine, Russian oligarchic philanthropy maintained a thin veneer of civic contribution — the Potanin Foundation funded arts and education, Usmanov invested in cultural institutions. The war stripped that veneer away. Russian oligarchic wealth was always entangled with the Kremlin — the philanthropy existed at the sufferance of the regime, and the regime has now redirected all available resources toward its war machine. Whatever philanthropic intentions existed have been subsumed by sanctions, asset seizures, and the insatiable appetite of a war economy. Philanthropy requires a functioning civil society. Russia destroyed its own.

The Neighbour Test: 6 out of 50

The lowest Neighbour Test score in the series. By a significant margin.

Every sub-parameter earns a 1 except long-term stewardship, which earns a 2 — a grudging acknowledgment that Russia’s vast Siberian forests, however unintentionally, serve as a significant global carbon sink.

Multilateral orientation earns a 1. Russia does not engage with the multilateral system. It weaponises it. It uses its UN Security Council veto to shield itself and its allies from accountability. It has withdrawn from or violated virtually every major arms control treaty. It deploys disinformation at industrial scale to undermine democratic institutions in other nations. It conducts cyberattacks against critical infrastructure worldwide. Russia’s relationship with the multilateral system is that of a saboteur, not a participant.

Response to global crises earns a 1. Russia is not responding to global crises. It is producing them. The Ukraine war, the Syrian intervention, the destabilisation of African nations through Wagner Group operations, the weaponisation of energy supplies to Europe — Russia’s contribution to global crises is measured not in humanitarian aid but in body counts.

Treatment of allies earns a 1. Russia has invaded or occupied territory in Georgia (2008), Ukraine (2014 and 2022), and Moldova (Transnistria, ongoing). It threatens the Baltic states with military exercises and cyberattacks. It poisons dissidents on foreign soil — including in the United Kingdom, a fellow permanent member of the Security Council. It conducts assassination operations in European capitals. Russia does not have allies. It has hostages, vassals, and targets.

Consistency of values versus interests earns a 1. A nation that claims to defend sovereignty while invading sovereign nations. That claims to fight fascism while practising authoritarianism. That claims to protect Russian-speakers abroad while sending Russian-speakers at home to die in a war of conquest. That claims to champion a multipolar world while demanding that its own sphere of influence be treated as inviolable. The gap between Russian rhetoric and Russian reality is not a gap. It is a chasm — and it is a chasm filled with the bodies of the deceived.

This is not the score of a nation that has failed to be a good neighbour. It is the score of a nation that has chosen, with deliberate and sustained malice, to be the worst neighbour on Earth.

Russia scores 53 out of 200 — second-lowest in the entire series. No nation has wasted more raw potential with more deliberate malice.

The Editorial Conclusion

Russia scores 53 out of 200 — the second-ranked underperformer and the lowest total score in the entire series. Lower than Saudi Arabia. Lower than China. Lower than the United States — whose score, while catastrophic relative to its capacity, still reflects a nation where democratic institutions exist, even if they are under assault, and where civil society functions, even if it is embattled.

Russia’s score of 53 reflects a nation where none of that is true. Where the institutions are hollow, the civil society is destroyed, the economy serves the war machine, and the talent that once made Russia extraordinary is fleeing as fast as the borders permit.

The wasted inheritance. Tolstoy’s country. Sakharov’s country. Mendeleev’s country. A nation that gave the world some of its greatest literature, science, music, and mathematics — and that is now, in 2026, the least peaceful country on the planet, governed by a kleptocrat, waging a war of aggression against its neighbour, and watching its best and brightest leave for nations that still value what Russia has chosen to destroy.

No nation in this series has squandered more raw potential with more deliberate malice. Russia did not fail because it lacked resources, talent, or opportunity. It failed because its leadership chose — over decades, with increasing aggression — to convert those assets into weapons of domination rather than tools of contribution.

Only one nation ranks higher on the underperformer list — not because its score is lower, but because its capacity is so much greater that the gap between what it has and what it gives is even wider than Russia’s.
