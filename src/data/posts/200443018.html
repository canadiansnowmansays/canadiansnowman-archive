The CBS Broadcast Center in New York. Once home to Murrow, Cronkite, and a trusted American news institution, now the site of rapid ideological transformation under new ownership.

Let us be direct about what has happened at CBS News, because the facts are not in serious dispute even if the outrage they deserve has not materialised.

A billionaire’s son acquired one of America’s most storied news institutions, installed an ideologically aligned editor with no broadcast experience and a documented hostility to the media tradition she was hired to lead, settled a politically motivated lawsuit against a sitting president at a cost calibrated to secure regulatory approval of the acquisition itself, cancelled the network’s highest-rated late-night programme within days of its host calling the settlement what it appeared to be, fired the executive producer and president of CBS News when they refused to bend, purged five senior 60 Minutes journalists and producers, shut down a ninety-nine-year-old radio news service that had begun broadcasting the same year Charles Lindbergh crossed the Atlantic, replaced the anchor of the CBS Evening News with a figure whose ratings have since achieved historic lows across every measurable demographic, and fired the network’s most decorated active correspondent for saying, in a staff meeting, that all of the above amounted to the murder of a news institution.

The network’s bias rating, according to independent assessors at Media Bias/Fact Check, has been formally reclassified from its historical left-centre orientation to Right-Centre — the first time in CBS News history. The same analysis flagged “emerging evidence that CBS News is censoring content unfavorable to the government,” downgrading its credibility rating accordingly. One veteran television news executive, speaking to the media newsletter Status, summarised the direction succinctly: CBS News is becoming “state-run media for Trump.”

And the American public has responded with something close to a collective shrug.

The question that demands an honest answer is: why?

The Nature of the Capture

What has happened at CBS is not simply a shift in editorial tone, the kind of drift that happens at any news organisation over time as leadership changes and priorities evolve. It is something more deliberate and more structurally significant — what media scholars are calling ideological capture, the systematic replacement of institutional editorial values with the political preferences of ownership.

David Ellison, whose Skydance acquisition of Paramount placed CBS News under new control aligned with Trump administration priorities.

The mechanism is not difficult to follow. David Ellison is the son of Larry Ellison, the Oracle co-founder and vocal Trump supporter whose company now controls TikTok’s American operations. David Ellison needed the Trump administration’s FCC to approve his $8.4 billion acquisition of Paramount. The FCC, under Trump appointee Brendan Carr, had signalled that the 60 Minutes Harris interview would be a factor in its review. The settlement removed that obstacle. The merger was approved. Carr announced that the deal included an “unprecedented promise” that CBS News would “root out bias that has undermined trust” — a formulation that, in its context, meant bias against the current administration.

The FCC chairman was, in other words, announcing as a condition of regulatory approval that a major American news division would henceforth conduct itself in a manner acceptable to the sitting government. Professor Victor Pickard of the University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg School told AFP that the entire Ellison approach at CBS was “primarily to appease or curry favour with the Trump administration in anticipation for an aspiring acquisition of Warner Brothers Discovery” — the next target.

This is not an accident or a drift. It is a strategy, and it is working. Secretary of Defence Pete Hegseth, at a press conference in March 2026, complained about CNN’s coverage of the Iran war before adding that he was looking forward to the day “David Ellison takes over that network.”

The consolidation is not finished.

What the Ratings Tell You

There is a certain grim satisfaction available in the observation that the audience, at least, is not playing along. The ratings under Bari Weiss’s CBS have been historically catastrophic.

The CBS Evening News under Tony Dokoupil — Weiss’s chosen anchor, promoted from the morning show in January 2026 as her signature programming overhaul — has posted its lowest numbers in a generation. By April, the broadcast was regularly falling below 3.9 million viewers, its worst monthly total since Dokoupil took the chair, hitting a low of 3.5 million in a single week. In the 25-54 demographic that Weiss specifically set out to capture, the show recorded its lowest April figures this century, down eleven percent from its own prior average — a steeper drop than either ABC or NBC suffered during the same period.

CBS Mornings recorded its worst ratings on record in the first quarter of 2026. The network trailed ABC’s World News Tonight by more than four million viewers on a typical night — ABC at 8.5 million, CBS at 3.8.

A veteran television news executive, quoted anonymously in Status, was unsparing: “Bari believed it couldn’t get any worse at CBS News and that everything needed to be blown up to turn it around. Guess what? Wrong again. It actually can get worse.”

Bari Weiss, installed as editor with no prior broadcast experience, overseeing the network’s shift from left-centre to right-centre orientation.

The audience, it turns out, watches journalism differently from the way it watches entertainment. Viewers who tuned in to CBS News for decades because they trusted the institution did not find that trust transferred to the replacement institution. Many simply stopped watching. They were not cancelling subscriptions in protest — they were just changing the channel, the way audiences have always registered displeasure with journalism they have lost confidence in.

This is not nothing. It is a market verdict on the Weiss experiment, delivered quietly and consistently every evening across millions of American households. But it is not the same as political accountability. Ellison doesn’t need CBS Evening News to be a ratings success. He needs it to be politically useful. Those are different requirements, and at the moment, only one of them is being tracked.

The Silence and Its Causes

The absence of sustained, organised public outrage over what has happened to CBS News is real, and it has multiple causes operating simultaneously.

The first is the most basic: most people don’t know. The destruction of CBS News’s editorial independence has been covered extensively in media newsletters, in the trade press, in columns at The Atlantic and The Nation and outlets that follow journalism closely. But it has not broken through to the mass consciousness in the way that a dramatic incident — a sudden firing, a street confrontation, a viral clip — would. It has accumulated, piece by piece, decision by decision, across thirteen months, in the inside pages of a story that most people encounter only as background noise. The firing of Scott Pelley received widespread coverage for a news cycle. The shutdown of CBS News Radio received a day. The dismissal of Bill Owens received a week. Each event competed with everything else happening in an administration that generates crises faster than any single one can be adequately processed.

The second cause is the normalisation problem. CBS is not the only institution that has capitulated. The Los Angeles Times’ new ownership moved it rightward before Weiss arrived at CBS. The Washington Post pulled a presidential endorsement at the last moment, to the audible fury of its own journalists. Disney suspended Kimmel, briefly. Meta ended its fact-checking programme. ABC’s parent company has its own FCC dependencies. The pattern is so widespread that it becomes, paradoxically, harder to hold any single institution accountable for it. When everything moves together, the movement feels like weather rather than decision.

The third cause is exhaustion of the specific kind produced by authoritarian environments. When the targets of political pressure are institutions rather than individuals — when the victim is “CBS News” rather than a named person you can follow on social media and identify with — the emotional engine of outrage runs on less fuel. People are angry about a great many things simultaneously. The capacity for sustained, organised protest is finite. And CBS is not, it must be said, a sympathetic victim in the same way that a fired individual is. It is a corporation that chose to settle, chose to hire Weiss, chose to cancel Colbert. That the choices were coerced does not make the institution entirely innocent, and the ambiguity of its culpability makes it harder to mobilise around.

The fourth cause is the one that Kimmel identified, slightly differently than he intended: the absence of a lever. What would a boycott of CBS even look like? You can cancel Paramount+, if you have it. You can not watch the network. But the network’s revenue comes primarily from advertising and from its relationship with affiliates, not from your direct subscription. The diffuse, non-transactional relationship between a broadcast network and its audience means that public displeasure registers as ratings decline, not as the acute financial pain that Disney felt when 1.7 million Disney+ cancellations landed in a single week. Ratings decline takes quarters to monetise. Disney+ cancellations take days.

And the fifth cause is the one that nobody quite wants to name: the left is tired. Two years into a second Trump term, living through simultaneous crises in democracy, civil liberties, geopolitical stability, and economic security, the progressive audience that would most naturally protest what has happened at CBS is already stretched thin across a dozen fronts. The cognitive and emotional bandwidth required to sustain outrage about media consolidation — a structural problem that operates slowly and invisibly — is genuinely difficult to spare when the immediate assaults on democratic institutions feel more acute and more reversible.

Why It Matters Anyway

None of these explanations make the silence acceptable. They explain it; they do not excuse it.

What has happened at CBS is not a side story. It is a central chapter in the project of democratic erosion that characterises this moment in American life. The network that housed Murrow and Cronkite and Hewitt and Letterman and Colbert was not merely a television brand. It was, for most of its existence, an expression of a principle — that a free press, accountable to the public rather than to power, was a structural requirement of self-government. That principle is now being replaced, at CBS, with the opposite principle: that a press accountable to its billionaire owners, and through them to the government whose regulatory approval those owners require, is the new normal.

The Roosevelt Institute’s comprehensive analysis of the American media landscape published in January 2026 put it plainly: “Even before Trump’s presidency, the US media landscape was vulnerable to authoritarian capture due to extreme consolidation.” The CBS story is not an aberration. It is the proof of concept for a playbook being deployed across the industry — acquire the outlet, install ideologically aligned leadership, settle politically, remove dissent, reclassify the editorial standards.

The FCC chairman has already telegraphed that CNN is next.

Murrow warned about something like this in 1958, in a speech to the Radio Television News Directors Association that remains one of the most quoted indictments of commercial media ever delivered. Television, he said, could be “used to illuminate and inspire,” or it could simply be “wires and lights in a box.” The determining factor was whether those who controlled it were willing to accept the obligations that came with using the public airwaves.

He was talking about entertainment crowding out journalism. He was not wrong about that. But he could not quite have imagined a scenario in which the journalism itself — the thing he and Cronkite had built, the institution that had held itself to the standard he set — would be quietly, efficiently, and profitably reconfigured as an instrument of the government it was built to scrutinise.

Dan Rather, in a statement when CBS News Radio signed off for the last time, called it “a national institution” that had “helped hold the country together.”

Nobody held a vigil. Nobody blockaded the building. Nobody trended a hashtag for longer than a news cycle.

The silence is not indifference. It is the sound of a public that has been so systematically overwhelmed — by the pace of events, by the scale of the losses, by the vertigo of living through institutional collapse on multiple fronts simultaneously — that it has lost the capacity to grieve each one adequately before the next arrives.

That is itself a political outcome, and it was not achieved by accident.
