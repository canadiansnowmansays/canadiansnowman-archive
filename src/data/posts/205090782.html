Stephen Gordon

The United States turns two hundred and fifty this weekend, and I have been trying to find the single number that explains what has happened to it.

This is, you will appreciate, an occupational hazard. I count things for a living now — essays, mostly, but the habit does not switch off, and when a country has a milestone birthday the temptation to reach for the summarizing figure is very nearly irresistible. Two hundred and fifty years. Forty-seven presidents. One republic, still standing, which as its own historians will tell you is the genuinely improbable part, most republics having the courtesy to collapse well before the bicentennial.

But none of those were the number I was looking for. I found the number I was looking for in, of all places, a Maureen Dowd column, and it is not a big number at all. It is the smallest number in this entire essay. It is a pair of shoe buckles.

Here is the ledger, and I want you to watch the two columns, because the whole story is in the gap between them.

As the New York Times reported this week, George Washington — the Father of the Country, the man who could have been king and declined the position — once agonized over whether he could accept a pair of shoe buckles. They were a gift from David Humphreys, his own aide-de-camp, a man who had served beside him through the Revolutionary War. Shoe buckles. From a friend. And Washington, the historian Ron Chernow told the Times, hesitated, because he was afraid that even a small present from a loyal aide might be interpreted as a bribe — that accepting buckles might, somehow, look like the beginning of the thing the whole revolution had been fought to prevent.

That is the left column of the ledger. Shoe buckles, agonized over, nearly refused, by a man terrified of the appearance of corruption.

Now the right column. In the first year of his second term, according to the same reporting, the forty-seventh president collected roughly $1.4 billion from his cryptocurrency ventures, and something north of $2.2 billion overall — and took delivery of a $400 million airplane from the government of Qatar, a jet with a faux library and massage chairs, which he intends to keep. When someone raised the small matter of the Constitution’s rather pointed language about presidents accepting gifts from foreign states, he explained that he could “be a stupid person and say, oh no, we don’t want a free plane.”

Shoe buckles on the left. A free plane worth four hundred million dollars on the right. Two hundred and fifty years between them, and the entire descent of a republic fits in the space between a man who lost sleep over buckles and a man who cannot believe you’d be dumb enough to turn down a jet.

A president once agonized over accepting shoe buckles. This is the $400 million plane, faux library included.

That is the number I was looking for. It turns out to be two numbers, and the distance between them is the essay.

I want to sit on that distance, because it is not really about money. Washington was not poor and Trump is not the first rich president. The buckles and the plane are not a story about wealth. They are a story about shame — specifically, about a man who had it and a man who does not.

Washington’s fear was not that the buckles were a bribe. They plainly weren’t. His fear was that they might look like one, and that the look alone could stain the office he was trying to invent from scratch. He understood something that his 250-years-distant successor does not appear to have encountered: that the presidency was brand new, that it had no traditions yet because he was busy being the tradition, and that every single thing he did would become the precedent for everyone who came after. So he agonized over buckles, because he was not really deciding about buckles. He was deciding what the office would be allowed to become.

He decided it would be the kind of office that worries about shoe buckles. And for a very long time, more or less, it was.

The current occupant has made a different decision, about a different gift, and it too will become a precedent, and that is the part that should keep you up the way the buckles kept Washington up. Because the plane is not the end of anything. The plane is a benchmark. Somebody will have to decide, someday, whether to accept the next free plane, and they will look back at this one, and the buckles will be so far behind them they’ll need a telescope.

There is a word that gives the whole thing away, and Dowd caught it, and I want to borrow it because it is perfect.

Signing a memorandum recently, the president described how he operates: “We rule,” he said, “by common sense.”

Rule.

It is the wrong verb, and it is the wrong verb in the most revealing possible way. You do not rule a republic. You govern one. Kings rule; presidents govern; and the entire point of the man whose birthday party this is — the entire content of his most famous decision, the one where he handed back power he could have kept — was to establish, permanently, that the United States would be governed and never ruled. Washington got off the horse. That was the whole thing. That was the miracle.

The founders feared the man on horseback. Washington got off the horse. That was the whole miracle.

The 18th century had a phrase for the nightmare the founders were trying to avoid, and Chernow supplied it: the man on horseback. The victorious general who wins the revolution and then, instead of going home, rides the victory straight into a throne. It was the oldest story in the world and they knew it cold — Caesar, Cromwell, every strongman who ever turned a liberation into a dictatorship. They built the entire American machine, every check and every balance, specifically to stop the man on horseback from arriving.

And here is the exquisite irony, the one I cannot get over: Washington was the man on horseback. He was literally that — the victorious general, beloved, holding an army’s loyalty, standing at the exact fork where every other general in history had chosen the throne. And he got off the horse. He wanted to be a farmer. He went back to Mount Vernon to fuss over his crops and his cocktail recipe and his terrible spring-loaded dentures, and in getting off the horse he became the only man on horseback the founders weren’t afraid of, and the reason the republic they built has lasted long enough to have a 250th birthday at all.

The founders feared the man on horseback. Washington answered the fear by dismounting. Two hundred and fifty years later, someone has climbed back on, and is confused about why anyone would ever get off.

Let me close with the cherry tree, because it deserves a place in the ledger too.

One president chopped down a cherry tree and couldn't lie about it. The other may chop them down for a golf course.

You know the fable. Young Washington chops down his father’s cherry tree, is confronted, and cannot tell a lie: I did it with my little hatchet. It never happened — it was invented by a biographer — but it stuck, for two and a half centuries, because it captured something people wanted to be true about the man: that at the root of him was an honesty he could not switch off even to save himself a scolding.

The fable is invented. The trait was real. Chernow’s phrase for Washington was “unimpeachable integrity — not the faintest hint of scandal.” The cherry tree is a myth that happens to be accurate.

And now, as the Times noted, there are real cherry trees in Washington — historic ones, along the Tidal Basin and East Potomac Park — that may come down so that a golf course can go in. Which gives us the final entry in the ledger, and the tidiest one: the first president is remembered, falsely but fittingly, for chopping down one cherry tree and refusing to lie about it. The current one may chop down actual cherry trees for a golf course, and the lying, one suspects, will not be a problem.

One couldn’t tell a lie about a single tree. The other can’t stop, and is coming for the trees besides.

So there is the ledger, on the occasion of the 250th birthday. Shoe buckles against a free plane. Govern against rule. A man who got off the horse against a man who got back on. A myth about honesty against a reality about golf.

I am Canadian, and it is not my birthday, and I am aware there is something presumptuous about a man in Toronto totting up the accounts of the republic next door on its own holiday. But I have spent a great many essays now watching that republic, with the particular affection you can only have for the enormous, exhausting, magnificent country next door, and I did not write this to gloat. Most of the ledger’s history is on the good side. Two hundred and fifty years is a genuine miracle; the man who got off the horse was a genuine miracle; the fact that it has lasted this long when almost no republic does is the most genuine miracle of all.

I wrote it because of the buckles. Because somewhere in the founding of that country was a man so careful of the public trust that he could not accept a gift from a friend without agonizing over how it would look — and I think, on the country’s birthday, it is worth remembering that this is what the office was supposed to feel like. Not gold. Not a free plane. Buckles, agonized over. A man who worried.

Happy 250th, America. You were founded by a man who wouldn’t take the buckles. Try to remember him. He is, it turns out, the whole point.
