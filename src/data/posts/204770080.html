There is a dam in British Columbia, on the Columbia River near Castlegar, that holds back the waters of the Arrow Lakes. For its first years it was called, prosaically, the Arrow Dam. In 1969 it was renamed, and it has been known ever since as the Hugh Keenleyside Dam. Hugh Keenleyside was my great-uncle. So you will forgive me if this essay, the one about water, is a little more personal than the others — though I will argue before I am done that the personal connection is not a digression but the whole point, because the story of that dam is the story of how a continent once chose to manage a shared resource under a shared climate, and the story of this decade is what happens to such arrangements when both the climate and the sharing fall apart.

First, the water itself, because it is the resource we understand least well precisely because we have always had too much of it to think about. Climate change is, in the popular imagination, a story about heat, and this series has spent ten essays largely on heat and its consequences. But heat is only the headline. The deeper story is water — its redistribution, its disappearance, its sudden catastrophic arrival in the wrong place. A warming atmosphere holds more moisture, which means that the same warming that bakes one region drowns another; that snowpacks which once melted slowly through the summer, metering out water like a careful steward, now vanish in spring floods and leave the late season parched; that the great rivers fed by glaciers are, for a few decades, running high on the meltwater of ice that will not be replaced, after which they will run low forever. Drought and flood are not opposites in this story. They are the same phenomenon — a disrupted water cycle — wearing two different masks.

That last clause — the ice that will not be replaced — is not a projection for some hazy midcentury. It happened, measurably, in the very weeks I was writing this series, in the mountains that serve as Europe’s water tank. The glaciers of the Alps feed the Rhône, the Rhine, the Po, and the Danube; drinking water, irrigation, hydropower, and the cooling ponds of nuclear plants all draw on ice that was banked over centuries, on the assumption that winter would keep making deposits. In the summer of 2026, the withdrawals ran ahead of schedule. As the New York Times reported on July 2, 2026, Switzerland’s glaciers entered the spring with a quarter less snow cover than the 2010–2020 average; then two record heat waves arrived in quick succession, and by June 29, 2026, the season’s entire snow layer was gone — the glaciers had melted back to where they stood before the winter began, a full month ahead of normal. Only 2022, the last record-breaking European summer, reached that grim milestone earlier, and only by three days.

Snow, you see, is the glacier’s sunscreen. Each winter’s accumulation shields the ice beneath through the spring; once the snow burns off, the sun goes to work on the glacier itself, and ice, unlike snow, does not come back. At one station on the Rhône Glacier, researchers from ETH Zurich measured a full metre of thinning in ten days of June 2026. On the Great Aletsch — the longest glacier in the Alps — the snow line climbed more than 1,300 feet in elevation during the June heat wave, and the snow that remained above 11,300 feet was slush. Matthias Huss, the glaciologist who leads Switzerland’s glacier monitoring program, put it plainly: “We are now in a state where we should normally be in August.” And his timeline for when Europe’s rooftop water tank stops being reliable is not a century out. It is ten to twenty years — inside the mortgage, as it were.

One detail from that reporting deserves a moment’s pause, because it is as tidy a summary of the whole subject as I can offer. Alongside the satellites and the infrared composites, the essential instrument of glaciology remains what it was in the 1800s: a long pole, sunk into a hole drilled in the ice, read like a bathtub ring as the surface melts down around it. The scientists drill their holes deep enough, in a normal year, to last the full season. This summer the ice is vanishing so quickly that they expect to climb back up and drill new ones before autumn, because the old holes will have melted clean away. The measuring stick cannot keep up with the thing it measures. Neither, as we shall see, can the treaties.

And water, unlike carbon, is intensely, almost violently local in its politics, which is what makes it the most dangerous arena in the whole climate crisis. Carbon is a global commons; everyone’s emissions mix into one atmosphere, and the tragedy is diffuse. But a river runs from somewhere to somewhere else, from an upstream nation to a downstream one, and when it carries less water than it used to, the question of who gets the remaining water is not abstract or generational. It is immediate, zero-sum, and historically a cause of war. The twentieth century fought over oil. There is a respectable argument that the twenty-first will fight over water, and the front lines will be the river basins that cross borders — the Nile, the Indus, the Mekong, the Tigris and Euphrates, and, closer to home, the Columbia.

I do not invoke the Indus idly, because what is happening to it right now is the dark mirror of what I am about to describe on my own continent, and readers of my India series will already feel the chill. The Indus Waters Treaty, signed in 1960 and brokered by the World Bank, divided the six great rivers of the Indus basin between India and Pakistan, and for more than sixty years it was held up as the most successful water-sharing agreement on Earth — so durable that it survived three full-scale wars between the two nations without either side touching the water. It was the proof that even bitter enemies could keep faith on the one thing neither could live without. And in April 2025, after a terrorist atrocity at Pahalgam, India put the treaty “in abeyance” — a careful diplomatic word for a unilateral suspension the treaty does not actually permit — and began altering flows, withholding the hydrological data Pakistan depends on, and fast-tracking dams on the western rivers. Pakistan, which draws something like seventy per cent of its agriculture from those waters, declared that any move to cut its supply would be treated as an act of war; its army chief mused publicly about missiles; India’s home minister vowed the treaty would “never” be restored. Modi had said it years before, with terrible economy: “water and blood cannot flow together.” The most successful water treaty in the history of the world, the one that outlasted three wars, is now a potential trigger for a fourth, between two nuclear states, with a changing climate shrinking the very resource they are quarrelling over. And the resource is shrinking on the same schedule as the Alps: the rivers of the Indus basin are fed by the glaciers of the Himalaya and the Karakoram — the “third pole,” holding more ice than anywhere on Earth outside the Arctic and Antarctic — and they are running the same experiment as the Rhône, a few decades behind. Hold that example beside the one I am about to give you, because the pattern is the same on both sides of the planet: the old water agreements, built for a stable world, are cracking exactly when the warming makes them indispensable.

Which returns me to my great-uncle’s dam. Hugh Keenleyside — whose career I have traced at length elsewhere, in The Reluctant Empire, because he keeps turning out to have been present at the hinges of the Canadian century — was a diplomat of the old school, a man who believed with his whole professional life that patient institutions could solve problems that passion could not. The Columbia River Treaty, signed in 1961 and brought into force in 1964, is one of the great pieces of transboundary water diplomacy in history — an agreement between Canada and the United States to jointly manage the Columbia for flood control and hydroelectric power. Canada built three dams, including the one that bears Keenleyside’s name, to store water that would protect American cities downstream from flooding and spin American turbines for power, in exchange for payments and a share of the electricity generated. It was not a perfect treaty; it drowned Canadian valleys and displaced communities and First Nations whose consent was never meaningfully sought, and those wounds are real and still raw. But as an instrument of international cooperation it worked, for sixty years, through Cold War and trade dispute and every political weather the two countries passed through. My great-uncle thought enough of it to write a defense of it in 1974, a decade in, answering the critics who said Canada had been fleeced. He believed in the thing — in the idea that two nations sharing a river could, with patience and good faith, share it well.

That treaty is now in trouble, and the manner of its trouble is a parable for everything this series has tried to say. The flood-control provisions negotiated in 1961 were written to expire in 2024, on the assumption — reasonable in 1961 — that sixty years would be enough time to renegotiate calmly. And for years the renegotiation proceeded exactly as it should have. By July 2024, after a decade of patient work, Canada and the United States announced an agreement in principle to modernize the treaty: new terms for flood storage behind the Keenleyside Dam, continued payments, provisions — new in spirit — to advance the rights of the First Nations the original treaty had run over, and to help restore the salmon the dams had blocked. The negotiators were, by reliable accounts, only weeks from completing the modernized text.

And then the 2024 American election intervened, and a decade of careful diplomacy ran straight into a president who refers to Canada as the fifty-first state and to its water as “a very large faucet” that might be turned toward the United States. The talks are now paused — suspended, the official language says, while the American administration conducts a broad review of its international commitments, which is the diplomatic register for we have other priorities and Canada is not one of them. The agreement in principle sits unfinished. An interim arrangement keeps the water flowing and the flood gates managed for now, but the modernized treaty — the one with the salmon and the First Nations provisions, the one that would carry the arrangement another sixty years — hangs in the air, hostage to a relationship that has soured at exactly the moment the warming climate makes the cooperation more necessary than ever.

Read the parable carefully, because it has two layers. The first is the obvious one: a man who does not believe in climate change, does not care about salmon, does not acknowledge Indigenous rights, and regards a shared river as a faucet to be commandeered is precisely the wrong person to hold the upstream end of the most important water treaty on the continent at the precise moment the water becomes scarce and the stakes become real. That is bad enough.

But the second layer is the one that should trouble us more, because it is structural rather than personal and will outlast any single president. The Columbia River Treaty was built, like everything else in this series, for a world that is leaving. It assumed a stable climate, in which the patterns of flood and flow that engineers measured in the 1950s would hold steady enough to write into a sixty-year contract. It assumed two nations of stable goodwill, who would treat the renegotiation as a technical matter between friends. Both assumptions are now in question — the hydrology destabilized by warming, the goodwill destabilized by a politics of grievance and faucets — and the treaty, that elegant old machine for sharing a river fairly, is straining at exactly the joints my great-uncle worked to reinforce. The water cooperation was a house built for a temperate political and physical climate. The climate, in both senses, has changed.

I find I cannot write about this with the detachment I have managed elsewhere, and I am not going to pretend to. Hugh Keenleyside spent his life on the proposition that nations can solve shared problems through patient institutions, that the careful work of diplomacy can build things that last. The dam with his name on it still stands, still holds back the Arrow Lakes, still does its quiet work. But the treaty that gives the dam its purpose is being allowed to wither, not because anyone has decided it should fail, but because the patient institutions he believed in are out of fashion in an age that prefers the faucet to the agreement, the grievance to the negotiation, the short and selfish answer to the long and shared one. The houses, the carbon tax, the insurance, the river — over and over, the same finding. We built well, for a world that is leaving, and we are not yet building its replacement.

Degrees

1961 — the year the Columbia River Treaty was signed, in an age that believed two nations could share a river for sixty years and then sit down, as friends, to share it for sixty more. The flood-control terms expired in 2024. The modernized treaty meant to replace them sits unfinished, paused, while the water — indifferent to all of it — keeps falling, and failing to fall, in the new and unfamiliar rhythms of a warming world.
