CanadianSnowmanSays May 9, 2026

This is an addendum to our February 24 essay, “Constitutional Whack-a-Mole: Trump’s Section 122 Gambit.” That piece predicted the legal challenge and its likely outcome. On May 7, it arrived. What follows is the update — and the reason it barely made the news.

On Thursday, the Court of International Trade ruled 2-1 that Trump’s 10% Section 122 tariff is unlawful. Two Obama-appointed judges formed the majority. A Bush-appointed judge dissented. The president blamed “two radical left judges,” because of course he did.

The ruling is thorough, intellectually serious, and almost entirely meaningless.

Here is why.

The court declined to issue a nationwide injunction — a direct consequence of the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. CASA, which narrowed the scope of universal injunctions. Instead, the relief applies exclusively to the named plaintiffs: Burlap & Barrel, a spice importer; Basic Fun!, a toy company; and the state of Washington, which qualified because the University of Washington had directly paid Section 122 duties. A spice shop, a toy store, and a university. Everyone else keeps writing cheques.

For the remaining millions of American importers, nothing changed on Thursday. Nothing changes today. As one trade policy expert told CBS News: “If I am a business today, for practical purposes, nothing changes today compared to yesterday.” Capital Economics put it more bluntly: the ruling has “no immediate implication for the U.S. tariff rate.”

And here is the part that makes it almost cosmically irrelevant: Section 122 tariffs expire on July 24 anyway. They were always designed to expire. The administration never pretended otherwise. Section 122 was a 150-day bridge — a placeholder statute to keep tariffs flowing while the U.S. Trade Representative built the permanent replacement using Section 301 investigations that are already well underway, targeting excess manufacturing capacity in 16 economies and forced labour practices across 60 more.

So the court struck down a tariff that applies to almost nobody, that was going to die in 77 days regardless, and that was always intended to be replaced by something bigger and more durable. The administration will appeal, not because it needs to win, but because conceding the legal point would complicate the refund calculus. The $8 billion per month in Section 122 duties already collected doesn’t refund itself.

“We always do it a different way,” the president told reporters Thursday evening. He has now said some version of this sentence after every court loss in this saga. It is becoming less a defiant catchphrase than a job description.

The United States Court of International Trade, established under Article III of the Constitution, has nationwide jurisdiction over civil actions arising out of the customs and international trade laws of the United States.

Why It Matters Anyway

If you have read this far and concluded this ruling is a waste of ink, you are half right. The practical impact is negligible. But the constitutional ledger is not.

This is the second time a federal court has struck down a post-SCOTUS tariff authority. Two statutes tried, two statutes rejected. The pattern the February essay identified — constitutional whack-a-mole, the administration grabbing the nearest untested statute and daring courts to keep up — is now judicially documented at every level except the Supreme Court itself.

The majority opinion was carefully crafted. It rests on originalist statutory interpretation — the phrase “balance-of-payments deficits” meant something specific in 1974, when the dollar was still adjusting to life after the gold standard, and you cannot stretch it to cover trade deficits in a floating exchange rate world half a century later. It invokes the major questions doctrine and the nondelegation principle, both of which featured prominently in the Supreme Court’s IEEPA ruling. The opinion was written, in other words, to survive appeal to the same Federal Circuit and the same Supreme Court that already said no once.

The message to the U.S. Trade Representative as it finalizes Section 301 tariffs is unmistakable: we are watching, and you had better dot every procedural i this time. Whether that message will be received is another question entirely.

And there is a second reason to pay attention, one that matters more to this Canadian observer than any doctrinal nicety: the timing.

The July Collision

Section 122 tariffs expire July 24. Section 301 tariffs are supposed to be ready by roughly the same window. And on July 1 — three weeks earlier — the United States, Mexico, and Canada face the first-ever joint review of the USMCA.

Under Article 34.7, the three governments must decide by that date whether to extend the agreement for another 16 years. If any party declines, the USMCA enters a cycle of annual reviews and, absent resolution, expires in 2036. July 1 is not a deadline for completing negotiations. It is the point at which the clock either resets or starts running down.

A clean extension by July 1 now appears unlikely. The review launched only on March 18 — bilaterally, between the U.S. and Mexico, not trilaterally — with no new text on the table and less than four months before the statutory decision date. Ambassador Greer has told congressional committees he is not prepared to recommend renewal without changes. Canada’s lead negotiator, Janice Charette, has signalled that July 1 should not be seen as a breaking point — which is the kind of thing negotiators say when they know the breaking point is approaching and they are not ready.

The most likely outcome, according to CSIS, is what they diplomatically call a “painful extension” — negotiations stretching into late 2026, concentrated on autos, energy, China-related supply chain rules, and enforcement, with Canada and Mexico making concessions to reduce tariff exposure. The second most likely outcome is serial annual reviews: no deal, sustained uncertainty, and a decade-long countdown to expiration that discourages exactly the kind of long-term cross-border investment the agreement was designed to encourage.

For Canada, the stakes are existential in a way that Section 122 refund litigation is not. The USMCA review will determine whether the economic architecture that has governed North American trade for thirty years — first NAFTA, then CUSMA — survives in recognizable form or enters a slow, politically poisoned decline. It will determine whether Canadian industries that have spent decades integrating with American supply chains can continue to plan beyond the next news cycle. And it will unfold against the backdrop of an administration that has now had two tariff authorities struck down by courts, is building a third, and whose president’s considered response to judicial review is: “We always do it a different way.”

That is the story. Not a spice importer’s refund. Not a toy company’s injunction. Not a statute that was dead before the ruling landed.

The story is July.

Readers of The Great Canadian Trade War series will recognise the architecture. Part XII documented the Section 301 forced labour investigations launched on March 13 — the one-year anniversary of Canada’s first retaliatory tariffs — as the fulfilment of the Candace Laing warning that anchored Part XI: “Canada should prepare for new, blunter mechanisms.” That essay ended with Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc leaving his first meeting with Ambassador Greer since October, getting into his car, and saying: “Have a good weekend.” That was a man who understood what was coming.

What was coming is now here. The Section 301 investigations are the replacement tariff architecture. The CIT ruling confirms the Section 122 bridge was always legally indefensible. And the USMCA review opens July 1 with Canada simultaneously under a forced labour investigation alongside Cambodia and Bangladesh — a country that passed supply chain legislation stronger than anything the United States had on its own books a decade ago, being told its homework isn’t good enough by a student who only started doing his in 2016.

Part XIII of The Great Canadian Trade War will pick up where this addendum leaves off — when the July collision arrives and the three dates that have been converging all year finally meet. The Section 122 expiry. The Section 301 conclusions. The USMCA review. Three fuses, one month, and a trading relationship that both countries will spend a generation trying to repair.

Laing called it. LeBlanc understood it. The rest is arithmetic.
