⚖️ Serious — one of the grave ones. It is about a death toll so large that, a century later, the best estimates still differ by a factor of five, and about how the one country that told the truth about it had the disease named after it. No wink. The dog stays out of it.

I count my essays, and at 1347 I wrote the one I said I could not make funny: the Black Death, the great mortality, the plague that took perhaps a third of Europe and left chroniclers guessing at the numbers for six centuries. I have been waiting, ever since, for its descendant. It arrives at 1918, in the last year of the war that essay 1914 watched begin, and it killed more people than that war did — in a fraction of the time, mostly in a few terrible autumn months, and very largely among the young.

How many more? That is the essay. No one knows.

The range

Here is the most honest sentence I can write about the influenza pandemic of 1918: it killed somewhere between seventeen million and a hundred million people. The figure most often quoted is fifty million. The spread between the low estimate and the high one is larger than the entire death toll of the First World War. We can name the dead of the war, many of them, on memorials in every town in my country. The dead of the flu are a range, a span so wide it is less a number than a confession.

Why can we not count them? Partly because most of the world did not keep the records. In 1918, across much of Asia and Africa and Latin America, deaths were not registered with any precision at all, and the flu cut through India in particular with a ferocity that may account for more than ten million deaths on its own — perhaps far more. The pattern this series traced at 1,857 holds once again: in the places where the people were counted about rather than counted by, the dead became an estimate. Partly, too, it is because influenza killed by opening the door to pneumonia, and a doctor in 1918 writing “pneumonia” on a certificate was not always writing “influenza,” so even the careful countries undercounted. And partly it is because the pandemic came in the shadow of a war, and in wartime, the counting of bad news is a military matter.

The name is a counting error

Which brings me to the name, and to the most perfect injustice of arithmetic I have found in this whole series.

We call it the Spanish flu. It did not start in Spain. Its origins are still argued — a military camp in Kansas, the British staging grounds in France, northern China have all been proposed — but the one place it almost certainly did not start is the place it was named for. It was called Spanish for one reason: Spain was neutral in the war, and Spain did not censor its newspapers.

In the warring countries — Britain, France, Germany, the United States — reports of a deadly epidemic sweeping the barracks and the cities were suppressed or played down as bad for morale. Newspapers were told what not to print. In Spain, which had no army at the front and no morale to protect, the newspapers reported the epidemic freely, including the illness of the King himself. So to the rest of the world, reading the only honest reports there were, it looked as if the disease were raging in Spain. The country that told the truth about the count was blamed for the thing being counted.

Sit with that as a ledger entry. The nations that suppressed their numbers got to keep their names clean. The nation that published its numbers had its name attached to the catastrophe permanently. This series began with a chronicler’s habit of naming wars by a round number, the Hundred Years’ War, that was not true. Here is a disease named by a censorship, which is worse. Every time we say “Spanish flu” we repeat the propaganda of 1918 without noticing, and punish, a century late, the one country that kept honest books.

The parade

There is a date I cannot leave out, because it is the clearest demonstration in these files of what happens when the count is ignored for the sake of the schedule.

On September 28, 1918, the city of Philadelphia held a great parade to sell war bonds — the Liberty Loan parade, some two hundred thousand people packed along the route to cheer the troops and buy the bonds that paid for the war. Influenza had already arrived in the city’s naval yard. Doctors had warned. The parade went ahead, because the bond drive was scheduled and the war needed the money. Within three days, every hospital bed in Philadelphia was full. Within weeks, more than ten thousand of the city’s people were dead, and bodies were stacked in the morgues and on porches because there were not enough coffins, nor enough men well enough to dig.

Essay 1914 ended on the observation that the count, once set moving, would not be stopped — that even the armistice had to wait for the eleventh hour. Philadelphia is the same lesson, applied to a virus. The parade was on the calendar, and the calendar outranked the doctors. Other cities, such as St. Louis, closed schools, theatres and churches early and kept them closed, and their death rates were markedly lower. The difference between the two cities was not medicine — there was no medicine. It was whether the people in charge would let a warning interrupt a schedule.

Who died

The 1918 flu broke the oldest rule of epidemic arithmetic. Ordinary influenza kills the very old and the very young; plot its deaths by age and you get a U, high at both ends and low in the middle. The 1918 flu drew a W. It killed the very old and the very young, and then, between them, a terrible spike of healthy young adults in their twenties and early thirties — the same people the war had just finished counting. Soldiers who had survived the trenches died in the troopships coming home. Nurses who had tended them died after. The disease seemed, with a cruelty no general could have planned, to go looking for exactly the generation the war had spared.

My own country lost something like fifty-five thousand people to it — very nearly as many as died in four years of fighting overseas. The soldiers’ names are carved in stone across the country, as they should be. The flu dead are mostly not, anywhere. They died at home, in their beds, in the autumn of the armistice year and the winter after, and were buried by families too sick to grieve properly, and were not, in any sense the state recognised, fallen. Yet it was this catastrophe, not the war, that pushed Ottawa to create the country’s first federal Department of Health, in 1919. A death toll too large to count still managed to change the ledger: it taught a government that the health of its citizens was something it was obliged to keep books on.

The undercount that teaches

This series has a recurring character, though it has no name: the dead who were not counted. The plague dead of 1347, guessed at by chroniclers. The six who died in the Great Fire of 1666, by official record, who were surely many more. The Indian dead of 1857, left as a range. The Chinese railway labourers of 1885. And now the largest uncounted dead of all — tens of millions of human beings who died in a single season and left behind not a number but an argument about a number.

The lesson I take from 1918 is not that counting is hopeless. It is that counting is a choice, made in advance, by the people who decide what is worth recording. The countries that had built public-health registries counted their dead closely; the ones that had not could only estimate. The countries at war hid their numbers; the one at peace published them and was punished for it. An undercount is never an accident of nature. It is a decision, made earlier, by someone who did not think those deaths would need to be known.

A century later, the world would face another pandemic and count it daily, by the hour, on every screen in every house — cases, deaths, curves to be flattened. Whether that count was honest is an argument for another dateline, and perhaps for another number in this series. I note only that the instinct to count the dying in public, every day, as a civic duty, was learned the hard way. Some of it was learned in 1918.

The closer

So 1918 is the year the war ended on schedule and a far greater killing went on without one. It left us a death toll measured in a range wider than the war, a name that rewards the censors and blames the honest, and a parade that is still the textbook case of a calendar overruling a warning. It is the descendant of 1347 and the ancestor of every daily case count since.

The count moves on, and after the war and the plague it arrives at the decade that thought it had put both behind it. The next landmark is essay 1,929, and it is the year the numbers themselves broke — when a whole society discovered that the prices on the ticker, the fortunes on paper, the wealth everyone had been counting so happily for ten years, had stopped meaning anything at all. In 1918 the world could not count its dead. In 1929 it found it could not count its money.

See you at 1929, where the ticker runs hours behind and the numbers stop meaning anything.
