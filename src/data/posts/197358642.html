Tomorrow, Canadians will celebrate Victoria Day.

For the benefit of international readers — including the Americans, who have spent the past week watching their president negotiate the architecture of the twenty-first century in Beijing and who have therefore not had time to wonder what their northern neighbour is up to — Victoria Day is a statutory holiday in Canada commemorating the birthday of Queen Victoria, who was born on May 24, 1819, reigned for sixty-three years over the largest empire in human history, and died on January 22, 1901.

She has been dead for a hundred and twenty-five years.

We light fireworks for her anyway.

This is Canada. This is what we do.

I should be clear that I say this with affection. Victoria Day is a fine holiday. It marks the unofficial start of the cottage season. The fireworks are cheerful. The weather, in most of Canada, is finally beginning to cooperate. In communities from Victoria, British Columbia — named for the queen, obviously — to the suburbs of Toronto, families will gather on lawns and in parks and at the edges of lakes to watch coloured light explode in the sky in honour of a monarch who, if she could see us, would likely be pleased that we remembered and mildly puzzled that we are still here as a separate country at all.

Victoria, after all, oversaw an empire. She did not oversee a world in which Canada politely declined to become part of the United States, had a trade war about it, and then watched from the porch while the United States went to Beijing to negotiate the next hundred years without us.

The porch, I should note, has a very good view.

This week, while Canada was preparing its fireworks, Donald Trump flew to Beijing for a state visit with Xi Jinping — the first American president to visit China in nine years, the last occasion being Trump himself in 2017, when Xi staged what analysts called a “state visit-plus” and Trump came home with $250 billion in announced deals, most of which did not materialize, including an $83.7 billion memorandum of understanding with West Virginia that exceeded West Virginia’s entire GDP and was never heard from again.

West Virginia remains. The memorandum does not.

This visit, the analysts tell us, is more modest. The goal, according to everyone who studies these things, is “stability.” Not breakthroughs. Not transformation. Not Nixon in China. Just: the two largest powers on earth agreeing to continue existing in proximity without immediately destroying everything, which in the current environment qualifies as a diplomatic achievement of the first order.

The agenda, as best as the public can determine, includes: tariffs, Taiwan, the Iran war, rare earth minerals, artificial intelligence, Boeing aircraft, American soybeans, and a proposed “Board of Trade” that sounds like something a Victorian municipality would establish to regulate the sale of dry goods.

Canada is not on the agenda.

Canada is rarely on the agenda.

This is also fine. Being on the agenda, in the current geopolitical climate, tends to mean that someone wants something from you. Taiwan is on the agenda. Taiwan is, by most accounts, terrified. The Taiwanese have a phrase for their position in American-Chinese negotiations: “on the menu.” They did not coin this phrase cheerfully.

Canada, by contrast, is not on the menu.

Canada is on the porch with the fireworks.

There is a tradition, in Canadian political commentary, of describing our relationship to American power through the metaphor of the mouse sleeping next to the elephant — Pierre Trudeau’s formulation, offered at the National Press Club in Washington in 1969, the same year I watched Neil Armstrong walk on the moon and assumed the future would ask more of all of us.

The metaphor has served us well for fifty-seven years. What it does not fully capture is the specific Canadian experience of watching the elephant fly to Beijing to have dinner with a different, equally large animal, while the mouse stands in the yard lighting fireworks for a dead queen and quietly hoping that neither of them lands on us.

This is a more complicated metaphor. I am working on it.

There are things Canada does very well that are worth noting on this Victoria Day, in the spirit of honest national accounting.

We have not, in recent memory, confused two active wars in a press availability.

We have not renamed a strait.

We have not declared a neighbouring country’s currency valueless on the grounds that it does not have a name.

We have, to our credit, maintained a functional relationship with the concept of geography, which is more than can be said for several participants in the current global order.

Our prime minister, whoever it is at any given moment — and this has been changing with a frequency that suggests we are running some kind of experiment — has not posted himself as a religious figure on social media at 4 AM. We regard this as a low bar. It is, given the times, a bar worth clearing.

Queen Victoria, to her credit, also never posted herself as a religious figure at 4 AM. She did commission a great deal of portraiture in which she appeared alongside her late husband Albert with an expression suggesting she was disappointed in everyone, which is different. We regard that expression as foundational to the Canadian temperament.

Here is what I actually feel on Victoria Day, if I am being honest about it:

Grateful.

Grateful that Canada is small enough and sensible enough and geographically blessed enough to be, at this particular moment in history, not quite interesting enough to be on the menu. Grateful for the porch. Grateful for a statutory holiday that requires no justification more sophisticated than “a queen was born here once, two centuries ago, and we liked the idea of a long weekend in May.” Grateful for a country that lights fireworks in her honour with absolutely no sense of irony, because the fireworks are pretty and the night is finally warm and the cottage season has begun.

There are worse things to be than irrelevant.

There are worse things to celebrate than the birthday of a dead queen.

There are worse ways to spend a Sunday night than standing in the yard with a sparkler, watching the sky, while in Beijing two men in suits discussed the architecture of the next hundred years without asking us.

We were not at the table.

We were never going to be at the table.

We have a very nice porch.

Happy Victoria Day.

Light the fireworks.

Mind the elephant.
