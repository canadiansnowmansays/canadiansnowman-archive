Every Canadian secession argument rests on an assumption so basic its authors rarely state it: the province is the unit. When Quebec or Alberta decides to go, the map goes with it. The border on the way out is the border on the way in.

Some people in this story have never accepted that assumption, and have said so, in referendums and courtrooms, for more than thirty years: the First Nations and Inuit whose treaties were signed with the Crown, not with Quebec City or Edmonton. Their question is the most awkward in the whole Neverendum. When a province leaves, whose land leaves with it?

The Referendum Before the Referendum

Canadians remember October 30, 1995, as the night the country nearly ended by 54,288 votes. Fewer remember the two votes held in the week before.

On October 24, 1995, the Grand Council of the Crees (of Quebec) held its own referendum across Eeyou Istchee, the Cree territory east of James Bay. The question was not whether Quebec should be sovereign. It was whether the Crees consented, as a people, to the Government of Quebec separating them and their traditional territory from Canada in the event of a Yes. With 77 per cent of 6,380 eligible voters taking part, 96.3 per cent said no, according to the Library of Parliament‘s background paper Aboriginal Peoples and the 1995 Quebec Referendum: A Survey of the Issues, by Jill Wherrett, February 1996. The count was 4,666 to 183.

Five days later, on October 29, the Inuit of northern Quebec voted on a plain version of the province’s own question: did they agree that Quebec should become sovereign? Roughly 75 per cent of eligible voters cast ballots, and 96 per cent said no, the same Library of Parliament paper records. Later retellings often round it to 95.

Set those beside the provincial result. Quebec as a whole voted No by 50.58 per cent. The Crees and Inuit, whose territories cover most of the province’s land mass, voted No by margins that would embarrass a Soviet returning officer. Had 1995 gone the other way by a few thousand votes, Quebec City would have held a bare-majority mandate, and the peoples of its northern two-thirds a mandate of their own, pointing in the opposite direction.

Sovereign Injustice

The Crees arrived at that vote with a brief. In October 1995 the Grand Council published Sovereign Injustice: Forcible Inclusion of the James Bay Crees and Cree Territory into a Sovereign Québec. Its argument, as the Library of Parliament summarized it, was that Aboriginal peoples have a right to self-determination that includes the right to remain in Canada, and that forcibly including the Crees in a new Quebec would lack legitimacy under international, Canadian and Aboriginal law. Read the title slowly. The Crees did not say Quebec independence was an injustice. They said taking them along without consent would be one.

The Grand Chief who carried that case was Matthew Coon Come, elected to lead the Grand Council in 1987, known internationally for the Crees’ campaign against Hydro-Québec’s expansion on their rivers, and later National Chief of the Assembly of First Nations from 2000 to 2003. His logic was the sovereigntists’ own, returned to sender. If Quebecers were a people with a right to decide their future, so were the Crees. If a referendum could carry a province out of Canada, a referendum could keep a nation in.

Ottawa, for once, was not ambiguous. In October 1995 Indian Affairs Minister Ron Irwin told chiefs that Ottawa would protect Quebec’s Aboriginal peoples and their territories if the province voted to separate.

The Precedent Was Signed in 1975

The Crees’ confidence rested on more than philosophy. It rested on paper Quebec itself had signed.

When Robert Bourassa’s government launched the James Bay hydroelectric project in 1971, it began building on Cree and Inuit land without their agreement. They went to court, and on November 15, 1973, Judge Albert Malouf of the Quebec Superior Court ruled in their favour, finding that the province had to recognize their unceded rights and negotiate, as Yanick Turcotte explains in The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s entry on “James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement”. The injunction was soon suspended, but the point had landed. On November 11, 1975, Quebec, Canada, Hydro-Québec, the Grand Council of the Crees and the Northern Quebec Inuit Association signed the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement, the first modern comprehensive land claims agreement in Canada. Quebec committed $225 million in compensation over 20 years, and the agreement’s land regime covered most of Quebec’s surface.

Two details matter. First, Canada is a party to the agreement, which is protected under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. The Crees and Inuit hold their rights not by grace of Quebec City but under a constitutional treaty the federal Crown signed. Second, as the same encyclopedia entry notes, the northern territories were only added to Quebec by federal statutes in 1898 and 1912, and those land transfers carried an obligation to negotiate the surrender of Indigenous land rights. Quebec’s northern border is younger than the Ford Motor Company of Canada, and it came with conditions attached.

Hence the Crees’ inconvenient proposition: a province that received the north from Canada on terms that included Indigenous rights cannot assume, on leaving, that the north follows like luggage.

Oka, Five Years Earlier

None of this was abstract in 1995. Quebec had recently watched what happens when a land dispute is left to fester.

On July 11, 1990, the Sûreté du Québec raided a barricade the Mohawks of Kanesatake had built to stop the town of Oka from expanding a golf course into the Pines, a forest containing a Kanesatake cemetery. SQ Corporal Marcel Lemay was killed in the gunfire; who fired the fatal shot has never been determined. Mohawks at Kahnawake blocked the Mercier Bridge, and by August 20 some 4,000 soldiers had taken up positions around both communities. The standoff lasted 78 days and ended on September 26, 1990, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s timeline “Kanesatake Resistance (Oka Crisis)”.

Oka was not a secession crisis, and folding it into one would misread it. It was a fight over one piece of land the Mohawks had long claimed. But it taught everyone something about the Quebec state and the nations inside its borders, and it did not make the Crees and Inuit keener to trade a federal Crown for a provincial one.

What the Court Said, and Didn’t

When the Supreme Court answered Ottawa’s reference on August 20, 1998, the Crees and other Indigenous interveners had argued their right to remain in Canada on the strength of their treaties and the 1995 votes. The Court was polite and brief. It counted Aboriginal rights among the values any secession negotiation must respect, and flagged the boundaries of a seceding Quebec, “with particular regard to the northern lands occupied largely by aboriginal peoples.” Then it declined to explore Aboriginal concerns any further, since the reference had not asked.

Parliament went further. The Clarity Act of 2000 requires the Commons to weigh the views of Aboriginal peoples’ representatives in judging both question and majority, and section 3(2) bars any minister from proposing a secession amendment unless Ottawa has negotiated, among other things, “the rights, interests and territorial claims of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada,” in the text of the Clarity Act on the federal Justice Laws website. Borders and Aboriginal claims sit side by side in that list. The drafters understood they were the same subject.

Treaty Country

Now go west. Nearly all of Alberta lies within three numbered treaties. Treaty 6 was signed at Fort Carlton on August 23, 1876, and at Fort Pitt on September 9, 1876. Treaty 7 was signed at Blackfoot Crossing on September 22, 1877, by the Siksika, Kainai, Piikani, Stoney-Nakoda and Tsuut’ina. Treaty 8, the largest numbered treaty by area, was signed at Lesser Slave Lake on June 21, 1899, as recorded in The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s treaty entries, including “Treaty 7” by Alex Tesar. Alberta did not exist as a province when any of the three was signed; it was created in 1905. The other party, in every case, was the Crown.

On January 9, 2026, the Confederacy of Treaty No. 6 First Nations, the Blackfoot Confederacy, Tsuut’ina Nation and Treaty 8 First Nations of Alberta issued a joint statement declaring their “unified opposition to Alberta separation” and calling it a violation of their treaties and the honour of the Crown, in the “Statement from the First Nations of Treaty No. 6, Treaty No. 7, and Treaty No. 8” posted by the Confederacy of Treaty Six. They closed with the old treaty promise that the agreements last as long as the sun shines, the grass grows and the waters flow. That is not decoration. It is a legal claim about duration, with no exception for provincial referendums.

The courts have twice agreed with them in substance. In December 2025, Justice Colin Feasby of the Court of King’s Bench found that a proposed independence question would violate treaty rights and section 35, writing that Treaties 6, 7 and 8 “are part of the Canadian constitution,” as Leanne Sanders reported for APTN News on December 8, 2025, in “Alberta judge rules proposed referendum question on separation is unconstitutional”.

Then came May 13, 2026. In Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation v Alberta (Chief Electoral Officer), 2026 ABKB 375, Justice Shaina Leonard quashed the Chief Electoral Officer’s approval of the Stay Free Alberta petition for a referendum on independence. The applicants were the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation and the three Blackfoot nations of Piikani, Siksika and Kainai. Applying the Supreme Court’s test from Haida Nation, she found that once the petition was approved, provincial law set off a chain of mandatory steps that engaged the Crown’s duty to consult, as Christina Joynt and Justin Thompson explain in JFK Law‘s analysis “Treaty Rights and Alberta Separatism,” May 20, 2026. Secession, she wrote, would plainly “have an impact on Treaties 7 and 8.” Her conclusion was four words long: “Alberta breached its duty to consult.”

Chief Clifford Poucette of Goodstoney First Nation said the premier was “officially on notice,” as reported by The Canadian Press and Hannah Kavanagh in “Alberta separation petition overturned as judge rules in favour of First Nations,” CP24, May 13, 2026. Premier Danielle Smith called the ruling anti-democratic and announced an appeal. Eight days later, on May 21, she added a tenth question to the October 19 ballot, asking Albertans whether the province should remain or begin the legal process toward a binding separation vote. The petition’s door had been shut, so the government opened its own.

The chiefs’ answer escalated. In June 2026 the Assembly of Treaty Chiefs voted unanimously to ask the RCMP to investigate whether Smith’s referendum amounted to treason. Smith called the accusation “disgraceful” and said “this kind of overwrought language has no place in a democracy,” according to Jack Farrell of The Canadian Press in Global News, June 17, 2026. The underlying claim is less overwrought than it sounds: a provincial government that sets out to detach treaty land from the Crown is, in the chiefs’ view, acting against the Crown.

Now the treaty nations are doing the most conventional thing imaginable: organizing to vote. On October 2, 2026, Chief Traveller Plaited Hair and the Blood Tribe Council told members to vote to remain, grounding their reasoning in Treaty 7 and a Blackfoot territory that extends beyond Alberta’s borders, in the Blood Tribe‘s “Community Notice: Communiqué on Question 10”. Angel Houle of the Confederacy of Treaty Six warned that “silence could be consent.” Tsuut’ina Head Chief Ellery Starlight asked why only First Nations, “the poorest demographic in Canada,” were mounting this kind of campaign, as Lisa Johnson reported for The Canadian Press in Global News, October 4, 2026. Nobody in Edmonton has answered it.

A Sovereignty Built With, Not Over

Back in Quebec, which elected a Parti Québécois minority government on October 5, 2026, the sovereignty movement has begun, unevenly, to absorb the lesson of 1995.

On July 3, 2026, Québec Solidaire released a manifesto, Nouveau Québec libre, presented by co-spokesperson Sol Zanetti, proposing a constituent assembly “co-constructed” with First Nations and Inuit, as Frédéric Lacroix-Couture reported for La Presse Canadienne in Le Canada Français. Its Indigenous commission describes a plurinational state of Quebec’s 11 First Peoples alongside the Québécois nation, with the whole path to independence co-built with them, in the preface published by Presse-toi à gauche! on August 18, 2026. It is the most serious sovereigntist answer yet to Sovereign Injustice, from a party that won only nine seats on October 5, though a minority PQ government may need them.

The Parti Québécois, which now governs, has been slower; its Livre bleu chapter on First Nations was still pending in September, as Gabrielle Cantin reported in Le Manic on September 9, 2026; in the same piece the University of Ottawa law professor Ghislain Otis called Indigenous participation “tout à fait incontournable,” absolutely unavoidable. The PQ says it has consulted more than 40 chiefs. Leaders interviewed by CBC News on September 30, 2026, were unpersuaded. Grand Chief Cody Diabo of the Mohawk Council of Kahnawà:ke said Quebec “can’t unilaterally just say that they’re leaving.” Francis Verreault-Paul, chief of the Assembly of First Nations Quebec-Labrador, posed the question in its plainest form: will they respect the sovereignty of First Nations?

Whose Land

Somewhere in Chicoutimi this morning, a man in a Tim Hortons will look at the news and say, “Again? Must be Tuesday.” It is Monday, and he is half right. The provincial question recurs on schedule. What he misses is the second question that has recurred alongside it every time, posed by people never asked whether they wanted to be in the province at all.

Here the series’ working thesis needs an amendment. Provincial separatism has functioned mostly as leverage, winning a great deal without leaving. The Indigenous counter-claim is something different: not a bargaining position aimed at Ottawa, but a reminder that the federation is not only a pact among provinces. It is also a web of treaties between the Crown and nations that predate every provincial border on the map, and in at least two provinces those nations have said, by ballot and in court, that they did not agree to be carried out the door.

None of this romanticizes the treaties, which were often signed under duress, honoured selectively and broken frequently, by the same Crown the nations now invoke. The chiefs are not defending Canada because Canada has been good to them. They are defending the treaty relationship because it is the instrument they have, and the alternative on offer is a new state with no treaty at all. That is not sentiment. It is lawyering, and on the evidence of May 13, 2026, good lawyering.

When a province leaves, whose land leaves with it? The honest answer, after thirty-one years and two court rulings, is that nobody knows, and that the people with the strongest claim to an opinion have already given theirs.
