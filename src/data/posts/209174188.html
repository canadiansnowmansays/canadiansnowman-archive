There is, by now, a hallway in this administration. It is not on any blueprint, and no contractor has broken ground on it, but anyone who has watched a full cabinet cycle knows exactly where it is: the corridor between “trusted lieutenant” and “former official,” a passage so well-traveled by this point that you could probably find the carpet worn thin if the White House allowed cameras that far back. Pam Bondi walked it in April, fired as attorney general for reasons Todd Blanche — her own deputy, and the man who inherited her job — insisted at the time that nobody, including him, understood. Kristi Noem walked it in March, eased out of Homeland Security and handed a decorative new title, “Shield of the Americas” special envoy, which she was gracious enough to thank the president for, the way a demoted employee thanks HR for the exit package. Tulsi Gabbard walked it in June, resigning from the top of an intelligence directorate she had spent her tenure shrinking by 40 percent, as though hollowing out the house counted as a moving-out fee.

This week, Todd Blanche appears to be approaching the same hallway, and the strange part is that nobody, including the president, seems entirely sure whether he’s walking toward the door or just standing near it for effect.

Here is what actually happened. Mr. Blanche, formerly the president’s personal defense lawyer, has been serving as acting attorney general since Ms. Bondi’s ouster, and his nomination for the permanent job has been stuck in the Senate Judiciary Committee, held up by two Republicans — John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina — over a Justice Department settlement that would resolve the president’s own lawsuit against the IRS over his leaked tax returns, reportedly by way of a $1.776 billion “anti-weaponization fund” that critics worry could ultimately benefit Trump allies. Cornyn and Tillis want written confirmation that certain provisions get stripped out before they’ll advance the nomination. On Thursday, the committee postponed the vote. And the president responded, in essence, by shrugging at his own nominee’s career in public.

“I have no objection to temporarily withdrawing Todd’s name,” the president wrote, “if they do not do the right thing, and putting him back after Cornyn and Tillis are out of office” — both senators being scheduled to leave the Senate in January, one having lost his primary to a Trump-backed challenger, the other having announced his retirement after clashing with the president over last year’s tax-and-spending bill. In the same breath, Trump called Blanche “a STAR.” This is worth sitting with. It is possible, apparently, to be a star and also a bargaining chip in the same social media post — to be praised in capital letters and shelved in the same sentence, the presidential equivalent of telling someone they’re doing great work right before putting them on furlough.

There is something almost admirably honest in how little effort went into disguising the mechanics here. The senators aren’t objecting to Blanche personally — nobody in this story is even pretending that’s the issue. They’re objecting to a side deal that would let the president’s own lawsuit against a federal agency settle on terms friendlier than an ordinary litigant could expect, and Blanche’s confirmation has simply become the object being held hostage until that deal either gets fixed or gets run out the clock. The attorney general nomination, in other words, is not being litigated on its merits. It is being used as leverage in an entirely separate financial dispute, and the man at the center of it is being told, essentially: hang tight, we’ll pull your name for a while, don’t take it personally, you’re still a star, we just need two guys to retire first.

If you squint, this is a peculiarly loyal way to discard someone. Bondi was simply fired, with no public accounting offered even to her own successor. Noem got a new title to soften the landing. Gabbard resigned on her own initiative, having already done the demolition work the administration wanted before anyone needed to push her out. Blanche’s situation is subtler and, in its way, colder: he isn’t being fired, he isn’t resigning, and he isn’t even necessarily losing the job. He’s being told his own confirmation might be paused — not because he did anything, but because two senators want a commitment on an unrelated tax settlement, and the president has decided that letting the nomination sit in limbo for five months is preferable to giving Cornyn and Tillis what they’re asking for. It is possible to read this as confidence in Blanche. It is also possible to read it as a demonstration of exactly how disposable even a “STAR” is when a $1.776 billion settlement is on the table and the two men blocking it are already on their way out the door anyway.

There is a version of this story where Blanche never actually leaves at all — he simply continues indefinitely as “acting” attorney general, a title that in this administration has proven remarkably durable, occupying the desk without ever quite occupying the office. Given that Cornyn and Tillis depart in January regardless of what happens this week, the practical difference between “confirmed now” and “withdrawn and renominated in six months” may be close to zero for Mr. Blanche personally. But the optics are the story here, not the paperwork. When a president is willing to publicly float pulling his own attorney general nominee rather than budge an inch on a settlement benefiting himself, the hallway metaphor writes itself: it is getting crowded not because everyone in it did something wrong, but because everyone in it eventually becomes less useful standing still than they were on their way somewhere else.

A JOB POSTING, DRAFTED IN THE SPIRIT OF HONESTY

Position: Attorney General of the United States (Acting, Nominee, or Acting-Nominee-in-Waiting, depending on the news cycle)

Reports to: The President, informally, and whichever senator currently holds the confirmation vote hostage over an unrelated matter

Compensation: Public praise, unlimited and enthusiastic, revocable without notice

Job security: Contingent on the retirement schedules of individual U.S. Senators, which the successful candidate does not control and cannot expedite

Required qualifications: Prior experience as the president’s personal attorney preferred. Willingness to remain in “acting” status indefinitely a plus. Comfort being described as “a STAR” in the same breath as being described as temporarily withdrawable is essential.

Duties include: Running the Department of Justice, denying that you understand why your predecessor was fired, and waiting to learn whether your own nomination will be paused for the convenience of a tax settlement you did not personally negotiate.

Benefits: A hallway. Increasingly well-traveled. Company optional but, by this point in the administration, likely.

THE ROLL CALL SO FAR

For those keeping a scorecard — and by this point in the administration, a scorecard has become a reasonable thing to keep — the hallway’s current occupants include a fired attorney general who claims not to know why, a former homeland security secretary re-titled into a role that sounds like a Marvel spin-off, an intelligence director who left after gutting nearly half her own agency, and now, potentially, an acting attorney general whose loyalty is being tested not by any failure on his part but by his usefulness as a pressure valve in someone else’s tax dispute.

What connects them is not ideology, or competence, or scandal, though all three make occasional appearances. What connects them is that each one, at the moment of departure, was still being spoken of warmly. Bondi was praised until the week she wasn’t. Noem got a title upgrade on her way out the door. Gabbard’s cuts were celebrated even as she left. And Blanche, this week, is “a STAR” in the very same post that floats shelving him. The praise, it turns out, isn’t a signal of job security. It’s closer to a going-away gift, handed out at the moment it stops costing anything to give.

The hallway carpet, if it existed, would by now show a very particular kind of wear: not scuff marks from people being dragged out, but a smooth, even path from people walking themselves toward the door, thanking the person who opened it on the way through.

In my day, The Kids in the Hall were comedy gold. Funny how things never change.
