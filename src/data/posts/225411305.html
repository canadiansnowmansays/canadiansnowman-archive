Every entry on this series’ scorecard has always had a name. Comey, Letitia James, Bolton, Lisa Cook, Ilhan Omar, Robert Garcia, Richard Blumenthal. Last week it gained two New York building workers who went unnamed in the reporting, but whose offence was perfectly clear: they had declined to let an FBI agent through a door.

This week the list gains entries with neither names nor offences. All we have is a count.

As Andrew Duehren reported for The New York Times on November 17, 2026, the Internal Revenue Service has opened at least four criminal investigations as part of the Trump administration’s campaign against left-wing groups. That campaign began after Charlie Kirk was killed on September 10, 2025. The source is an internal status report circulated among the leaders of I.R.S. Criminal Investigation. It covers March to July 2026. It also records at least four tax-exempt groups referred to the civil side of the agency for audit, and I.R.S. officials assigned to nine grand juries.

The memo runs to eight pages. The Times saw six. The last two are an appendix identifying the targets, and those the Times did not see. Nor is it known where any of the four cases now stands.

So this part begins with what it cannot say: who is under investigation. Federal taxpayer privacy law, the same law that keeps your return out of the newspapers, protects the people in the appendix. It also protects the campaign that put them there. Six pages of an unsigned memo are the first look anyone outside has had at what the I.R.S. has actually done for the White House.

How the tax collector joined the hunt

The order came in the days after Kirk’s death. President Trump told agencies across the government to investigate the antifascist movement, or antifa, and other groups that espouse what the order called “extremism on migration, race and gender.” The I.R.S. was told to find out whether nonprofits were “directly or indirectly financing political violence or domestic terrorism.” In March 2026, officials from its criminal branch began working with a task force based at FBI headquarters. A dozen I.R.S. special agents and analysts were assigned to it.

The agency does not dispute any of this. Its spokesman told the Times that Criminal Investigation “has collaborated with federal law enforcement agencies, including the F.B.I., to investigate individuals and entities that may be funding domestic terrorism or political violence.”

On paper that sounds unremarkable. Following the money is what I.R.S. criminal agents are for; they are the people who convicted Al Capone. But the order did not ask them to follow money toward a crime. It asked them to follow money toward a set of opinions, and then see whether a crime turned up. Readers of Part XXVI will remember the first mark of a manufactured case: the investigation postdates the grievance. Here the grievance came first, then the order, then the task force. Only after all that did the I.R.S. start looking for something to investigate.

Nonprofits have feared exactly this since 2025, when Mr. Trump publicly called for the I.R.S. to strip elite universities of their tax-exempt status. An organization that loses its exemption loses the deductibility of every gift it receives. It does not need to be convicted to be ruined, only audited long enough.

Venmo, tattoos and Delaney Hall

The memo’s most concrete passages concern Newark. Delaney Hall is an immigrant detention centre there, and protesters and Immigration and Customs Enforcement have clashed at its gates again and again. The memo describes the I.R.S. detail doing “rapid response” to events at the site. That meant reviewing financial information, including subpoenaed Venmo records, of people arrested at the protests, and of businesses the officials believed might be funding them. At one point, the memo says, I.R.S. officials used facial-recognition and tattoo-analysis databases to help identify individuals.

Read that sentence again with the agency’s name in it. The tax collector ran tattoo analysis on protesters.

A Venmo history records who paid whom for pizza, rent, a ride home, bail. Subpoenaing the Venmo records of people arrested at a protest is not an investigation of terrorist financing. It is a map of the protesters’ social lives, drawn by an agency that already holds their tax returns.

Delaney Hall has been in this story before. In May 2025, Representative LaMonica McIver of New Jersey was charged with assaulting federal agents during an encounter at the site, after she and other members of Congress went there to inspect the facility. She is the Democratic member of Congress the Times mentions among those charged. The prosecutor who brought that case was the interim U.S. attorney for New Jersey at the time, Alina Habba.

What due process looks like, for comparison

Habba, now Alina Habba Mildon, is having a difficult November. As Tracey Tully reported for The New York Times on November 10, 2026, New Jersey’s Office of Attorney Ethics has recommended that she be disciplined for her part in settling a sexual harassment complaint at Trump National Golf Club Bedminster in 2021.

The facts in the complaint are not pretty. Mrs. Mildon was a dues-paying member of the club. She befriended a 21-year-old waitress, Alice Bianco, who had alleged that her boss engaged in a pattern of sexual harassment, including “quid pro quo sex in exchange for continued employment.” According to the complaint, a series of texts and one-on-one conversations led Ms. Bianco to drop her lawyer and accept $15,000 on “terms favorable to the golf club and unfavorable to Bianco.” The deal also included a nondisclosure agreement, which the ethics office notes is illegal under New Jersey’s workplace discrimination law. Mrs. Mildon told investigators she saw herself as a neutral party whose “job was to make everybody happy.” The office’s director, Johanna Barba Jones, found that she “could not have been a neutral in this matter.” Ms. Bianco later sued and settled for $400,000. Soon after the first settlement, Mrs. Mildon became Mr. Trump’s personal lawyer, and in March 2025 he made her the state’s top federal prosecutor.

Her lawyer, Kim D. Ringler, calls the claims “narrow” and the complaint “the first step in a long process.” She is right about the process, and the process is the point.

Look at what Mrs. Mildon is getting: a three-year inquiry that included an interview with her, a written complaint she can answer, a hearing before a special ethics adjudicator, and a final decision by the New Jersey Supreme Court. Every step is slow, documented and open to challenge. Nobody ran her tattoos through a database. Nobody subpoenaed her Venmo account to see who she had lunch with. The state’s lawyers will have to prove their case, and she will have every chance to say they cannot.

That is not a scandal; it is how accusing someone is supposed to work. The scandal is the contrast. The woman who ran the prosecutions arising from Delaney Hall gets three years of due process for her own alleged misconduct. The people arrested outside its gates get a financial dragnet built to find a crime that the government had already decided must exist.

Her tenure ended in December 2025, when a panel of federal judges ruled she had been serving unlawfully. She moved straight into a post overseeing U.S. attorneys nationwide for the attorney general, which she held until early 2026.

The memo argues with itself

Here is where the memo becomes more interesting than its critics might expect. Most of it is not a list of triumphs. It is a long complaint.

The I.R.S. agents blame the FBI’s “continual turnover” of task-force leadership and its “culture of lack of information sharing.” Their own colleagues ignored their emails. Overall, the memo says, opportunities have been limited. The FBI did not respond to the Times’ request for comment.

Then comes the sentence that matters most. The group did identify some potential financial crimes, the memo says, but they are not related to the task force’s work “because there was no specific threat directed at a government entity, its property or a government employee.”

Put plainly, career investigators went looking for left-wing money behind political violence and could not find it. What they found was ordinary financial wrongdoing with no connection to violence. That is what a working investigation produces when the premise is false. It is also consistent with everything else on the public record. Prosecutors have charged Tyler Robinson, 23, with killing Kirk, and they have not accused him of working with any outside group. The crime that launched the whole effort has no financier.

The memo’s answer to its own findings is the part to watch. The group recommends being given a broader remit, so it can focus on “investigating the misuse of 501(c)(3) organizations and/or individuals with nexus to terrorism.” In the language of Part XXVI, when the evidence does not fit the brief, the brief gets enlarged. The investigators are not asking for a better theory. They are asking for a bigger net.

And there is a prize at the end of that net. Under federal law, any organization the federal government officially identifies as a terrorist group immediately loses its tax-exempt status. No audit and no hearing are needed, and no New Jersey Supreme Court reviews the decision. The label does the work. That is why “domestic terrorism” is the phrase this administration keeps reaching for. It is not a description of what the agents found. It is the only category that would let the government skip the process Mrs. Mildon is now getting.

The shortcut nobody has signed yet

The Times also reports that I.R.S. officials have discussed a new process for revoking exemptions. According to three people familiar with the discussions, any organization that has been indicted, or whose senior leaders have been indicted, would face an expedited audit lasting a matter of months and could lose its tax-exempt status at the end of it. I.R.S. lawyers have raised concerns about the legal rigour of such a process, and the agency has not implemented it.

An indictment is an accusation, and this series has spent thirty-seven parts documenting a Justice Department whose accusations keep falling apart once a judge looks at them. Under the proposed process, the accusation would not need to hold together. The indictment would trigger the audit, and the audit could end the organization before any trial began.

The obvious candidates are already on the record. In the days after Kirk’s killing, the Justice Department pushed U.S. attorneys to investigate George Soros’s Open Society Foundations, a major donor to liberal causes. In spring 2026, the government charged the Southern Poverty Law Center, which has tracked hate groups for decades, with defrauding its donors. Under the proposed rule, that charge alone would put the Center’s tax status on a clock. The I.R.S. lawyers who have objected so far deserve the credit for the fact that it is still only a proposal.

Everything but taxes

The final pages the Times saw describe the I.R.S. detail’s other assignments. It worked on the federal response to the shooting at the White House Correspondents’ Association dinner in spring 2026, and to a shooting at a mosque in San Diego in May. It also assisted with the Ultimate Fighting Championship event Mr. Trump hosted on the White House lawn in June, though nobody seems able to say what it did there.

Criminal Investigation, roughly 3,000 people, has become the administration’s spare pair of hands. An inspector general’s report in 2026 detailed the time I.R.S. criminal agents spent patrolling the streets of Washington and Memphis, and found that it came at the expense of criminal tax investigations. Somewhere in America this year, a payroll fraud went uninvestigated while a tax agent stood guard at a cage fight.

“Too tough”

The same week the memo surfaced, Mr. Trump gave his first interview since the midterms. As Erica L. Green reported for The New York Times on November 8, 2026, he told 60 Minutes that his deportation policy “might be too tough,” and added: “That hurt me.” He conceded that people on “the sort of pretty far right,” including some in his administration, “would like everybody taken out.”

The concession came the same day he named Stephen Miller, the architect of that crackdown, as his chief of staff in place of Susie Wiles. Only one of those decisions governs what agents do on Monday morning, and the day after the election Miller was on a call pressing officials to carry on exactly as before.

So the protesters at Delaney Hall, who were objecting to the very policy the president now calls too tough, remain in an I.R.S. file of subpoenaed payments and tattoo matches.

The interview ended with Ross Douthat asking whether Mr. Trump could forgive his political enemies, naming Nancy Pelosi and James Comey, if that were all it took to get into heaven. The president said he could not.

This series has resisted calling the Enemies List a literal list. Asked to choose between his enemies and his salvation, he chose his enemies, on camera. Then he went back to an administration in which the tax agency keeps an appendix.

The scorecard

  • Unnamed targets, four (new). Method: Criminal investigations opened by I.R.S. agents on the FBI “domestic terrorism” task force. Status: Names held in a two-page appendix the Times did not see; current status unknown.

  • Unnamed tax-exempt groups, four (new). Method: Referred by the task force’s I.R.S. group to the civil audit division. Status: Unknown; taxpayer privacy law bars disclosure.

  • People arrested at the Delaney Hall protests (new). Method: Subpoenaed Venmo records, review of businesses believed to fund them, facial-recognition and tattoo-analysis databases. Status: No terrorism-financing charges reported.

  • Rep. LaMonica McIver. Method: Charged in May 2025 after an encounter with federal agents at Delaney Hall, by the office Alina Habba then led. Status: Pleaded not guilty in 2025.

  • Southern Poverty Law Center. Method: Charged in spring 2026 with defrauding donors. Status: Would face an expedited revocation audit under the I.R.S. proposal, not yet adopted.

  • Open Society Foundations. Method: Justice Department pushed U.S. attorneys to investigate after Kirk’s killing. Status: No charges reported.

  • James B. Comey and Nancy Pelosi. Method: Named to Mr. Trump on 60 Minutes as enemies he could not forgive, even for heaven. Status: Comey faces the pending prosecutions covered in Parts XXXV and XXXVII.

Alina Habba Mildon does not go on the scorecard. She is not a target of political prosecution. She is a former enforcer now going through an ordinary disciplinary process, and she is entitled to every protection that process gives her. The trouble is that she is getting those protections and the people in the appendix are not.

The Enemies List began as a roster of famous names. It now includes people whose names the agency’s own memo keeps on two separate pages, held back from the reporter who saw the other six. When a campaign against political opponents goes from naming its targets to concealing them, it has not become more careful. It has only become harder to watch.
