“The lights going out in New York City” — the kind of visible, singular catastrophe that has historically unlocked regulation.

Near the end of a conversation about AI regulation on The Daily, the New York Times podcast, host Michael Barbaro asked Tripp Mickle the question that this series has been circling for forty-five parts.

“I wonder if in the end what ultimately may lead to meaningful regulation of this industry is what leads to meaningful regulation of any industry — some sort of massive crisis.”

Mickle, who covers technology for the Times and has spent weeks reporting on the internal White House battle over the executive order, did not flinch. “Given the paralysis on Capitol Hill, I think that’s the right way of looking at this. But there needs to be not just a Mythos that isn’t released publicly — there needs to be a Mythos that has been released and the lights need to go out in New York City or something like that.”

And then, almost in the same breath, Mickle offered the counterexample that undoes the comfort of the crisis theory.

“I can’t help but think that here we are, fifteen years into the social media revolution that Facebook brought about. We’ve been dealing with tremendous polarisation. There have been mental health issues for young people. There have been concerns about child sexual abuse material. And there’s not been a single rule. That segment of the tech industry remains unregulated.”

The slow, distributed crisis of social media — a teenager walking into an algorithmic world with no Grand Canyon moment, yet profound accumulated harm. The counterexample that haunts AI’s regulatory path.

Two histories. One in which crisis produces regulation. One in which crisis produces nothing. The question this essay asks is which history AI is following — and whether we can know before the answer is irreversible.

The Crisis Theory of Regulation

The crisis theory is not cynicism. It is the historical record.

The Federal Aviation Administration was created in 1958, after a series of mid-air collisions over populated areas — most notably the Grand Canyon collision of 1956, which killed 128 people and made the inadequacy of existing airspace management impossible to ignore. The crashes did not cause the regulation. They made the regulation politically possible. Before the crashes, the airline industry had been largely self-governing. After them, the argument that safety was too important to leave to the industry became unanswerable.

The 1956 Grand Canyon mid-air collision headline that made the creation of the FAA politically inevitable. Crisis turns warnings into action.

The Securities and Exchange Commission was created in 1934, after the 1929 crash and the Depression it produced exposed the frauds, manipulations, and structural inadequacies of unregulated financial markets. The Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial and investment banking in 1933. Dodd-Frank, the most comprehensive financial reform since the New Deal, was passed in 2010, two years after the 2008 financial crisis nearly destroyed the global economy. In each case, the regulation followed the catastrophe, not the warning.

The warnings were always there. Charles Mitchell of National City Bank was selling speculative securities to retail investors and inflating asset prices throughout the 1920s. Anyone who looked could see the fragility. The regulation waited for the crash.

The warnings about AI are also there. They are documented in the forty-five parts of this series. They are in the polling data — 57 percent of voters saying AI’s risks outweigh its benefits. They are in the academic research — the Carnegie Mellon study showing measurable cognitive decline after ten minutes of AI use, the Copenhagen study showing systematic political bias in AI models. They are in the papal encyclical, the Leiden Declaration, the Gillibrand and Slotkin bills, and the testimony of a company’s own co-founder at the Vatican that his company’s incentives can conflict with doing the right thing.

The crisis theory of regulation says: none of this is sufficient. What is sufficient is the lights going out.

The Mythos Trigger

The executive order 6signed on June 2, 2026 is the clearest evidence yet that the crisis theory has some force — and that the crisis does not need to be a disaster to move the regulatory dial. It needs to be a credible demonstration that disaster is possible.

Anthropic’s Claude Mythos Preview was that demonstration. The company announced a model capable of identifying thousands of previously unknown software vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure at scale. Jen Easterly, the former CISA director, described the dual-use nature precisely: a model capable of finding dangerous vulnerabilities across widely used software could give defenders an extraordinary advantage. In the wrong hands, it could give adversaries the same advantage. “A powerful model released without meaningful access controls is not an American asset; it is a global one.”

Mythos was not released publicly. Anthropic restricted access to Project Glasswing — initially forty organisations, later expanded to 150 organisations in more than fifteen countries — specifically because the company judged the model too dangerous for general release. That restraint, Easterly noted in the Times, is “a signal of how seriously these companies judge the risks of what they have built.”

The restraint triggered the regulation. Microsoft called the administration saying this was a big deal. Jamie Dimon called. Bessent and Wiles worried about being blamed if a Mythos-equivalent were used to take down a hospital network. The crisis had not occurred. The credible demonstration that it could occur was sufficient to move the White House — barely, partially, after Sacks stripped the mechanism of its mandatory teeth — toward its first formal AI oversight action.

The crisis theory, revised: you do not always need the crash. You need the near-miss to be visible enough, and the near-miss to involve people with enough access to the people who make the decisions.

The University of Toronto computer science paper published the same week as the executive order provided the near-miss’s confirmation: researchers had used AI to create a computer worm capable of targeting any known flaw in the world’s computers. The paper was published. The worm was not deployed. The regulation arrived, such as it was, before the catastrophe.

The Social Media Counterexample

The problem with the revised crisis theory is Mickle’s counterexample.

Social media has produced fifteen years of credible near-misses. The 2016 election and Russian interference. The Cambridge Analytica scandal. The January 6 Capitol attack and the role of Facebook’s algorithm in radicalising participants. The teenage mental health crisis and the internal Facebook research showing the company knew Instagram was harming girls. The child sexual abuse material on multiple platforms. The New Mexico jury finding in March 2026 that Meta had misled people about the safety of its products and enabled sexual exploitation, ordering $375 million in damages.

Each of these was a near-miss. Each was visible. Each involved people with access to decision-makers. None produced federal regulation of social media. The United States still has no comprehensive federal law governing social media platforms, no Children’s Online Safety Act that has cleared both chambers and been signed, no federal standard for algorithmic transparency, no enforceable right for users to understand why they are seeing what they are seeing.

The difference between social media and the airline industry is not the severity of the harm. It is the diffusion of the harm. Plane crashes kill identifiable people in a single event. The harms of social media are distributed across millions of people over years, producing anxiety, polarisation, addiction, and suicide in ways that are difficult to attribute to any single platform decision, any single algorithm change, any single event that can serve as the regulatory inflection point. There is no Grand Canyon collision of social media. There are fifteen years of Grand Canyon collisions, each too diffuse to serve as the decisive moment.

AI harms may be more like social media than like plane crashes. The cognitive atrophy documented in Part 37. The oracle problem documented in Part 40. The crisis of agency documented in Part 42. The moral abdication enabled by the character sheet documented in Part 45. These are diffuse harms, distributed across populations, accumulating over time, resistant to the single-event narrative that crisis-driven regulation requires.

Unless Mythos escapes. Unless the lights go out.

The Obernolte/Trahan Moment

The legislative response Easterly pointed to — a bipartisan bill from Representatives Jay Obernolte of California and Lori Trahan of Massachusetts — represents something the series has been watching for since Part 1: a congressional response that does not wait for catastrophe.

The bill had not been released publicly as of this writing. What Easterly described suggests it would create focused, durable obligations for the most advanced models — robust safety and security testing, secure development practices, responsible controls on deployment — framed around the dual-use technology analogy. Not rules that treat every AI startup as a national security threat. Obligations proportionate to capability, targeted at the frontier.

If this framing holds, the Obernolte/Trahan bill would be the first attempt to create what the executive order explicitly forbids: a mandatory, durable oversight framework for AI models with demonstrated offensive capabilities. The executive order’s own text — the line Sacks insisted on, forbidding “mandatory licensing, preclearance, or permitting” — would need to be superseded by statute. Congress writing the law that the White House negotiated away.

Congressional hearings on AI — the political stage where a near-miss or full crisis could turn possibility into inevitable legislation.

The bipartisan signal is real. Sanders from the left calling for a moratorium and public ownership. Bannon from the right calling AI “totally incompatible with a free society.” Gillibrand and Slotkin from the Democratic centre introducing military AI constraints. Obernolte and Trahan from both parties on a cybersecurity framework. The political coalition for mandatory AI governance is assembling across lines that have not been aligned on anything in years.

What the coalition lacks is the triggering event. The crisis that makes the legislation inevitable rather than merely possible.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s Three Laws did not wait for catastrophe. They were written before the first robot was built, in full awareness that the catastrophe they prevented was not hypothetical — that a robot capable of physical strength without moral constraint would eventually harm someone, and that the harm, once it occurred, could not be undone by laws passed afterward.

The premise of the Three Laws was precautionary. Not crisis-driven. The laws came first because the humans who wrote them understood that coming second would mean coming too late.

The crisis theory of regulation is the inverse of the Asimov premise. It says: wait for the harm, then respond. Build the FAA after the crashes. Write Dodd-Frank after the collapse. The laws come second because the political system requires the evidence of harm before it can act.

The social media counterexample suggests a third possibility, which is the one this series has been dreading: the laws do not come at all. The harm is real but diffuse. The evidence accumulates but never produces the decisive moment. The regulatory window — which Lila Shroff and Matteo Wong noted in The Atlantic is “rapidly closing” — passes without the mandatory framework being established. And the technology, embedded in economic infrastructure generating a third to more than half of GDP growth, becomes too consequential to regulate in the ways that regulation would require.

Tripp Mickle put it plainly: the AI industry has “a really big bully stick,” and that bully stick is how much it is driving the US economy. The same thing that makes the industry dangerous to leave ungoverned makes it difficult to govern.

The crisis question does not have an answer yet. The near-miss — Mythos, contained — produced a voluntary order with mandatory governance explicitly prohibited. The next near-miss may produce the Obernolte/Trahan bill, or something stronger, or nothing at all. The catastrophe — the lights out, the hospital network down, the election oracle demonstrably compromised — may produce the comprehensive framework that forty-five parts of this series have been calling for. Or it may produce the same diffuse outrage that fifteen years of social media catastrophe produced, and the same absence of law.

What Asimov understood is that by the time the answer is clear, it is too late to change it. The Three Laws came first because they had to. The question this series cannot stop asking is whether the laws governing AI will also come first.

Isaac Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics — precautionary rules written before catastrophe, not after. The inverse of the crisis theory this essay examines

The evidence, as of June 2026, is mixed. The laws are beginning to come. Whether they are coming first — before the catastrophe that would make their absence irreversible — remains the question.
