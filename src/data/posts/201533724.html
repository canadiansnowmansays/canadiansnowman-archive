A formal U.S. federal appellate courtroom, representative of the D.C. Circuit deliberating amid converging court, market, and national-security pressures.

The supplemental briefs were filed on June 4. The D.C. Circuit has not yet ruled. In the appellate courts, this is normal — a panel that issued a per curiam order on its own motion, requesting additional briefing after oral argument, is working carefully, and careful panels do not rush. The written opinion could arrive in days. It could arrive in weeks. In complex cases involving novel statutory questions, it sometimes takes longer.

What is not normal is what is happening while the panel deliberates.

Three separate countdowns are running simultaneously — each one adding pressure to the ruling, each one ensuring that whenever the opinion arrives, it will land in a context considerably more complicated than the one in which the case began.

The first clock is in the courts. The second is on Wall Street. The third is inside Fort Meade.

Clock One: The August Deadline

The Presidential Directive that launched this litigation gave federal agencies six months — until late August 2026 — to fully transition away from Anthropic’s products. That deadline has not moved.

Judge Lin’s preliminary injunction in San Francisco currently prevents enforcement of the directive across most federal agencies. But the Ninth Circuit appeal of Lin’s ruling remains live. If the Ninth Circuit reverses Lin — which the government is actively arguing for — the August deadline is immediately operational, and federal agencies that have continued using Claude under the shelter of the injunction will face an abrupt transition requirement with weeks to spare.

The Pentagon’s internal position adds a layer of complexity. A March 6 memo from Pentagon Chief Information Officer Kirsten Davies told senior leaders that Anthropic use could continue beyond the phase-out period in “rare and extraordinary circumstances” for “mission-critical activities directly supporting national security operations where no viable alternative exists.” That memo was not a legal carve-out — it was an internal guidance document. It does not override the Presidential Directive. What it reveals is that even inside the Pentagon, the practical consequences of actually removing Anthropic from every system have been recognized as operationally significant.

A formal U.S. Court of Appeals courtroom setting. The D.C. Circuit’s ruling will interact with the live Ninth Circuit appeal and the late-August federal phase-out deadline.

The D.C. Circuit’s ruling will determine whether the supply chain designation survives judicial review. But even a favourable ruling for Anthropic at the D.C. Circuit does not automatically resolve the Ninth Circuit appeal or the August deadline. The litigation is running on two tracks, in two circuits, and the clocks on both are ticking.

Clock Two: The IPO

On June 1, 2026 — the same day Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier filed his complaint against OpenAI — Anthropic confidentially submitted its S-1 prospectus to the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The filing came days after the company closed a $65 billion Series H funding round, setting its valuation at approximately $965 billion. The October 2026 listing window on NASDAQ is the target. Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and Morgan Stanley are expected to compete for lead roles. If the debut goes as planned, Anthropic will join SpaceX and OpenAI as one of three trillion-dollar listings in a single calendar year — a sentence that would have been implausible eighteen months ago.

The prospectus must disclose material legal risks. The supply chain designation is a material legal risk. The D.C. Circuit appeal is a material legal risk. The Ninth Circuit appeal is a material legal risk. The criminal investigation by the Florida AG running parallel to the Florida Docket civil complaint — which names Anthropic’s products in passing as part of the broader AI harm landscape — is at minimum a reputational risk that belongs somewhere in the S-1. The August deadline is a material operational risk.

Institutional investors who read prospectuses for a living will see a company that a federal government agency has formally designated a threat to national security — using a statute designed for Huawei and ZTE — even as two federal courts have found that designation likely unconstitutional. They will note that the same executive branch is simultaneously deploying the company’s most advanced model in offensive cyber operations against foreign adversaries. They will price this. How they price it will depend, in part, on what the D.C. Circuit says.

The active trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange. Anthropic’s IPO timeline and S-1 disclosures make the D.C. Circuit opinion a material event for investors.

The Webcoda analysis put it with precision: if the court upholds the ban, it becomes a material risk event in the middle of the roadshow. If it strikes the ban down, it becomes a headline in the roadshow deck. Either way, the ruling is now effectively a valuation event. Three judges in Washington are writing an opinion that will, whether they intend it to or not, appear in Anthropic’s IPO risk factors.

Clock Three: The Engineers Inside Fort Meade

On June 5, the Financial Times reported what may be the most significant single development since the lawsuit was filed.

While the Department of War designates Anthropic a supply chain risk, while Pete Hegseth reaffirms that Claude cannot be trusted near military systems, while federal agencies are transitioning away from Anthropic products under the terms of a Presidential Directive — approximately six Anthropic engineers are embedded inside the National Security Agency, deploying Mythos, the company’s most advanced AI model, for offensive cyber operations.

The operations, according to sources familiar with the arrangement, include targeting the networks of China and Iran.

Mythos is not Claude. That distinction matters, and it is the government’s only available answer to the paradox the arrangement creates. Emil Michael, the Pentagon’s chief technology officer, has described Mythos as “a separate national security moment” — not a formal legal carve-out, not an exception written into any statute or court record, but a practical distinction the administration is drawing between the Department of War supply chain designation and the NSA intelligence partnership.

That distinction does not survive scrutiny as a legal theory. The NSA is part of the same executive branch as the Department of War. The Presidential Directive ordered all federal agencies to transition away from Anthropic. The NSA is a federal agency. There is no public record of a formal exemption. There is Emil Michael’s press statement, which is not the same thing.

What Mythos is also matters. Anthropic’s own red team reported that Mythos Preview could find and exploit zero-day vulnerabilities in every major operating system and every major web browser when directed to do so. The model is currently restricted to approximately 150 organizations across 15 countries, a list curated specifically for its sensitivity. It is, by Anthropic’s own assessment, an instrument capable of nation-state-grade offensive cyber operations. The NSA has decided it is too valuable to leave on the table — and Anthropic, which refused to remove guardrails protecting Americans from domestic surveillance, has placed engineers inside the NSA to make the deployment work.

NSA headquarters at Fort Meade. Reports describe Anthropic engineers embedded here to support Mythos for offensive cyber operations, creating a sharp tension with the Pentagon designation.

The Canary, in its reporting on the arrangement, noted the apparent tension with Anthropic’s own usage guidelines. Anthropic’s response — implied rather than stated — is consistent with its February 27 statement: its two exceptions were always specifically about protecting Americans from autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance. Offensive cyber operations against foreign adversaries are, by that framing, on the other side of the line. The company’s ethics permit what the NSA is doing; they prohibit what the Department of War wanted.

That distinction is coherent as a moral framework. As a legal matter, it creates a government contradiction the courts have not yet been asked to address.

The Axios reporting added a detail that completes the irony: even as the Trump administration argues in court that Anthropic poses a national security risk, agency heads across the federal government are scrambling to protect their own systems against potential cyberattacks — attacks that might be carried out using Mythos, the very model Anthropic engineers are deploying at the NSA. The government is simultaneously suing to remove Anthropic’s products from federal systems and deploying Anthropic’s most dangerous product to attack foreign ones.

No one in the administration has publicly reconciled these positions. The D.C. Circuit may be the first institution required to try.

What Hegseth’s June 4 Rejection Actually Concedes

The June 4 rejection of reconsideration deserves more scrutiny than it has received.

The panel, at the May 19 argument, raised the possibility of a pause while the Pentagon reconsidered its position. This was an invitation — a diplomatic off-ramp that would have allowed the government to step back from the designation without a court order compelling it. The panel does not extend invitations casually. When a court asks whether a party wants to reconsider, it is usually signalling something about where the deliberation is heading.

Hegseth rejected the invitation. In doing so, he also quietly revised the theory that had driven the original action.

The original designation documents, and the government’s initial public framing, had leaned heavily on the “remote kill switch” theory — the idea that Anthropic could reach into deployed military systems and disable Claude in the middle of active operations. This was the “headline-grabbing theory” that Kelly Dunbar dismantled at oral argument: factually incorrect, she told the panel, because Anthropic has no post-deployment visibility or control over Claude once it is running in classified networks.

In the June 4 reconsideration rejection, Hegseth clarified that the supply chain risk designation was based on a loss of trust and pre-deployment risks — not on any belief that Anthropic could alter Claude in real time after deployment. Anthropic immediately disputed even this narrowed framing.

But the clarification is a concession embedded in a defiance. The government abandoned its most aggressive theory — the kill switch — under pressure from the argument, while maintaining the designation the theory was used to justify. The designation stands; the reason given for it has shifted. In administrative law, this is precisely the kind of post-hoc rationalization the arbitrary-and-capricious standard under the Administrative Procedure Act is designed to catch. The government cannot designate a company a supply chain risk on Theory A, watch Theory A collapse under judicial scrutiny, and then recharacterize the designation as having always been based on Theory B.

Whether the D.C. Circuit will apply that reasoning to the June 4 submission is the question the supplemental briefs were, in part, designed to address.

The White House Wildcard

One element of the litigation has received less attention than it deserves: the apparent disconnect between the Pentagon and the White House.

Multiple reports have described meetings between Dario Amodei and White House officials as “productive.” An executive order on civilian AI access — which would have created a legal pathway for Anthropic to continue non-military government work even under the supply chain designation — was described as being workshopped. It has not appeared.

The Pentagon and the White House appear to be conducting two separate conversations about Anthropic, and those conversations have not converged. Hegseth reaffirms the designation. The NSA deploys Mythos. The White House has productive meetings. The executive order does not materialize.

This incoherence is not unusual for a large government managing competing institutional interests. But it places the D.C. Circuit in an awkward position: it is being asked to adjudicate a dispute in which the executive branch’s own position has shifted repeatedly, its own agencies are acting in ways that contradict the legal theory being argued in court, and the political resolution that might moot the litigation has been described as imminent for three months without arriving.

Courts prefer not to write opinions that events render irrelevant before the ink dries. The longer the panel deliberates, the more the landscape shifts. The August deadline approaches. The IPO roadshow begins. The Mythos operations continue.

Three Verdicts Pending

The Safety Trial is not one proceeding. It is, at this moment, at least three.

The D.C. Circuit is deliberating on the supply chain designation — the question of whether the Pentagon can use a foreign-adversary statute against a domestic company for its publicly stated safety beliefs.

The Ninth Circuit is considering the government’s appeal of Judge Lin’s California preliminary injunction — the question of whether the Presidential Directive and the Hegseth Directive survived constitutional scrutiny.

And the underlying merits in California, before Judge Lin, remain to be argued on a full evidentiary record once the preliminary injunction phase resolves. The constitutional questions she identified — First Amendment retaliation, Fifth Amendment due process, APA violations — have not been finally adjudicated. They have only been assessed for likelihood of success.

Three courts. Three proceedings. One question underneath all of them: can the United States government punish a private company for the content of its AI safety commitments?

The supplemental briefs are filed. The clocks are running. The opinion will come.

The Safety Trial will be there when it does.
