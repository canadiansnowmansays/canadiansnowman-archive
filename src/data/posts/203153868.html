For thirty-six instalments, this series has documented a machine that does not stop. Tennessee eliminated a majority-Black district in a week. Louisiana signed Cleo Fields’s seat out of existence and discarded forty-five thousand ballots to do it. Alabama ran out the election calendar. Florida’s Supreme Court held the door while a voter-written constitutional amendment was walked around. The machinery delivered, again and again, across the Deep South, with extraordinary speed.

On June 16 and 17, in Georgia, it stopped.

It stopped in the two places this series had been pointing to since Part XXXI — the gubernatorial runoff and the special redistricting session, both scheduled for the same forty-eight hours, both expected to advance the same consolidation of power. Instead, within a single day, the man at the centre of both lost the first and was denied the second.

Tuesday: The Runoff

Rick Jackson projected as the winner of the Georgia Republican gubernatorial runoff. Georgia Republicans chose a different standard-bearer — declining to hand the governorship to the most documented election denier on the ballot.

Part XXXII of this series was titled “The Man From the Willard Hotel.” It documented, in exhaustive detail drawn from a New York Times investigation, what Burt Jones did in the winter of 2020 and 2021 — the texts demanding Brad Raffensperger be brought down “a notch or 4 spots,” the effort to organise a special session to override the election, the State Senate hearings arranged for Rudolph Giuliani’s false claims, the amicus brief, the fake elector slate, the trip to the Willard Hotel on January 5, the letter carried to Mike Pence’s residence. That essay closed with a single open question: not what Jones would do with power, since he had already shown that in 2020, but whether Georgia would give it to him.

On Tuesday, Georgia answered. As NBC News reported, Rick Jackson defeated Jones in the Republican gubernatorial runoff, 52.7 percent to 47.3 percent — 373,406 votes to 335,847. Jones had carried a Trump endorsement and a late nod from Governor Brian Kemp. He had been the frontrunner since the moment the president backed him. He lost anyway.

“Looks like we’re going to come up a little short here tonight,” Jones told his supporters, “and that’s unfortunate. We had a great Election Day. We just didn’t have enough runway to get it all the way there.”

The runway had run out. The fake elector who once texted that everything had “gone off the rail” watched his own campaign do exactly that.

It is worth being precise about what this loss is and is not. Jackson is no moderate; he is a billionaire healthcare executive who campaigned as the authentic MAGA outsider, telling voters he could not be bought and would not back down. This was not Georgia repudiating Trumpism. It was Georgia Republicans choosing one version of it over another — and in doing so, declining to hand the governorship to the most documented election-denier on the ballot. The Loyalty Trials, the companion series tracking Trump’s primary scorecard, gains another entry: the president’s endorsement, decisive in so many of the retribution primaries this series has chronicled, did not carry Burt Jones across the line.

Wednesday: The Session

Lt. Gov. Burt Jones speaking during his gubernatorial campaign. Despite a Trump endorsement, he lost the Republican runoff on June 16 — and the next day watched the redistricting session he championed get canceled.

The morning after his defeat, Jones still had one instrument of power left. As lieutenant governor, he presides over the Georgia Senate. And on June 17, that Senate was convening for the special redistricting session Kemp had called — the session this series flagged in Parts XXXI, XXXII, and XXXIII as the day Georgia’s machinery would grind forward to redraw the maps governing four million Black Georgians.

It did not grind forward. As the New York Times reported, less than an hour before the session was gavelled in, top Republican legislative leaders stood under the capitol dome and announced that redistricting was off. House Speaker Jon Burns said Republicans would not take up congressional or legislative maps for the 2028 cycle during the session. The crowd that had gathered inside erupted in cheers.

The reversal was not granted from above. It was forced from below. Civil rights activists and protesters had assembled in Atlanta — the cradle of the movement — marching on the capitol, gathering that morning at Big Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church, long a cathedral of the civil rights struggle. “We have been here before,” Bishop Michael L. Mitchell told them. Gerald Griggs, a civil rights lawyer, claimed the credit plainly: “They said protesting doesn’t work.” This time, he said, it did.

The Republican leaders offered more decorous explanations — a desire for a methodical process, a wish to see how the legal challenges in other states fared before exposing Georgia’s maps to the courts. Senate President Pro Tempore Larry Walker said Kemp’s instruction had been to consider maps for 2028, and that leadership judged it imprudent to rush. But underneath the procedural language sat the calculation the Times made explicit: redistricting for 2028 less than five months before a 2026 election with the governorship, a Senate seat, and statewide offices on the ballot risked energising Democrats in precisely the swing state where Republicans could least afford it. Georgia has become the most important swing state in the Deep South. The Atlanta suburbs, the Black political strongholds, the David Scott vacancy, the simple arithmetic of a competitive November — all of it made the maps too expensive to draw right now.

And Burt Jones, presiding over the chamber, freshly defeated, watched Democrats rise repeatedly to condemn the assault on Black voting power. Eventually he cut them off.

“Y’all know it isn’t happening, right?” he said. “I don’t know about you, but I’m ready to get out of here.”

Protesters inside the Georgia State Capitol on June 17, 2026, rallying against the special redistricting session. Organized civic pressure helped force Republican leaders to shelve the maps — for now.

It was, in its way, the perfect closing line for the man this series has tracked. The day before, he had demanded the redistricting proceed — “Failure to deliver is not an outcome I am willing to accept,” he had said. Twenty-four hours after losing his own bid for power, he was gavelling out a session that would not deliver the maps he wanted, telling the chamber he was ready to leave. The machinery he had championed for the better part of a year stopped under his own gavel, on his way out the door.

Why Georgia Was Different

This series has been careful not to mistake a pause for a victory, and Georgia demands the same discipline. But it is worth understanding why the machinery that rolled through Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama, and Florida stalled here.

The answer is that Georgia is not those states. In Tennessee, Louisiana, and Alabama, Republicans hold the governorship and legislative supermajorities, and the political cost of redrawing maps was low. In Georgia, Republicans control the levers too — but the margins are thin, the electorate is genuinely competitive, and the Deep South’s most important swing state has a Black and Democratic political infrastructure, built across decades of movement organising, large enough to make aggressive gerrymandering genuinely risky. The 2026 primary saw Democratic turnout exceed Republican turnout for the first time since 2006. The protesters who filled the capitol were not a symbolic presence. They were a political fact the leadership had to price in.

Democracy fought back in Georgia in the most concrete sense this series has documented — not through a court ruling, not through a single senator’s conscience, but through the oldest mechanism in the republic: people physically assembling to make a thing too costly to do. Saira Draper, a Democratic state representative, named it exactly: “It shows that political civic engagement works when the people are paying attention and responding.”

Where Democracy Fights Back

Every instalment of this series asks the same question. Georgia, this week, gives the clearest affirmative answer it has yet produced — and the loudest warning against celebrating it.

Because every actor involved was careful to mark the victory as provisional. “This is a victory, for now,” said Representative Draper. Gerald Griggs, the civil rights lawyer, told the crowd: “In the spirit of our ancestors, let’s enjoy this for about five minutes — and understand it’s a long fight.” The Republican leaders did not say redistricting was dead. They said it was not happening during this session. The faction that wanted the maps drawn immediately — Jones among them, and Senator Greg Dolezal, who said he had always believed they should act as soon as possible — lost this round, not the war. State Senator Larry Walker was explicit that the Supreme Court’s weakening of the Voting Rights Act “left no doubt that we would need to draw new maps. The question was when.”

When, not whether. The machinery did not break. It was deferred, by a calculation of political cost that the November election could just as easily reverse. If Republicans hold Georgia comfortably this fall, the maps return in a quieter season, with the protesters gone home and the cameras elsewhere.

But for one week, in the cradle of the civil rights movement, the thing that does not stop was stopped. A documented participant in an attempt to overturn a presidential election was denied the governorship on a Tuesday, and on a Wednesday the maps he demanded were shelved before he could bang the gavel to begin. The crowd that did it knew exactly how temporary it was, and cheered anyway, and then reminded itself it was a long fight.

That is what democracy’s immune system looks like when it works — not a cure, but a response. Contingent, exhausting, purchased by people who showed up, and fully aware it will have to be purchased again.

Burt Jones was ready to get out of there. The people in the gallery had just shown him the door.
