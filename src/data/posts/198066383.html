“The island has been beautiful for six thousand years.” — Taiwan’s central mountain range at dawn, evoking the Portuguese name Formosa (“beautiful”).

We have come a long way from a hotel in Shimonoseki.

We began in Nanjing, in April 2026, with a Taiwanese opposition leader laying flowers at the tomb of Sun Yat-sen and explaining that Japan, not the civil war, not the Communist victory, not Beijing’s authoritarian consolidation, was responsible for the division of China and Taiwan. We went back to the Dutch, who arrived in 1624 looking for a trading post and stayed thirty-eight years until Koxinga’s fleet arrived with four hundred ships and a dynasty to mourn. We followed the thread through the Qing annexation, the fifty years of Japanese colonialism, the twenty-nine words of the Treaty of Shimonoseki, the deliberate void of the San Francisco Peace Treaty, the silicon shield, and the auction now underway in Washington. We visited Canada’s most distinguished diplomat, who raised his country’s flag in Tokyo in 1929 and spent the rest of his career trying to make his government understand things it preferred not to know.

Now we must try to say something honest about where all of this leads.

The Taiwan question is not a problem awaiting a solution. It is, as this series title suggests, a beautiful problem — beautiful in the mathematical sense, meaning elegantly constructed, internally consistent, and resistant to simple resolution. Every element of it reinforces every other element. Taiwan’s legal ambiguity reinforces the impossibility of clear international commitment to its defence. The impossibility of clear commitment reinforces Beijing’s calculation that the window for resolution is open. That calculation reinforces the preparations — military, diplomatic, economic — that make the situation more dangerous with each year it goes unresolved. And the danger reinforces the reluctance of the international community to push toward resolution, because resolution requires confrontation, and confrontation is costly.

Four hundred years of this history have produced one reliable lesson: the problem does not resolve itself. Every time the international community has declined to address Taiwan’s status clearly — in 1895, in 1945, in 1951, in 1972, in 1979, in the various moments since when clarity might have been possible — the ambiguity has deepened and the stakes have risen. The deliberate void that the San Francisco conference built in 1951 has grown more dangerous every decade, not less. The argument that maintaining the status quo is the safe option grows weaker with every patrol that the PLAN makes around the island’s perimeter, with every arms sale that arrives years late or not at all, with every American administration that discovers a new reason why this particular moment is not the right moment to push back.

The Beijing summit of May 14-15, 2026, was the latest of these moments, and perhaps the most revealing. Xi Jinping told Donald Trump that Taiwan is “the most important issue” in the bilateral relationship and that mishandling it would produce “clashes and even conflicts.” Trump told Fox News afterward that he wants things to “stay the way they are,” which sounds like a commitment to the status quo until you notice what followed: wavering on the arms sales, dismissing the legal commitments, calling Taiwan “a place,” speculating that China might move after he leaves office. The status quo that Trump says he supports is a status quo in which America’s commitments are increasingly hollow, Taiwan’s arms pipeline is increasingly uncertain, and Beijing’s confidence is increasingly justified. Staying the way things are, when the way things are is a trajectory toward abandonment, is not stability. It is managed decline.

Here is what the evidence of this series supports, stated plainly.

Taiwanese democracy in action — citizens defending their right to self-determination. One of Asia’s most successful political transformations in a single generation

Taiwan is a functioning democracy of twenty-three million people. Its government has the consent of its population. Its population has consistently and clearly expressed a preference for self-determination over absorption into the People’s Republic of China. These facts do not disappear because they are inconvenient for Beijing, or because the San Francisco Peace Treaty failed to specify a recipient for Japanese sovereignty, or because the Trump administration finds Taiwan useful primarily as a semiconductor supplier and a tariff negotiation variable.

Beijing’s claim to Taiwan rests on a theory of historical sovereignty that has genuine weight but cannot substitute for the living consent of the people whose future it seeks to determine. The weight of the historical argument — the wound of 1895, the wartime agreements, the principle of state continuity — is real. It does not override the democratic principle that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. It never has, and it should not start now.

The American security commitment to Taiwan, always ambiguous by design, is more genuinely uncertain now than at any point since the Taiwan Relations Act was passed. The transactional logic of the current administration creates conditions in which Taiwan could be traded — not sold outright, but gradually, incrementally, in a series of adjustments to the strategic landscape that cumulatively produce the result without any single decision being clearly defensible as betrayal. This is the most likely path by which Taiwan’s de facto independence ends: not a dramatic military invasion that the world can respond to clearly, but a negotiated diminishment of commitments and space, conducted across multiple summits — Beijing in May, Washington in the autumn, and whatever follows — in language designed to be interpreted charitably.

The military dimension is real but not destiny. China’s military capacity across the strait has grown substantially. The window in which Taiwan could be defended at acceptable cost to the United States is narrowing. But military force remains enormously costly to the initiator as well as the defender, and the economic consequences of a conflict that disrupted TSMC’s production would reverberate through every major economy including China’s. Glaser and Hsiao are probably right that Beijing is playing a long game — that the PLA purges suggest Xi is not prepared to use force imminently, and that Beijing believes time is on its side. The military option exists. It is not inevitable. What makes it more or less likely is the calculation Beijing makes about what the international community would do — and that calculation is shaped, more than anything else, by what the international community actually does.

So what should happen?

The status quo should not be allowed to deteriorate quietly while each successive administration finds reasons not to address it. Taiwan’s meaningful participation in international organisations — the World Health Assembly, Interpol, the International Civil Aviation Organization, the bodies that govern the global commons — should be supported rather than blocked by the democracies that claim to value multilateralism. Taiwan’s democratic achievement should be named as what it is: one of the most remarkable political transformations of the late twentieth century, a society that emerged from martial law into full democracy within a generation, and that has maintained that democracy in the face of constant pressure from a nuclear-armed neighbour.

Any resolution of Taiwan’s status must have the consent of Taiwan’s people. Not as a procedural nicety but as the foundational condition of legitimacy. A resolution imposed over their heads — by Washington and Beijing across a series of summits, by the gradual erosion of the commitments that make Taiwanese self-governance possible, by the quiet adjustment of the diplomatic landscape that leaves Taiwan with fewer and fewer options — is not a resolution. It is a conquest conducted in diplomatic language, and it should be named as such by anyone who claims to believe that democratic consent matters.

The argument that this position is unrealistic — that the power differential is too great, that the American commitment is too uncertain, that the world cannot afford a confrontation with a nuclear-armed China over the preferences of twenty-three million people — is the argument that was made in every previous iteration of the Taiwan question, and it has never led to a stable outcome. The stability it purchases is temporary and declining. The instability it defers is compounding.

There is a Canadian dimension to all of this, and we should end there.

Hugh Keenleyside died in 1992, at the age of ninety-four, having lived long enough to see the Cold War end and the world he had spent his career navigating transformed beyond recognition. He never stopped believing that the knowledge governments acquire about the world obliges them to act on it — that understanding is not a spectator sport, that the diplomat who sees what is coming and stays silent is not a realist but a coward.

Canada is, at this precise moment, rediscovering what it means to be smaller than the power next door, to have sovereignty threatened by a neighbour that has decided its interests supersede yours, to need the support of a community of nations that is calculating its own interests while you wait. It is not a comfortable rediscovery. Canadians who are experiencing it for the first time might find it clarifying.

Twenty-three million Taiwanese people have known this feeling for the entirety of their democratic existence. They have built, in the space that the deliberate void allowed, one of the most successful democracies in Asia — open, pluralist, creative, deeply committed to the institutions of self-governance that they have developed through enormous effort and at real cost. They have done this while sitting one hundred and thirty kilometres from the coast of a country that insists they are a province, under the nominal protection of a security guarantee of increasingly uncertain reliability, on top of the world’s most strategically important industrial infrastructure, in a legal status that the world’s most powerful nations have conspired to leave permanently unresolved.

They are not a problem to be managed. They are not a place, whatever the American president calls them. They are a people with a right to a future they have chosen.

The island has been beautiful for six thousand years. The Portuguese saw it from the sea in 1544 and called it Formosa and sailed on. The Dutch came and built Fort Zeelandia and stayed thirty-eight years. Koxinga brought four hundred ships and expelled them in nine months. The Qing came and debated whether to keep it. Japan came and built railways and took everything. The Republic of China came with two million mainlanders and imposed martial law for thirty-eight years. And the people of Taiwan emerged from all of it with something that none of the powers that had passed through their island had given them: a democracy they had made themselves.

That democracy is now at its most dangerous moment. The forces arrayed against it are larger, better resourced, and more strategically positioned than at any point in the past seventy years. The commitment to its survival that previous American administrations maintained — imperfectly, transactionally, but genuinely — is being reconsidered by a president who has returned from Beijing calling the island a place, wavering on its weapons, and telling its people to feel neutral about their prospects.

What the world does with this moment will define what the international order actually means for the next half century. Not what it says it means. What it means.

He was right. His government was not listening.

Some things do not change. But some things must.

This concludes Formosa: The Beautiful Problem.
