At 1:12 in the morning on November 4, 2026, with the midterm results in, Elon Musk posted a single line on X: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.”

Part XXIII of this series treated that post as a punchline, and it worked as one. Musk had just watched somewhere between $445 million and $475 million of his money, depending on who is counting, buy almost nothing. Ken Paxton lost Texas. Republicans lost the House and, by all appearances, the Senate. A man who had spent the year talking about the end of democracy woke up to find that democracy had answered him.

But the post was not only a joke, and this instalment takes it at its word. Look at what else happened on November 3, and at what Musk’s own companies have already filed with the federal government, and the line reads less like a wisecrack than a business plan. It is also, I think, the most honest thing he has said about what he wants: a data centre with no neighbours.

The night the cloud lost

Start with what happened on the ground, because the national results buried it.

Matteo Wong reported in The Atlantic on November 4 that residents of at least 10 states voted on dozens of measures to restrict data-centre development. More than 30 local referendums passed, in red counties and blue ones, and by his count only one failed, in North Dakota. At least three mayors, in Oklahoma and Ohio, were recalled for championing local data-centre projects. At least nine governors who support a statewide data-centre ban will be in office next year. Kathy Hochul, whose New York adopted the first statewide pause on hyperscale projects in July, won easily. Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott in Texas partly by hammering his support for data centres and his late change of heart. Democrats in Pennsylvania campaigned on the issue and took the state Senate.

Wong is careful not to overclaim, and so should I be. A September poll found that AI was the top issue for fewer than one per cent of voters. Prices, the war in Iran and an unpopular president did most of the work. But that caution cuts the other way too. Data-centre scepticism has become so common, on the left and the right, that it no longer separates the parties. Many of the moratoriums passed in counties Trump carried by double digits in 2024, after he told Americans on Truth Social that the only reason a community would refuse a data centre was if it wanted to stay backward and poor.

Paris Marx, whose new book Hyperscale is about exactly this, explained the anger in The Atlantic in October. A hyperscale data centre can house hundreds of thousands of servers and draw hundreds of megawatts from the grid, about what a medium-sized city uses. It needs water to cool. It hums all night and lights up the sky. And since ChatGPT arrived at the end of 2022, the industry has been building them everywhere. An August poll found three Americans in four opposed one near where they lived. Bernie Sanders called for a national moratorium. So, in his own fashion, did Ron DeSantis. Denmark has pushed data centres to the back of the queue for grid connections, and Thailand has paused 49 projects already under construction.

Put simply: the American public has found the one part of the AI boom it can see, and it has started saying no to it, one town hall at a time.

Musk’s own neighbours

Musk is not a bystander to this fight. He is one of its best examples.

His AI company, xAI, built its first Colossus supercomputer in a former Electrolux appliance factory in South Memphis in 2024, in a matter of months, to train the Grok chatbot. The site had nowhere near enough grid power, so the company brought in gas turbines. When it built Colossus 2 nearby, it put the power plant just across the state line, in Southaven, Mississippi.

On April 14, as the Southern Environmental Law Center announced, the NAACP, represented by the SELC and Earthjustice, sued xAI in federal court in northern Mississippi. The suit says the company has been running 27 gas turbines in Southaven without the air permits the Clean Air Act requires, about half a mile from homes and a mile from an elementary school, in a largely Black community that has spent decades living next to industry. In September, a group of Memphis homeowners filed a separate class action over the noise.

Then the federal government stepped in, and not on the side of the neighbours. According to Earthjustice, the Justice Department intervened in the case and tried to have the citizens’ suit dismissed, and asked the Fifth Circuit to halt the proceedings while it argued the point. The appeals court refused. On October 19, the district court ruled that the department has no power to unilaterally shut down a citizen suit under the Clean Air Act, and found no reasonable reading of the statute that would give it one. Eleven days later, the court denied xAI’s own motion to dismiss. The case goes on.

Readers of the Blanche Files will know the department in question. This is the same Justice Department that its attorney general told Fox News is the lawyer for everyone in the administration. In Memphis, it turned out to be willing to act as something close to a lawyer for Elon Musk as well. A judge said no.

That is the pattern worth noticing. Wherever Musk’s data centres touch the ground, they run into people who can sue, vote, recall a mayor, pack a zoning meeting or elect an attorney general. In Texas, as The Atlantic reported on November 10, the new attorney general, Nathan Johnson, says there will be no “force field” around Musk on his watch. Federal help can be bought, or at least courted. Local consent cannot.

The plan already on file

Which brings us back to the 1 a.m. post.

Orbital data centres are not a new idea for Musk, and they are not a joke inside his companies. On January 30, 2026, SpaceX asked the Federal Communications Commission for permission to launch as many as one million satellites as what it called an orbital data centre system. Three days later, on February 2, SpaceX announced it had acquired xAI in an all-stock deal, and named orbital compute as the reason, as Satellite Today reported. The combined company’s pitch is straightforward: the sun shines all the time in orbit, there is no grid to strain and no water to drink, and Starship will make it cheap to send up the hardware.

The plan moved from paperwork to purchase orders on November 10, a week after the midterms. Singulus Technologies, a mid-sized German equipment maker, announced that SpaceX had ordered a complete production line for heterojunction solar cells, among the most efficient kind made, worth more than €60 million, with more lines to follow, as Interesting Engineering reported. The first line is due in the first half of 2027 for SpaceX’s solar plant in Bastrop, Texas, and the cells are for Starmind, the name SpaceX has given its orbital computing satellites. Musk put the scale at 100 gigawatts a year of manufacturing equipment, and the purpose in one line on X: “This is for Starmind to create a sentient sun.” Singulus’s shares jumped by as much as 80 per cent, according to Euronews, for a company whose revenue last year was €48.3 million. There is a small irony in the location. Even the escape route to orbit begins in a Texas county with neighbours, a zoning process and, from January, an attorney general who has promised no force field.

There are serious engineering objections. Computers generate heat, and in a vacuum the only way to get rid of it is to radiate it away, which means radiators far larger than anything yet flown, as Astronomy magazine pointed out. Hardware fails, and nobody drives out to orbit to swap a board. Writers at The Motley Fool described the idea as a long-shot option sitting on top of SpaceX’s real businesses, launches and Starlink, rather than a proven moneymaker. That is a polite way of describing most of the Stack.

But I want to set the physics aside, because this series has learned not to bet against Musk on engineering alone. The more interesting question is what problem orbit solves for him. The official answer is energy. The answer the midterms suggest is jurisdiction.

The jurisdiction you can’t vote in

Think about who gets a say over a data centre in Memphis or Southaven. The city council. The county health department, which issues air permits. The state environmental agency. The neighbours, who can sue under federal law. The voters, who can replace the mayor, the governor or the attorney general. On November 3, Americans showed they were willing to use every one of those levers.

Now think about who gets a say over a satellite. Essentially one body: the FCC, a federal agency whose chair is appointed by the president and whose current chair, Brendan Carr, has welcomed SpaceX’s spectrum deals as more investment and more competition. There is no zoning board in low Earth orbit. There is no county health department. Nobody lives half a mile away. Nobody gets recalled.

That is why “data centres in space” is not merely the Stack’s habit, described in Part XXIII, of replacing a disappointing product with a bigger promise further away. It is a particular kind of promise. It is an exit. When the town said no to the factory, the answer was to move the factory somewhere no town exists.

Musk has done this before, on a smaller scale. When the local authorities around his rocket site in South Texas got in the way, the answer was to turn the site into a city. In May 2025, residents of Starbase, most of them SpaceX employees, voted to incorporate, and the company town got its own government. Mars, which Musk has talked about for two decades, is the same idea at the largest possible scale: a society begun from scratch, with nobody already there to object.

None of this is sinister in itself. Engineers have always been drawn to blank sheets. But a political reading of the Stack has to notice that the blank sheet keeps turning up right after the voters do.

What the midterms actually showed

The Silicon Valley version of the night, as Wong describes it, is that tech money met organised public anger for the first time on a national ballot and mostly lost. Crypto and AI super PACs spent about $250 million on federal races. Musk alone spent nearly half a billion. In California, tech investors poured more than $300 million into defeating a one-time tax on billionaires, and voters rejected that measure, so the industry did not lose everything. But the money that tried to buy a friendly Congress and friendly statehouses mostly bought defeats.

What it could not buy was the local veto. The more than 30 referendums Wong counted were small, cheap, and almost impossible to outspend, because they were about one specific building in one specific place. A billionaire can flood a Senate race with advertising. It is much harder to persuade the people who live beside a gas plant that they can’t hear it.

Musk’s post at 1:12 a.m. was the reaction of a man who understood that perfectly. If the problem is people, go where there are none.

A note from the Canadian side

From Canada, which gave Musk his first passport, the instinct is familiar enough. Every generation of resource money has preferred places where the locals were few, far away and easy to ignore. What has changed in our own country, slowly and incompletely, is the recognition that the people who live near a project get a real say in it, and sometimes a veto. That principle is costly and slow. It has also prevented a great deal of damage.

The American towns that voted on November 3 were exercising the same principle in a cruder form. They were saying that the costs of the AI boom, the power bills, the water, the noise and the air, have to be justified to the people who bear them. Musk’s response was to point at the sky.

It may never work. The radiators may be too big, the launch costs too high, the failure rates too punishing. But the impulse is the thing to watch. The Stack has always been about one man accumulating positions that would each be unremarkable in separate hands. Its newest layer is the most ambitious yet: not a company or a platform, but a place beyond the reach of anyone who might tell him no.
