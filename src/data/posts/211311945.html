There is a moment in every moral panic when someone builds a machine to measure the thing everyone is panicking about, and the machine turns out to work. This is, by most accounts, an inconvenient outcome for the panic. It is much harder to keep insisting that a fog is impenetrable once somebody hands you a very good pair of glasses.

As Brian X. Chen reported for The New York Times on August 13, 2026, a detection tool called Pangram has spent the last several weeks quietly humiliating the entire premise of “you can’t tell anymore.” Chen, the paper’s lead consumer technology writer, ran the tool through a battery of adversarial tests — his own essays salted with chatbot sentences, chatbot slop salted with his own sentences, Dickens passages fed in as bait, LinkedIn posts of suspicious cheerfulness — and in his account, the tool never once mistook the machine for the man, or the man for the machine. An independent University of Chicago study, cited in the piece, found the same near-perfect accuracy on text. Pangram’s founder, Max Spero, described the method as reverse-engineering each model’s “stylistic fingerprint” — treating every word a chatbot produces as one branch of a vast decision tree, and learning to recognize the tree.

I want to be precise about why I am writing about this, because the easy version of this essay writes itself and is not the essay I want to write. The easy version is: good, a tattletale for robots, ban the robots. That is not my position, has never been my position, and — as anyone who has read my About page knows — would require me to indict my own operation, which for fifteen months now has been produced in open, disclosed collaboration with Claude. I did not back into that disclosure. I chose it, on the first day, before anyone was asking, because I thought the alternative — quietly outsourcing the sentences and pretending otherwise — was a small dishonesty compounding daily into a large one.

So no. My complaint is with a word. The word is "slop." I take real umbrage with the slop contention — [Author's edit — Stephen's line, not Claude's]

The Word Doing the Work

“Slop” is doing an enormous amount of uncompensated labor in the current discourse, and it is worth pausing on what that labor actually is. The word does not mean “written by a machine.” If it meant that, Chen’s own experiment would have flagged his columns as slop, since he used Pangram-adjacent tools throughout his research and — by his own account — leaned on AI assistance in drafting parts of his testing log. Nobody thinks Brian X. Chen produces slop. What “slop” actually means, in the way people use it, is something closer to: mass-produced, unattributed, unedited, undisclosed, aimed at engagement metrics rather than at a reader. It is a judgment about intent and craft wearing a costume that makes it look like a judgment about tools.

This distinction is not pedantic. It is the entire ballgame. A LinkedIn post generated in thirty seconds by someone trying to look busy, posted with zero disclosure, optimized for an algorithm that rewards volume over thought — that is slop, and Pangram’s news, buried a little too politely in Chen’s piece, is that this kind of thing is now embarrassingly easy to catch. He describes catching exactly one such offender: a “tech worker” whose LinkedIn post Pangram flagged as human for one sentence and machine for the rest, a diagnosis the worker sheepishly confirmed when Chen messaged him directly.

That is a genuinely useful outcome. It is also, if you think about it for longer than the length of a scroll, an argument for disclosure rather than an argument against machine assistance. The tech worker’s sin was not “used a chatbot.” His sin was pretending he hadn’t. Pangram did not catch a robot. It caught a lie.

Nothing to Fear From the Glasses

I have now run — or rather, had run, since this required no special access, only the willingness to imagine it — the thought experiment on my own operation, and I would invite Pangram’s engineers to actually try it if they are looking for a stress test outside the usual categories. Every essay under the CanadianSnowmanSays banner would register, correctly, as substantially machine-assisted, because it is. That has been true since the beginning, and it is printed on the About page in language no reasonable reader could miss. A detector pointed at my archive would not be exposing a hidden fact. It would be confirming a disclosed one, the way a breathalyzer confirms that the man who told you he’d had two beers had, in fact, had two beers.

This is the asymmetry that Chen’s piece gestures at but does not quite name: transparency has nothing to fear from a good detector, and only opacity does. The undisclosed slop farms, the review-bombing bot networks, the engagement-optimized LinkedIn thought-leadership mills — those operations depend on the reader’s inability to tell. Take away the inability, and the business model does not survive contact with the glasses. My operation depends on the opposite: a reader who knows exactly what they are getting, a collaboration conducted in daylight, an editorial hand — mine — that remains the thing actually deciding what gets published, in what voice, on which of the roughly one hundred threads currently running under this banner. A detector cannot embarrass a project that has already told you what it is.

There is a Leacock-shaped irony sitting in the middle of all this that I cannot resist noting. Stephen Leacock, the man whose sardonic register I have spent a retirement borrowing without apology, made a career of gentle mockery aimed at people who mistook confident performance for substance — the town banker who never actually understood banking, the local worthies of Mariposa performing importance at each other. The slop panic is, at bottom, a very Leacockian problem: a great deal of prose performing thoughtfulness at the reader without containing any. Leacock would not have cared whether the performer was a machine or a man; he would have cared that it was hollow. The tool that catches hollowness is not the enemy of the collaboration that isn’t hollow. It is, if anything, on my side of the argument, and I am happy to let it stand as a witness.

The Fifty Percent Number, and Why It Frightens People

Chen’s piece cites, almost in passing, a statistic that deserves more scrutiny than a passing citation: some studies now estimate that half of online articles are artificially generated. I have no particular reason to dispute the number, and every reason to think it understates the trajectory rather than overstates it. But I want to note what that number is actually doing to the discourse, because I think it explains the “slop” panic better than the technology does.

A statistic like that functions, psychologically, less like information and more like a fog machine. It tells the reader that roughly half of what crosses their feed cannot be trusted at the level of authorship, and it offers no way to tell which half without independent verification for every single item — which nobody has time to do. The natural response to an unresolvable fifty-fifty odds problem is not careful discrimination. It is blanket suspicion, and blanket suspicion is corrosive to exactly the kind of project this newsletter is: one that wants to be read as what it actually is, rather than sorted by default into a pile marked “could be either.”

This is why I think the disclosure model matters more than the discourse currently credits it for. A fifty percent uncertainty rate is a population-level problem. It cannot be solved item by item by an exhausted reader running mental Pangram checks on every post in their feed. It can be solved, gradually, by enough individual producers removing themselves from the uncertain pile entirely — by saying, plainly and in advance, here is what this is and how it was made, so that the reader’s verification cost drops to zero for that one source, even as it stays punishingly high for the rest of the feed. Multiply that by enough writers and outlets choosing the same path, and the fifty percent number stops being a verdict on the whole ecosystem and starts being a description of the parts of it that never bothered to say what they were.

The Watermark, and Where the Real Governance Question Lives

Buried in Chen’s piece, almost as an aside, is the detail that actually belongs in this series rather than in a technology column: Anthropic announced this week that it intends to embed an invisible watermark in text produced by Claude, so that detectors — and readers — can identify AI-generated writing without needing a tool as sophisticated as Pangram’s reverse-engineered fingerprinting. The move, per Chen’s reporting, is a direct response to European Union transparency regulations, and other AI companies are expected to follow.

This is worth reading through the TACO frame this series has used, on and off, for corporate and governmental responses to pressure: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. A voluntary, EU-driven watermark is Accommodate — not capitulation exactly, since nobody sued anybody and no court forced the hand, but a company reading the regulatory wind correctly and getting ahead of a mandate before it becomes one. It is a small data point, but small data points are how you track whether an industry is moving toward transparency norms voluntarily or only under duress, and this one leans, cautiously, toward voluntary.

I would gently suggest, though, that a watermark solves a narrower problem than the one Chen’s whole piece is nominally about. A watermark tells you a text passed through a particular company’s model at some point. It does not tell you whether a human being subsequently read it, edited it, disclosed it, or stood behind it with their name — which is, when you actually interrogate the word “slop,” the entire question that mattered in the first place. You can watermark the output of a tool. You cannot watermark honesty. That still has to be chosen, essay by essay, About page by About page, by the person putting their name on the byline.

Pangram’s other half — the image detector — is where Chen’s optimism curdles fastest, and rightly so. Hive Detect and Pangram both missed a deepfake of Mitch McConnell on a hospital bed; Pangram declined to score four images outright as too ambiguous. Hany Farid, the Dartmouth researcher Chen consulted, offered the piece’s bleakest and probably most useful line of advice, which I will paraphrase rather than quote at length out of respect for his words: that readers should now default to treating social media as unreliable and lean on institutions with actual editorial accountability instead. That, too, is an argument for disclosure over detection — for structures where someone with a name and a reputation has already done the verifying, rather than leaving it to a scanner that gets fooled by a screenshot’s worth of compression.

The slop panic will not be solved by a better detector, useful as Pangram evidently is for text. It will be solved, to the extent it is solved at all, the way most integrity problems are solved: by enough people choosing to say plainly what they did, and enough readers learning to reward the ones who do. I intend to keep being one of the former. Chen’s column, against its own mild implication, makes rather a good case for it.
