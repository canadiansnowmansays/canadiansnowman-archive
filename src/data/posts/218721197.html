On 2 October, Steven Rattner published a guest essay in The New York Times under a confident headline: “Six Charts That Show Just How Much We Need A.I.” Rattner is a Wall Street financier who ran President Obama’s rescue of the car industry, and he writes the way a good banker builds a model: assumptions first, then the arithmetic, then the conclusion. “The walls are closing in on artificial intelligence,” he began, listing the data-centre moratoriums, the job fears and the alarm over safety. “All valid worries. But none are sufficient to turn away from one of the greatest technological innovations in history.”

Then he did something columnists rarely do. In the comments under his own essay, he invited readers to argue with him: “whether you disagree or agree, I’d welcome your thoughts.” Within hours there were 344 replies. Read together, the essay and its comments are the clearest picture this series has found of the gap between the economic case for AI and the public’s reasons for doubting it.

A disclosure, in keeping with this series’ premise: an essay about whether AI’s benefits will reach ordinary people is here being written with the help of an AI. Readers should keep that in mind.

The case, fairly stated

Rattner’s argument starts from economics at its most basic. Growth comes from two things, more workers or more output per worker, and only the second raises living standards. Workers rarely become more productive on their own; technology does it for them. His first chart shows American productivity growth stuck below its post-war pace for half a century, apart from the brief internet boom. Ending that slump, he argues, “should be a top national priority, and using A.I. is the most promising way.”

He has examples. Siemens says its AI assistant has cut unplanned factory maintenance time by a quarter. Kaiser Permanente’s AI scribe has saved its doctors nearly 16,000 hours of note-taking. John Deere’s camera system sprayed herbicide only where it saw weeds and saved farmers 31 million gallons of the mix in 2025. He has history too. Robert Solow complained in 1987 that the computer age showed up “everywhere but in the productivity statistics,” and a decade later it did. Henry Ford doubled his workers’ pay and still watched profits soar as the price of a Model T fell.

The jobs argument rests on two more charts. Between 1990 and 2025, computing created 4.1 million jobs for developers, programmers and analysts, while secretaries, typists, bookkeeping clerks, data-entry keyers, switchboard operators and file clerks together lost about 2.8 million. In 1900, 41 per cent of American workers were on farms; by 2022 the figure was 1.2 per cent, and the economy absorbed them. “No technological improvement in the history of the world,” Rattner writes, “has ultimately failed to create more jobs than it destroyed.”

What he concedes

It would be unfair to call this cheerleading. Rattner concedes more than most AI boosters, and two of his six charts make his critics’ case for them.

His chart of early winners and losers, from May 2023 to May 2025, shows data scientists up 36 per cent and electricians and HVAC installers rising, while data-entry keyers fell 18 per cent, telecom equipment installers 12 per cent, and customer-service representatives and bookkeeping clerks 9 per cent each. His chart of national income is starker. Wages and salaries have fallen to about 41 per cent of national income, while after-tax corporate profits have climbed to about 12 per cent. “Thus far,” he writes, “the gains from artificial intelligence have accrued not to workers, but to investors.”

His remedies are those of a centrist Democrat, and some are bolder than anything Washington is considering. He wants the beneficiaries to pay: a “token tax” on AI usage, the same idea Bill Gates puts forward in Part 87; an end to the generous capital-spending deductions AI companies receive; curbs on states’ sales and property tax exemptions for data centres; and a corporate tax rate edging back towards 35 per cent. On safety he goes further than the voluntary pledge the companies signed at the White House on 29 September. New AI products, he writes, “must be tested thoroughly by a governmental entity before they can be deployed,” and governments should consider mandating kill switches. “Those calling to stop A.I. development and those insisting that no regulation is necessary are both wrong.”

The gap between his proposal and that pledge is wider than it looks. As John Power reported for Al Jazeera on September 29, 2026, the document Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia signed, the Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities, asks the companies to install monitoring controls, appoint internal teams to oversee safeguards, hire independent external auditors and meet regularly to agree standards. It is nonbinding, and it says only that it "may make sense" to put such measures into law one day. President Trump explained the approach in his own words: "There's a belief that there should be tremendous self-regulation, and we automatically have regulation with the Department of Justice, the FBI, all of that." The A.I. researcher Toby Walsh asked the obvious question: "What other trillion-dollar industry marks its own homework?" Rattner, a Wall Street man who knows what an audit is for, wants a government examiner. What Washington has so far produced is an auditor the companies hire themselves.

The replies

The readers did not take the bait of the charts. They went straight for the step between the charts and the conclusion.

The most-recommended reply in the first hour, from Kristi in Atlanta, drew 45 recommendations, more than Rattner’s own invitation. “If you lose your job because it has become obsolete,” she wrote, “you will not reap any of the benefits.” Others brought their own numbers. HJ in San Francisco and DavidD in Massachusetts both pointed to the long gap between productivity and pay: productivity has risen far faster than the typical worker’s compensation for decades. Those are readers’ figures rather than Rattner’s, but the pattern they describe is well documented, and his own chart of the shrinking wage share says much the same thing. “In the long run, we are all dead,” DavidD added, borrowing Keynes’s line to answer Rattner’s use of Keynes.

John G. Franklin of Durham put the charts’ own message most sharply: “All the losses in your charts are solidly middle class. All the gains are solidly upper class.” Ken in Virginia questioned one of Rattner’s brightest statistics, that Americans could now work seven hours a week and afford their grandparents’ 1930 lifestyle, by doing the sum on a median weekly wage. Phillip in Hunt explained that the scepticism was not ignorance but experience: people who came through the dot-com crash and the housing crisis “lower on the ladder holding the dirty end of the stick” have learned that “we prosper at the whims of those with control.”

The best reply was not hostile at all. James Lepone of Mohnton, Pennsylvania, said he agreed with Rattner’s premise and then offered what he called the steelman he hadn’t answered. A tractor replaced one kind of physical labour, and a computer automated particular tasks. “AI is potentially a substitute for increasingly general human cognitive labor, including the ability to learn new tasks.” The question, Lepone wrote, “isn’t whether technology has created jobs in the past. It’s whether human labor remains scarce and valuable when the technology being introduced can increasingly replicate human intelligence itself. What say you?”

Where the farm analogy breaks

Lepone’s question is the one the charts cannot answer, because every chart looks backwards. The farm transition worked because there was somewhere for the farmhands to go. The factories needed hands, and later the offices needed minds. Each wave of machinery pushed people towards the kind of work machines could not yet do, and for two centuries that kind of work always existed. A reader signing as No Way Out made the same point: earlier disruptions always created “less physical and more cognitive, higher value jobs,” while AI goes straight for the cognitive work at the top.

This series will hear the same objection from Bill Gates in Part 87. He calls AI “utterly, absolutely, completely, totally different” from every earlier disruption, because it spreads across the whole economy at once and leaves no untouched sector to absorb the displaced. Part 104 will look at how the damage is showing up so far: not in mass layoffs but in flat wages and fewer first jobs, which is exactly where Rattner’s early-loser chart begins to point.

None of that makes Rattner wrong about productivity. It makes him wrong to treat the jobs chart as settled. History says the jobs came back every time. It cannot say they will come back when the new machine can learn the new jobs too.

The view from Trenton

There is one piece of evidence the readers did not have, and it arrived four days later. On 6 October, Beth Simone Noveck, who was New Jersey’s first chief A.I. strategist, made the case in a Times guest essay, “How A.I. Can Boost Democracy,” that government can put the technology to work for the people Rattner’s readers say get left behind.

Her best example is small and concrete. Since 2024, the federal government has paid families $120 per eligible child for summer groceries, but children qualify through several programmes run by different agencies, so many were missed. New Jersey built an A.I. tool to match records it already held. Benefits reached 106,000 more children in the first year and 96,000 the next, and no family filled in a form. California used A.I. to sort more than 1,500 responses from survivors of the 2025 Los Angeles wildfires into a recovery plan, and has made the effort permanent in law. The British government says its tool, Consult, sorted more than 50,000 public comments in about two hours. Pennsylvania found that the state employees it trained saved an average of 95 minutes a day.

Noveck is honest about the risk as well. In Victoria, Australia, a child-protection worker with no training in A.I. used ChatGPT to draft a report for the Children’s Court, and the chatbot misread severe danger signs that the worker failed to catch. Her answer is training, shared tools and public ownership of what public servants build, not abstinence.

It is not an answer to Lepone’s question about whether human work stays scarce. But it is a partial answer to Kristi’s. The gains of a new technology do not have to flow only to investors. They flow where an institution decides to send them, and in Trenton, at least, someone decided.

The hinge is politics, not economics

The deepest disagreement in the comments was not about economics at all. Rattner’s own plan agrees that the market will not share the gains on its own, which is why he wants new taxes, retraining, wage insurance and a stronger safety net. The readers’ reply was that everything therefore depends on a government willing to do those things, and they doubted they would get one. “All these fantastic advances,” wrote a reader signing as Here, “would require a 180 degree change in the tax code, a code completely dictated by the very class of people who will be enormously enriched.” Elizabeth in Virginia was blunter: “Don’t ask the American people to accept a vision we currently do not have the capacity to realize.”

The record of the past few weeks supports them. Part 78 recorded Congress going home for the midterms without passing a single measure on AI, not even a toothless one; the House headed home to campaign in mid-September, CNBC reported, The Washington Post reported on September 30, 2026 that the Senate was leaving town until after the midterms, and The New York Times reported the same day that Congress was leaving Washington “With No A.I. Progress.” Nothing will move before the November 3 vote. The tax breaks Rattner wants curbed were made permanent in the tax law President Trump signed in July 2025. The way out of the arms race that Fudenberg and Koh describe in Part 78 needs the companies to share information they keep secret. Rattner’s economics may be sound. The politics that his plan assumes has not yet appeared in Washington, though New Jersey suggests what it might look like when it does.

The ledger

Asimov saw this argument coming. In The Caves of Steel, published in 1954, Earth’s crowded cities have begun replacing human clerks and shop assistants with robots, and the people displaced riot in the stores. The book’s detective, Elijah Baley, dislikes the robots for the same reason: he fears the system will one day declassify him too. The answer the book reaches is neither to smash the machines nor to trust the people who own them. It is for Earth’s people to leave their sealed cities and build somewhere new: a frontier for human work, of the kind the farmhands once found in the factories.

Rattner’s six charts are an honest case that the pie can grow. His 344 readers are an honest record of why most people no longer believe they will get a bigger slice. Both are right. The Asimov Gap sits between them: the distance between what the technology could do for everyone, and what anyone in power has shown they will make it do.
