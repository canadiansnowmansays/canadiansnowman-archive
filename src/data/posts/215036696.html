There is a moment near the start of a recent conversation between economist Jeffrey Sachs and historian Yakov Rabkin, Professor Emeritus at the University of Montreal, where Sachs — describing his own upbringing as an American Jew — says he was taught that being Jewish and being Zionist were simply the same thing. Then he tells a story: standing on 47th Street outside the United Nations, decades ago, and finding himself in the middle of a hundred Orthodox rabbis holding signs that read “Zionism is against the Torah.” Not Palestinian activists. Not secular leftists. Rabbis, from the Satmar community in Brooklyn, protesting the existence of the state of Israel on religious grounds.

That scene belongs in this series. Thirteen parts and an addendum into “The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest,” this project has traced how a British diplomat’s letter, a set of colonial ambitions, and seventy-five years of American cover produced the catastrophe now unfolding in Gaza and the West Bank. All of that stands. But there is a piece of the story this series has told mostly from the outside — the assumption, shared by supporters and critics of Israel alike, that Zionism simply is the Jewish position, that opposing the Israeli state is definitionally a position taken against Jews rather than one held by a great many of them, from the very beginning, on explicitly Jewish grounds. As Rabkin explained to Sachs on Book Club with Jeffrey Sachs, that assumption is not just an oversimplification. It gets the origin story backwards.

The idea was Protestant before it was Jewish

Start with a fact that rarely makes it into conventional accounts: the political programme of gathering Jews in Palestine did not originate with Jews. It originated in 17th-century England, among Protestant literalists who — unlike the Catholic Church, which read scripture through centuries of commentary — took the Old Testament at its word and concluded that the in-gathering of the Jews in the Holy Land was a precondition for the Second Coming. Isaac Newton wrote about it. So did the chemist Joseph Priestley. By the 19th century this had fused with British imperial calculation: Lord Shaftesbury published an open letter to the monarchs of Europe urging the restoration of the Jews to Palestine, and did so, by Rabkin’s account, without apparently consulting a single synagogue in London — because his other project was chairing a society for the conversion of Jews to Christianity. The two goals, in-gathering and conversion, were never separate.

The direct institutional link between this Christian project and the Jewish movement that would eventually bear its name runs through Theodor Herzl himself. Herzl — a secularised, German-speaking Viennese playwright who first proposed mass conversion to Catholicism as his solution to antisemitism before settling on Zionism as his second choice — found his most important early patron in William Hechler, the Christian Zionist chaplain of the British embassy in Vienna. Zionism, in other words, was a Protestant theological project for two centuries before it became, in the 1890s, a secular Jewish nationalist one. That sequence matters, because it means the movement was never simply an organic expression of Jewish communal will finding its voice. It was, from the outset, a convergence of interests — millenarian Christian eschatology, British imperial strategy in the eastern Mediterranean, and the personal crisis of a handful of assimilated Central European Jews confronting a resurgent European antisemitism that had nothing to do with religious observance and everything to do with race.

Three kinds of no

Once Herzl’s movement existed, it was not the Jewish communities of Europe who rallied to it. It was opposed, and opposed hard, from at least three distinct directions — and understanding all three matters, because each survives today in a different form.

The first was religious. Rabbinic authorities across Eastern Europe regarded the Zionists as, in the plainest sense, dangerous heretics. Not because Judaism forbade any relationship to the land of Israel — the land is central to Jewish liturgy and longing — but because the Talmud, compiled between the third and sixth centuries in the aftermath of Rome’s expulsion of the Jews from Jerusalem, contains what is known as the three oaths: adjurations against a mass, organised return to the land; against the use of force to reconquer it; and a corresponding oath binding the nations of the world not to oppress the Jews excessively in the meantime. For observant communities, converting a scattered religious people into a secular nation-state on the European model was not a technical disagreement. It was a violation of a millennium and a half of theological restraint, and it is why Satmar Hasidim protest Israel’s existence to this day. This was also, notably, opposition to the Zionists’ overt secularism as much as to the political project itself — many of the early Zionist organisers, Russian revolutionaries and Viennese journalists, observed none of the commandments at all, which made them doubly suspect to the rabbis they were trying to recruit.

The second was assimilationist, and numerically it may have been the largest. Jews in France, Britain, Germany, and Austria had only recently won full civic equality and were climbing the social and professional ladder for the first time in European history. The last thing they wanted to hear was that they did not really belong — that their true homeland was a strip of Ottoman territory in the eastern Mediterranean — because that was precisely what the antisemites of the period were saying. Some of the Christian Zionists agitating for Jewish restoration to Palestine were, in fact, overt antisemites themselves; the goals of gathering the Jews in the Holy Land and converting them to Christianity were never fully disentangled. American Reform rabbis, meeting at the end of the 19th century, explicitly rejected the idea that Jews constituted a separate nation in need of territory; one is reported to have told his congregation, of the United States, “this is our Palestine.”

The third was socialist. The Bund, the Jewish labour movement of Eastern Europe, regarded Zionism as a nationalist distraction from the class struggle that ought to unite Jewish and non-Jewish workers alike against the conditions that produced antisemitism in the first place.

None of this is to suggest Jewish opinion was static, or that these objections held forever. Millions of Jews did eventually go to Palestine — pushed by Polish antisemitism, driven out by Hitler after 1933, radicalised by the collapse of the old Russian Empire. But the point stands: the movement that built the state of Israel spent its first half-century arguing not with Palestinians or the British, but with the large majority of world Jewry, who considered the entire project either a heresy, a threat to their newly won citizenship, or a betrayal of working-class solidarity.

The one Jew in the cabinet who called it what it was

Part IV of this series covered the Balfour Declaration as the moment Britain promised the same land to two peoples in the same breath. One detail deserves to be added to that account. Edwin Montagu, the only Jewish member of the British cabinet in 1917, formally objected to the declaration in writing, accusing his own government of antisemitism. His argument was precise: a British government pledging to establish a Jewish national home in Palestine was implicitly telling its own Jewish citizens that they were foreigners, that their permanent home lay elsewhere. Balfour, for his part, had in 1906 authored legislation restricting the entry of Russian Jewish refugees into Britain — a fact that gives his 1917 declaration a considerably less generous cast. And in private correspondence, Balfour was blunter than the declaration’s careful public language ever allowed, writing that the point was to help the Jews gather in Palestine without particular regard for the wishes of the Arab population already living there. The declaration’s own protective clause — that nothing should be done to prejudice the rights of “existing non-Jewish communities” — was, on this evidence, never meant as more than a diplomatic fig leaf.

Jabotinsky’s wall, and the line to Netanyahu

The most consequential figure this series has under-credited, though, is Ze’ev Jabotinsky. An Odessa-born Russian-language writer of real talent, Jabotinsky joined the Zionist movement and split from its socialist leadership over one core disagreement: Jabotinsky argued, plainly and in print, that a Jewish presence in Palestine could be established and held only by force — that the native population would never consent, and had no obligation to, so Zionism would have to build what he called, in a 1923 essay, an “iron wall” the Arabs could not breach. He was pushed out of the mainstream movement by the socialist David Ben-Gurion, and his Revisionist Zionism remained a marginal current until decades after the state’s founding.

Rabkin’s sharper point is that the ideological distance between Ben-Gurion’s Labor Zionism and Jabotinsky’s Revisionism was always smaller than the two camps’ rhetoric suggested. Both accepted that the land would have to be taken and held by force over the objection of the people living on it; the socialists simply dressed the same project in the language of egalitarian nation-building, while Jabotinsky’s heirs stopped bothering with the decor. That distinction collapsed for good with the 1977 election that brought Menachem Begin’s Likud to power — the direct institutional descendant of Jabotinsky’s Revisionist movement — and it has personal weight in the current government: Benjamin Netanyahu’s father served as Jabotinsky’s private secretary, and Netanyahu grew up inside that intellectual and political household. Today’s Israeli government is not simply a harder-right version of Ben-Gurion’s Israel. It is the political tradition Ben-Gurion spent his career trying to keep out of power, now permanently in charge of it.

One more thread worth noting for texture, if not weight: Ben-Gurion, Jabotinsky, and — through his father — Netanyahu all trace their family origins to the Russian Empire’s Pale of Settlement. Naftali Bennett is, by Rabkin’s account, the only Israeli prime minister without that lineage. The revolutionary political culture of the late Russian Empire — its absolutism, its comfort with disregarding ideological enemies entirely — is not a throwaway detail. It is, on this reading, baked into the temperament of the state’s founding and ruling class alike.

The theology that came after 1967

Part IX of this series covered the settlement project as slow annexation — the mechanics of 700,000 settlers making a Palestinian state physically impossible. What deserves adding is the theological rupture that accompanied it. The national-religious settler movement that emerged after Israel’s conquest of the West Bank and Gaza in 1967 was not a continuation of secular Labor Zionism’s caution, nor of rabbinic Judaism’s historical restraint. Its adherents were observant Jews who read the biblical land promises literally and concluded that the Talmud’s three oaths — the same oaths that had kept religious Jews out of the Zionist movement for half a century — no longer applied, because, as they put it, Jews now needed “a Torah of the Land of Israel” suited to the fact of occupation rather than the fact of exile. This is a movement whose leading voices are, by most accounts, personally sincere: their words and their actions do not diverge the way they do for so many secular Zionists who prefer not to look too closely at what the settlement project requires. That sincerity is precisely what makes the current governing coalition — Smotrich, Ben-Gvir, and the settler movement they represent — a different and in some ways more dangerous formation than Ben-Gurion’s Israel ever was. It is not a secular state occasionally making religious gestures. It is, for a critical mass of its most motivated actors, a theological project that has already answered every question a two-state solution would require reopening.

What this changes, and what it doesn’t

None of this rescues Zionism’s founders from the colonial calculus this series has documented in detail, and none of it should be read as minimising the scale of what is happening in Gaza and the West Bank today. If anything, tracing the Jabotinsky-to-Netanyahu line and the post-1967 theological turn makes the “original sin” harder to treat as a single, bounded act of 1917 or 1948 — it was, and remains, a live ideological current with an unbroken institutional lineage to the present cabinet. But it does complicate a habit this series, like most writing on the conflict, has occasionally fallen into: treating “Zionism” and “Jewish opinion” as interchangeable, and treating Israeli government policy as an expression of Jewish consensus rather than the victory, after a century of argument, of one faction among several that Jewish communities themselves spent decades trying to defeat. The distinction is not a rhetorical nicety. It is the difference between an honest accounting and a caricature — and this series has tried, from its title onward, to be the former.
