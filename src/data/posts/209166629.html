Somewhere in an online gift shop’s warehouse in the spring of 2020, someone decided that what the American public needed, in the middle of a pandemic that would eventually kill more than a million of them, was an Anthony Fauci bobblehead. It sold. So did the socks, the T-shirts, the buttercream-frosted donuts shaped like his face, and, more strikingly, the votive candles — sold under names invoking “Saint Fauci,” his gray hair ringed on the candle’s glass in a hand-painted golden halo, marketed earnestly to a customer base that had decided, in the absence of any other reliable authority figure, that the government’s seventy-nine-year-old chief infectious disease adviser was the closest thing available to a patron saint of scientific truth. Yard signs went up in numerous American front lawns reading, without apparent irony, In Fauci We Trust. A man who had spent the majority of his career deliberately avoiding this kind of visibility had become, within a matter of weeks, something closer to a religious figure than a civil servant.

The scale of this merchandising economy is easy to understate in retrospect. Etsy sellers reported thousands of Fauci-themed sales within weeks of his becoming a fixture of the daily briefings; a Brooklyn brewery, and later others, released beers named in his honor; a doll manufacturer produced a limited-edition Fauci action figure, complete with face mask, that reportedly sold out its initial run within hours. Saturday Night Live cast Brad Pitt to portray him in a cold open that spring, an unusually high-wattage casting choice for a sitting government scientist that itself signaled how thoroughly Fauci had crossed over from public health official into cultural phenomenon. He had, by that point, already crossed into more mainstream forms of recognition as well: Time put him on its cover, and People named him one of its People of the Year. In April 2020, Kim Kardashian convened three dozen movie stars and athletes on a private video call so Fauci could brief them directly on masks and crowd safety — an hour of his time, by her reasoning, that could reach millions of their combined followers more effectively than any government press release. None of this activity originated with Fauci himself; he did not commission the bobbleheads, request the Brad Pitt impression, or organize the Kardashian call. But the sheer volume of it — a wave of consumer and entertainment-industry attention typically reserved for movie stars and pop musicians, attached instead to a career immunologist in his eighth decade — created the specific conditions under which his own private reactions to that attention, later exposed in the diary, would read as either endearingly human or damningly vain, depending entirely on which side of the backlash the reader had already landed.

It is worth taking this phenomenon seriously as a genuine sociological event rather than dismissing it as an embarrassing footnote, because it explains an enormous amount about what happened next. Americans in March and April of 2020 were living through a crisis with no reliable precedent in living memory — a novel virus, a shuttered economy, mounting deaths, and a federal government whose most visible spokesman, the president himself, was frequently contradicting the clinical evidence in real time. Into that vacuum of trustworthy authority stepped a calm, credentialed, grandfatherly figure who spoke in measured sentences about viral transmission and appeared, unlike almost everyone else on television that spring, to actually know what he was talking about. The deification of Fauci by a meaningful slice of the American public was not really about Fauci the individual research administrator. It was about what people desperately needed him to represent at a moment when very little else in American public life seemed trustworthy at all.

That is precisely why the backlash, when it came, was so disproportionate to anything Fauci himself had actually done. A symbol invested with that much unearned significance becomes, almost by definition, an equally convenient vessel for the opposite reaction once the initial crisis emotion curdles into resentment over lockdowns, school closures, and economic damage that took years to fully register. Joe Rogan, whose podcast reaches tens of millions of listeners, began describing Fauci as a monster. Elon Musk called him, in a widely circulated post, a “freaking demon.” Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s 2021 book about him ran to roughly five hundred pages and accused him of engineering something close to a historic coup d’état against Western democracy — an escalation in rhetorical scale that had almost nothing to do with anything Fauci had actually said or done and everything to do with how completely the earlier sainthood narrative had primed his critics to reach for equally maximalist language once the mood shifted. As Katherine J. Wu reported for The Atlantic, this dynamic — beloved and reviled in roughly equal, and roughly simultaneous, measure — had made Fauci, by his mid-eighties, into one of the most polarizing unelected figures in recent American public life, a status he had spent the preceding half-century of his career actively trying to avoid.

Fauci’s own conduct during this period does not entirely absolve him of contributing to the dynamic, and a fair accounting has to hold that alongside everything above. His diary entries from 2020 through 2022, later released amid this year’s controversy, catalog an extraordinary volume of celebrity encounters and personal flattery with what reads, even to a sympathetic reader, as genuine relish rather than the detached bemusement a more self-aware public figure might have maintained. He recorded conversations with Barbra Streisand, Julia Roberts, Joan Baez, and Dionne Warwick with the enthusiasm of someone cataloging trophies rather than someone embarrassed by the strangeness of his own sudden celebrity. He wrote, without apparent self-consciousness, about the “intense publicity” surrounding him, about petitions circulating to vote him “Sexiest Man Alive,” about beer named after him at a Georgia brewery and bobbleheads manufactured in bulk overseas. Perhaps most tellingly, he told MSNBC directly, in 2021, that criticism of him amounted to criticism of science itself — a line that, whatever legitimate frustration prompted it, handed his critics an argument they could not have constructed more usefully themselves: proof, in his own words, that he had begun to conflate his personal reputation with the institution of scientific consensus he was supposed to be representing rather than embodying.

It would be a mistake, though, to let that genuine vanity do more explanatory work than it can actually bear, because the scale of the backlash Fauci eventually faced was wildly disproportionate to the scale of the flaw. Enjoying a phone call from Barbra Streisand is not, by any reasonable standard, a moral failing worth threats against a man’s family, and the death threats Fauci and his three daughters received throughout 2020 and beyond were real, numerous, and serious enough that his NIAID staff began flagging the most violent examples for a security detail, a detail Fauci was paying for himself within days of the Trump administration’s return to office in January 2025. The process his staff developed for this — triaging incoming mail and electronic messages for specific, actionable threats rather than mere hostility, then referring the most serious cases to federal law enforcement — became, by 2021, a routine and permanent part of NIAID’s operations, an institutional accommodation to sustained menace that no NIAID director before Fauci had ever needed. One man who threatened to have Fauci and his family “dragged into the street, beaten to death, and set on fire” was convicted and sentenced to more than three years in federal prison — a conviction that ought to settle, at minimum, the question of whether the hostility directed at Fauci by 2021 had moved well past the boundaries of ordinary political criticism into something considerably darker.

It is worth noting that Fauci was not the only pandemic-era official who could have absorbed this particular kind of backlash, and the fact that so much of it landed specifically on him, rather than spreading more evenly across the officials actually responsible for specific policy decisions, tells its own story. Deborah Birx, the White House’s coronavirus response coordinator, made many of the concrete calls on testing strategy and reopening guidance that Fauci is now blamed for; Robert Redfield, the CDC director throughout the same period, oversaw an agency whose early testing failures did more concrete damage to the American pandemic response than anything within NIAID’s actual authority. Neither became a cultural lightning rod on anything close to Fauci’s scale. The difference was not responsibility — by any organizational chart, Fauci’s institute funded research, it did not set national testing policy or manage the CDC’s laboratory failures — but visibility and affect: Fauci was the recognizable face at the podium, speaking in complete, confident sentences, in a way that made him legible as either hero or villain to an anxious public in a way Birx’s charts and Redfield’s more technocratic demeanor never quite achieved. Becoming the symbol, in other words, had less to do with what Fauci actually controlled than with how effectively he communicated, which is itself a small, bitter irony: the same skill that made him useful to six previous presidents made him the most convenient target once a seventh decided targets were useful.

Not every sympathetic reader of the diary has reached the same forgiving conclusion this essay has, and a fair account of the fame years owes that dissent its due. As Mark Leibovich argued in The Atlantic, the diary can just as reasonably be read not as the disoriented record of a decent man overwhelmed by unwanted celebrity, but as confirmation of Washington’s own worst instincts — evidence that Fauci, whatever his genuine service, had grown “high on his own supply” of capital adulation at precisely the moment the country’s death toll was climbing fastest. Leibovich’s version of Fauci is a recognizable Beltway type: the insider who insists he finds the fawning attention unnerving while cataloging, in granular and evidently gratified detail, precisely which A-list guests attended which dinner in his honor. Both readings can be true of the same set of entries, which is itself the more interesting point. A diary this extensively concerned with status is not incompatible with a diary written by an exhausted man trying to process real trauma; it is entirely possible to be both genuinely disoriented by sudden fame and genuinely, uncomfortably delighted by it, and Fauci’s pages read, on a fair accounting, like both things at once rather than either one alone.

Polling across this period traces the arc as clearly as anything in the historical record. Surveys conducted in the spring of 2020 found Fauci among the most trusted public figures in the country, with favorability ratings that crossed party lines in ways almost no other figure associated with the pandemic response could claim, Trump included. By 2022, that same trust had fractured almost entirely along partisan lines, with Republican confidence in Fauci collapsing even as Democratic confidence remained comparatively durable — a split that tracked not any specific new revelation about his conduct, but the broader realignment of pandemic-era grievances (lockdowns, school closures, vaccine mandates) into an increasingly partisan framework in which Fauci had become shorthand for the entire apparatus of pandemic-era public health authority, fairly or not. That a single unelected scientist’s personal favorability could become so cleanly a proxy for one’s broader political identity says at least as much about the country’s diminishing capacity to hold a shared set of facts as it does about anything Fauci personally did to earn either the trust or its collapse.

What makes this period genuinely tragic, rather than merely embarrassing, is the asymmetry between what Fauci actually was and what both his admirers and his detractors needed him to be. He was never a saint, and the sainthood narrative — however understandable as a psychological response to a frightening moment — was never something Fauci earned through any specific action beyond speaking calmly on television while his government’s other spokesman spoke less calmly beside him. But he was also never the demon or the coup plotter his most extreme critics eventually made him out to be; there is no credible evidence in any of the released diaries, emails, or subsequent investigations that Fauci deliberately hid a known lab origin for the virus, engineered a democratic coup, or profited improperly from his position, whatever the volume and intensity of the accusations claiming otherwise. Somewhere between the golden halo on the votive candle and the demon Musk described sits an actual eighty-five-year-old man — vain in ways this series has not shied away from naming, genuinely accomplished in ways the current moment has mostly forgotten, and entirely unequipped, after fifty years of institutional deference from the presidents he served, to manage the specific modern experience of simultaneous mass adoration and mass hatred, both directed at a version of him that had very little to do with who he actually was.

That gap between the myth and the man is, in the end, what the diary controversy actually exposed when it surfaced this past weekend — not evidence of a cover-up, but evidence that a private man’s ordinary, very human vanity looks considerably worse when it is read against a backdrop of mass death, and considerably worse still when it is released by political enemies with every incentive to select for exactly the entries that make him look worst. The diary itself, and what it does and does not actually reveal, is where this series turns next.
