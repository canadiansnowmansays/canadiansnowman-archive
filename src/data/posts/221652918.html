Today, Monday, October 26, was meant to be opening day of the Future Investment Initiative, the Crown Prince’s “Davos in the Desert.” Last year it drew more than 8,000 people, Larry Fink and Jamie Dimon among them. A week ago the organisers sent participants a notice, seen by the New York Times: the conference was postponed to 2027. “The new dates will be communicated very soon.” Riyadh’s main airport, closed to the public since the October 10 strike, reopened on Sunday, October 25, as Vivian Nereim reported for the New York Times. Within hours the Houthi military spokesman, Yahya Saree, told airlines and travellers to stay away “because it is a military target,” and warned that Riyadh would “bear responsibility for the consequences.” The coalition reported intercepting missiles and drones aimed at the capital on Friday, Saturday and Sunday.

Two weeks ago, in Part CI, the President promised voters he would not attack Iran before the midterms. Last week, in Part CII, he told the Houthis he would “be there after the Elections.” This week the plan behind those promises came into view. It has targets, rehearsals, a delivery date for the forces, and a preference for long nights. By Wednesday, October 21, the President was saying it himself. Washington was not negotiating with Iran, he told reporters before boarding Air Force One, and “something one way or the other” would happen “fairly soon.” A White House official had already told The Atlantic that “one way or the other, the conflict in Iran will be over very soon.” By Saturday, senior officials were telling the Times that in private the President “appears to be leaning toward another round of major strikes inside Iran.”

The midterms are eight days away, and Israel votes tomorrow. Everyone else involved in this war spent the week acting. Washington spent it waiting for its chosen date.

The Longer Nights

On Wednesday evening, five reporters from the New York Times, led by Julian E. Barnes and Eric Schmitt, published what this diary has been inferring since Part XCIX. The American and Israeli militaries “have developed specific plans for renewed strikes against Iran after the American midterm elections.” The plan is short and intense. American and Israeli aircraft have been flying over Iraq to practise strike manoeuvres. When Defense Secretary Hegseth met Prime Minister Netanyahu at Ovda air base in southern Israel on Monday, October 19, they discussed new strikes, according to U.S. officials.

The likely targets are Iran’s missile and drone arsenal, its nuclear facilities and its energy infrastructure. Some planners believe “an intensive three-day campaign” could cause enough economic damage to change Tehran’s position. Some officials said last week that the targeting would avoid refineries and oil infrastructure, because the President is eager to keep gas prices from rising further. The Atlantic’s sources listed energy infrastructure among the targets. By Saturday the argument appeared to be settling. An official with direct knowledge told the Times that Central Command’s plans now include an expanded target list, “including energy infrastructure and other sites vital to the Iranian economy.”

The timing is the most revealing part. The full force (three aircraft carriers, thousands of Marines, fighter jets and special operations forces) will not be in place until early November. The American military prefers to strike at night, and, the Times notes, “there will be increasing hours of darkness over Iran early next month.” The election, the arrival of the force and the longer nights all point to the same week. When this diary first wrote that the war was being scheduled around the vote, that was an inference. It is now a planning assumption.

None of this means the President will say yes. He has refused before. Barnes and Schmitt counted five vetoed proposals in Part CI, and the new reporting describes another last-minute halt early this month, “after both militaries had made extensive preparations and placed their air forces on the highest state of alert.” It is not clear whether that halt is one of the five or a sixth. One commander described the result as “day-to-day whiplash.” That restraint deserves to be acknowledged again, even if it is restraint by indecision.

The President also has a favourite weapon in mind. Last week, the Times reports, he said he was considering the 22,000-pound MOAB against Pickaxe Mountain, the deeply buried nuclear site Iranians call Kooh Kolang. Officials say he is “enamored” with it partly because of its nickname, the Mother of All Bombs. Specialists note that it explodes above ground, and Pickaxe is buried in granite. Asked on Newsmax about the reports, he offered a reassurance nobody had requested: “I wouldn’t use nuclear. We don’t have to. We’re the greatest nuclear power in the world,” according to CNBC.

Dana Stroul, the Pentagon’s senior Middle East official under President Biden, gave the Times the strategic verdict. Neither leader “has made a compelling case for how more war would change this strategic dilemma.”

Pretexts

On Friday, October 23, the warning came from the other direction. The director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, made public intelligence that Iran is in the “advanced stages” of planning attacks on Americans, including ambassadors in the Middle East and officials on American soil. The State Department began reducing staff at embassies and consulates. As a team led by David E. Sanger reported for the New York Times on Saturday, officials are taking this intelligence more seriously than any warning since the war began. Two Iranian officials, one of them from the Revolutionary Guards, confirmed the plotting to the Times. Because Israel and the United States have killed dozens of Iranian officials and some of their relatives, they said, Americans are now “fair game.”

The threat has a history behind it. In 2024 a Pakistani man was charged with a Revolutionary Guards plot to assassinate Donald Trump, and a jury convicted him in March. But the same reporting is careful about what the intelligence shows. Iran’s ambitions “often outstrip its capabilities,” and it has repeatedly hired hit men who were arrested before they could do any harm. Only the eight most senior members of Congress had been briefed before the warning became public. Some officials are puzzled that the administration released a letter it could not support with evidence of specific plots.

That is how wars widen, several diplomats told the Times. If the intelligence is right, “and it probably is,” a senior European official said, one gunshot aimed at an American official could start a conflagration. The reporters recall Iraq, where “selective intelligence can be used as an actual pretext for military action.” The elections add to the danger. Even if he is defeated on Tuesday, Netanyahu “could decide to launch one last strike that he may believe his successor would hesitate to execute.” And the President, who knows how unpopular the war is even among his strongest supporters, may want to show that his authority as commander in chief is undiminished.

Tehran is talking the same way. Mahdi Mohammadi, an adviser to Iran’s chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, says Iran is “in a surprise phase of the war, and we will not fight a classic war.” Another senior official promised surprise attacks with “zero red lines.”

None of this can be checked from outside. What is clear is that every side now has a pretext available, and two election calendars give them reasons to use one.

Why the Pledge Was Made

Part CI read the October 8 pledge as a message to the oil market. Brent had eased the moment the post appeared, and this diary wrote, “That tells you who it was written for.” On Thursday, Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire of The Atlantic filled in the other audience. After their October 7 report on a possible strike before the vote, “the behind-the-scenes blowback from fellow Republicans was so intense that the president posted on Truth Social that he would not attack again before the election.” The pledge was written for his own party as well as for traders.

Their piece also describes the two arguments the President has been hearing. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the blockade and sanctions will force Iran to give in. Netanyahu, in a series of calls, has pushed for one more round of joint strikes to bring down the regime. The administration now appears to be combining the two: several days of strikes after the midterms, on the theory that Iran’s economy is already close to the edge. “The economic pressure is working,” one outside adviser told The Atlantic, and new strikes “would push them over.”

The President had originally hoped Iran would come “crawling back” to the table before the midterms. It did not. The blockade has kept at least $25 billion of Iranian crude off the market, according to Admiral Brad Cooper of Central Command. But Iran’s negotiators still refuse to discuss the nuclear programme until later in the talks. Danny Citrinowicz, a former Israeli intelligence officer, explained what the planners leave out: “you cannot plan for a three-day war, because it is not a unilateral decision. You cannot control how Iran responds.”

Tehran has said how it would respond. Mohammadi called the idea of a 72-hour war a “fantasy” and noted that the President had said in February the war would last two weeks. “Our attacks will be more fierce, creative and different.” At Tehran’s Holy Defence Museum, a Revolutionary Guards spokesman, Brigadier General Hossein Mohebbi, named seven undersea fibre-optic cables in the Strait of Hormuz as “new targets.” The Tehran analyst Peiman Salehi told the Times that if Pickaxe were hit, Iran would strike Saudi refineries, Aramco’s included, because “the best leverage for pressuring Trump is increasing oil and energy prices because it will hurt the American people.”

Washington had talked about sparing Iran’s refineries to protect American drivers. Iran’s answer is to hit Saudi refineries to hurt those same drivers. If the expanded target list holds, both sides will be aiming at the gas pump.

Two Elections, One War

Israel votes on Tuesday, October 27, and America a week later. The war is on the ballot in both.

On Sunday, October 18, the President publicly endorsed Netanyahu. The next day his Defense Secretary flew in unannounced to meet him, as Natan Odenheimer reported for the New York Times. Netanyahu, trailing Gadi Eisenkot, his own former army chief of staff, in the polls, now campaigns as the man the President wants beside him to “finish the job once and for all.” A White House official confirmed to The Atlantic that Netanyahu pushed for strikes before his own election. His office has denied it.

If he loses, he becomes a caretaker, and analysts say a caretaker would find it difficult to start a war. Mazal Mualem, his biographer, says he could use the strike plan to press his rivals into a unity government: “If Iran attacks, that’s one thing, but initiating an offensive strike would be extremely difficult for him.” Raz Zimmt of Tel Aviv’s Institute for National Security Studies described the expectation in both countries plainly: “it’s a matter of when the war restarts, not if.” He also described the motive almost exactly as The Atlantic’s Republican source did in Part CI. A strike “might give Trump an image of victory and a way out of the war.” Two sources, two weeks apart, on two continents, describe the same purpose: an image of victory.

The American election is now part of the war in a way the American government did not plan. As Michael Crowley reported for the New York Times, Iranian state media are tracking American pump prices and citing polls on voter anger. Ghalibaf mocked rising Treasury yields: “We’ll return you to 1970s rates, plus high gas prices, diesel shortages and bell-bottoms. Enjoy the nostalgia!” The President’s response was that Iran is “desperate to try and affect the election, so that we can get a nice weak group of people in there.”

There is a precedent, and Crowley cites it. In 1980 a CIA analysis, since declassified, concluded that Iran was dragging out the hostage crisis “to bring about President Carter’s defeat.” Vali Nasr of Johns Hopkins warns Iran what may follow: “Once the elections are done, he may be free to do whatever he wants.”

Voters have already decided what they think. A Pew survey cited by The Atlantic finds 65 per cent of Americans disapprove of the President’s handling of the war and 33 per cent approve. Republican approval has fallen from 69 per cent in March to 63. Nearly half expect the war to last more than a year. The administration’s first estimate was four to six weeks. The doubts have reached the President’s own family. Vivian Salama and Ashley Parker of The Atlantic report that Donald Trump Jr. opposed the war from the outset and told his father so in private. In April 2024 he had posted: “Remember when they said Trump would get us into a full-blown war with Iran?”

The war shows up most clearly in one place. Virginia’s Second District wraps around Naval Station Norfolk and is home to more than 80,000 veterans and 30,000 active-duty and reserve personnel. Since 2008 it has changed parties four times, each time matching control of the House. As Madeleine Carlisle reported for The Atlantic, the Republican incumbent, Jen Kiggans, a former Navy helicopter pilot, has voted six times against resolutions to end the war and says her Democratic opponent, the former Navy commander Elaine Luria, has “chosen the side of Iran.” The House Armed Services Committee, on which Kiggans sits, has not held a public hearing on the war since May 19. When a reporter asked Kiggans whether the war had affected her campaign, a staffer cut in: “We’re not going to talk about that.”

That staffer’s line comes close to summing up the administration’s position until November 3.

Everyone Else Moved

While Washington waited, everyone else acted.

The Houthis kept attacking Saudi Arabia. On the evening of Wednesday, October 21, a strike on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam killed a Saudi and an Indian resident and wounded eight, as Ismaeel Naar reported for the New York Times. The same day, debris from a drone shot down over Aramco’s Shaybah oil field set a contractor’s vehicle on fire, killing a Filipino and a Nepali. Houthi attacks have now killed at least 22 civilians in the kingdom. On Thursday, Aramco’s headquarters staff in Dhahran were told to work from home. The State Department ordered non-emergency staff and their families out of Saudi Arabia, citing “mounting civilian casualties.”

Britain announced on Tuesday, October 20, that RAF Typhoons would fly “defensive counter air missions” over the kingdom, as Michael D. Shear reported for the New York Times. “The continuing attacks from the Houthis on our ally Saudi Arabia are absolutely despicable,” said the defence secretary, Wes Streeting. Egypt’s National Defence Council pledged “all necessary and possible forms of support,” although parliament adjourned without voting on military aid. Pakistan and Turkey have already invoked the Mecca pact.

Against all that, the American position, as the Times put it again this week, is that the President “rebuffed Saudi appeals” for strikes while the United States provides “significant military support, including intelligence and data to help with selecting targets.” London sends fighter jets, Cairo sends promises, and Washington sends target data and a date after the election.

Taiz and Al Alam

The war in Yemen is getting worse. On Monday, October 19, three Houthi ballistic missiles hit Al Alam camp in Shabwa province, a training centre for police and security recruits, as Saeed Al-Batati and Ismaeel Naar reported for the New York Times. The provincial governor now puts the death toll at 73, making it one of the deadliest attacks of the renewed war. The Houthis called the camp a Saudi “troop buildup.” They also say 110 Saudi airstrikes hit Sanaa the day before. That claim cannot be checked.

Taiz is in the third week of a siege. Al-Batati and Vivian Nereim reported for the New York Times that the Houthis took the last road to the south in early October. Families are boiling salty tap water and cooking over burning cardboard. “Anyone who happens to have vegetables at home is considered fortunate, as if they were royalty,” said Alhan Al-Shaibani, who lives in a household of seven. Her mother and grandmother, both diabetic, cannot find their medication. At al-Thawra, the main hospital, the head of infection control expects “the deaths of many people with chronic illnesses and cancer patients” if the siege is not lifted. On Tuesday, the UN human rights chief, Volker Türk, called on the Houthis to let aid in. The Houthis blame the government for the blockade.

On the city’s edge, a government colonel, Abdul Basit al-Baher, says his men have no resupply. “To die while resisting is better and more honorable than to die while surrendering,” he said. “Life under the rule of the militia is, in itself, a form of death.”

The World Health Organization counts at least 1,574 people killed in Yemen since August. The UN counts some 260,000 displaced.

The Strait

At Hormuz, the attacks this week came from small boats as well as missiles. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, which works with the Royal Navy, reported a rocket-propelled grenade, a skiff firing on an LNG tanker and two projectiles hitting a tug, all between October 16 and 18. On Thursday the Revolutionary Guards claimed to have struck four tankers and said three more ships had hit mines. None of that could be confirmed. This week’s reporting sometimes calls the strait closed. It is not, and this diary will keep saying so. It is a toll booth, and every ship pays more to get through, in money, in risk and in lives.

In Tehran, the Wait

Iranians are waiting too, and they know what is being planned. On Saturday, the first day of the Iranian working week, Farnaz Fassihi and Leily Nikounazar of the New York Times spoke to about 18 people inside the country. In Tehran, people were filling their tanks, taking cash out of banks and stocking up on food. “I just told my wife not to wake up our son in the morning, and keep him home, we can decide day by day how to proceed,” said Majid, a 42-year-old businessman. His wife works at a ministry that could become a target, and she planned to call in sick. Shadi, a schoolteacher, intends to go to work unless the schools close. “But in the back of my mind I keep thinking about how I can protect my students, how to act so they are not scared, if explosions happen during school hours.” On February 28, an American strike hit an elementary school in Minab and killed 156 children and teachers. American officials blamed outdated targeting data.

Behnam, a 38-year-old musician, says two rounds of fighting have left him numb. “What worries me and those around me most is the lack of money and simply surviving.” Iran’s Red Crescent counts at least 1,900 civilians killed in the 40 days of bombing earlier this year. The government puts the damage at about $300 billion, and some basic foods now cost more than three times what they did. Alireza, a 54-year-old veteran of the Iran-Iraq war in Kerman, is angry at his own government’s secrecy: “People are under enormous pressure and are being crushed by the economic siege.”

The hard-liners who rally every night for revenge are a minority, the Times reports. A reformist politician, Javad Emam, addressed the government directly: “The responsibility for today’s decision lies with you; the cost of war will be borne by the nation.” Diplomacy by Pakistan, Qatar and Russia last week produced nothing.

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude hit $110 on Friday, October 16, for the first time since May, and settled at $109.86. West Texas Intermediate gained more than 10 per cent that week. On Thursday, October 22, as word of the strike plans spread and Iran hit more tankers, Brent passed $112, its highest since mid-May, and the stock market fell. A plan built partly around keeping gas prices down has already pushed them up.

Pentagon munitions stocks were “dangerously low” at the height of the war, The Atlantic reports. The Pentagon’s own estimate of the war’s cost stood at $43.6 billion through September 3, before this month’s deployments. Congress has still not authorised the war, and the committee meant to oversee it has not met in public since May.

  • One: day to Israel’s election.

  • Three: aircraft carriers due in the region by early November.

  • 72 hours: the planned length of the strike campaign, which an Iranian adviser calls a “fantasy.”

  • Seven: undersea fibre-optic cables the Revolutionary Guards have named as targets.

  • 22,000: pounds, the weight of the bomb the President likes for its name.

  • At least five: strike plans the President has vetoed, plus one more halted at the last minute this month.

  • 65 per cent: of Americans who disapprove of his handling of the war.

  • At least 22: civilians killed by Houthi strikes inside Saudi Arabia.

  • 73: recruits killed at Al Alam camp.

  • 1,574: people killed in Yemen since August.

  • 1,900: Iranian civilians killed in 40 days of bombing earlier this year, by the Red Crescent’s count.

  • 2027: the new year, not yet a date, for the Crown Prince’s investment summit.

And Narges Mohammadi, for whom this section is always kept. She is out of Evin on medical leave and still under sentences totalling 44 years, and her memoir, smuggled out of prison a few pages at a time, is now out in eleven languages, though not yet in English. Two governments are preparing to bomb her country after their elections. Her own government answers by threatening the undersea cables that carry the region’s voices to the world. She has spent her life arguing that Iranians should have a say in how they are governed. Iranians do not get a vote on November 3 or October 27, and they have no voice in what will be decided after either. In Tehran this weekend, a father told his wife to keep their son home from school, and to decide day by day.
