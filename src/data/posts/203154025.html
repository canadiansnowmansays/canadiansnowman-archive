Election workers process ballots in Maricopa County, Arizona — where control of the counting machinery has become the battleground

For thirty-seven instalments, this series has tracked a machine that operates from above. Legislatures redraw maps. Governors sign them. State supreme courts hold the door while the clock runs out. The pressure has come down from the heights — from Tallahassee, from Atlanta, from the marble buildings where the powerful decide how the powerless will be counted.

Maricopa County, Arizona, offers something the series has not yet documented at this scale: the capture of democracy not from the top, but from the inside.

There is a man named Justin Heap, and he is the recorder of the fourth-largest county in the United States. He oversees the votes of 2.6 million people — more than half of Arizona’s electorate, in the swing county that has, for a decade, decided which way the state falls. He was elected in 2024 on a promise to “end the laughingstock elections,” a phrase that should have been a warning. He has spent his eighteen months in office proving it was a promise, not a slogan.

And the question Maricopa now forces — the one The Atlantic‘s Yvonne Wingett Sanchez lays out in a piece published this morning — is whether the American election system, built deliberately to prevent any single official from controlling it, can survive an official who has decided that control is exactly the point.

The Design Was the Defense

Here is the thing worth understanding before anything else: the fragmentation was intentional.

Security at a Maricopa County facility amid election operations — the physical and procedural safeguards now at the center of the internal power struggle

In Arizona, as in much of the country, the law splits the running of an election between two offices that must cooperate or fail. The county recorder handles voter registration and early voting. The board of supervisors handles Election Day operations and tabulation. Neither can run an election alone. The system was built on the assumption — quaint now, but reasonable for most of American history — that two offices forced to work together would check each other’s worst impulses. Cooperation was not a courtesy. It was load-bearing.

This is the architecture that worked, as Sanchez notes, for decades. It worked because the people inside it agreed, tacitly, that the machinery mattered more than the fight. Even in 2020 — when the tabulation center drew protests, when Trump and his allies pressed Maricopa to overturn his loss, when election workers absorbed years of death threats — the Republicans who ran the county resolved their conflicts. They quarreled. They had egos. But they did not blow up the system, because they understood the system was the only thing standing between Maricopa and chaos.

The new recorder does not share that understanding. He appears to regard the cooperation requirement not as a safeguard but as an obstacle — a piece of machinery to be seized rather than shared.

The Lawsuit, the Win, and the Cyclone

Justin Heap, Maricopa County Recorder

The story of how Heap has nearly pulled it off is, on its surface, a dispute about org charts and IT departments. That is how it will be described by the people who want it to sound boring. It is not boring.

When Heap took office in January 2025, he tore up a power-sharing agreement his predecessor — Stephen Richer, the rare Republican who defended the 2020 results and lost his primary for the sin of it — had struck with the board. That agreement had moved the recorder’s IT department, its personnel, and roughly $4.5 million in funding under the board’s control. Heap called it punitive. He may even have had a point about the timing. But rather than negotiate new terms — and the county attorney authorized two outside lawyers, including a former state Supreme Court justice, to help him do exactly that — Heap eventually brought in America First Legal, the outfit co-founded by Stephen Miller, and sued. The suit landed in June 2025, after months of failed shared-services negotiations and a board budget vote that, in Heap’s telling, cemented the seizure through the funding process.

In April, he largely won. An Arizona judge ruled the board had “acted unlawfully and exceeded its statutory authority” by seizing the recorder’s staff and systems, and ordered them returned — or a new system funded from scratch. The judge went further, finding that election duties the board had long considered its own actually belonged to the recorder.

And here is where the design’s defense became its vulnerability. The ruling arrived with early voting for a July 21 primary weeks away. The board and its election staff said, plainly, that they could not untangle a decade of interlocking procedures, build new protocols, and retrain staff in the time available. An attorney for the board warned that the recorder’s “burgeoning cyclone of chaos also threatens to envelop the voters.” An appellate court agreed to halt the changes — describing the fight, with unusual candour for a court, as “no mere backroom dispute over accounting principles or organizational charts” but “a live conflict hurtling toward real-world consequences in elections about to begin.” The panel leaned on the Purcell principle, the doctrine that courts should be reluctant to order last-minute changes to election rules.

Here Heap has a genuine argument, and honesty requires stating it. No Arizona court, he points out, has ever adopted Purcell as a binding rule. And the April ruling came down in April — the board had nearly eight weeks to begin implementing it before the calendar tightened. Instead, Heap and his lawyers contend, the board largely ignored the order, ran out the clock, and then invoked the resulting time crunch as grounds to freeze the order in place. It is, in this telling, an emergency the board manufactured and then asked the court to remedy on its behalf. One does not have to accept the framing to see its force: a rule that lets any official defy a court order long enough to hide behind the next election is a rule with a sizeable hole in it. The board’s answer — that splitting interlocking election databases mid-primary is itself the reckless act — is not nothing either. Both things can be true. That is what makes Maricopa hard.

The case is now before the Arizona Supreme Court, which took it up the week of June 23. The justices did not lift the stay, and did not reinstate Heap’s win. They did something more telling: they ordered both offices back toward a settlement conference, demanded expedited briefing on the disputed duties and the election calendar, and asked specifically whether some narrower, interim division of authority could be stood up just to get through the 2026 primary — rather than the all-or-nothing seizure the lower court had ordered. Early voting, meanwhile, has begun; roughly 1.4 million early ballots went out to Maricopa voters in late June. The cyclone has not cleared. It has only gained a new altitude — and a higher court now standing in it.

The Tell: Cleta Mitchell as “Neutral Arbiter”

If the IT dispute is the body of the story, the tell is in a single proposal that went nowhere — and the fact that it was made at all.

Before the war fully exploded, Heap’s attorney suggested the two sides mediate their dispute using Cleta Mitchell — the lawyer who worked with Trump to try to reverse his 2020 defeat, who was on the Georgia call where Trump asked for 11,780 votes, and who told Sanchez in February that “Maricopa County is a complete disaster” deserving federal investigation. Heap’s attorney called her “ideal” and cited “her expertise.”

Sit with that. The proposed neutral arbiter of a fight over who controls Maricopa’s election machinery was one of the principal architects of the attempt to overturn the last contested election Maricopa decided. It is the kind of suggestion that reveals what a person actually believes neutrality looks like. To Heap’s office, a 2020 coup lawyer is the reasonable centre. That is not a negotiating position. That is a worldview.

The county staff who lived through 2020 were, per Sanchez’s reporting, astonished. They should not have been. The proposal was simply honest in a way the IT briefs are not.

Why This Belongs in This Series

I have spent thirty-seven instalments documenting attacks on democracy that share a family resemblance: they come from people with the power to redraw the rules, aimed at people without it. Maricopa is different, and the difference is the point.

Trump has spent his second term trying to nationalize elections — to pull them up to Washington, where his voter-ID push and his Justice Department probes could reach them. Sanchez’s verdict on that effort is blunt: it has yielded very little. The federal lever, it turns out, is hard to pull.

So his allies found a lower one. They went into the county offices — the recorder’s seat, the unglamorous administrative box where the real work of counting happens — and won there. America First Legal, having lost the argument that Washington should run elections, is now running the argument that a single county recorder should. One person on the board’s side told Sanchez this is “a new front in what appears to be a long-term play by America First to change how elections are run” — to make them answerable not to the Republican Party, but to the MAGA movement specifically.

This is the through-line of everything I have written under this banner. The redistricting wave I traced from Florida to Georgia was about controlling who wins. Maricopa is about controlling who counts. The first is theft of outcome. The second is theft of the apparatus that produces outcomes — which is more durable, harder to see, and far more dangerous, because a gerrymander only lasts until the next map, while control of the counting machinery lasts as long as you hold the office.

And the fear the board’s allies have named aloud is the one that should chill anyone watching: that the chaos is not a bug of Heap’s campaign but a feature of it — that a “burgeoning cyclone” enveloping the November count could become the pretext to contest results MAGA does not like, in House races that could decide control of Congress.

The Standoff Now

As this is published, the two offices are not so much governing as circling. Heap’s America First lawyer has threatened the criminal prosecution of supervisors and election staff who do not comply with an order that has itself been stayed. Heap has asked that the board be fined $100,000 a day — money, a county official noted, that taxpayers would pay. Poll workers have declined to work the election because, in the words of the board’s attorney, “they fear the Recorder’s threats of retribution.” A judge, ordering the two sides to try once more to work things out, said: “I know it would be a miracle.”

The live flashpoint has narrowed to something concrete: the early voting sites. Heap says the board is still running in-person early voting locations that state law places under his office, and that supervisors have instructed their poll workers to disregard anything the recorder directs. The board says it is Heap who keeps refusing to meet to finalize a new shared-services agreement. Each accuses the other of bad faith; each is partly right; the voters in line are the ones standing in the gap between them.

And now a third party has forced its way into the room. When the Arizona Supreme Court ordered both offices back to a settlement conference, it was doing what the lower courts could not — compelling cooperation by decree from people who had run out of the trust to manage it themselves. Whether that order produces a workable interim split before July 21, or simply a more formal venue for the same war, is the question the next three weeks will answer.

A miracle. That is what it now takes to make two Republican-controlled offices in a single American county cooperate enough to count votes — and a state supreme court has now essentially ordered the miracle into being. The system that ran on trust has run out of it, and the man who drained the tank is the one demanding everyone else refill it on his terms.

Where Democracy Fights Back

It is fighting back, here, in the least cinematic way imaginable — through a board of Republican supervisors who will not break.

This is worth naming precisely, because it complicates the easy story. The people standing between Heap and full control of Maricopa’s machinery are not progressives. They are conservatives — a 4–1 Republican majority, in the Richer mold: people who believe the 2020 election was legitimate, who will not chase rigged-machine fantasies, and who have absorbed a year of lawsuits, contempt threats, and six-figure fines rather than hand over the apparatus. They won a stay at the appellate level; the case has since climbed to the state supreme court, which has so far refused to let the machinery be seized before an election rather than after. They are forcing the fight into courts that, for all their slowness, keep declining to hand over the apparatus on the eve of a vote.

The Republican county attorney, Rachel Mitchell, is fighting too — her lawyer arguing that America First Legal is using its representation of the recorder as a “launching pad for an unprecedented power grab,” usurping her authority in the process. These are not heroes of the resistance. They are institutionalists, and in this moment the distance between an institutionalist and a defender of democracy has collapsed to nothing.

That is the uncomfortable lesson Maricopa teaches. When the machinery is under assault from the inside, the line that holds is not partisan. It runs between people who believe the counting must be protected and people who believe the counting must be won. On one side of that line this week stood a recorder, a coup lawyer floated as “neutral,” and Stephen Miller’s legal foundation. On the other stood four Republican supervisors, a Republican prosecutor, an appeals court using the word “consequences,” and now a state supreme court that has refused to be rushed.

The wall in Florida did not break; the court walked around it. In Georgia, the machinery stopped at the capitol door. In Maricopa, it has not stopped — but it has not won either. It is jammed, mid-seizure, in the gears of a system that was built, with more foresight than its designers are usually credited for, to make exactly this kind of capture require a miracle.

The miracle the judge asked for is cooperation. The miracle democracy needs is that the people refusing to cooperate are, for once, refusing on the right side.
