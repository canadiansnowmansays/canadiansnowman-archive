Part XXIII closed with a general abolishing a six-century dynasty and building a republic on its ruins, and that act turned out to be a preview of the century that followed. Across the twentieth century, crowns fell faster than at any other time in the history this series has traced. Emperors and sultans, shahs and tsars, kaisers and kings gave way almost everywhere to presidents, premiers, general secretaries, and chairmen, most of them governing under written constitutions that explicitly rejected hereditary rule as a relic of a less enlightened age. And then, in a surprising number of those new republics, something curious happened. The dynasty came back. It did not return under its old name, with a coronation or a crown, but in the form this series has taken to calling the family business: a ruler who keeps every outward form of a republic, the constitution, the elections, the parliament, the title of president, and then, when the time comes, hands the country to his son anyway.

This part belongs in a series about wannabe kings because it shows the ambition this series has been tracking in its most fully realized form. Part XXV will turn to men who want a throne’s authority without having earned a throne’s legitimacy. The rulers examined here got something more valuable than authority. They secured the one feature of monarchy that every other form of personal rule has found almost impossible to replicate, an answer to the question of what happens after the ruler dies. Every monarchy this series has examined, from the Mughal wars of succession in Part IX to the Ottoman fratricide law in Part XXIII, treated succession as the single most dangerous moment in the life of a regime. The family business solves that problem the oldest way there is, and then insists, with a perfectly straight face, that it has done nothing of the kind.

North Korea offers this series the purest example anywhere in the modern world, and the one that has travelled furthest from its own founding principles. The Democratic People’s Republic of Korea was established in 1948 as a Communist state, under an ideology that regarded hereditary privilege as the defining evil of the feudal order it had come to abolish, and its founding leader, Kim Il Sung, ruled it for nearly half a century until his death in 1994. His son, Kim Jong Il, had been groomed as his successor for two decades before that, and inherited not merely his father’s office but a fully elaborated mythology to go with it. Official North Korean histories place Kim Jong Il’s birth in a secret guerrilla camp on the slopes of Mount Paektu, the sacred mountain of Korean national legend, heralded by a double rainbow and a new star in the sky. Soviet records indicate that he was in fact born in a military camp near Khabarovsk, in the Russian Far East, in 1941. The gap between those two accounts is the whole story of the family business in miniature: a regime that had abolished divine kingship reached back almost immediately for the oldest legitimating tools this series has examined, sacred geography and miraculous birth, the same equipment Part XI traced through the Solomonic legend of Ethiopia’s emperors.

The mythology did not stop with the son. North Korea’s 1998 constitution named the deceased Kim Il Sung the Eternal President of the Republic, a title he retains to this day, making North Korea, in a strict legal sense, a state governed by a dead man, and after Kim Jong Il’s own death in December of 2011, he too was elevated to an eternal title. Their grandson and son, Kim Jong Un, then in his late twenties and with almost no public profile beyond his lineage, inherited the country within days. North Korean propaganda has come to call this line the Paektu bloodline, and the phrase is revealing, because it is not the language of a Communist party at all. It is the language of a royal house. Kim Jong Un’s subsequent consolidation of power followed a pattern this series recognizes from its darkest chapters. In December of 2013 his uncle, Jang Song Thaek, long regarded as the regime’s second most powerful figure, was arrested at a party meeting in front of television cameras and executed within days. In February of 2017, Kim Jong Un’s older half-brother, Kim Jong Nam, a potential rival claimant who had lived for years in exile, was killed in the departure hall of Kuala Lumpur’s international airport when two women smeared the nerve agent VX on his face. The Ottoman sultans examined in Part XXIII at least wrote their fratricide into law. Since late 2022, Kim Jong Un has made a point of appearing at missile launches and military parades with his young daughter, known to outside observers as Kim Ju Ae, and intelligence agencies in South Korea have openly speculated that she is being positioned as a fourth-generation heir. A state founded to end feudalism may be about to produce the fourth hereditary ruler in a single unbroken line, a record of dynastic continuity that several of the European royal houses examined in Part XXVI cannot match within living memory.

Syria’s version of the family business ended where North Korea’s has not yet ended, and its collapse is instructive. Hafez al-Assad, an air force officer, seized power in a 1970 coup and ruled Syria for three decades through a combination of Baath Party ideology, a security apparatus drawn heavily from his own Alawite minority community, and episodes of spectacular violence, most notoriously the 1982 destruction of the city of Hama, where the army killed many thousands of people to crush an Islamist uprising. Hafez had groomed his eldest son, Bassel, as his successor, but Bassel died in a car crash in 1994, and the family recalled his younger brother, Bashar, from London, where he had been training as an ophthalmologist. When Hafez died in June of 2000, Syria’s constitution required a president to be at least forty years old. Bashar was thirty-four. Within hours of his father’s death, Syria’s parliament amended the constitution to lower the minimum age to thirty-four, and Bashar was confirmed as president in a referendum shortly afterward. It is difficult to imagine a more precise illustration of this part’s argument: a republic’s constitution was not abolished to make way for an heir, merely adjusted, overnight, to fit his exact measurements.

Bashar al-Assad’s inheritance lasted twenty-four years. When the Arab Spring uprisings examined in Part XVII reached Syria in 2011, he answered peaceful protest with the full weight of the security state his father had built, and the country descended into a civil war that killed hundreds of thousands and displaced roughly half of its population. The Gulf monarchies of Part XVII survived those same years by spending money; Assad survived them with Russian air power, Iranian and Hezbollah fighters, and his own army’s willingness to bomb its own cities. When a rebel offensive swept toward Damascus in late 2024, those foreign patrons were distracted or exhausted, and on the eighth of December, 2024, Bashar al-Assad fled to Moscow. The family business had lasted fifty-four years, two generations, and it ended exactly as this series has seen so many true monarchies end, with the ruler departing into foreign exile while the palace was looted behind him.

Azerbaijan shows the family business conducted with considerably more procedural polish. Heydar Aliyev, a former officer of the Soviet KGB who had led Soviet Azerbaijan in the 1970s and 1980s, returned to power as the president of an independent Azerbaijan in 1993 and ruled until 2003, when, gravely ill, he stood aside as a presidential candidate in favour of his son, Ilham. Ilham Aliyev won the election of October 2003, which international observers criticized sharply, and his father died in a hospital in Cleveland, Ohio, that December. A 2009 referendum removed presidential term limits entirely. And in 2017, Ilham appointed his wife, Mehriban Aliyeva, to the newly created post of First Vice-President, placing her first in the constitutional line of succession, a detail that makes Azerbaijan’s republic, on paper, one of the very few in the world whose presidency could pass legally from husband to wife. Azerbaijan’s oil and gas revenue, meanwhile, has financed the same kind of bargain this series traced through the Gulf in Part XVII, a family whose hold on the state rests less on any theory of legitimacy than on its ability to keep the money flowing.

Cambodia offers this series its most pointed irony, because it is a country that has a family business and a king at the same time. Cambodia’s actual monarch, King Norodom Sihamoni, whose restored throne this series will note in Part XXVI, presides over a purely ceremonial constitutional role. The real power has rested for four decades with Hun Sen, a former Khmer Rouge battalion commander who defected to Vietnam, returned with the Vietnamese army that toppled the Khmer Rouge, and became prime minister in 1985. He governed for thirty-eight years, steadily hollowing out the opposition, and in 2023, after an election from which the main opposition party had been barred on a technicality, he handed the office of prime minister to his eldest son, Hun Manet, a graduate of the United States Military Academy at West Point. Hun Sen then took the presidency of the Senate, placing himself in the position of acting head of state whenever the king travels abroad. Cambodia therefore has two dynasties: one that reigns and owns the palace, and one that rules and owns the country.

The list could continue for most of this essay’s remaining length. In Haiti, François Duvalier, known as Papa Doc, declared himself president for life in 1964 and was succeeded on his death in 1971 by his nineteen-year-old son, Jean-Claude, until a popular uprising drove the younger Duvalier into French exile in 1986. In Nicaragua, the Somoza family governed as a dynasty from the 1930s until the Sandinista revolution of 1979, and in one of history’s more mordant turns, the revolution’s own leader, Daniel Ortega, has since governed with his wife, Rosario Murillo, first as his vice-president and, following a 2025 constitutional reform, as his formal co-president. In Turkmenistan, Gurbanguly Berdimuhamedov handed the presidency to his son Serdar in 2022. In Togo, Gnassingbé Eyadéma died in 2005 after thirty-eight years in power, and the army installed his son Faure. In Gabon, Omar Bongo ruled for forty-one years and was succeeded by his son Ali in 2009, until the army deposed Ali in 2023. In Chad, when President Idriss Déby was killed at the front in 2021, a military council installed his son Mahamat, who was later elected president in his own right. In Equatorial Guinea, the world’s longest-serving head of state, Teodoro Obiang, has made his son Teodorín vice-president. Across four continents and several political traditions, Communist, Baathist, anti-Communist, and nominally none at all, the same solution kept appearing, because the same problem kept appearing.

One counterexample deserves more than a line, because it is the family business’s equivalent of Bhutan’s voluntary abdication in Part XXI. Chiang Kai-shek ruled the Republic of China, first on the mainland and then in exile on Taiwan, under martial law, and on his death in 1975 power passed, after a brief interval, to his son, Chiang Ching-kuo, who became president in 1978. Everything in this part so far would predict a third generation. Instead, in 1985, Chiang Ching-kuo stated publicly that no member of the Chiang family could or would succeed him. He chose as his vice-president a Taiwan-born technocrat, Lee Teng-hui, legalized opposition parties in practice in 1986, lifted martial law in 1987, and died the following year. Lee went on to become the first directly elected president in Taiwan’s history in 1996. The son of a dictator used an inherited office to dismantle the inheritance, much as Jigme Singye Wangchuck used absolute power to give absolute power away. It happens. It happens rarely enough that both cases stand out in this series like lighthouses.

Then there are the rulers who have built everything a family business needs except, so far, the family succession itself. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, prime minister of Turkey from 2003 and president since 2014, converted Atatürk’s parliamentary republic into an executive presidency through a 2017 referendum, and at one point appointed his son-in-law finance minister. Alexander Lukashenko has governed Belarus since 1994, removed term limits by referendum in 2004, survived mass protests over a disputed election in 2020, and has spent years bringing his youngest son to state occasions. Xi Jinping, himself the son of a senior Communist revolutionary, one of the so-called princelings whose family standing eased their ascent within the party, secured the removal of presidential term limits in 2018, reopening the question of personal succession that Part III traced through three thousand years of Chinese dynasties. None of these men has named a hereditary heir, and the difference matters. But each has dismantled the mechanism that would have made a successor someone else’s decision, which is the first and hardest step in any family business.

What these regimes demonstrate, taken together, is that the problem this series has been examining since Part I did not disappear when the crowns did. Every political system has to answer the question of who rules next, and most of the twentieth century’s dictatorships, having rejected hereditary monarchy by name, discovered that they had no better answer to offer. Parties can split, juntas can quarrel, and a strongman’s lieutenants tend to eye each other the moment his health falters. A son, by contrast, is an answer everyone understands, one that the old elites can accept without having to fight each other, and one that carries the ruler’s own name and networks forward intact. That is exactly why monarchy became the default condition of organized human society in the first place, as this series has argued from its opening parts. The family business is monarchy rediscovered by people who had publicly sworn never to rediscover it.

It is also monarchy without the one feature this series has found most important to any throne’s durability, the story. The Habsburgs, the Solomonic emperors, the Chrysanthemum Throne, even the Romanovs, all told their subjects openly what they were, and their legitimacy, where it held, held because enough people believed that story. The family business cannot tell its story openly, because its founding documents forbid it. It must present each succession as an accident, an election, a party decision, or a constitutional adjustment, and that permanent pretense leaves it brittle in exactly the way this series has found stolen or improvised legitimacy to be brittle. The Duvaliers, the Somozas, the Assads, and the Bongos all discovered that a borrowed crown, worn under a different name, falls off just as easily as a real one when the army or the street decides it no longer believes.

Part XXV turns, at last, to the comparison that first prompted this entire series, and to the men who want a throne’s authority without ever having earned, through bloodline or covenant, a throne’s legitimacy, from Julius Caesar and Napoleon to the rulers of the present day.
