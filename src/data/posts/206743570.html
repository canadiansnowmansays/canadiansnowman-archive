Part XXII closed with a Thai monarchy that survived colonial partition through geography and diplomacy alone. The Ottoman Empire, ruling from Anatolia across the Balkans, the Middle East, and North Africa for over six centuries, faced no comparable colonial threat for most of its existence, having been for long stretches of its own history the very colonial power other states in this series feared rather than the party being colonized. Its own eventual end, when it finally arrived, came neither through foreign conquest of the capital nor through popular revolution against the reigning sultan personally, but through the deliberate, calculated decision of a victorious general who concluded, correctly as it turned out, that the empire’s survival and the sultanate’s survival were no longer the same question at all.

The dynasty traces its founding to Osman I, a minor Turkic frontier chieftain in northwestern Anatolia around 1299, whose small principality expanded steadily over the following century and a half through a combination of military conquest, strategic marriage alliance, and the incorporation of defeated rivals’ territories and fighting men into an increasingly formidable and religiously legitimated frontier state, a pattern of steady territorial accretion this series recognizes broadly from the early Mesopotamian city-state expansion examined in Part I, though compressed here into a considerably more explicitly Islamic legitimating framework from its very outset. The empire’s defining early achievement came under Sultan Mehmed II in 1453, when Ottoman forces, employing massive purpose-built siege cannons of a scale previously unseen in the region, finally breached the ancient walls of Constantinople and captured the Byzantine Empire’s capital after an eight-week siege, ending the last direct institutional continuation of the Roman Empire this series traced in Part IV, surviving in its eastern, Byzantine form nearly a full millennium after the western Roman line Augustus founded had already collapsed.

The empire’s legitimating theology deepened considerably in the following century under Sultan Selim I, who in 1517 defeated the Mamluk Sultanate and brought Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem under Ottoman control, at which point the Ottoman sultans began asserting the title of caliph, claiming succession to the historical office understood by much of the Muslim world as the legitimate temporal and spiritual leadership of the entire global Islamic community, a claim to universal religious authority this series recognizes as functionally similar in ambition, if considerably different in specific theological mechanism, to the divine kingship claims examined across Egypt in Part I and Japan’s own imperial line in Part XVI. This combination of caliphal religious authority and vast, multi-continental territorial reach reached its peak under Suleiman the Magnificent, reigning from 1520 to 1566, whose armies pushed as far as the gates of Vienna in 1529, whose legal reforms earned him the alternate title Kanuni, the Lawgiver, among his own subjects, and under whose reign Ottoman naval power, architecture, and court culture reached a level of sophistication and international prestige that made Constantinople, by most contemporary European accounts, the most genuinely cosmopolitan and powerful capital on earth.

The Ottoman dynasty also developed one of the most brutal succession mechanisms examined anywhere in this series, a formal legal practice known as fratricide, codified explicitly in the Kanun-i Osmani, the dynastic law code attributed to Mehmed II himself, which held that upon a new sultan’s accession, he was entitled, and in practice frequently expected, to have all of his own brothers executed in order to eliminate any possibility of a competing claim or civil war over succession, a policy Mehmed’s own law code justified explicitly on the grounds that securing the wellbeing of the world outweighed the cost of a few princes’ lives. This practice reached its most extreme documented instance when Sultan Mehmed III, upon his own accession in 1595, ordered the execution of nineteen of his brothers, several of them still infants, a scale of dynastic bloodletting this series has not encountered in quite this systematized and legally sanctioned a form anywhere else in its survey, considerably more thorough than even the ad hoc succession wars this series traced through the Mughal Empire in Part IX. The practice was eventually softened during the seventeenth century in favour of a system of confinement, in which royal princes were instead kept secluded within a section of the palace known as the Kafes, or the Cage, isolated from any military or administrative experience that might prepare them to rule effectively, a change that reduced the immediate bloodshed of succession but arguably damaged the empire’s later governance just as severely, since sultans who ascended the throne after decades of enforced isolation frequently proved poorly equipped for the demands of actually ruling once they finally did.

The institutional mechanism underpinning much of this early military success deserves particular attention, because it represents a genuinely unusual legitimacy and administrative strategy this series has not encountered in quite this form elsewhere: the Janissary corps, an elite infantry force recruited through the devshirme system, a periodic levy of Christian boys from conquered Balkan territories, forcibly converted to Islam, rigorously trained from childhood, and bound by law to lifelong loyalty to the sultan personally rather than to any competing regional noble family or hereditary military aristocracy. This system gave the Ottoman state a standing military and administrative class whose members owed their entire position, wealth, and identity to the sultan’s own favour rather than to any independent inherited claim, a deliberate structural solution to exactly the kind of noble-power-fragmentation problem this series traced destabilizing feudal Europe in Part V, though it carried its own eventual cost: by the seventeenth century, the Janissaries had evolved from a disciplined instrument of sultanic power into an increasingly hereditary and self-interested military caste in their own right, resistant to reform and capable of deposing or even assassinating sultans who threatened their accumulated privileges, a genuine irony this series notes as a demonstration that even a legitimacy mechanism explicitly engineered to prevent independent noble power can, given sufficient time, simply recreate the very problem it was designed to avoid.

The empire’s long decline relative to a rapidly industrializing and militarily modernizing Europe, beginning roughly from the late seventeenth century onward and accelerating through the nineteenth, produced the phrase by which the Ottoman state became best known in Western diplomatic circles during its final century, the sick man of Europe, reportedly first used by Tsar Nicholas I of Russia in a diplomatic conversation in the 1850s to describe an empire whose continued territorial integrity, in his own estimation, depended entirely on the other European powers’ unwillingness to let any single rival claim the spoils of its collapse rather than on any genuine internal strength remaining in Constantinople itself. The Ottoman state responded to this relative decline with a series of genuinely significant reform efforts, most substantially the Tanzimat reforms beginning in 1839, which attempted to modernize the empire’s legal, military, and administrative systems along broadly European lines, guarantee equal legal rights to non-Muslim subjects for the first time, and reorganize taxation and conscription on a more centralized, rational basis, a reform program this series recognizes as broadly comparable in ambition, though considerably less successful in ultimate outcome, to the modernization campaigns this series traced through Meiji Japan in Part XVI and Kalākaua’s own Hawaii in Part XIX.

These reforms proved insufficient to halt the empire’s continued territorial contraction, particularly across the nineteenth century’s succession of Balkan nationalist uprisings, in which Greek, Serbian, Bulgarian, and Romanian populations successively won full or partial independence from Ottoman rule, each loss further straining the empire’s finances and its sultans’ own claim to represent a unified and stable political order. A group of reformist military officers and intellectuals known as the Young Turks, increasingly frustrated by Sultan Abdul Hamid II’s own retreat into a more autocratic and repressive style of governance during the final decades of the nineteenth century, forced a restoration of a previously suspended constitutional and parliamentary system in the Young Turk Revolution of 1908, and after a brief counter-coup attempt the following year, formally deposed Abdul Hamid in favour of his more compliant brother, Mehmed V, while the Young Turks’ own leadership, organized as the Committee of Union and Progress, increasingly dominated the empire’s actual governance from behind an increasingly ceremonial sultanic throne, a division of practical and symbolic authority this series recognizes immediately as broadly similar in structure to Japan’s own centuries-long shogun-emperor arrangement examined in Part XVI, though considerably more compressed here into barely a single decade before the empire’s final collapse.

Before turning to that final war, it is worth briefly noting the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913, fought only a year or two before the wider European conflict began, because they demonstrate just how far Ottoman territorial control had already collapsed even before the First World War delivered its own final blow. A coalition of Balkan states, including Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, and Montenegro, exploited the empire’s evident weakness to seize nearly all remaining Ottoman territory in Europe within a matter of months, reducing centuries of Ottoman presence on the continent to a small remaining foothold around Constantinople itself, and the scale and speed of this collapse gave European observers, and Ottoman officials themselves, a stark preview of just how little the Committee of Union and Progress’s own reforms had actually reversed the empire’s underlying military and administrative decline, regardless of the constitutional modernization the Young Turk Revolution had promised only a few years earlier.

The First World War proved catastrophic for whatever remained of Ottoman territorial integrity and sultanic authority alike. The empire entered the war allied with Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1914, suffered enormous military losses across multiple fronts including the defence of Gallipoli against a failed but costly British and allied invasion in 1915 and 1916, and emerged from the war’s end in 1918 facing occupation of Constantinople itself by Allied forces and the harsh, deeply punitive Treaty of Sèvres in 1920, which would have reduced Ottoman territory to a small rump state in central Anatolia while carving up the remainder among Greek, French, British, Italian, and Armenian claims. It was the rejection of this treaty, and the successful Turkish War of Independence that followed between 1919 and 1923, led by the former Ottoman military commander Mustafa Kemal, later honoured with the surname Atatürk, or Father of the Turks, that produced both a renegotiated and considerably more favourable settlement for a newly defined Turkish national state and, in the same historical moment, the final end of over six centuries of Ottoman dynastic rule.

The Turkish War of Independence that followed the armistice deserves a moment’s further detail, since it is easy to lose sight of just how genuinely uncertain its outcome remained for several years. Atatürk, technically still an officer of the defeated Ottoman government when he landed in the Black Sea port of Samsun in May of 1919 under the nominal pretext of overseeing demobilization, instead began organizing resistance to both the occupying Allied powers and to the Ottoman government in Constantinople itself, which he and his fellow nationalists regarded as having become a puppet administration incapable of defending Turkish national interests. His forces fought simultaneous campaigns against Greek forces advancing from the west, Armenian forces in the east, and French forces in the south, a multi-front war waged with a fraction of the resources any of his opponents commanded, and it was the decisive defeat of the Greek army at the Battle of Dumlupınar in August of 1922, followed by the recapture of İzmir the following month, that finally forced the Allied powers to abandon the punitive Treaty of Sèvres and negotiate the considerably more favourable Treaty of Lausanne in 1923, which formally recognized the sovereignty of the new Turkish state within borders substantially similar to modern Turkey’s own today.

Atatürk’s own approach to the sultanate deserves close attention as this series’ final case study in how a legitimating institution can be dismantled through calculated political sequencing rather than sudden violent overthrow. Rather than abolish the sultanate and the caliphate simultaneously, an approach that risked uniting conservative religious opposition with royalist sentiment into a single unmanageable resistance, Atatürk’s new Grand National Assembly first abolished the sultanate alone in November of 1922, while explicitly preserving the caliphate as a separate office, held by a member of the same Ottoman family but stripped entirely of any temporal political authority, a manoeuvre that split apart the two claims this series noted Selim I had originally fused together four centuries earlier, allowing the more overtly political sultanate to fall first while religious sentiment attached to the caliphate remained temporarily undisturbed and therefore less immediately provoked to organized resistance. The last reigning sultan, Mehmed VI, departed Constantinople aboard a British warship in November of 1922 rather than face formal deposition, living out his remaining years in exile in Italy, and his cousin, Abdulmejid II, was permitted to hold the now purely ceremonial caliphate for a further two years before Atatürk’s government, having by then consolidated its own authority considerably further, abolished the caliphate entirely as well in March of 1924, formally separating religious and state authority in the new Turkish republic and ending, in that single legislative act, the last globally recognized claim to the historic caliphal office that had passed, however contested its exact legitimacy along the way, from the death of the Prophet Muhammad’s own immediate successors through nearly thirteen centuries of Islamic history.

What the Ottoman collapse adds to this series’ broader argument, considered alongside the deliberate, calculated dismantling of Japan’s own wartime imperial theology examined in Part XVI, is a further demonstration that even a legitimating claim resting on genuine centuries of accumulated religious and dynastic authority can be unwound through careful political sequencing rather than requiring the kind of total violent rupture this series documented in France and Russia in Parts XIV and XV. Atatürk did not need to execute Mehmed VI or forcibly suppress a caliphate loyal to him; he needed only to separate the sultanate from the caliphate, remove the more politically dangerous office first, and wait two years before removing the second, a patient dismantling this series recognizes as a genuinely distinct fourth strategy alongside violent overthrow, negotiated buyout, and voluntary self-limitation for how a monarchy’s authority can ultimately be brought to an end.

Part XXIV returns to the modern era, and to a form of monarchy that never uses the word at all: republics that keep their constitutions, their elections, and their presidents, and quietly hand the country from father to son anyway, the family businesses of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, from Pyongyang to Baku to Phnom Penh to Damascus.
