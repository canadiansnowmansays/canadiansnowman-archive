Parts 96 and 97 covered Washington’s paralysis and the industry’s own reckoning with Amodei’s essay. This entry covers something narrower: not that the administration has chosen not to act, but that the specific reasons it gives for not acting frequently contradict its own stated policy, sometimes within the same news cycle.

The unprecedented ask, and the answer that cited nothing

As Jeffrey D. Sachs wrote for Common Dreams on September 14, 2026, the request Amodei, Altman, and Musk made of the industry — asking to be restrained against their own immediate financial interest — has no real precedent among industries of comparable scale. Amodei made his case while Anthropic prepares what could be the largest IPO in history; Musk, who had called Anthropic “misanthropic and evil” as recently as February, endorsed the request anyway. “Tobacco executives never did this,” Sachs writes. “Neither did the oil majors, the chemical companies, or the banks.” Trump’s response, delivered from a golf course in Ireland within hours, contained — in Sachs’s own accounting — no reference to any government assessment, briefing, finding, adviser, or classified estimate. Not one. Speaker Mike Johnson’s own response was more explicit about the reasoning behind the inaction than Trump’s was: “Congress is obviously less qualified than the people who are pushing this frontier to know all the ins and outs of it,” he said, before proposing that the appropriate legislative response was a single meeting with the executives themselves — a policy instrument, Sachs notes dryly, that reflects the fact that Congress abolished its own Office of Technology Assessment in 1995 and never replaced it with anything.

Sachs’s most concrete data point is worth stating without softening: across the 25 cabinet-rank posts in the Trump administration, not one is held by someone with a PhD in any field, and exactly one is held by someone with a STEM degree, at the bachelor’s level. Twelve hold law degrees. China’s and Russia’s cabinets, by Sachs’s count, each include more than ten PhD holders and more than ten additional officials trained in science or engineering. Whether or not one shares Sachs’s broader argument — that this same technical illiteracy explains the administration’s posture on Covid’s origins, the Iran negotiations, and energy policy generally — the underlying credentialing gap is a fact independent of the argument built on top of it, and it bears directly on this series’ repeated finding that nobody in a position to regulate this technology appears to understand it well enough to regulate it credibly.

The policy that undercuts its own stated rationale

Trump’s standard justification for refusing any AI slowdown — that regulation would hand China the lead — runs directly into a policy record that moves the opposite direction. As The Atlantic‘s Rogé Karma reported on September 15, 2026, the Biden administration’s 2022 export ban on Nvidia’s most advanced chips to China was, by multiple independent accounts, working: DeepSeek’s own CEO, Liang Wenfeng, told a Chinese outlet in 2024 that “money has never been the problem for us. Bans on shipments of advanced chips are the problem.” Over the course of 2025, the Trump administration steadily unwound that leverage — first pausing enforcement against third-country smuggling in May, then lifting controls on Nvidia’s H20 chip in July, then removing the ban on the more powerful H200 in December, a chip Karma reports is nearly six times more capable than anything China had direct legal access to before. The Institute for Progress estimated that full chip restrictions would have given the US a 20-to-50-times compute advantage over China by 2026; unrestricted H200 exports shrink that gap to nearly nothing. Multiple Chinese firms have released models performing close to Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s best this year, at a fraction of the cost, and Epoch AI estimates up to 59 percent of China’s AI-chip stockpile at the end of 2025 had been smuggled in through exactly the kind of third-country routing the administration briefly paused and then let resume — including, per Karma, China’s most advanced model, Kimi-K3, reportedly trained via a Thailand-based cluster of Nvidia’s own chips. Karma’s own comparison is the sharpest version of this contradiction available: it is the equivalent of insisting on staying ahead of a rival nuclear power while simultaneously selling that rival uranium.

The internal White House split nobody outside it can see clearly

The administration is not, on the evidence, unified even on its own terms. As The Atlantic‘s Jonathan Lemire and Toluse Olorunnipa reported on September 14, 2026, people familiar with internal deliberations describe White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent as sympathetic to additional AI regulation, set against Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s preference for minimal interference and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s demand — already covered elsewhere in this series — that Anthropic abandon safeguards against mass surveillance and autonomous lethal targeting in its Pentagon work, a demand Anthropic refused, resulting in Hegseth cutting ties with the company outright. Vice President Vance supplied the administration’s most cynical public reading of the entire episode, calling tech companies “begging the government to regulate them” a “Trojan horse” — an accusation of bad faith aimed at the same executives this essay’s opening section described making an unprecedented request against their own financial interest. David Sacks’s own fuller quote, beyond what this series has already recorded from him, makes the administration’s preferred alternative explicit: AI companies should “stop pretending you need anyone else’s permission. Stop pretending antitrust law has to be suspended so you can form a cartel.” MacKenzie Arnold of the Institute for Law and AI supplied the detail that undercuts Sacks’s framing: antitrust law would, in fact, likely block AI companies from coordinating on a mutual safety-pacing agreement absent a congressional waiver — meaning Sacks’s “just do it yourselves” challenge describes an action that may not currently be legal for the companies to take collectively, whatever any individual company chooses unilaterally.

Who gets a seat, and who gets a ban

The New York Times’ David E. Sanger, Jonathan Swan, Cecilia Kang, and Dustin Volz reported on September 18, 2026 that the access asymmetry inside this fight is as stark as the policy contradictions. Few expect Dario Amodei to be among the invitees if Trump’s long-discussed meeting with AI executives actually happens: Anthropic’s products have been banned by the Pentagon, and Amodei appeared to anger Trump directly by proposing the industry pace itself and admit outside inspectors — a proposal Trump dismissed while denouncing an unnamed class of “Conspiracy Theorists, Treasonists, Traitors and Leakers” politicizing the AI debate. Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown, by contrast, “raises less ire in the White House” and talks to officials regularly, while Trump had also spoken in the days before that report with Sam Altman and Elon Musk, both of whom endorsed Amodei’s slowdown plan in at least general terms — meaning the White House appears willing to engage with the same policy position when it arrives from anyone other than the man who first proposed it.

The prediction about the guest list turned out to be wrong, though not in a way that resolves the contradiction. The meeting took place over lunch at the White House on Tuesday, September 29, 2026, and Amodei was there. Sara Dorn reported for Forbes that the attendees included Musk, Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, Jensen Huang, Amodei, Sundar Pichai and Alex Karp, and that Trump, Musk, Zuckerberg, Huang, Amodei, Pichai and OpenAI’s president, Greg Brockman, signed a “White House Accord on Super Intelligence” of just over 300 words. It commits the companies, voluntarily, to internal controls on dangerous capabilities, internal monitoring teams, independent external auditors and board committees to receive their reports. The president called it “morally binding.” The document itself says only that “over time, it may make sense to codify these steps into laws or regulations.” So the man whose call for outside inspectors Trump dismissed in mid-September signed, less than two weeks later, a version of that proposal with the government’s enforcement role left out.

The reporting also confirms, with named officials, exactly the split Lemire and Olorunnipa’s account gestured at: Wiles began organising an informal group this year specifically to think through AI risk, with Bessent as its chief recurring member and other experts rotated in by topic. The group’s foundational text, per the Times, is Wiles’s own May 6 social media post: “This administration has one goal: ensure the best and safest tech is deployed rapidly to defeat any and all threats,” balanced against a promise to let “America’s great innovators, not bureaucracy,” drive deployment. Asked directly about Trump’s “hoax” framing, White House spokeswoman Elizabeth Huston offered a reply that reframes the entire debate as partisan theater rather than engaging its substance: “President Trump is saying that Democrats using A.I. concern as a way to score points is a hoax, just like their claims that climate change would kill everyone in six years.” That is not a rebuttal of the underlying risk case this series has spent nearly a hundred parts documenting. It is a claim that the risk case itself is a Democratic talking point — which, if taken at face value, would mean Musk, Altman, and Amodei are all unwittingly carrying water for the opposition.

Nobody’s job

The clearest structural explanation for the contradictions this essay has catalogued is that there is, per the Times reporting, no single person in the Trump White House actually coordinating AI policy. National Security Adviser Marco Rubio is rarely discussed as a player on these issues. The administration eliminated the Biden-era post of deputy national security adviser for cyber and emerging technologies outright. The national cyber director, Sean Cairncross, a former senior Republican National Committee official, had limited cyber or AI experience before taking the post, and his own office lost two top policy officials this summer. Sriram Krishnan, the White House’s senior policy adviser for AI, has also left; in mid-September, Sihao Huang, a top AI policy official in the Office of Science and Technology Policy, announced he was leaving too — to join Anthropic, the same company the administration has spent this essay’s earlier sections either courting or freezing out depending on which official is speaking. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has shed roughly a third of its workforce, about a thousand positions, since Trump returned to office, though its acting director said in September that it was hiring some 250 new employees back.

The result, in the Times’ own framing, is that AI has become the administration’s “latest hot potato,” with different officials cycling in and out of influence depending on the topic or the day of the week — a bureaucratic vacuum entirely consistent with everything this essay has already documented: an unprecedented industry request met with a policy citing no evidence, an export-control reversal undermining the administration’s own stated China rationale, and now, confirmed by name, no one actually in charge of reconciling any of it.

Two smaller details round out how thin the administration’s own certainty is. Sam Altman, in a mid-September weekend interview, reportedly appealed to Trump’s own vanity rather than his policy instincts, suggesting that a genuine US-China AI accord struck alongside Xi Jinping could deliver Trump the Nobel Peace Prize he has coveted — a strategy aimed less at Trump’s stated China-competition logic than at bypassing it entirely. And Nate Cohn, The New York Times’ chief political analyst, offers a useful corrective to how far this reckoning has actually traveled politically: writing the same week as the Times/Siena poll already covered in Part 96, Cohn notes that only 82 percent of voters have any opinion at all about which party would better handle AI — a strikingly low number next to the 97 percent who have an opinion on immigration, suggesting this is less a settled partisan fight than an issue voters, parties, and evidently the administration itself are all still working out in real time. The voluntary accord of September 29 did not change that. As of early October 2026, Washington had reached no binding conclusion, and the government’s own internal contradictions are themselves evidence of how unsettled the question remains.
