Last weekend, I published Essay #23 — “Under Our Feet” — about geothermal energy, the clean baseload power source that this series had somehow never examined. I closed with a line about how the energy source nobody’s talking about is the one we’re standing on.

Today, courtesy of Brad Plumer in the New York Times, I discover that the fuel nobody’s talking about is down there too.

And the story starts in Quebec.

Thetford Mines

A drilling site operated by Vema Hydrogen in Thetford Mines, Quebec.

Outside the city of Thetford Mines, in a region that once supplied the world with asbestos, a startup called Vema Hydrogen has drilled two test wells a thousand feet into the bedrock. They are injecting treated water into iron-rich rocks to trigger a chemical reaction called serpentinization — a process that occurs naturally when certain minerals react with water and rust, producing hydrogen gas.

The rocks here are ophiolites, created more than 400 million years ago when a massive chunk of oceanic crust was thrust upward. These formations made Thetford Mines the asbestos capital of the world, until the health risks became undeniable and the mines closed. A town built on a mineral that killed people may now become a town built on a fuel that could help save the climate.

“The potential is massive,” Vema’s CEO Pierre Levin told the Times. “You can find rocks like this all over the world, enough to produce billions of tons of hydrogen.”

I need to stop here and explain why this matters to a series about energy.

The Hydrogen Problem

Hydrogen, when burned, emits only water vapour. It could theoretically replace fossil fuels in shipping, aviation, steel production, fertiliser manufacturing, and chemical processing — the industries where electrification is hardest and where solar panels and wind turbines simply cannot reach. These are the sectors responsible for some of the most stubborn emissions on earth.

The problem has always been making the stuff.

Most hydrogen used today — mainly for fertiliser and refining — is produced from natural gas in a process that generates substantial emissions. That’s called grey hydrogen. In recent years, governments have invested billions trying to produce “green” hydrogen using wind and solar power to split water through electrolysis. It works, but it’s expensive — roughly $6 per kilogram.

Now a growing number of companies think a better answer lies underground. The Earth’s crust naturally produces hydrogen through serpentinization, and scientists have published estimates suggesting that natural hydrogen deposits could supply the world’s needs for hundreds of years. The U.S. Department of Energy has estimated that geologic hydrogen could be produced for less than $1 per kilogram — cheaper than fossil-fuel hydrogen, and one-sixth the cost of green hydrogen from renewables.

One dollar per kilogram. If that number proves achievable at scale, it changes the economics of decarbonisation overnight.

The First New Primary Energy Source in 70 Years

Vema's drilling platform; a handful of rock sediment showing a greenish hue from serpentinization.

Koloma, the best-funded company in the space, calls natural hydrogen “the first new primary energy source the world has found since nuclear power was developed in the 1950s.” That is not hype — it is a defensible claim. Solar and wind are ways of capturing existing energy flows. Batteries are storage. Nuclear fission has been around for seven decades. Geologic hydrogen, if it can be extracted commercially, would represent a genuinely new addition to humanity’s energy portfolio.

Koloma has raised over $400 million from investors including Bill Gates’ Breakthrough Energy Ventures, Amazon’s Climate Pledge Fund, and United Airlines. The company has drilled exploratory wells in Kansas, Iowa, and Idaho, using satellite-based imaging and AI-driven subsurface modelling to identify formations where hydrogen may have accumulated in extractable quantities.

In Australia, Gold Hydrogen reported hydrogen purity up to 86 percent from a well in late 2023. In Finland, a deep drill hole detected concentrations up to 46 percent in 2024. In January 2026, MAX Power Mining confirmed Canada’s first natural hydrogen drilling discovery at its Lawson project.

The Philippines held the world’s first competitive tender for native hydrogen in January 2026. Michigan’s Governor Whitmer ordered state agencies to study geologic hydrogen and identify barriers to development. The U.S. Air Force is exploring it as an energy source for its bases. HyTerra, an Australian firm, is searching for hydrogen in Kansas and Nebraska and has signed an agreement to explore opportunities in Oman, home to the world’s largest ophiolite formation.

The geological hydrogen sector has shifted decisively from theoretical discussions to active, funded exploration. But — and this is the honest caveat — high commercial risk persists. Nobody has yet proven that hydrogen accumulates in large, high-pressure, extractable reservoirs at commercially viable flow rates. Koloma, despite $400 million in funding, is notably quiet about its Kansas results. The technology is real. The economics are unproven. The timeline is uncertain.

Two Approaches: Find It or Make It

The industry has split into two camps, and both are fascinating.

The first camp — Koloma, HyTerra, Gold Hydrogen — is searching for natural hydrogen deposits that have accumulated underground over geological time, the way oil and gas accumulated in reservoirs. The challenge is that hydrogen molecules are tiny and may slip through rock formations rather than pooling in extractable concentrations. The early days of oil exploration saw many dry holes before the gushers came. Geologic hydrogen is at that stage now.

The second camp — Vema, GeoRedox out of MIT — is trying to engineer the production. Rather than hoping to find hydrogen that nature made millions of years ago, they inject water into iron-rich rock to accelerate serpentinization and produce hydrogen on demand. Vema’s Levin calls their precise combination of water, temperature, pressure, and chemical catalysts their “special sauce.”

The engineering approach is elegant but unproven at scale. The hydrogen could leak before it’s captured. Subsurface microbes might consume it. Injecting water into rock can cause swelling, creating risks of surface deformation or earthquakes — the same induced seismicity concern I flagged yesterday for geothermal.

Vema hopes to begin full-scale production in 2028. If it works, the question becomes what to do with the fuel. Hydrogen is notoriously difficult to transport and store, which means it likely needs to be used close to the production site. The leading proposals: convert it to clean methanol for shipping, produce sustainable aviation fuel, make greener fertiliser, or power local data centres.

That last one caught my attention. After twenty-two essays documenting the AI energy crisis, the idea that geologic hydrogen could power data centres — cleanly, locally, around the clock — connects this new technology directly to the central problem of this series.

Quebec and the Canadian Angle

The city of Thetford Mines nestled against the site of a former asbestos mine.

Brad Plumer’s reporting is set in Quebec for a reason. The ophiolite formations around Thetford Mines are geologically ideal for hydrogen production. But ophiolites are not unique to Quebec — a ridge of similar rock stretches from Costa Rica to Alaska, running through Oregon and California along the way. Vema is already looking at sites on the American West Coast.

But the broader Canadian picture is striking when placed beside yesterday’s geothermal essay. Canada has the geological conditions for both geothermal power and geologic hydrogen. It has a workforce trained in exactly the skills required — drilling, subsurface modelling, reservoir management, fluid handling. It has inactive oil and gas wells scattered across the western provinces that could potentially be repurposed. And it has remote northern communities that currently depend on diesel generators for power and could benefit from locally produced clean fuel.

What it still doesn’t have — for either technology — is a national strategy.

The Cascade Institute has argued that Canada can and should become a global leader in ultra-deep geothermal. The geology supports it. RBC’s Climate Action Institute said the same in February 2026. Now add geologic hydrogen to the portfolio of subterranean clean energy resources that Canada is sitting on without a plan to develop.

The World Geothermal Congress comes to Calgary in June. Perhaps someone should add a hydrogen session.

What Lies Beneath

In two days, this series has covered two technologies that share a common insight: the clean energy future may not be above us — in the sun, the wind, the orbiting solar arrays I wrote about in earlier essays — but below us. In the heat of deep granite. In the hydrogen produced when iron-rich rock meets water.

Both technologies are early. Geothermal is further along — Fervo’s Cape Station is delivering power this year, Google and Meta are signing gigawatt-scale agreements, and the economics are already competitive. Geologic hydrogen is perhaps five to ten years behind — the science is real, the investment is pouring in, but commercial viability remains unproven.

But together, they represent something this series has been building toward: a vision of clean energy that doesn’t depend on any single technology but on a portfolio of solutions, each suited to different problems. Geothermal for baseload electricity. Hydrogen for the sectors electrification can’t reach. Solar and wind for the vast middle. Nuclear for scale. Batteries and sodium-ion for storage. And all of it, potentially, available almost everywhere — because the heat and the hydrogen are underneath every country on earth.

The obstacle, as always, is not the physics. It is not the engineering. It is not even the economics, which are improving faster than most policymakers realise.

The obstacle is the same one I’ve documented across twenty-four essays: the gap between what we know how to do and what we choose to do. A government that pays $928 million to kill wind farms is not going to lead on geologic hydrogen. A country without a national geothermal strategy is not going to leapfrog into engineered serpentinization.

But the rocks don’t care about politics. The reaction happens whether Washington notices or not. Water meets iron, iron rusts, hydrogen rises.

The fuel is under our feet. The heat is under our feet. The question, as always, is whether we’ll look down.
