Ken Paxton’s campaign had planned to finish at the Alamo.

The rally was set for the evening of 2 November 2026, the last night of the Texas Senate race, in front of the most famous building in the state. It would have closed out what Paxton called his “Come and Take It” tour, named for the cannon flag of 1835 and the defiance of the men who held the mission against Santa Anna. The bus carried the cannon on its side.

The Alamo rally was cancelled without explanation. Paxton’s finale instead took place at Matt’s Rancho Martinez, a Tex-Mex restaurant beside a Quality Inn on the expressway north of Dallas. About 150 supporters came for the free nachos and margaritas. Matt Viser and Nick Miroff of The Atlantic were there. They noted that the slogan, in the circumstances, sounded less like defiance than an invitation to the Democrats to come and take the seat.

On 3 November they did. James Talarico beat Paxton by about 12 points, roughly 55 per cent to 43 according to The New York Times‘ count the next morning. Texas had not elected a Democrat to the Senate since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988, or to any statewide office since 1994. It did both on the same night: Gina Hinojosa, a state representative written off for most of the campaign, ousted Greg Abbott, a three-term governor. Hinojosa will be the state’s first Hispanic governor. Vikki Goodwin ousted Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick. Democrats even won the race for railroad commissioner, held by Republicans for 36 years, where Jon Rosenthal, a Jewish engineer, beat Bo French, who had once asked on X whether Muslims or Jews were the bigger threat to America. Adam Serwer wrote in The Atlantic that Texas Republicans had not been “washed like this” since the end of Reconstruction.

This series has followed the race since February. It is worth looking back at what the earlier entries got right, and what the result says that none of them quite anticipated.

The corruption referendum, decided

In August, “The Corruption Referendum” argued that the race would turn less on ideology than on Paxton himself: the impeachment by his own party, the securities case, the long list of scandals. Viser and Miroff found the clearest evidence for that in Bastrop, in the corner of the state locals call “Elon Land.”

Elon Musk poured almost $200 million into Texas, most of it to help Paxton. His Boring Company, SpaceX and Starlink are all in Bastrop. On election eve the reporters met a SpaceX project manager there, a conservative in a Carhartt hat and a silver cross, who had just paid $100 for three-quarters of a tank of gas. He said Paxton belonged in jail, and voted for Abbott and a third-party Senate candidate. Across several hours and more than a dozen conversations with people whose livelihoods depend on Musk, the reporters could not find one person voting for the candidate Musk had spent so much to prop up.

In Plano, a truck driver who had voted for Trump three times told them he did the same thing: Abbott for governor, the safe choice, and Talarico for the Senate, because he did not trust Paxton.

The map bore him out. A Times analysis found that every one of Texas’s 254 counties shifted toward the Democrats, and that Talarico carried 28 counties Trump had won two years earlier. Among them was Collin County, north of Dallas, Paxton’s home base, once so reliably red that it was known as “Paxton country,” as The New York Times reported. In rural Brewster County, home to Big Bend National Park, where the administration had tried to clear land and put up barriers, voters switched sides too.

James Henson of the Texas Politics Project at UT Austin gave the most concise verdict. In Paxton, he said, Republicans had the worst statewide candidate to top their ticket in the entire history of Republican dominance in Texas. In Talarico they faced an unusually skilled Democrat who arrived at the right moment.

The lawn ornament, confirmed

The last entry before the vote, “The Lawn Ornament,” took its title from a pickup truck in the Rio Grande Valley. The Democratic consultant Chuck Rocha had told Nick Miroff, in The Atlantic, about a man who pointed at his truck and said he could no longer afford to drive it. The piece argued that the Hispanic voters who swung to Trump in 2024 were swinging back over diesel prices and the deportation raids on their workplaces.

That turned out to be, if anything, an understatement. Democrats won all four Texas congressional districts that border Mexico. Vicente Gonzalez held the 34th, the district where Democrats had paid for signs showing his opponent with Trump under the words “Support ICE!” Majority-Hispanic counties in Texas swung almost 36 points toward the Democrats compared with 2024. Turnout in Texas reached about 10 million, a record for a midterm and close to presidential levels. It held up best in the counties Talarico won, including the heavily Hispanic counties of Cameron and Webb.

The polls missed it too. Marc Novicoff noted in The Atlantic that Hispanic Texans swung 42 points toward the Democrats, ten more than the polls predicted. Border voters had backed Trump’s promise to deport criminals, and got raids on people who had lived in the country for decades. Jessica Cantu Vuittenot, a former Trump supporter whose brother was detained after checking in with immigration officials, told the Times what that did to a family: “Now it’s not just hurting you—it’s hurting your parents, it’s hurting his children, his grandchildren.” In one South Texas House district drawn to be safe for Trump, the Tejano singer Bobby Pulido won by 25 points.

Mike Madrid, a consultant who studies Hispanic voting, told Viser and Miroff that more early ballots in Texas came from Hispanic voters than from any other group, something he had not seen in 35 years. He repeated the warning “The Lawn Ornament” ended on: Hispanic voters are more populist than ideological, and if the economy were doing well, we would not be seeing this.

The machine, outrun

“The Machine,” in October, looked at the obstacles to voting in Texas. They did not go away. Earlier in the year the Republican-led commissioners court in Tarrant County eliminated more than 90 polling places, most of them, Democrats said, in Black and Hispanic neighbourhoods. On election day a library in Fort Worth that had stopped serving as a polling place was still turning away about five confused voters an hour, as The New York Times reported.

The machine was built for a close election. It was not built for a 12-point one.

The new congressional map was part of the same machine. Texas Republicans redrew it in 2025 on the assumption that the Hispanic swing toward Trump in 2024 would last. Instead, Democrats won four of the five districts drawn to capture that swing, and nearly two-thirds of the state’s Hispanic vote, as Reid J. Epstein and Jennifer Medina reported in the Times. Abbott, now a lame duck, could still call a special session and ask for yet another map.

The irony is that Texas Democrats won by building a machine of their own. For the first time in the state’s history, according to J. David Goodman of The New York Times, a party fielded candidates in every state and federal race on the ballot, all 150 state House districts included. Talarico turned Paxton’s nickname for him into a fundraiser: more than 43,000 people bought “I’m a Talafreako” T-shirts, netting his campaign over $1 million. Cristóbal Alex, who advised a super PAC backing Talarico, summed up the Trump–Paxton partnership: “It’s like the two of them are part of this political A team. Except the A team was the C team.”

The drag, measured

In October “The Drag” reported Paxton’s own leaked complaint that Trump’s convention in Dallas had hurt his numbers. The results suggest he was right, and that the drag ran the whole length of the ticket. At Paxton’s election-night party in Dallas, a Republican activist walked out of the ballroom and blamed the president directly. Telling everyone to vote as if he were on the ballot, he said, was absolutely insane.

Dade Phelan, the former Republican Speaker of the Texas House, put it more bluntly the next day. Trump had asked Texans to “pretend I’m on the ballot,” he told the Times. “Well, voters imagined he was on the ballot. And it was an absolute apocalypse for Republicans.” Down the ticket the pattern held. Mayes Middleton had branded himself “MAGA Mayes” to win the attorney general’s primary, then outspent Nathan Johnson by more than six to one and lost anyway. “I was delighted when Mayes Middleton nicknamed himself ‘MAGA Mayes,’” Johnson said, “because I saw this coming.”

Not every Republican shared the mood. Before the Times had even called the race, Senator John Cornyn, the man Trump pushed aside in the primary for Paxton, posted his congratulations to Talarico and offered to help with the transition.

“The Contingency Plan” asked whether Paxton would accept defeat or fight it. He did not fight it. Before midnight on election night he conceded the race to Talarico in a speech to his supporters in Dallas, as KPRC Click2Houston reported. He never phoned the winner; Talarico said he watched the speech on television instead.

The wrong kind of Christian, elected

In October “The Wrong Kind of Christian” looked at Talarico’s faith, which he talked about more openly than any Texas Democrat in memory. He took it onto Joe Rogan’s show and into Fox News studios. He avoided naming Trump, so as not to lose the voters who had backed the president and whom he needed. In his final rally, at Stubb’s in Austin, he asked nearly 2,000 people to be the slingshot for his David against Paxton’s Goliath.

Trump called him “Talafreako.” It did not stick. Helen Lewis noted in The Atlantic that the rumours that he was secretly gay, or a vegan, or had checked into a mental hospital went nowhere, and the flood of ads on transgender issues did no better. Neither did the machines. Texas was supposed to be a proving ground for deceptive artificial intelligence, and it got something sillier. Paxton’s campaign paid for a television ad in which an AI-generated Talarico repeated things he had said in the past. Experts could find no deepfake of him posing as genuine footage, as Stuart A. Thompson reported for The New York Times. What they found instead were dozens of AI videos of Talarico dancing and singing, watched millions of times. The nearest thing to a real deception was an AI version of Vikki Goodwin, then running for lieutenant governor, appearing to endorse a “death tax.” “There are a lot of people who don’t know me yet, who might think this is me,” she told the Times in September. The campaign behind it called it “an obvious parody produced to entertain.” She won by more than six points.

At Paxton’s last rally a state senator warned that if Texas turned blue, their children and grandchildren would be enslaved. On the Democratic side Hinojosa spoke to the suburban women Republicans call “MAGA moms” about the privatisation of public schools, and the two ran as a pair. Beto O’Rourke had told Hinojosa, when she was deciding whether to run, that no single person can win Texas. It takes two.

“We just took back Texas,” Talarico told his supporters on election night. “We are tired of being told to hate each other.” He also had a message for the man who had spent nearly $200 million against him, and for “every billionaire megadonor, every trillionaire megadonor”: “We will not be conquered.” For a campaign that began under the shadow of a cannon flag, it was the right slogan, borrowed back from the other side.

What it means

Bill Miller, an Austin lobbyist who works with both parties, offered Viser and Miroff the cold-water reading. People who win always think it was about them, he said, when it is really about their opponent. People vote against people.

Serwer offered a different reading. Republicans have run Texas for more than 30 years, so when prices rose there was no one else to blame. For a decade the state had served as the party’s laboratory: voting restrictions, gerrymanders, book bans, an abortion ban enforced by a bounty law. Its Republicans had grown used to outflanking each other from the right in primaries, and were unprepared for a competitive general election. They got walloped, he wrote, “like Santa Anna at San Jacinto.”

Miller’s reading is partly true. Paxton was a uniquely weak nominee, and Trump made him the nominee. But Texas did not elect only Talarico. It elected Hinojosa against a popular incumbent governor, a Democratic railroad commissioner and a sweep of the border. Texas has grown by more than 8 per cent since 2020, more than any other state. Its new residents are more diverse and more likely to have degrees, and Hispanics are now a plurality. At Paxton’s restaurant rally, a Republican precinct official was asked whether Texas would be a purple state in 2028. “It already is,” he said.

The sweep reached the legislature as well. Democrats took control of the Texas House for the first time since 2003. Republicans kept the Texas Senate, but Vikki Goodwin, as lieutenant governor, will preside over it. Senfronia Thompson of Houston is the early favourite for Speaker, which would make her the first Black Texan and the first woman in the job. Gene Wu, who leads the House Democrats, set the tone before driving to Austin on 4 November: “Democrats have to deliver. No excuses, no whining, no crying.”

Alexandra Petri, writing in The Atlantic the next day, imagined the new order in Blue Texas, where people must now shout “Remember the Alamo—in its proper historical context.” Texas Republicans spent thirty years making sure nobody would ever have to.

Texas will carry 40 electoral votes into the next presidential election. The last Democrat to win it was Jimmy Carter, in 1976. For thirty years “Let’s talk Texas” was something Democrats said to cheer themselves up in October. On 3 November 2026 the state talked back.
