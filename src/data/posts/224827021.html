Sunday Satire. The members-elect, their districts and their circumstances are real, drawn from reporting in The Atlantic. The Office of Unanticipated Arrivals, its forms and its staff are not.

OFFICE OF UNANTICIPATED ARRIVALS Subcommittee on Surprises · Room B-0 (Basement, Down the Hall Past Where You’d Expect) Packet UA-2026 · Revision 4 (revised three times since Wednesday as further results came in)

A Word of Welcome

Congratulations, Member-elect.

If you have been handed this packet, our records show that as recently as late October your race was rated “Solid Republican,” “Safe Republican,” or, in at least one case, not worth rating at all. You are one of at least fifteen Democrats who have flipped House seats in districts President Trump carried by double digits two years ago. The standard orientation binder assumes you planned for this. This supplement assumes you did not.

There is no shame in it. Every member-elect we have spoken with insists they always believed they could win. We accept this. We simply note that believing you could win and booking a holiday for the week after the election are not, strictly speaking, the same belief.

Please work through the sections below in order. Section 5 applies only to members whose races have not yet been called. Section 7 will find you whether you read it or not.

Section 1: Compensation (Frequently Asked Questions)

Q. How much do I make? A. $174,000 a year.

Q. Are you sure? A. Yes.

Q. I was mayor of a small seaside city. That’s more than ten times what I was paid. A. We are aware. Several of you have now Googled it. The Office kindly asks that you stop phoning the Clerk to confirm. The figure has not changed since 2009, which is a subject you may wish to raise once you are settled, though not, we would suggest, in your first week.

Q. Does it come with a pension? A. After five years. Please win again first.

Q. Free health care for life? A. No. Your constituents will tell you otherwise in the comments sections. You will buy coverage through the D.C. exchange, and you will sit through the benefits session with everyone else. Even members of Congress must choose a health plan. Many find it humbling. Some find it informative, which is better.

Section 3: Wardrobe

Orientation is a little like the first day of school. Cliques form quickly, by region and by ideology, and people notice what you wear.

Acceptable attire depends on your district. A crisp suit is always safe. A trucker hat and cowboy boots are acceptable wherever your voters would find the suit suspicious. Members unsure which category they fall into should look at what they wore while winning and keep wearing it.

Members who arrived with only the clothes they campaigned in are directed to the attached map, which marks the nearest place to buy button-down shirts in bulk: the Uniqlo at Union Station, a few blocks from the Capitol. Shirts may be stuffed into a messenger bag on the walk back. The Office has observed that this is now the established route.

Photo calls. If you are summoned at short notice to a group photo (for example, with the Blue Dogs, gathered around a co-chair’s white 1997 Toyota Land Cruiser), do not apologise for being late. Take your place at the outer edge of the crowd. Every freshman class has someone at the edge. It is a fine place to start, and you will move toward the middle for as long as you keep the job.

Section 4: Housing

4.1 Notice regarding sleeping in your office. A number of your predecessors have slept on cots in their congressional offices to save on Washington rent. Members from across the aisle have been warning the new arrivals against it, and at least one member-elect has said she didn’t know people did this. The Office confirms that they do, that it is not a good idea, and that the cleaning staff have opinions.

4.2 Two landlords. Members who keep a home in the district will now have two landlords, two leases and two sets of pipes. This is called representation.

4.3 Roommate Matching Questionnaire. Several members-elect are considering sharing an apartment, following the example of Democrats now finishing their first term. The Office endorses this. A generation ago, members routinely roomed together, and some of the House’s best working relationships began over a shared kitchen. Please complete the following:

Section 5: Provisional Attendance (Races Not Yet Called)

Members-elect whose races have not been called may attend orientation provisionally, as a precaution. This applies, for example, to a challenger leading a long-serving incumbent by 335 votes, up from 99 when the doors of his watch party burst open behind him at one in the morning.

Provisional attendees will be issued a Provisional lanyard. They may attend briefings, receptions and the benefits session. They may sit at the back of the committee room. They may not order business cards.

Q. Isn’t it strange for someone who is 64 to be going to freshman orientation? A. No. This year’s class runs from 31-year-olds to people in their sixties starting a second career. Age is not the qualification; winning is. If your count holds, the lanyard is exchanged for a pin. If it does not, you keep the lanyard as a souvenir, and the Office will remember you fondly.

Section 6: Election Night, Reviewed

Several members report that they were somewhere other than their own victory party when the results came in. One was exercising in the dark on his tree farm and assumed the whooping in the distance was coyotes. It was his family and friends, running out to tell him he had won.

Section 7: Notice of Retroactive Support

Many of you contacted the national party committees during your campaigns and heard nothing back. One of you was outraised six to one in a district gerrymandered so heavily that no Democrat bothered to run in it two years earlier. Another got by on about $59,000 against an opponent with more than $2 million. You will now find that the committees have your number.

So that you will recognise it when it arrives, the Office has prepared a copy of the standard letter.

Dear Member-elect [NAME],

On behalf of everyone at national headquarters, congratulations on a victory we never doubted.

Our records indicate that we did not return your calls of [DATE], [DATE], [DATE] and [DATE]. Hey, turns out we were wrong. We look forward to a long and productive partnership.

Enclosed please find your fundraising target for the coming cycle.

Warmly, and from now on with great interest, National Headquarters

Members are advised that the enclosure is not optional, and that the warmth of the letter will be in proportion to the enclosure.

Section 8: Prior Commitments of a Personal Nature

One member-elect, who won a Missouri district that hasn’t been represented by a Democrat in more than thirty years, got engaged in 2024 and put the wedding off until after the campaign. It is now set for the week of Christmas.

The Office has checked the calendar. The House does not sit that week. The member-elect’s stated plan, to get through the campaign first and then seal the deal, is approved without amendment. This is the only provision in this packet that requires no paperwork. Congratulations.

Annex A: A Note from the Other Chamber

The Senate’s equivalent office reports that it has a member-elect in the same position: a pediatrician who spent fourteen years at a children’s hospital, says she never wanted to go into politics, and has just become the first Democrat elected to the Senate from South Carolina since 1998, in a state Trump won by almost 18 points. She has been advised that not wanting to go into politics is the correct attitude for a senator. Senate staff are watching closely to see whether it lasts.

At the request of the Office of the Attending Physician, the Senator-elect has also agreed to keep weekly office hours, open to members of all three branches of government, for conditions most often seen in children aged two to three. Presenting complaints are expected to include:

  • Whining and snivelling

  • Tantrums, particularly late at night and in capital letters

  • Refusal to share (credit, toys, classified documents)

  • Name-calling

  • Insisting the other side started it

  • Inability to hear the word “no”

  • Persistent claims that you would have won if only you had been on the ballot

Treatment is a time-out, a juice box and a gentle reminder that the election is over. A lollipop is given on completion.

The Office has been advised to expect the heaviest demand from the executive branch, where a single patient is projected to account for most of the caseload. Staff have set aside every Thursday afternoon for him, stocked the waiting room with Sharpies and a weather map he is allowed to draw on, and gilded the naughty step.
