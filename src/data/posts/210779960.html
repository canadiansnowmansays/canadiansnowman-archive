A comedian named Jamison Daniel, who posts as @AntiquarianMuse, put up a 69-second clip on X last week: a pitch-perfect impression of a White House press secretary explaining the Strait of Hormuz ceasefire, delivered as one long, breathless, self-contradicting sentence. His caption read, simply: “This is my favorite because it’s 100% accurate.” It has since been watched hundreds of thousands of times, and reposted with captions to the same effect: not “this is funny because it’s exaggerated,” but “this is funny because it isn’t.”

We think that reaction is worth taking seriously, so below is Daniel’s bit, more or less verbatim, followed by what it would mean if a real press secretary actually said it — which, per several hundred thousand viewers, is not much of a stretch at all.

THE BIT: “Not sure why we’re all still having trouble with this, let’s try it again from the top. This is a victory for the U.S., because our goal from day one was for Iran to open the Strait — which never closed, until after we attacked it, at which point it was completely controlled by their military. We had total dominance over it, and it was a waterway we could have taken ourselves, but instead we asked for help we didn’t need, and we’ll remember that our allies didn’t give it, because of the negotiating power of a Nobel Peace Prize recipient’s threat to annihilate civilization, in order to liberate them from a regime change that was not our goal — but we’ll take credit for it anyway, from an old man who hates us, to his younger son, who hates us for those reasons, and also because we killed his father. And while it’s not clear what Iran’s remaining nuclear capabilities are, following the ones we obliterated last year —”

IF THIS WERE REAL: The Strait was open. Then it closed, because of something we did. Then we said Iran closed it. Then we said we reopened it, alone, through strength. Then it turned out other countries actually brokered the reopening, which we did not want, need, or ask for, except for the asking. The regime we insist we were not trying to change now dislikes a different member of the same family than it used to, which we would be counting as a strategic outcome. And the nuclear question — the one that actually matters — gets one clause before the room moves on.

THE BIT: “— but that’s going to be a problem in two weeks. One thing is for sure: they now have more money and more control of the Strait than they had under the old deal, the one we ended, because it was terrible.”

IF THIS WERE REAL: A new arrangement, arrived at after a war, leaving Iran with more leverage over the Strait than the diplomatic deal it replaced — the deal that was scrapped specifically for giving Iran too much leverage. Not framed as an outcome. Framed as a scheduling issue, revisitable “in two weeks,” which is the same unit of time this administration has previously applied to healthcare, infrastructure, and, at various points, several other wars — a distance measured not in days but in how long anyone is expected to keep checking.

THE BIT: “I think that’s enough about authoritarian propagandistic regimes. Does anybody have any questions about the Trump Presidential Library golden statue?”

IF THIS WERE REAL: No further questions. There rarely are.

WHY IT LANDED

Comedy about a subject usually works by exaggerating it. This bit doesn’t really exaggerate anything — it compresses. Every individual claim in it traces back to something that has actually been said in some form over the past several weeks: that the Strait was never closed, that it was reopened through American strength, that a foreign leader’s intervention was somehow both essential and unwanted, that the war wasn’t about regime change even as the administration counts a regime’s shifting animosities as a win. Daniel’s contribution wasn’t invention. It was arrangement — taking claims that are usually spaced out across separate news cycles, where each one can be absorbed on its own, and putting them all in one paragraph, where they can’t.

That’s also, roughly, how this entire war has been narrated from the start: the three-day pause that collapsed within days, the “obliteration” claims revised more than once, the strikes that widened into Egypt and pulled Saudi Arabia in directly while the public messaging stayed fixed on total victory throughout. There’s a name for the pattern in some circles — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — and a 69-second comedy bit just did a more efficient job of demonstrating it than most of the actual briefings have.

FOR THE RECORD

The Strait of Hormuz is, as of this writing, open. Iran’s control over it is, by the administration’s own account, greater than it was before the war began. Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison. Senator Kennedy of Louisiana remains the sole Republican willing to say any of this out loud. And somewhere out there, a comedian who compressed six weeks of contradictions into one sentence is still racking up views for a bit whose only real joke is that it didn’t need one.
