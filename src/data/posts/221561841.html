On October 21, 2026, David Frum opened his show for The Atlantic with a warning about the next Congress. More of its members, he expects, will campaign on Medicare for All than American politics has seen in years. The bills already exist. Senator Bernie Sanders and Representatives Pramila Jayapal and Debbie Dingell reintroduced the Medicare for All Act on April 29, 2025, with 15 Senate co-sponsors and more than a hundred in the House, according to Jayapal’s office. Frum’s advice was to take the idea seriously, which in his case meant asking how anyone would actually get there.

His answer is worth hearing in full, because it is the best short case against single-payer I have read from someone who is not against universal coverage. It is also, uncomfortably, a fair description of what this series did in December 2025.

The argument

Frum’s case has three steps.

The money is real, even if most of it isn’t new. He accepts the proponents’ strongest point. Americans already pay for their health care, mostly through employer insurance funded out of wages they never see. Moving that spending onto the federal books is largely a transfer, not an addition. The Congressional Budget Office’s own analysis, published December 10, 2020, supports him on this: across five illustrative single-payer designs, total national health spending in 2030 would change by anything from $0.7 trillion less to $0.3 trillion more. The federal share is another matter. CBO put the increase in federal health subsidies at $1.5 trillion to $3.0 trillion in 2030 alone.

One correction, in the spirit of this series’ own corrections note: Frum attributes a ten-year cost of “about $30 trillion” to CBO. CBO’s 2020 work gave annual figures for 2030, not a ten-year total. The ten-year estimates above $30 trillion that circulated during the 2020 campaign came from outside analysts, the Mercatus Center in 2018 and the Urban Institute in 2019 among them. Frum’s broader point survives the correction. His guess that a plan starting in 2030 would cost “closer to $40 trillion” over its first decade is a guess, but not an unreasonable one.

The transfer needs a tax, and the tax is visible. Here is the step proponents prefer to skip. When employers stop buying insurance, the theory says, they hand the savings back to workers as higher wages, and workers pay the new tax out of the raise. On a blackboard, it nets out. In life, Frum argued, some people come out well ahead, some well behind, and everyone sees a large new deduction on the paycheque. In his words, the first job of the next president and Congress who meant it would be passing “the biggest and most far-reaching tax increase that Americans have seen on their paychecks since World War II.”

The workarounds are worse than the tax. He walked through the attempts to hide it. He described Senator Elizabeth Warren’s 2019 plan, which charged large employers directly instead, as creating a cliff that would discourage any firm with 49 employees from hiring a 50th, on the eve of an A.I. shift that already has employers rethinking how many people they need. And the claim that billionaires alone can pay for it, he said, is sloganeering: the arithmetic doesn’t work, and the very rich are the people best at not paying.

What politicians say: Medicare for All will cost you nothing, because you’re already paying.

What the transition says: you will pay a new tax, your employer may or may not raise your wage to match, and you will find out which in your first paycheque.

Translation: even if the destination is cheaper, the road there runs through the largest visible tax increase in eighty years.

Where I would push back

Frum went further than his argument needed. He described Sanders’s career as “decades in public life, almost no accomplishments of any kind,” and the senator as the one man in the Senate who can safely promise the impossible because nobody will ever let him try. That’s a debating line, not a record. In 2014 Sanders, then chairman of the Senate Veterans’ Affairs Committee, wrote the veterans’ health reform law with John McCain after the waiting-list scandal. A man who can do that is capable of the incremental work Frum asks for, which makes it fair to ask why he campaigns on something else. But it is not fair to say he has never done it.

The Canadian view supports Frum more than Medicare for All’s advocates might expect. People often point to this country as proof that single-payer works, and on universal coverage it does. But we did not get there in one Congress. Saskatchewan introduced public hospital insurance in 1947. Ottawa offered to share hospital costs with the provinces in 1957. Saskatchewan added doctors’ services in 1962, through a twenty-three-day doctors’ strike. The federal Medical Care Act followed in 1966, the last provinces joined in 1972, and the Canada Health Act consolidated the whole thing in 1984. That’s thirty-seven years, province by province, each step paid for and argued over before the next. And Canadian medicare still doesn’t cover most prescription drugs, dental care or eyeglasses; a national pharmacare law passed only in 2024, and covers only some medicines. This is not offered as a model for Americans to copy. It is offered as evidence for Frum’s real point: the countries that got there did it in pieces.

The confession

The part of Frum’s monologue that stung was his account of why American politics has become so demoralised. Too many politicians, he said, promise things they know cannot happen: manufacturing revived by tariffs, tax cuts with no spending cuts, and now Medicare for All. “Gravity, math — they all exist.”

In December 2025 this series published a bill. Part 10 called it “10 titles, 52 sections, fully scored.” It had five titles and twenty-two sections, had never been scored, and counted savings to employers and families as savings to the federal government. It listed senators as sponsors who had never agreed to anything. The corrections note, “Code Blue: Getting It Right,” sets out all of that, and the bill has been rebuilt. It was, in short, the kind of promise Frum is describing: confident, comprehensive and impossible to keep as written. I mailed it to Congress. Nobody answered, and on reflection they were right not to.

The rebuilt version, set out section by section in the Code Blue Ledger, is close to what Frum says reform has to look like. It has twelve sections, each drawn from ideas that have already won votes from both parties. It restores the enhanced premium subsidies for three years, renews the incentive for states to expand Medicaid, writes price transparency into law, ends surprise ambulance bills and funds 14,000 new residency positions. It pays for them mainly with site-neutral Medicare payment, an idea Republican health leaders have championed for years. It costs about $120 billion over ten years against about $160 billion in scored savings. And a pay-as-you-go safeguard means the new spending takes effect only if CBO certifies that the savings cover it. No mandate, no public option and no new tax on anyone’s paycheque: those moved to a separate “Going further” menu, each with its estimated cost beside it. It is a smaller bill than the first one. That is the point. A promise is worth what the person making it can deliver.

The smallest promise

This week brought a reminder of what breaking even a small promise costs. The New York Times reported on October 30 that premiums in the Obamacare marketplaces will rise by an average of 13 per cent in 2027, according to a KFF analysis of figures the administration released that afternoon as a data file. That comes on top of a 26 per cent average rise in 2026, the year the enhanced subsidies were allowed to expire. KFF estimates that individual-market enrolment had fallen by more than 4.6 million people by June, and a Commonwealth Fund survey finds the uninsured rate among working-age adults has risen.

Kate and Mark Dwyer, in rural Oregon, have already moved to the cheapest plan available. Next year it will cost them nearly $3,000 a month, more than a third of what she earns at a non-profit. “We are looking at going without next year,” she told the Times. “There is no middle ground. It’s either $3,000 or nothing.”

Most enrollees, those earning less than about $64,000, are still subsidised, and taxpayers will absorb their increase. The Dwyers belong to the roughly one in ten above that line, mostly older, self-employed or early-retired, who get no help at all. “These people are getting hit the hardest by rising health care costs,” said Cynthia Cox of KFF, “and they don’t get any help.”

What Washington says: the government’s open-enrolment fact sheet, published the same afternoon, did not mention the increase. It noted instead that three-quarters of subsidised customers can still find a plan for less than $50 a month. Many customers also received a one-time $500 cheque this month.

What the market says: open enrolment begins on November 1, two days before the midterms, at prices 13 per cent higher than this year’s.

Translation: a single $500 cheque, mailed a month before an election, covers one month of the Dwyers’ increase.

The campaign trail has noticed. In western Wisconsin, Rebecca Cooke, a Democrat who dropped her own marketplace coverage after the credits expired, is running on health costs. She supports expanding Medicare, but not Medicare for All: the distinction Frum asks candidates to make. Her opponent, Republican Representative Derrick Van Orden, broke with his party to vote for the extension, calling it a “temporary patch” for a broken system (The New York Times, “In Dueling Health Messages, Democrats Focus on Cost and Republicans on Fraud”, October 30, 2026). On the smallest promise, at least, the two candidates in that race agree.

Restoring those subsidies for three years is the first section of the rebuilt bill. It is also the piece of reform that has come closest: the House voted for it in January, and the Senate never took it up. If Democrats win the House on Tuesday, aides to Hakeem Jeffries say he will schedule a vote on it in the first days of the next Congress. It is exactly the kind of promise Frum says politicians should make: small, costed and half-kept already. It still isn’t kept.

The Pattern

  • 1947: Saskatchewan insures hospital care. Canada’s universal system begins with one province and one service.

  • 1965: Congress creates Medicare and Medicaid, for the old and the poor, not everyone. The partial step holds for sixty years.

  • 1993–94: The Clintons propose a comprehensive plan. It never comes to a vote in either chamber.

  • 2010: The Affordable Care Act passes by building on private insurance rather than replacing it. Twenty million gain coverage, and it survives repeal attempts.

  • 2019–20: Medicare for All becomes a presidential primary litmus test. No version gets a committee vote.

  • 2025: The enhanced ACA subsidies are left to expire. The comprehensive bill is reintroduced the same year.

  • 2026: The House passes a three-year extension; the Senate never votes. Premiums rise by an average of 26 per cent, and nearly five million people leave the marketplaces.

  • 2027: Premiums rise another 13 per cent. A new Congress arrives with more single-payer advocates in it, and the same arithmetic.

The partial steps passed. The comprehensive ones didn’t. That isn’t an argument against the destination. It is a record of which roads have actually reached anywhere.

Bottom Line

Frum’s closing line was that anyone promising non-incremental change in a country as large and complicated as the United States “is promising something they can’t deliver and probably don’t mean.” I’d soften the second half. Many people who campaign for Medicare for All mean it entirely. Meaning it isn’t the same as having a way to pay for it.

Universal coverage remains this series’ goal, and the ledger keeps it there. But the honest route to it starts with a bill that can pass, does what it says, and costs what it claims. Senator Hawley, that bill now exists, and it is smaller than the one I sent you. Its first section may be on the House floor within weeks. It is also the first bill that deserves an answer.
