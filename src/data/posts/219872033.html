Nicholas Soames was born on February 12, 1948, in Croydon, the son of Mary Soames — youngest of Winston Churchill’s five children, whose own remarkable life this series covered in Part X — and Christopher Soames, the diplomat and politician who had served as Churchill’s parliamentary private secretary through his final premiership and later as Britain’s last Governor of Southern Rhodesia. Nicholas was, in other words, a grandson on the maternal side rather than carrying the Churchill surname directly, in the same way this series has already noted about the family’s daughters: the name itself skipped a generation on Mary’s branch of the family even as the connection to Winston himself remained, by any measure, entirely direct.

He was educated at Eton, following the family’s now well-established pattern, and at the Mons Officer Cadet School rather than university, commissioning into the 11th Hussars and serving in the British Army until 1975, including a posting as equerry to the Prince of Wales — a genuinely close position within the royal household, attending to the future King’s official engagements, that placed Nicholas inside the establishment’s inner workings well before he ever stood for elected office. He worked briefly in the City of London and, by his own later account, spent time attached to a United States Senate office as well, before making his first attempt at elected office in the 1979 general election, standing in Central Dunbartonshire in Scotland, where he lost decisively to Labour’s Hugh McCartney by over twelve thousand votes — a defeat considerably heavier than his cousin Winston Jr.’s narrow 1967 loss, and one that meant Soames, like nearly every Churchill descendant this series has examined, entered Parliament only after first absorbing a genuine electoral rejection.

He entered the Commons successfully four years later, in 1983, as the Conservative Member for Crawley, a seat he held until boundary changes in 1997 shifted him to the newly configured Mid Sussex constituency, which he then represented for a further twenty-two years.

Soames’s actual parliamentary career, unlike his cousin Winston Jr.’s across the same decades, reached genuine ministerial rank — he served as Parliamentary Secretary at the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food from 1992 to 1994, and then as Minister of State for the Armed Forces from 1994 to 1997, a substantive defence portfolio covering procurement and military policy at a moment of real institutional consequence for the post-Cold War British armed forces. He later served as Shadow Secretary of State for Defence in opposition from 2003 to 2005, consistently arguing for stronger British military capability and firmer NATO commitments across a career that, unlike Winston Jr.’s more marginal backbench trajectory, actually touched the machinery of government policy directly. He was knighted in 2014, becoming Sir Nicholas Soames, and by the time of his own retirement had sat in the Commons for thirty-six years — the longest continuous Churchill-family parliamentary tenure this series has yet recorded, considerably outlasting his grandfather’s own periods in and out of office and dwarfing his cousin’s twenty-seven years at Stretford and Davyhulme.

This series has tried, at every turn, to resist writing hagiography, and Soames’s own record includes a considerably less admirable thread that deserves inclusion here rather than convenient omission. A 2005 study of conduct in Parliament, the book “Women in Parliament,” identified Soames as among the most persistent sources of sexist remarks toward female colleagues in the Commons, with several women MPs describing vulgar comments directed at them over the years; further allegations, reported separately, described repeated crude gestures made toward women attempting to speak in the chamber. In January 2017, he was obliged to apologize publicly after making mocking noises at a Scottish National Party MP, Tasmina Ahmed-Sheikh, while she questioned the Foreign Secretary — an incident that drew a formal complaint and became, for a period, a genuine embarrassment rather than a minor footnote. When his own friend Boris Johnson was later accused by a journalist of inappropriate physical contact at a private lunch, Soames’s own public comment — that such behaviour was, in his phrase, already “priced into the character” the way Donald Trump’s conduct had become for his own supporters — reads, in retrospect, as a fairly candid admission of exactly how thoroughly personal loyalty could outweigh the same standards Soames himself had been called out for failing to meet. None of this erases the genuine substance of his ministerial record or the principle of his eventual break with Johnson over Brexit. It does mean that any honest account of his career has to hold the defence minister, the Brexit rebel, and the MP repeatedly named for demeaning conduct toward female colleagues in the same hand, rather than choosing whichever version makes the tidier story.

The manner of that tenure’s ending is where this part of the series properly belongs, because it is one of the more genuinely pointed ironies this entire inheritance audit has turned up. On September 3, 2019 — the precise eightieth anniversary of the day Britain declared war on Nazi Germany in 1939, the declaration that set in motion the whole wartime premiership on which Winston Churchill’s global reputation still substantially rests — Prime Minister Boris Johnson expelled twenty-one Conservative MPs from the parliamentary party for defying his government on a vote intended to prevent a no-deal exit from the European Union. Nicholas Soames was one of them. The other twenty included several former cabinet ministers and, in Kenneth Clarke, a man who had sat as a Conservative MP for forty-nine years without interruption — a mass expulsion widely described, even by figures within Johnson’s own party, as one of the most ruthless single acts of internal party discipline in modern British political history. Soames himself, in comments to the BBC afterward, called the vote a matter he had not taken lightly, noting that in thirty-seven years in Parliament he had defied his own party’s whip only three times — a lifelong loyalist, in other words, cast out in a single stroke over a fourth act of conscience.

The irony writes itself almost too neatly, and this series would be remiss not to name it directly: Boris Johnson, the prime minister who ordered the expulsion, had at that point already published his own full-length biography of Winston Churchill, “The Churchill Factor,” and was on record repeatedly describing Churchill as one of his personal political heroes. The same politician who had built part of his own public identity around admiration for the grandfather expelled the actual grandson from his own party on the exact calendar anniversary of the grandfather’s most consequential decision. It is difficult to construct a cleaner illustration of this series’ recurring argument — that the Churchill name functions, in modern British politics, considerably more as a rhetorical resource to be claimed by whoever finds it useful than as a living inheritance owed any particular deference by virtue of actual bloodline. Johnson could invoke Churchill’s memory freely in his own political self-presentation while simultaneously expelling Churchill’s own descendant for the crime of exercising exactly the kind of independent judgment against executive overreach that Churchill’s own wartime reputation is, in no small part, built on having exercised himself, in different circumstances, against Chamberlain’s government eight decades earlier.

Soames did not contest the next general election, announcing his retirement from Parliament rather than seeking re-election as an independent or under any other banner, and his departure closed out, at least for the moment, the specific inheritance this series has been tracing since Part XII: some Churchill or Churchill-adjacent presence in the House of Commons across most of the twentieth century and into the twenty-first, running with real gaps but real persistence from Winston Churchill’s own first election in 1900 through Randolph’s wartime seat, a quarter-century absence after 1945, Winston Jr.’s Stretford and Davyhulme tenure beginning in 1970, and finally Soames’s own thirty-six years at Crawley and Mid Sussex — well over a century, across four generations, ending not in defeat at the ballot box but in expulsion by a prime minister who professed to admire the family’s own founding figure more than almost anyone else in British public life. Soames himself, in his own farewell remarks, described Britain’s declining international standing amid the Brexit crisis as being in “free fall,” a judgment considerably more pessimistic about the country’s post-Brexit trajectory than anything this series has yet quoted from any other family member, and one that reads, in the context of everything Parts VI through VIII established about his grandfather’s own vision of Britain’s role in the world, as something close to an epitaph for the entire imperial and Atlanticist worldview this family had spent five generations helping to build and defend. It is a long way from the confident Atlanticism of Anthony Scaramucci’s “The Long Trade”, the essay that prompted this series — and the dissent came from inside the family itself.

After his expulsion and eventual retirement, Soames moved into the private sector, taking on the chairmanship of Aegis Defence Services, a private military and security contracting firm, and of an investment trust — a fairly conventional landing spot for a former defence minister, and one that stands in interesting contrast to his brother Rupert Soames, who built an entirely separate public reputation of his own as a leading British industrialist, running the temporary-power company Aggreko and then the outsourcing giant Serco, before serving as president of the Confederation of British Industry. Between the two brothers, the family’s twentieth-century political and military inheritance appears to have split cleanly into two considerably more conventional twenty-first-century career paths: one brother in government and defence contracting, the other running large public companies — neither path bearing much resemblance to the singular, stage-sized dramas of comeback and catastrophe this series has traced across the family’s first five generations.

There is a quieter observation worth making before this part closes, because it bears on the series’ larger argument about what actually gets inherited across a family this consequential. Soames’s actual ministerial record — defence procurement, agricultural policy, a substantive shadow cabinet portfolio — represents, by any conventional measure, the most genuinely successful political career any Churchill descendant has managed since Winston himself left office in 1955. It was not spectacular. It generated no legendary speeches, no wartime mythology, no comeback narrative of the kind this series has traced since Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street ruins. It was simply competent, sustained, substantive governmental service, delivered over decades, ending in an act of principled defiance rather than personal scandal or electoral defeat. If this series’ running argument is that the family’s inherited recovery template — catastrophic risk, followed by public redemption on a stage large enough to make the redemption meaningful — became progressively less available to each successive generation as the stages available to them shrank, then Soames represents something like the template’s quiet inversion: a Churchill descendant who built a solid, unglamorous, genuinely competent career precisely by not chasing the kind of dramatic risk his family’s men had specialized in for five generations, and who was, in the end, expelled anyway, for a single act of conscience, by a government led by the one person in modern British politics most publicly invested in his grandfather’s legacy.

Part XIV turns from Parliament to the family name’s remaining cultural and commercial currency — what “Churchill” still buys, eighty years on, for descendants who hold it without holding any office at all.
