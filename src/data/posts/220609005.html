In September 2016, a few weeks before the election, an essay appeared on the website of the Claremont Institute’s journal, the Claremont Review of Books, under the pen name Publius Decius Mus. It was called “The Flight 93 Election,” and its argument fit in a single sentence: the country was a hijacked airliner, and conservatives had a choice between charging the cockpit with Trump or dying in the crash with Hillary Clinton. “Charge the cockpit or you die,” the author wrote.

The essay was written by Michael Anton, a former speechwriter and Wall Street communications man who would go on to serve in Trump’s first National Security Council and, in the second term, to run the State Department’s policy planning office. It was the first sign that Trump, who had captured the Republican Party without reading much of anything, was going to acquire readers.

Part II of this series ended with a party that belonged to a man who promised “I alone can fix it.” A slogan of that kind is enough to win a primary. It is not enough to staff a government, train a generation of lawyers, or survive the death or departure of the man who said it. For that, a movement needs intellectuals. Between 2016 and 2024, Trumpism got them.

The settlement they came to bury

To understand what the new intellectuals were rebelling against, it helps to understand what they replaced.

From the 1960s until roughly a decade ago, the American right ran on a settlement called fusionism. The term is associated with Frank Meyer, an editor at William F. Buckley Jr.’s National Review, who argued that traditionalists and libertarians belonged in the same movement. Traditionalists cared about virtue, religion and moral order. Libertarians cared about free markets and limited government. Meyer’s insight was that each needed the other: virtue that is coerced is not virtue, and liberty without virtue degenerates.

Stephanie Slade, in her exchange with John Guida published by the New York Times on 5 October 2026 and republished by the Salt Lake Tribune, puts it in a sentence: fusionism is “the idea that both liberty and virtue are necessary for human flourishing.” Anti-communism held the coalition together through the Cold War, and Ronald Reagan was its electoral expression: tax cuts for the libertarians, judges and school prayer rhetoric for the traditionalists, and a defence build-up for everyone.

Fusionism had its weaknesses, and its critics were not wrong about all of them. It paid lip service to working-class voters while delivering mainly for capital. It assumed free trade would lift everyone, while whole regions of the industrial Midwest were hollowed out. It assumed a shared moral culture that was visibly dissolving. Pat Buchanan, as Part I described, had been saying all of this since 1992. What was new after 2016 was that people with doctorates started saying it too.

The books

The post-liberal turn arrived in hardcover.

In January 2018 Patrick Deneen, a political theorist at Notre Dame, published Why Liberalism Failed. His argument was that liberalism, the whole tradition of individual rights and limited government going back to Locke, had not failed because it was betrayed but because it succeeded. By freeing individuals from family, church, community and place, it produced atomised, rootless people, and a ruling class that despised them. The book was praised by, among others, Barack Obama, which tells you something about how broadly its diagnosis landed. Deneen’s prescription arrived five years later, in Regime Change (2023), which called for a new elite to replace the old one, and which was much less widely praised.

In September 2018 Yoram Hazony, an Israeli-American philosopher, published The Virtue of Nationalism, arguing that the nation-state was the best guarantor of freedom and that liberal internationalism was a form of empire. Hazony would become the organiser of a movement.

In 2020 Adrian Vermeule, a Harvard law professor and convert to Catholicism, proposed in an essay in The Atlantic what he called “common good constitutionalism,” arguing that conservatives should abandon originalism and read the Constitution in light of substantive moral ends. His book of that title followed in 2022. In the same period Oren Cass founded American Compass, which argued for industrial policy, tariffs and a pro-worker conservatism that would have made Reagan wince.

These thinkers disagreed with each other about a great deal. What united them was a conclusion: fusionism was dead, liberal neutrality was a fraud, and the right should stop pretending that it did not want to use the state to shape society.

The conference

On 14 July 2019, Hazony’s Edmund Burke Foundation opened the first National Conservatism Conference at the Ritz-Carlton in Washington. The speakers included Tucker Carlson, Senator Josh Hawley, John Bolton and Peter Thiel. The organisers framed the gathering as a declaration of independence from the libertarian and neoconservative wings that had run the movement since Reagan.

The conference has met almost every year since, and its speaker list over time reads like a staffing chart for the second Trump administration. JD Vance became a regular. At the 2021 conference in Orlando, he told the audience that the universities were the enemy, and he urged conservatives to attack them honestly and aggressively.

Slade’s verdict on this project is blunt. National conservatives, she writes, “bet all their chips on Trump and Trumpism as the future of the conservative movement,” and that bet now “looks at the very least more questionable.” That is a judgment about 2026. In 2019 the bet looked brilliant.

Theory meets temperament

There was always something odd about the marriage between the national conservatives and Trump. The intellectuals spoke of virtue, family, piety and the common good. Their champion was a thrice-married casino developer who had boasted about grabbing women and could not name a favourite Bible verse.

Sohrab Ahmari, then an editor at the New York Post, resolved the contradiction in a May 2019 essay in First Things titled “Against David French-ism.” French, an evangelical conservative lawyer and writer, stood for a politics of civility, pluralism and persuasion. Ahmari argued that this was surrender. The goal, he wrote, was to fight the culture war “with the aim of defeating the enemy and enjoying the spoils.”

It is hard to think of a cleaner statement of what Slade calls “will-to-power politics”: politics as “purely a war to determine which side will get to wield power to reward its friends and punish its enemies,” in which “hatred toward the opposition is treated as a virtue.” The intellectuals did not have to like Trump’s character. They needed his willingness to fight. He, in turn, needed someone to tell him what to fight for.

The Claremont Institute supplied a more aggressive version of the same bargain. Anton’s Flight 93 logic — that the country was in an existential emergency in which ordinary rules no longer applied — became a habit of mind. By January 2021, a Claremont-affiliated lawyer, John Eastman, was writing memos arguing that Vice-President Mike Pence could set aside electoral votes. The distance from political theory to a plan for overturning an election turned out to be shorter than the theorists had imagined.

The manual

Ideas need institutions, and the institution that turned the new right’s theories into a governing programme was one of the oldest and most respected in conservative Washington.

The Heritage Foundation had been Reagan’s think tank. Its 1981 Mandate for Leadership became a blueprint for his first term. In December 2021, Heritage appointed Kevin Roberts, a historian and former president of a Catholic college in Wyoming, as its president. Roberts moved the institution decisively toward the national conservatives.

The result was Project 2025, a coalition of more than a hundred conservative organisations led by Heritage. Its centrepiece, a new Mandate for Leadership, ran to roughly 900 pages and laid out, department by department, how a Republican president could take command of the federal government in his first hundred days. It proposed reclassifying tens of thousands of civil servants so they could be fired at will, bringing independent agencies under direct presidential control, and placing the Justice Department more firmly under the White House. Behind it sat a training programme and a database of vetted personnel ready to serve.

In July 2024, Roberts described the movement’s moment as “the second American Revolution,” which, he said, “will remain bloodless if the left allows it to be.” Trump, under pressure in the campaign, said he knew nothing about Project 2025. After he won, a substantial number of its authors and contributors took senior posts in his administration. Russell Vought, who had written the chapter on the executive office of the president, became director of the Office of Management and Budget.

This was the decisive change between the first and second Trump terms. In 2017 Trump arrived with a slogan and a family. In 2025 he arrived with a manual and a cadre. The first term had been constrained by people who did not share his instincts. The second was staffed by people who had spent years preparing to act on them.

The heir

Every movement that hopes to outlive its founder needs a successor, and the new right found its candidate early.

In 2016, JD Vance was the author of Hillbilly Elegy, a memoir of Appalachian dysfunction that became a bestseller and a guide for confused liberals trying to understand Trump voters. He was also, at the time, a vocal critic of Trump, calling himself a “never-Trump guy.” In a private message that surfaced in 2022, he had even wondered whether Trump might be “America’s Hitler.”

By 2022 he had converted to Catholicism, embraced the post-liberal and national-conservative ideas described above, won Trump’s endorsement and a Senate seat in Ohio. In July 2024 Trump chose him as his running mate. Slade calls Vance “an open proponent of anti-fusionist, Dissident Right ideas.” Unlike Trump, he can explain those ideas in complete paragraphs, and he has spent years cultivating the intellectuals who produced them.

That is what makes the question at the centre of this series serious. If Trumpism were only Trump’s temperament, it would end with him. But it now has books, conferences, think tanks, a federal personnel pipeline, a cohort of lawyers and an heir who believes the theory more sincerely than the founder ever did.

The crack in the theory

There is one more thread worth pulling before we turn to what Trumpism did with power, because it foreshadows Part V.

Movements that define themselves by what they are against have trouble deciding where to stop. National conservatives argued that the liberal consensus had wrongly excluded people from respectable debate. Some of the people they let back in turned out to be the reason they had been excluded. By late 2025, as Slade recounts, the Heritage Foundation itself was convulsed when Roberts defended Tucker Carlson’s friendly interview with Nick Fuentes, a young white nationalist whose followers call themselves “groypers.” Scholars, donors and board members left that winter. Many conservatives, Slade writes, “wanted nothing to do with groyperism,” and saw in the episode “ideological extremism and moral rot that had taken root on parts of the right.”

The intellectuals had set out to give Trump’s instincts a theory. They had not decided in advance which instincts to leave out. Heritage, which had built the manual, became the first institution to discover that a movement founded on will-to-power politics does not easily accept limits, even those set by its own architects.

That is a story for later in the series. First, Part IV turns to the question every theory eventually has to answer: what happened when it got the keys?
