Back in Part 2 of this series, I described the machinery aimed at the Smithsonian: the executive order titled, with perfect inversion, “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History”; the demand that nineteen museums hand over their exhibit text for vetting; the impeachment display “restored to 2008”; and the order to post presidential warning labels outside the National Museum of American History, like an explicit-content sticker on the door of the nation’s memory. Last time, in Part 11, I followed the same campaign into its quietest room: a one-sentence footnote on the Smithsonian’s operating money, designed so that nothing would ever visibly be taken down, because nothing would ever be put up. Both were grim essays, as most in this series are, and both ended where the others end — with the erasing still underway.

I want to return to the Smithsonian once more to report the other side of the ledger, the thing neither of those essays could. It is this: the attack on the Smithsonian may be the one erasure in this entire series that the public simply will not permit. Not because the administration lacks the will, and not because the courts have stopped it, but because of a stubborn and rather beautiful fact about museums — a fact the whole apparatus of the Ministry of Memory appears not to have anticipated. You can delete a webpage. You can pull a plaque. You can repaint a wall. You cannot, it turns out, easily erase an object that thousands of people a day walk into a building to stand in front of, and trust.

What the Public Actually Thinks

Let me lay out the numbers, because they are unusually clear and they cut against the administration’s assumption that attacking a history museum is a base-pleasing move.

When YouGov asked, in August 2025, how much influence the president should have over exhibits and programming at federally funded museums, fifty-seven percent of Americans answered “none” or “not much.” Only about seventeen percent of Republicans took the president’s own stated position — that he should have a “great deal” of say. When the American Alliance of Museums, working with Wilkening Consulting, asked whether museums under political pressure should remove content on subjects like slavery and climate change, sixty-three percent of conservatives and eighty-three percent of liberals said no — a bipartisan majority refusing the erasure on both sides of the aisle. And when researchers asked whether Americans believe museums push a political agenda, fewer than a quarter said yes — against well over half who believe the same of the news. Liberals and conservatives alike agreed, overwhelmingly, that museums are “welcoming to people like me.”

Set those figures against the backdrop of the age. Public confidence in almost every institution that traffics in information — newspapers, television news, universities, public schools — sits at or near historic lows, and the administration has spent years driving it there, on the sound authoritarian logic that a public which trusts no one can be told anything. But the museum, alone among the memory-and-information institutions, has stayed trusted. It ranks, in survey after survey, among the most trusted sources of information in American life, in the company of public libraries and one’s own family and friends. Gallup does not even include museums in its institutional-confidence surveys — a measure of how long they sat outside the culture war’s blast radius.

That is the ground on which this particular erasure is being attempted, and it is bad ground for the eraser. The administration’s usual method — discredit the institution, and the public will not mourn what you do to it — depends on the public already distrusting the target. Here, it does not. Coming after the Smithsonian, the data suggest, is not the crowd-pleaser the White House imagined. It is a move against one of the last things Americans across the divide still hold in common.

Why the Object Resists

The interesting question is why. Why has the museum stayed trusted while the newspaper and the university have not? And the answer is the reason this essay belongs in this series, because it is the same answer I reached in Part 9, about the witnesses — only relocated, now, into the objects themselves.

A newspaper article is an argument you receive secondhand. You must trust the reporter, the editor, the outlet, the sources you cannot see. Every link in that chain is a place for doubt to enter, and a hostile administration has spent years prying at each one. But a museum, as the researchers who study this kept telling the reporter who assembled these findings, is an in-person encounter with a physical thing. You stand in front of the actual object. The gun John Wilkes Booth actually fired. The chair John Hancock actually sat in. The desk on which Jefferson actually drafted the Declaration. You are, as one museum scholar put it, faced with the truth of the real stuff — and you cannot argue with the real stuff. The object does not ask to be believed. It simply is, in the room, in front of you, and its mere presence carries an authority no wall text and no press release and no presidential disclaimer can supply or subtract.

This is the deep reason the object resists erasure in a way the dataset and the plaque did not. The earlier erasures in this series worked on representations of the past — a caption, a sign, a spreadsheet, a name, a portrait. A representation can be edited, because it is already one step removed from the thing it represents; you change the words and the referent is untouched but unreachable. The object is not a representation. It is the past itself, physically surviving into the present, witnessed directly by whoever walks in. You cannot “restore it to 2008.” You cannot repost it with the terms swapped. You cannot patch the wall and pretend it was never there, because it is not on the wall — it is the chair, and the chair is still the chair, and everyone in the room can see that it is.

An object, in other words, is a witness that cannot be sent home. In Part 1, the administration could remove Sandy Weiswasser, the eighty-seven-year-old guide who remembered what the Kennedy Center had been, because a human witness can be dismissed. But you cannot dismiss Jefferson’s desk. It will testify to anyone who stands before it, in a language older and harder than words, and it does not report for a shift that can be cancelled.

The Disclaimer as Confession

Which reframes the strangest artifact from Part 2 — the order to post warning signs outside the Museum of American History — and reveals it as something I underread the first time.

I called those signs, in the earlier essay, a doubt stationed at the entrance. I want to go further now. The disclaimer order is a confession. It is the administration admitting, in the form of its own remedy, that it cannot do to the Smithsonian what it did to the parks and the datasets. It cannot simply delete what is inside, because what is inside is objects — trusted, physical, witnessed, owned by the institution and displayed in an authoritative building the public believes. So it does the only thing left available when erasure is impossible: it stations a sign at the door begging visitors to distrust what they are about to see with their own eyes, and directs them instead to government-preferred sources. Don’t trust the desk. Trust us.

That is not the move of a Ministry that expects to win the object. It is the move of one that has discovered the object cannot be taken, and has fallen back on trying to poison the visitor’s mind before they reach it. It is the difference between burning a book and standing outside the library telling people the book inside is lying. The first is the confidence of a censor who controls the shelf. The second is the desperation of one who does not — who knows the reader will get to the book, and can only hope to have discredited it in advance. The warning label is what erasure looks like after it has failed, and been forced to settle for slander.

The footnote from Part 11 does not undo this argument; it confirms it. That footnote is aimed at what the Smithsonian might do next — the exhibit not yet mounted, the label not yet written, the travelling show quietly deferred. It is dangerous precisely because it works on the future. But it cannot reach what is already in the room. A budget line can stop a new exhibition from opening. It cannot unmake the desk.

The Room We Have to Share

Let me end on the finding in all this that moved me most, because it points past the politics to something the series has been circling from the start.

A museum, one scholar told the reporter, has become a kind of secular church — a rare physical place where people who agree about nothing else go to stand, together, in an act of common appreciation before a shared object. In an age that has sorted us into information silos, each of us sealed among people who already agree with us, the museum is one of the last rooms we still enter together. And the reason is precisely its objects, because an object cannot be forked into two partisan versions. As the reporter put it, in a line I will paraphrase because it deserves to be encountered rather than merely quoted: you cannot saw Thomas Jefferson’s desk in half and give one half to the right and one half to the left. There is one desk. If we all want to see the desk on which the Declaration was written, we have to be willing, first, to stand in the same room.

That is the quiet threat the Smithsonian poses to a Ministry of Memory, and the quiet hope it offers the rest of us. Every other erasure in this series works by separation — pulling the sign so you never see it, deleting the dataset so you cannot find it, sending the witness home so she cannot tell you. The museum defeats separation by its nature. It gathers the divided public into one room, in front of one undeniable object, and lets the object speak for itself to all of them at once. You can post a sign at the door. Sixty-three percent of conservatives and eighty-three percent of liberals have already said they do not want the thing inside removed. They still want to see the desk. And to see the desk, they will have to keep doing the one thing this whole apparatus of forgetting is designed to prevent: standing in the same room, before the same real object, agreeing at least on this — that it is there, that it is real, and that no sign at the door can make it otherwise.

You cannot argue with the real stuff. It may be the most hopeful sentence this series has yet produced, and unlike most of what I have reported here, it comes not with a caveat but with the numbers behind it.

This is Part 12 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory. It returns to the Smithsonian — the subject of Part 2’s account of the attack and Part 11’s account of the footnote on its money — to report why that attack may be the erasure that fails: a bipartisan majority of Americans reject political control of museums, museums remain among the most trusted institutions in the country, and, most durably, you cannot erase a physical object a trusting public walks in to see. The warning label ordered posted at the museum’s door is not censorship’s victory but its confession — the move erasure makes when it has discovered the object cannot be taken. This essay is the counterpart to Part 9: the artifact, like the archivist, is a witness that refuses to be sent home.
