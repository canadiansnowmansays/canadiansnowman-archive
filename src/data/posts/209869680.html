Every country this series has touched so far has treated spectrum as sovereign territory, jealously guarded, doled out on its own schedule. And yet SpaceX keeps getting in. Not because sovereignty is fading, but because the reasons a government decides to let a satellite company near its phone network turn out to be wildly different from place to place. Necessity looks nothing like ambition, which looks nothing like protectionism, which looks nothing like plain old competitive convergence. Four countries make the point better than any general theory could.

Ukraine: connectivity as survival infrastructure

Start with the country where the normal rules simply don’t apply, because there is no normal left to apply them to.

Kyivstar, part of the Veon group, went live with Starlink direct-to-cell in the fourth quarter of 2025 — the fastest a major national carrier has moved from signed deal to commercial service anywhere in this series. Speed like that isn’t achievable through ordinary regulatory process. It’s achievable when terrestrial infrastructure has been deliberately targeted and destroyed, when cell towers are casualties of war rather than long-term capital assets, and when a government has no appetite left for the years-long consultation process that countries like Canada are still working through for the exact same technology.

Elon Musk had already signalled the posture that made this possible. Back in 2024, he said Starlink cell coverage would be offered free “for mobile phones for people in distress,” worldwide, “subject to approval by country governments.” Ukraine is the clearest test case that promise has ever received. When the alternative to satellite connectivity isn’t a slower rollout but no connectivity at all, the calculus that governs every other country in this series — auction versus administrative allocation, exclusive versus non-exclusive partnership, MVNO structure versus vertical integration — simply doesn’t apply. There is no retention desk to negotiate with when the terrestrial network has been shelled. Necessity is its own regulatory approval.

This is also, worth noting, the case that most cleanly validates SpaceX’s stated ambition. If the point of direct-to-cell is eliminating dead zones and functioning through disasters — the same language Shotwell used in Part III to describe the American opportunity — Ukraine is the closest thing to a live demonstration the company has. A market that would have taken a decade to develop under normal telecom timelines was live in months, precisely because the value proposition stopped being theoretical.

There’s a broader lesson buried in Ukraine’s case for how this series should think about SpaceX’s stated 2028 timeline. Every other country in this set involves some negotiation over the pace of adoption — a regulator weighing costs and benefits, a carrier weighing whether to commit early or wait. Ukraine involved no such weighing. When infrastructure is destroyed faster than it can be rebuilt through conventional means, satellite connectivity stops competing against an existing terrestrial network on price or quality, and starts competing against nothing at all. That is the single fastest path to adoption this series has documented anywhere, and it’s also the least replicable — no government wants the circumstances that made Ukraine’s rollout so quick to repeat themselves elsewhere.

Nigeria and Airtel Africa: skipping the tower entirely

If Ukraine is about restoring something destroyed, the Airtel Africa story is about building something that was never fully there in the first place.

In December 2025, Airtel Africa signed a partnership with SpaceX covering all fourteen of its African markets — Nigeria, Kenya, and a dozen others — putting Direct-to-Cell in reach of 174 million existing Airtel customers. What makes this case genuinely distinct from Canada, Japan, or even Ukraine is that there’s no incumbent terrestrial network to disrupt or restore. Vast stretches of the territory Airtel serves never had reliable cell towers to begin with; the economics of building conventional infrastructure across widely dispersed rural populations never penciled out for any carrier, foreign or domestic. This is the mobile-phone equivalent of a country that skipped landline telephones entirely and went straight to cellular — except this time the leapfrog is from patchy 2G coverage straight to a satellite constellation, with the intervening step of “build millions of towers” simply never happening.

That absence changes the entire competitive dynamic this series keeps returning to. There’s no retention desk to beat in rural Nigeria, because there was often nothing to retain a customer from. There’s no AST-versus-Starlink hedge to manage, because Airtel doesn’t yet face the luxury problem Rogers, Bell, and Telus have of choosing between two credible satellite partners — it’s securing baseline coverage, not premium bandwidth. And there’s a regulatory dimension too: each of the fourteen markets requires its own country-specific approval, so even a single continent-wide carrier deal doesn’t move at one continent-wide speed. Some countries will clear Airtel’s rollout quickly; others will take considerably longer, and the result will be a patchwork within a single carrier relationship, not just across different ones.

It’s worth pausing on what this means for the economics of the whole venture, because it cuts against the instinct to read Airtel Africa as a charity project wearing a business-development press release. A hundred and seventy-four million existing customers is a genuinely enormous addressable base, and unlike a market such as Canada or Japan, where satellite service supplements an already-monetized terrestrial connection, in large parts of Airtel’s footprint satellite coverage IS the first monetizable connection many of those customers will ever have had. That’s not a marginal upgrade to an existing revenue stream. It’s a wholly new one, layered directly onto a customer base that already exists and already trusts the Airtel brand — arguably the cleanest, lowest-friction expansion in this entire series, competitive dynamics aside.

India: the billionaires’ proxy war

If Ukraine is necessity and Nigeria is absence, India is pure, undiluted protectionism — a government forced to referee a fight between two of the country’s most powerful men over how a limited natural resource should be allocated.

The dispute, in its cleanest form: should satellite spectrum be auctioned, the way India auctions terrestrial spectrum, or allocated administratively by the regulator, the way most of the world handles satellite frequencies? Reliance Jio’s Mukesh Ambani — having spent roughly nineteen billion dollars building India’s dominant terrestrial network through auctioned spectrum — argued for an auction, on the explicit grounds that it would create “a level playing field” against foreign satellite operators entering the market with a fundamentally different cost structure. Musk called any move toward an auction “unprecedented,” pointing out that satellite spectrum has long been designated by the International Telecommunication Union as shared spectrum, administratively allocated in nearly every other country on Earth.

India’s government sided with Musk. In October 2024, Communications Minister Jyotiraditya Scindia confirmed the country would allocate satellite spectrum administratively rather than through auction, and by the first quarter of 2026, Starlink had cleared its final regulatory approval to operate in the country. It’s worth sitting with how unusual that outcome is, given everything else this series has documented about spectrum-as-sovereignty. A government known for protecting domestic industry against foreign entrants, in a market projected to grow 36 percent annually to nearly two billion dollars by 2030, chose the path more favourable to Musk over the path favoured by its own most powerful telecom billionaire. That’s not a small thing, and it’s not obviously explainable by the “necessity” logic that governed Ukraine or the “nothing to disrupt” logic that governed Nigeria. It looks much more like a government betting that keeping a global satellite operator’s market-entry costs low serves a billion-plus population’s connectivity goals better than protecting Jio’s competitive position does — a genuinely different calculation than any of the other three countries in this set. There’s an irony worth noting, too: Ambani’s argument for an auction rested explicitly on preventing foreign monopolization of a strategic resource, yet an auction would have required deep pockets to win — pockets Reliance itself has in abundance and few domestic challengers do. The regulator’s choice of administrative allocation, aimed at accommodating a foreign satellite operator, may end up doing more to preserve competitive diversity in Indian telecom than the auction Ambani said he wanted to protect it.

Japan: convergence, and the one holdout

Where India split its politics down the middle, Japan’s carriers mostly didn’t split at all — and the exception is more interesting than the consensus.

KDDI moved first, launching “au Starlink Direct” in April 2025 — Japan’s first live satellite direct-to-cell service. A year later, on the same day in April 2026, both NTT Docomo and SoftBank launched nearly identical Starlink-powered services of their own, completing what Japanese coverage has called the picture of all three of the country’s major carriers converging on the same satellite partner. Three fierce domestic competitors, normally locked in combat over every other dimension of their networks, all arrived at the identical conclusion about who should supply their satellite layer.

The interesting case isn’t the three that converged. It’s the one that didn’t. Rakuten Mobile — Japan’s newest and most disruptive carrier, the one that built its entire brand on doing things differently from the legacy three — signed instead with AST SpaceMobile, and did so exclusively, meaning none of its domestic rivals can piggyback onto that relationship the way Bell and Telus effectively share an AST allegiance in Canada. Rakuten is betting, in other words, on exactly the “quality over quantity” thesis from Part II, chasing genuine broadband rather than a shared, undifferentiated text-and-basic-data service — and betting that being the one truly distinct network in an otherwise converged market is worth more than joining the consensus.

This produces a curious mirror image of the Canadian story. In Canada, the incumbent leader, Rogers, went with SpaceX while the more cautious Bell and Telus aligned with AST. In Japan, it’s the three legacy incumbents who converged on SpaceX, and the challenger brand betting against the crowd on AST instead. Same two satellite companies, same basic strategic fork, and completely opposite casting depending on which country’s competitive history you’re reading it against.

There’s a further wrinkle worth flagging, because it previews the theme of the next instalment in this series. With three of Japan’s four carriers now running functionally identical satellite technology, competition has already shifted away from the satellite layer itself and toward what carriers build on top of it. KDDI, first to market, has moved to defend that lead with a dedicated satellite SOS centre and IoT-oriented services rather than any claim about network superiority — because with Docomo and SoftBank running the same Starlink backbone, there’s no superiority left to claim. When the underlying technology stops being a point of differentiation, carriers are forced to compete on service design instead, which is a preview of exactly the kind of disruption Part V will take up directly: what happens to an industry’s competitive structure once the scarce resource stops being scarce.

What the pattern actually is

Line the four up and the lesson isn’t that spectrum sovereignty is dissolving. It’s that every government’s reason for opening the door is local, contingent, and largely unrelated to what convinced the government next door. Ukraine let SpaceX in because the alternative was silence. Nigeria let it in because there was nothing established to protect. India let it in over the objections of its own most powerful domestic telecom interest, on a bet about long-term market growth. Japan’s incumbents converged on it simply because a shared, adequate satellite layer wasn’t worth fighting over, while the one carrier that saw an opportunity to differentiate went the other way entirely.

SpaceX’s 2028 global timeline assumes, implicitly, that these four logics — and however many more exist in the roughly forty countries still on its expansion map — will keep resolving in its favour quickly enough to hit the date. The evidence so far suggests that’s plausible precisely because the company doesn’t need one global argument to win. It needs a different, locally convincing argument in every single country, and four very different governments have now supplied four very different reasons to say yes.
