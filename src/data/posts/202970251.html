There is a scene that every student of the genre knows by heart. Something has gone wrong in the family — a shipment lost, a body found, a deal that curdled — and the Don sits in the dim study while the men file in. He does not ask what happened. He asks who. The distinction is everything. What happened invites an accounting; it points inward, toward decisions made and corners cut and the boss’s own fingerprints on the thing. Who did this points outward, toward a name, a face, a fall guy who can be produced, punished, and paraded so that the family’s honor is seen to be defended. The competent Don never asks what happened. He has people for that, and they know better than to answer honestly. He asks who, and the room understands that a name is required, and that the name need not be the truth. It need only be available.

This week, between the Washington Monument and the Lincoln Memorial, the United States government asked who. And the apparatus, with the smooth professionalism of any organization that has done this many times before, produced a sixty-seven-year-old retired Olympic canoeist on a bicycle.

I want to be careful here, because this is the tenth entry in a series about how power in America has learned to enforce, and I have tried throughout to keep the satire tethered to the documented record, on the theory that the documented record is funnier and more frightening than anything I could invent. So let me lay it out plainly before I let the metaphor run, because the facts, as is so often the case lately, do most of the work unassisted.

I. The Failure That Could Not Be Named

We have been here before, you and I. In a piece I called The Algae of Envy, I described the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool — that long, flat rectangle built to hold the sky and the obelisk and a century of national solemnity — turning bright green within days of being ordered, by presidential fiat, to turn blue. I described the $14-million-and-change renovation, the figure climbing from an early estimate of under two million with the serene inevitability of all government numbers. I described the leaking pipes that the project never fixed, the city official who declined to confirm that visibly green water contained algae, the announcement that nanobubble technology had “successfully destroyed” the algae, and the algae’s prompt and total indifference to this announcement.

I thought, when I wrote it, that I was describing the end of the story. The mirror had been asked to flatter, had refused, and had shown the man precisely what he brought to the water’s edge. A tidy little fable. I should have known better. Under this administration, the failure of a thing is never the end of the story. It is the beginning of the search for who failed it.

Because here is what the chemistry has since revealed, and it is worth dwelling on, since it is the sentence the entire week has been organized to avoid. As CNN’s water testing found, the record algae bloom was driven by high concentrations of phosphate — which is to say, fertilizer, the stuff you would deliberately add to a pond if your goal were to grow as much green slime as physically possible. And where did the phosphate come from? Reporting at the scene indicates the pool was refilled not with its usual water drawn from the Tidal Basin, but with city water — water that should not have carried that fertilizer load, and that points, however quietly, toward a decision made by someone, somewhere, in the rush to get the thing gleaming for the Republic’s two hundred and fiftieth birthday.

Add to this the matter the engineers flagged from the start. As the New York Times reported back in May, the renovation ignored the pool’s actual underlying problem — the old pipes that leak under the pressure of the surrounding soil, so that the water level cannot be held steady, so that the basin cannot stay full enough to stay still enough to reflect anything at all. The experts warned, in the plainest possible terms, that unless the pipes were fixed the algae would simply come back. The pipes were not fixed. The algae came back. The paint, applied in haste over a basin nobody had repaired, began to peel and float on the surface in rubbery sheets.

Now. Assemble these defendants in the Don’s study and ask them, one by one, who did this.

The pipe is summoned. The pipe says: NOT ME. I have been leaking since 1922; I leaked when this pool was built; everyone with a clipboard was told I would keep leaking. The phosphate is summoned. The phosphate says: NOT ME. I was poured in, or pumped in, by a procurement decision nobody will sign their name to. The peeling paint is summoned. The paint says: NOT ME. I was slapped onto a wet, unfixed, century-old basin by a contractor working at speed, and I am behaving exactly as paint behaves under those conditions. The no-bid contract is summoned — and the no-bid contract, which in a properly functioning Republic would be the most interesting person in the room, has already left the building, because the no-bid contract is family.

So the Don has a problem. He has asked who, and every honest answer is some version of the operation itself, which is the one answer that cannot be spoken, because to speak it is to say me. The genius of the Blanche maneuver — the NOT ME that has anchored this whole series — was never that it deflected blame onto a specific other. It was that it kept the blame moving, a hot coal passed hand to hand, never allowed to settle on the one set of hands actually holding the contract. And when the coal cannot be allowed to settle on the operation, and cannot be allowed to settle on the boss, it has to be placed, physically, into the hands of someone outside the family entirely. Someone available. Someone who happened to be at the wrong rectangle of fertilized water at the wrong time.

Enter the cyclist.

II. The Available Man

His name is David Hearn. He is sixty-seven, of Bethesda, Maryland, and on Friday he was most of the way through a bicycle ride of more than fifty miles when he swung past the Lincoln Memorial to see the famous green pool for himself — as some thousands of curious citizens have done, the failure having become, by this point, a tourist attraction in its own right.

I must pause to establish his credentials, because they are the part of this story that would get a screenwriter fired for laying it on too thick. David Hearn is a three-time Olympian. He paddled canoe slalom for the United States at Barcelona in 1992, at Atlanta in 1996 — where he finished ninth in the C-1 — and at Sydney in 2000. He won six consecutive world championships in the C-1 team event between 1979 and 1989, and two individual world titles besides. And until his retirement, he ran a company that manufactured composite materials used to build watercraft. He is, in other words, very possibly the single most qualified human being in the greater Washington metropolitan area to look at a peeling synthetic liner at the bottom of a pool and form an expert opinion about why, exactly, it was peeling.

This is the man the apparatus arrested.

He noticed, he says, a piece of the new “American flag blue” liner partly detached from the bottom — already peeling, already lifting on its own, a two-millimeter rubbery flap that the unfixed basin and the fertilized water had loosened without any human assistance whatsoever. And because he is a man who spent a career in composite materials, he did the thing such a man does. He reached into the water to feel it. To learn what it was made of. To understand the failure with his hands, the way an old mechanic will run a thumb along a cracked gasket.

“I reached in there, and I was able to grab the end of that flapping piece, the already peeling piece,” he told the Washington Post. “It was still attached to the bottom. I didn’t remove anything.”

Moments later, as he prepared to get back on his bicycle, U.S. Park Police arrested him on a misdemeanor charge of destruction of government property. He was detained, by his account, for some hours — surrounded first by National Guardsmen, then by Park Police — and given a date to appear before the D.C. Superior Court on the ninth of July. He has, he says, already been offered pro bono legal representation, and is getting a great deal of support from his community, which I do not doubt for a moment.

Consider the elegance of the inversion, because it is the whole point and I do not want it to slide past. The one person at that pool genuinely equipped to diagnose the regime’s incompetence — the materials man, the composite specialist, the fellow who could tell you in technical detail why a liner applied to an unprepared basin sheds like a snake in August — is the precise person the regime placed in handcuffs. The enforcement did not merely miss the truth. It pointed, with the accuracy of a compass needle, away from the truth, and arrested the witness. In the family, you do not silence the man who knows nothing. You silence the man who knows.

There is, I am obliged to report, a grace note. According to the contemporary record, Mr. Hearn was once before detained near these waters — arrested in 1996 for the crime of canoeing on the Potomac, a charge later dropped. So the government’s case, such as it is, rests on a known repeat offender: a man with a documented prior history of being near Washington’s water in possession of expertise about boats. Lock the Tidal Basin. He has done this before.

III. The Numbers of the Frame

A frame-up, to be persuasive, requires more than one body. A single arrest looks like an incident; a dozen arrests look like a crime wave, and a crime wave is precisely what the Don requires, because a crime wave explains a failure that incompetence would otherwise explain all by itself.

And so the numbers were produced. As the conservative journalist Emily Miller reported from the scene — and I note for the record that Ms. Miller’s video of the cyclist’s arrest was the very footage the President amplified, making her less a neutral chronicler than a kind of court stenographer for the prosecution — seven people were detained on Friday and five more on Saturday, in separate incidents, for the apparent offense of getting into the pool. Twelve citizens, total, processed by the machinery of the state for their proximity to a rectangle of fertilized water that the state itself had ruined.

To guard this rectangle, the National Guard was deployed. I want to sit with that sentence for a moment, the way one sits with a strange noise in the night. Photographs from the scene show Guardsmen in full camouflage patrolling the marble steps of the Lincoln Memorial, walking in formation along the stone lip of the basin, standing at intervals with the Washington Monument visible across the green water behind them. Camouflage. At a reflecting pool. One struggles to imagine the threat profile against which a soldier might need to blend into the landscape of the National Mall — perhaps the ducks, of whom more shortly — but there they are, deployed in the patterns of a forest battlefield to defend a puddle from the public that paid for it.

And over all of it, the boss narrating, in real time, on his platform, the way the Don holds court. The vandals, he posted, had “poured corrosive and destructive chemicals” into the basin — offered, as is traditional, without a particle of substantiation. They had, in a later telling, taken “some form of knife or blade” and carved a “250-foot-long gash” into the facade — a gash that no photograph from the heavily photographed site appears to corroborate. The pool, he insisted, had “worked perfectly,” with a “mirror like finish” reflecting both monuments “which it never had before” — a claim contradicted by the basin’s entire documented century of leaking, and indeed by the green algae and floating paint visible in every image taken that week.

And then, the line that gives the whole game away, because it names a target the Don could not resist naming. The peeling, he announced, was the work of “Lightweight ABC Reporter, Jonathan Karl,” who had been “seen sticking his hand into the Pool, and trying to rip the rubber off of the surface.” A reporter. Documenting the failure of a public works project. Recast, by presidential decree, as a saboteur. There is no clearer statement of the operating principle: the failure is not the failure. The failure is the people who looked at it. The crime is the witnessing.

IV. Team Algae

I would be failing in my duties as a foreign correspondent of the absurd if I did not record that the resistance has organized, and that it is magnificent.

There is now, at the edge of the green pool, a daily gathering of citizens who have named themselves Team Algae. They come, apparently, to stand in solidarity with the slime — to celebrate, I gather, the one participant in this entire affair that has behaved with perfect integrity. The President himself, unable to leave any provocation un-amplified, reposted them, thereby granting the algae’s fan club the same promotional megaphone he granted the journalist who filmed the arrest. There is a lesson in there about the discipline of staying off one’s own platform, but it is not a lesson this administration has ever shown the slightest interest in learning.

I confess a fondness for Team Algae that I cannot fully justify. They have understood something. The algae is the only honest thing in the story. It was told the water would be blue, and it consulted the phosphate, and it grew. It was declared destroyed, and it came back. It does not deny what it is. It does not blame a cyclist. It does not deploy the National Guard. It simply is what the conditions produced, with a fidelity that shames every official narrative arranged around it. To stand with the algae is, in a small and ridiculous way, to stand with cause and effect itself — with the radical proposition that things have reasons, that fertilized water grows slime, that unfixed pipes leak, that paint slapped on a wet basin peels, and that none of these reasons can be arrested.

I walked Dior this morning — she is a husky, and regards all standing water as either a beverage or an enemy — and I found myself thinking she would make an excellent member of Team Algae, on the grounds that she, too, is wholly unimpressed by official claims that contradict the evidence of her own senses. Tell Dior the pool is blue when it is green and she will look at you with the flat contempt of a creature who has never once in her life been successfully gaslit. The ducks I described in my earlier piece are presumably still in residence, paddling through the murk, indifferent to the camouflaged soldiers, supremely unbothered by the $14 million spent ostensibly on their behalf. The ducks, the algae, the dog, and Team Algae form a small coalition of the unfoolable. They are, at present, the most credible bloc in Washington.

V. The Sentence That Explains Everything

Let me return, in closing, to the Don in his study, because the whole grim comedy lives in the gap between the question he asked and the question he refused.

He asked who. And the apparatus answered: a cyclist, an Olympian, a materials expert, twelve detained citizens, a reporter, the National Guard, the algae’s fan club — anyone, in short, who came near enough to the failure to be charged with it. Anyone except the operation that produced the failure, and anyone except the man who ordered the operation.

The question he refused — the only question that actually accounts for the green water, the floating paint, the leaking pipes, the fertilized refill, the seven-fold cost overrun, and the whole humiliating saga — is what happened. And the answer to what happened is the one sentence this entire apparatus of arrests and Guardsmen and Truth Social accusations exists to prevent anyone from saying out loud:

We did this. We did it to ourselves, and we did it badly, and the only people who behaved with any honesty throughout were the slime, the ducks, and the man we put in handcuffs.

That sentence will never be spoken from the study. It is structurally unspeakable there. And so, in its place, the machine produces defendants — because a regime built on the principle that the boss is never wrong cannot generate accountability, only suspects. Incompetence, fed into this apparatus, does not come out the other end as a lesson learned or a contractor reviewed or a pipe at long last repaired. It comes out as a sixty-seven-year-old man with a July court date, a frame-up at the water’s edge, a fall guy for a failure that fell on no one’s head but the family’s own.

They will drain the pool now, the President says — drain much of the water, for the necessary repairs. There is a metaphor in that I am too tired to reach for. A mirror that would not flatter, first painted, then defended by soldiers, and at last simply emptied, so that there is nothing left to reflect the failure back. You cannot arrest the algae. So you remove the water in which it grew, and you charge the man who reached in to understand it, and you call it law.

The pipes, I should mention, will still leak.

They were always going to leak. Everyone was told. NOT ME, said the pipe, and for once in this whole sorry business, the pipe was telling the truth.
