Ethan Mollick, a professor at the Wharton School who studies AI and work, has a thought experiment he runs with AI chatbots. He asks them to describe the future of work in an AI era.

The chatbot will usually produce something like: Marcus Chen, a software developer, goes into the office, reads reports from his AI agents about what work has been done, and uses his judgment to assign them new tasks.

Mollick then asks: why is he going into the office if the AI is doing the work?

The chatbot corrects itself: Marcus Chen wakes up at his beach house and checks in on his AI agents.

Mollick asks: why is he even checking in if the agents are doing all the work?

The chatbot apologizes again: Marcus Chen sits at the beach.

The thought experiment ends there, which is where the serious questions begin. Marcus Chen is at the beach. His AI agents are doing the work. The question that the chatbot cannot answer — the question that Mollick, Daron Acemoglu, Clara Shih, and Dean Ball were assembled by the New York Times to wrestle with — is the question this series has been approaching from a governance angle since Part 1.

How many Marcus Chens can the American economy employ?

The People Who Are Not Marcus Chen

Acemoglu, the MIT economist who shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in economics, answered the question with characteristic precision: “It’s just not realistic to think that 100 million people will work like Marcus Chen.”

Shih, who left senior AI roles at Salesforce and Meta to build a nonprofit helping entry-level workers navigate the transition, gave the other characters their names.

Stacey Smith is a health insurance claims adjuster. She and millions like her are employed in insurance and banking because regulatory requirements prevented those jobs from being off-shored. Now AI agents can handle approvals more consistently, detect fraud more cheaply, and make decisions more reliably than Stacey can. What happens to the Stacey Smiths making decent wages in Kentucky and Mississippi?

Bob Johnson is a long-distance truck driver. He is one of 3.5 million truck drivers in the United States — the breadwinner for his family, a pillar of his community, earning a decent wage doing work that has not yet been automated but is moving rapidly in that direction. What happens when the autonomous vehicle technology diffuses past the remaining regulatory barriers?

Ben Casselman, reporting for the New York Times in June 2026, added a third character that the AI discourse has largely ignored. Not a software engineer. Not a lawyer or a consultant. A medical records specialist, earning around $50,000 a year, working from home, no college degree required — an ideal role for many mothers with young children. More than 90 percent of those jobs are held by women. The same is true of billing clerks, payroll specialists, customer service representatives, bookkeepers, executive assistants at major financial institutions. These are back-office roles — not the main function of the company, as one Stanford economist put it, but cost centres to be optimised.

Molly Kinder, a researcher building an organisation focused on AI’s impact on workers, gave the sharpest formulation of what is at stake: “I worry that AI will be to high-school-educated women what deindustrialization was to high-school-educated men.”

The parallel is exact and the history is instructive. Deindustrialisation did not destroy the American economy. It destroyed specific communities, specific families, specific career pathways — the ones that working-class men without college degrees had used to reach the middle class. The jobs that replaced them were worse: more precarious, more physically demanding, lower paid, with fewer routes to advancement. The political consequences of that destruction are still unfolding.

The back-office automation that AI is accelerating is the same destruction, applied to the jobs that working-class women without college degrees used to reach the middle class. Customer service. Medical records. Payroll. Billing. Human resources. The jobs that paid a decent wage without requiring a college degree, that could be done from home, that offered a pathway upward. Economists at Northwestern, recalculating AI exposure measures based on the actual composition of the workforce rather than early adopters, found that “the most affected jobs are secretaries, are routine clerks — not computer scientists or data scientists at all.”

Marcus Chen is at the beach. Stacey Smith and Bob Johnson are the people whose jobs funded the infrastructure of the economy in which Marcus Chen grew up. The transition the AI discourse celebrates — the productivity gains, the supercharged entrepreneurship, the blurring of professional roles — is a transition whose costs are distributed across 100 million people who are not software developers and are not going to the beach.

Shih put it plainly. It is, she said, “wonderful and horrible.” The wonderful part is Marcus Chen. The horrible part is Stacey and Bob and the medical records specialist working from home. The governance question — which this series has been asking and which the panel was also asking — is whether the wonderful and the horrible are being distributed in any way that resembles fairness, and whether anyone is making decisions about that distribution, or whether it is simply happening.

The Apprenticeship That Disappeared

Mollick identified the most consequential governance failure in the panel discussion, and it is one that no legislation currently addresses.

“We had this great technique, which was apprenticeship. It’s worked for 4,000 years. I hire a white-collar worker, and they do grunt work for me and they work really hard, and I get to assess how good they are at the grunt work. And as a middle manager, I get the advantage of having someone do the work I don’t want to do, and they learn, and I assess them, and everyone gets paid.”

AI has automated the grunt work. The entry-level tasks that junior workers used to perform — the research synthesis, the first-draft writing, the data cleaning, the code debugging, the document review — are now performed by AI agents, faster and cheaper than a 24-year-old with a college degree. Companies have discovered that they can operate with fewer junior workers, and they are operating with fewer junior workers.

What they have not discovered — what no one has yet figured out — is how to train the senior workers of 2035 without the apprenticeship pipeline that the AI has absorbed. “You can’t just say, Oh, we should hire junior workers, if we haven’t thought about how to train junior workers,” Mollick said.

The dependency problem documented in Part 37 was about the skills individuals lose when AI performs their cognitive tasks. The apprenticeship problem is the institutional version of the same failure, operating at the level of the economy rather than the individual. A society that has automated the entry-level work has automated the training ground. The senior workers of the next generation will need to have developed judgment, domain expertise, and the capacity to evaluate AI outputs — but they will have developed those capacities through what process, exactly, if the process of doing the work that builds judgment has been handed to machines?

Mollick’s answer points toward universities, which are “actually quite good at training and assessing” and could extend or change professional education to fill the gaps. Acemoglu’s answer is that the manufacturing, health care, and education sectors — the ones that most need AI’s productivity gains — are the ones where the bottlenecks are highest and the road maps are most absent. Ball’s answer is to measure the problem better before legislating, and to worry about the states that will sleepwalk into labor protections that lock in the wrong responses.

Casselman’s reporting adds a structural dimension that the panel did not reach. The apprenticeship problem is not only about junior workers failing to develop expertise. It is about the destruction of the career ladder itself — the sequence of progressively better jobs through which workers without college degrees have historically moved up. Someone who starts as a receptionist moves into a customer service role that pays modestly better but opens pathways to human resources, then management. Each step builds skills and credentials for the next. AI does not merely eliminate the entry-level position. It eliminates the rung. Mark Muro of Brookings and his coauthor Justin Heck put the question directly: “What happens if we’re no longer building those skills on the job? Where is there available to move up? What are the ramifications three years from now, when workers remain stranded in low-wage work, and employers are struggling to fill high-wage roles because we’ve carved out the middle?”

The answer to that question is visible in the history of deindustrialisation. The workers who lost manufacturing jobs did not move smoothly into better work. They moved into worse work, or left the workforce, or stayed in communities that hollowed out around them. The political consequences are still unfolding. The AI version of that story is being written now, in real time, in the back offices of every major corporation in America. It is being written without a legislative framework, without a retraining infrastructure, and without a governance requirement that the companies eliminating the rungs account for what they are removing.

None of these answers is wrong. None of them is a governance framework.

The Goal-Setting Problem

Shih’s closing observation is the one that earns the record for this series, because it states the Asimov Gap thesis in the language of a Silicon Valley insider who has thought carefully about what is actually happening.

“All AI agents have a goal,” she said. “And it depends on who deploys it, because whoever deploys it gets to set the goal. Maybe the goals of AI so far haven’t been aligned with the goals of regular people, with everyday workers. But that’s a choice we can make.”

Who is deploying AI? The companies that stand to benefit from the productivity gains. Who is setting the goals? The people for whom Marcus Chen’s beach life is the desirable outcome. Who is not setting the goals? Stacey Smith. Bob Johnson. The 24-year-old whose entry-level position was eliminated before she learned enough to be indispensable. The electrician apprentice who might be ten times more productive with the right AI tool but for whom no one is building that tool because the investment returns are in enterprise software, not in trades training.

The goal-setting problem is the governance problem stated at the level of individual deployment decisions. Every company that deploys an AI agent to replace an entry-level worker is making a goal-setting decision. That decision is made by the company, in the interests of the company, for the benefit of the company’s shareholders. It is not made with reference to Stacey Smith’s mortgage or Bob Johnson’s family or the 24-year-old’s need for an apprenticeship. It is not required to be. There is no law, no regulation, no enforceable standard that requires the goals of AI deployment to be aligned with the goals of the workers whose jobs the AI is absorbing.

Acemoglu put the structural argument in terms that connect directly to this series’ spine: “Artificial intelligence is quite different from human intelligence. When two things are different, the last thing you want is to try to mimic one with the other. It is a fool’s errand to try to get one to do everything that the other one does. They should work together. And those aren’t the kinds of models we’re trying to develop.”

The models being developed are the ones that maximise what AI can do by absorbing what humans used to do. Complementarity — the model in which AI amplifies human capacity rather than replacing it — is the stated aspiration of most of the companies building AI. It is not the model the market is rewarding. The market is rewarding displacement. The companies deploying AI to reduce headcount are returning value to shareholders. The companies deploying AI to make their existing workers ten times more productive are also returning value to shareholders. The difference, from a market perspective, is marginal. From the perspective of Stacey Smith and Bob Johnson, it is the difference between having a job and not having one.

The One-Third Who Are Angry

Ball, the former Trump AI adviser, offered an observation that connects the labor economics to the political moment documented in Parts 39 and 46.

“I guarantee you that 100 percent of the unemployment in 2028 will be blamed on AI by the American public and by lots of opportunistic politicians.” He worries that the policy responses will be blunt — labor protections that lock in rigidity, state-level legislation that nobody in Washington is tracking, a sleepwalk into a bad political economy.

Shih added the number that belongs in the series: a Gallup poll showing that a third of Gen Z Americans describe their feelings toward AI as anger. “These are people we want embracing AI so that they can help build this economy and they can find work. But they’re rejecting it on a moral basis.”

A third of the generation that will comprise the majority of the workforce within a decade is angry at the technology on moral grounds. The commencement booing — graduates at multiple colleges heartily booing speakers who talked about AI — is the anger made visible. These are not Luddites. They are people who were told that learning to code was a path to a six-figure salary, who graduated into a market where human programmers look like an endangered species, and who are now being told that the same technology that eliminated the career they were promised will, with the right attitude and skills, create opportunities they cannot yet see.

The Bad Feeling documented in Part 39 has a generational dimension that the polling confirmed but the series has not yet named directly. The people with the most negative views of AI are not old people resisting change. They are young people who understand, at the level of lived experience, that the technology arriving in their professional lives was not built with their interests as a design goal.

Shih’s conditional optimism — “the future isn’t automatic” — is the right frame. The choices have not all been made. The apprenticeship pipeline has been damaged but not destroyed. The Stacey Smiths and Bob Johnsons are still employed, for now. The window that Easterly identified in Part 46 as “rapidly closing” applies here too: the moment to build the governance framework that aligns AI deployment with the goals of the people living with its consequences is before the displacement is complete, not after.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s Susan Calvin — the roboticist who understood robots better than anyone else in his fictional universe — spent her career insisting that the question was not whether robots could do human work. The question was what kind of humans the robots would leave behind.

The Spacers, who delegated everything to robots, left behind humans who could not tolerate each other’s presence. The Earth, which resisted robots, left behind humans who had maintained their capacities at the cost of poverty and crowding. Neither outcome was the Three Laws’ intention. The Three Laws governed what robots could do to humans. They did not govern what robots would do to human society — the slow, accumulating, individually rational set of delegations that produced, over generations, civilisations incapable of the things they had chosen not to do.

The apprenticeship is one of those things. Four thousand years of training the next generation through the doing of work that needs doing — and the AI industry has automated it away in three years, not through malice, not through a governance decision, but through the market logic of cost reduction.

Nobody decided this. Nobody had to. That is precisely the governance failure.

The laws that would require AI deployment to preserve human apprenticeship pathways do not exist. The laws that would require goal-setting to include the interests of the workers whose tasks the AI absorbs do not exist. The laws that would require companies to account for the training pipeline they are eliminating when they eliminate the entry-level positions that comprised it do not exist.

Marcus Chen is at the beach. The question the series has been asking since Part 1 is who is responsible for the answer, and whether the answer will be chosen or merely arrived at.

It is still a choice. The window is closing.
