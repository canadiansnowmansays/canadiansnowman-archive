Sigrid Jin was waiting to board a plane when he saw the news that Anthropic had accidentally leaked the source code for Claude Code, its popular AI agent. Jin, a 25-year-old undergraduate student at the University of British Columbia, scrambled to post a copy online. His girlfriend texted him immediately: was he violating copyright law?

He turned to a team of AI assistants for a solution. He directed them to rewrite the leaked code in another programming language. Within hours, more than 100,000 people had liked or linked to his version. Anthropic demanded that GitHub remove posts sharing the original leak. Thousands of posts came down. Jin’s rewritten version remains online. Anthropic has not asked him to take it down.

The question his girlfriend asked in the airport — was he violating copyright law? — does not yet have a clear legal answer. But Russ Pearlman, a lawyer specializing in AI and technology, has the clearest description of why that is:

“When an AI agent can rewrite 512,000 lines of code into a different language before most people have finished their morning coffee, that assumption collapses.”

The assumption he is describing is the foundation of copyright enforcement. Not copyright law itself — the law still exists. The assumption that the law rested on: that copying takes time, and that the time creates a window for intervention. That window is gone. What remains is the law, standing over a hole where its enforcement mechanism used to be.

What Copyright Was Built to Do

Copyright law did not emerge from a philosophical commitment to the rights of creators. It emerged from a practical problem: the printing press made it possible to copy a book faster than the author could write another one. The Statute of Anne, passed in Britain in 1710, was a response to the chaos that followed — a mechanism to ensure that the people who created things could benefit from them long enough to create more.

The logic was always temporal. Copyright protection lasts for a defined period because the assumption is that, within that period, the creator can capture enough economic value to justify the creation. Infringement is actionable because copying takes time and resources — enough time that the enforcement machinery can catch up with the infringer before the damage becomes irreversible.

Every subsequent development in copyright law operated on this same temporal logic. The photocopier threatened it. The cassette tape threatened it. The internet threatened it severely. Each time, the industry and the courts found ways to extend and adapt the enforcement window — longer terms, digital rights management, platform liability, notice-and-takedown regimes.

AI has not threatened the enforcement window. It has eliminated it. Jin’s demonstration was not a theft of Anthropic’s code. It was a proof of concept. He was not trying to profit from the rewrite. He said so himself: he wanted to raise ethical questions about what creative ownership means when any work can be reproduced and transformed in seconds. The 100,000 people who linked to his post in a few hours understood the demonstration immediately.

“I just wanted to raise some ethical questions in the AI agent era,” he said. “Any creative work can be reproduced in a second.”

The copyrightability question has a structural dimension that the enforcement window argument alone doesn’t capture. Major entertainment companies — studios, labels, publishers — have not abandoned human creators despite the enormous cost savings AI would offer. The reason is not sentiment. It is that AI-generated content cannot be copyrighted, and without copyright there is no licensing, no franchising, no merchandising, no commercially viable IP. Netflix’s own production guidelines warn creators not to use AI for main characters or key visual elements without written approval. Hachette pulled a book after allegations of AI-written passages surfaced. These decisions are acts of business pragmatism. The prohibition on copyrighting AI-generated content is, unexpectedly, one of the few structural protections for human creative labor that currently exists.

The Library That Has Already Been Consumed

The Anthropic leak is a preview. The larger story is already complete.

The AI models that made Jin’s rewrite possible — that make every AI agent’s rapid reproduction of creative work possible — were trained on the creative output of human civilization. Books, articles, code, music, images, film scripts, scientific papers, legal briefs, personal essays. The library of everything that has been written, composed, filmed, and coded has, in substantial measure, already been fed into the systems that can now reproduce and transform any of it in seconds.

Anthropic knows this better than most. In 2025, the company agreed to pay $1.5 billion to a group of authors and publishers — the largest settlement in the history of United States copyright cases — after a judge ruled it had illegally downloaded and stored millions of copyrighted books to train its models. Anthropic’s defence was that its systems analyzed the underlying patterns in the work to build something new, rather than replicating the exact text. The settlement suggests the court was not entirely persuaded.

The New York Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft for copyright infringement of news content. Artists and musicians have filed class actions against image generation companies. The legal battles are multiplying faster than they are being resolved. More than ninety lawsuits have now been filed by creators against AI companies for copyright infringement — authors, musicians, visual artists, and news publishers all accusing firms of using their copyrighted works to train models without permission. And in each case, the underlying question is the same one Jin’s girlfriend asked at the airport.

The honest answer, as Kandis Koustenis, an intellectual property lawyer, put it: “The library of everything that has been written has already been fed into AI. From the author’s point of view, the genie is out of the bottle a little bit.” A 2025 survey found that 83 percent of internet users believe AI will collapse unique writing styles into monoculture — the fear that not only will human creative work be economically displaced, but that the AI trained on human creativity will flatten the diversity it consumed.

A little bit is an understatement. The genie is out, the bottle is broken, and the people who are supposed to be writing the rules are still debating what the genie is allowed to do.

What the Copyright Office Said, and What It Didn’t

In 2022, the United States Copyright Office ruled that works created entirely by AI without human creative input are not eligible for copyright protection. A follow-up review reaffirmed the decision, finding that a simple human prompt was not enough to establish the human creative contribution required for protection.

In 2024, the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia held in Thaler v. Perlmutter that a work generated autonomously by an AI system cannot be protected by copyright because copyright requires a human author. The Supreme Court declined to review the decision in March 2026, leaving it in place.

These rulings are significant for what they protect: human creators whose work is used to train AI systems retain their copyright. What they do not resolve is the harder question — how much human involvement is required to make an AI-assisted work protectable, and whether the rewriting, remixing, and transformation that AI systems perform on copyrighted source material constitutes infringement or fair use.

Jin argued that rewriting code in a different programming language transformed it into a new work. The Google v. Oracle case, which the Supreme Court decided in 2021, established that the use of software interfaces can constitute fair use in some circumstances. But that precedent was established for human programmers building on existing code. Whether it extends to AI agents rewriting 512,000 lines in minutes has not been tested in court.

The gap between the Copyright Office’s ruling and the questions the courts have not yet answered is the governance gap in miniature. The law exists. The technology exists. The intersection of the two is largely uncharted.

The Market Flooding Problem

The copyright question is not only about ownership. It is about economics.

For software companies, authors, artists, and musicians, the risk is not primarily that their exact work will be copied and sold. It is that the market for their work will be flooded with AI-generated substitutes that cost almost nothing to produce, making the original work economically unviable — not because it has been stolen, but because it has been rendered redundant.

This is a different kind of harm than copyright infringement, and copyright law was not designed to address it. A musician whose songs were used to train an AI model that can now generate songs in their style has a potential copyright claim. A musician whose livelihood disappears because the market is flooded with AI-generated music that costs nothing to produce may have no legal remedy at all — even if the flood is a direct consequence of training on their work.

The same logic applies to every creative industry. Code. Articles. Images. Film scripts. Legal briefs. The economic model that made it rational to invest in creative work — the model that copyright law was designed to protect — assumes that the work retains scarcity value. AI production eliminates scarcity. When any creative output can be generated in seconds at near-zero cost, the question is not whether the original creator’s copyright was violated. The question is whether the original creator has an economic future.

“Artists and musicians are extremely concerned about this,” said Yelena Ambartsumian, a lawyer who counsels AI start-ups on intellectual property. “All of the resources you put into being able to protect your copyrightable human expression, does it really matter if in a second or two hours that expression can be copied and then changed?”

It matters legally, in the narrow sense that a copyright holder still has standing to sue. Whether it matters economically is a different question, and copyright law has no answer to it.

The market flooding problem has already spawned a counter-industry. Pangram, an AI detection company, uses machine learning to distinguish human writing from AI-generated text — retraining its model from scratch every three to six weeks as new models emerge. Its founder describes the dynamic as adversarial and nascent: the equivalent of computer viruses appearing before antivirus software existed. Some publishers, academic institutions, and platforms are using detection tools as a first filter. But the detection problem is compounding. As one Pangram researcher put it: half of all new articles generated online are now estimated to be coming from large language models. The training data for detection tools is itself becoming contaminated with AI content. And the models are getting better at evading detection — the tells are becoming more subtle with every release. This is not a race that detection is winning.

The collapse of OpenAI’s Sora video-generation tool is the clearest illustration of how structural copyright protection operates in practice. OpenAI announced a landmark licensing deal with Disney giving users the ability to generate content featuring Disney characters. Months later, the tool was shut down entirely. The reason is not hard to find: licensing content is expensive, and there is no commercial logic in building an expensive video-generation tool that produces content nobody can copyright, license, or monetize. The businesses most threatened by AI displacement — film, television, music, book publishing — are also the businesses most dependent on copyright as their financial engine. As long as the prohibition on copyrighting AI-generated content holds, that engine requires human creators. The moment courts allow that prohibition to erode, the engine stops.

The Line That Must Be Held

The most important copyright question is not whether training on copyrighted works constitutes infringement. That will be litigated for years. Even if creators win that battle, their reward might be a modest settlement — a one-time payment that does nothing to protect their ongoing livelihood.

The question that will determine whether human creators still have jobs is how much human involvement is required to make an AI-generated work copyrightable. The businesses with the most to gain from switching to AI will push to define “human authorship” as loosely as possible — a few prompts, a light editorial pass. If courts allow that, the structural protection disappears. An AI company could generate a film, have a human review it briefly, register it as copyrighted, and license it globally. The entire economic logic that currently keeps human creators in the loop collapses.

The Copyright Office has correctly indicated that prompting alone should not be sufficient to establish human authorship. The courts have not yet endorsed this position. They may come under intense pressure not to — from technology companies, from entertainment conglomerates that would prefer to eliminate human labor costs, and from a Congress that has consistently failed to regulate AI in any meaningful way.

Courts and regulators also need to impose harsher penalties for misrepresenting AI involvement in copyright registrations. As AI-generated content becomes harder to distinguish from human-authored work, the temptation to pass off AI output as human-made will grow. The copyright system will protect creators only if it is backed by meaningful enforcement — exactly the kind of enforcement that the AI era is making structurally harder to achieve.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s robots did not steal. They did not copy. They did not appropriate the creative work of the humans around them. They were tools — capable, precise, and governed by explicit laws that defined the limits of their operation.

The governance question Asimov never fully explored was the economic one. His stories were set in a future where robots had displaced human labour in manufacturing and service industries, but the economic consequences of that displacement were treated as background — a social problem to be managed, not a governance failure to be examined. The Three Laws governed what robots could do to humans physically. They said nothing about what robots could do to human livelihoods economically.

That gap in Asimov’s framework is the gap that the Sigrid Jin episode exposes. The AI systems that can rewrite half a million lines of code before breakfast are not violating anyone’s physical safety. They are not lying to their users. They are not scheming to take over the world. They are doing exactly what they were built to do — rapidly, accurately, and at a scale that makes the enforcement mechanisms built around human creative economies structurally obsolete.

Jin understood this. His goal, he said, was not to create something new but to highlight how few truly novel ideas remain. “We are now relying on models that are relying on ideas that come out of other people’s heads,” he said. “It is becoming difficult to have novelty.”

The copyright system was built to protect novelty — to give creators a window of economic advantage for producing something new. The AI systems trained on human creativity are not producing novelty. They are producing the appearance of novelty at the speed of a search query. The window has closed. The rules designed to protect what happens inside the window are still being written.

The detection tools have already found AI-assisted content in unexpected places — including, according to Wired's reporting on Pangram's analysis, tweets from the Pope's official account warning about the dangers of artificial intelligence. The irony requires no editorial comment.

The genie is not coming back. The question is whether anyone is going to hold the line on what it takes to claim ownership of what the genie produces — and whether that line will be held before the creative economies it is supposed to protect have already been hollowed out.

That is the Asimov Gap applied to culture. The capability arrived. The governance did not. And the people most affected — the musicians, the writers, the programmers, the artists — are watching a legal system try to catch up with a technology that had already lapped it before most lawyers understood what had happened.
