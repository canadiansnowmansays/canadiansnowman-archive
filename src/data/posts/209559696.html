I. The Speech in Shanghai

Xi Jinping, speaking at the World AI Conference in Shanghai on July 17, 2026, offered the kind of line that sounds generous until you notice who’s holding the microphone: AI development, he said, “should not be a solo performance by a single country, but a symphony of global collaboration.” He cast open-source AI as a “rare and historical opportunity,” warning that failing to share the technology with developing nations risked “new historical injustices.” It is, on its face, the opposite of everything this series has spent sixty-four parts documenting about the American approach — a governance vacuum filled not by law, but by whichever company gets there first.

The distinction Xi is drawing is real. It is also not disinterested.

II. Openness as Strategy, Not Virtue

China is, by most measures, still behind the United States in frontier AI — but closing fast, and closing specifically through the open-source lane that Xi’s speech elevated to a moral principle. Companies like DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Zhipu have built their competitive position on giving away what American labs treat as crown jewels. Moonshot’s Kimi K3, unveiled the same day as Xi’s remarks, is a case in point: a model the company claims performs on par with Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s best, arriving with an announced plan to open-source it by the end of July.

That is not altruism competing against American greed. It is a company behind on compute and chips — Nvidia still dominates the hardware China can’t easily access — choosing the one lever available to it: give the model away, and let adoption do the work that capital expenditure does for its American rivals. Openness, in this context, is what you sell when you can’t yet out-spend the competition. Xi’s speech dresses that necessity in the language of justice for the developing world, which doesn’t make the argument false. It makes it convenient.

III. The Gap, Exported

This series has defined the Asimov Gap as the space that opens when deployment outruns law — a space that, in the American telling, gets filled by whichever company moves fastest and asks forgiveness later. Xi’s pitch is that China will fill a parallel gap on the international stage: a World AI Cooperation Organization, created a year prior to give developing nations “a greater say in A.I. governance,” positioned as the alternative to a Western order that, as this series has documented, is still arguing internally about wage insurance and sovereign wealth funds rather than binding rules.

The pitch is seductive precisely because the American vacuum is real. But a governance body convened by the country whose leader has openly exhorted it to “occupy the commanding heights” of the technology is not obviously less self-interested than the American companies drafting their own oversight. It is the same gap, wearing a different flag.

IV. The Injustice That Doesn’t Fit the Speech

Xi’s language about avoiding “new historical injustices” sat uneasily next to the same week’s other story out of the region: the Philippines formally condemned, as racist, an AI-generated video posted by a Chinese state-run news outlet that depicted the country as a karaoke-singing monkey doing the bidding of the United States and Japan. Researchers have separately documented Chinese government efforts to deploy AI-adjacent capabilities to monitor and shape public opinion in Hong Kong and Taiwan. A symphony of global collaboration, it turns out, still has room in the score for a state-run insult aimed at a neighbor it wants to peel away from Washington’s orbit.

That contradiction is not a gotcha so much as the whole argument in miniature. Every power center currently vying to fill the Asimov Gap — American labs, Chinese state media, both governments — asks to be trusted with the terms of the transition while actively practicing the behavior the transition is supposed to guard against.

V. TACO, Run Through Beijing

The TACO framework built for Washington translates cleanly here. Threaten is present, if softened into geopolitical framing — the implicit warning that developing nations left out of China’s “win-win cooperation” risk being locked out of AI’s benefits entirely, a threat dressed as an invitation. Accommodate is the entire speech: technology transfers, training offers, a seat at a new governance table for “friendly” nations — friendly being the operative qualifier that limits how generous the accommodation really is. Capitulate, a genuine surrender of unilateral control, is nowhere in the speech; the World AI Cooperation Organization is Chinese-convened, not internationally chartered. And Obfuscate shows up exactly where it did in Part 64 — in the gap between “openness” as a stated value and the karaoke-monkey video sitting one news cycle away from the speech that claimed it.

VI. The Walls Both Capitals Are Building

Four days after Xi’s Shanghai speech, reporting from the New York Times‘s DealBook desk (July 21, 2026) showed how much daylight sits between the “symphony of global collaboration” and what both capitals are actually doing. The Trump administration is reportedly weighing whether to add Chinese labs like Z.ai and Moonshot to the Commerce Department’s “Entity List” — a move that would effectively cut off U.S. access to their open-weight models, which have grown cheap and capable enough to worry Washington. The National Security Agency is said to have considered a parallel advisory warning U.S. companies off Chinese AI tools altogether. Meanwhile, China’s own commerce ministry is weighing limits on foreign access to key AI and chip-making data — protectionism running in both directions, dressed in the language of national security on one side and self-reliance on the other.

The debate inside Washington undercuts any tidy East-versus-West reading of who believes in openness. Dario Amodei and Sam Altman — occupying the same seats they hold in Part 63’s transition-fund story — are reportedly pushing for mandatory government testing and licensing of frontier models, and arguing against open-source release generally. Bill Gurley, the venture capitalist, called the position out directly in the Washington Post, framing the push to treat open models as a security threat as an attempt to suppress ordinary competition. David Sacks, until recently the White House’s own AI czar, made the same point more bluntly on X, characterizing it as a duopoly trying to legislate away its open-source rivals.

That is the same critique this series leveled at Xi’s speech, now aimed at the opposite pole. China’s open-weight strategy is competitive necessity dressed as generosity; the American labs’ push for licensing and restriction is competitive self-interest dressed as safety. Both governments are moving toward walls. Both sets of companies are supplying the rationale for the walls their own governments are building — one arguing for openness because it can’t yet out-spend the incumbents, the others arguing for closure because openness threatens the moat they already hold. Washington and Beijing are set to discuss the matter directly ahead of a Trump-Xi meeting on September 24 — a negotiation this series would note is being shaped, on both sides, by the very companies whose fortunes the outcome will decide.

VII. The Gap, Restated for a Second Capital

Sixty-seven parts into this series, the pattern is not that one country has solved the problem the other hasn’t. It’s that every capital currently capable of shaping AI’s path — Washington, Beijing, and the boardrooms of San Francisco — has discovered the same rhetorical move: describe your self-interest in the language of stewardship, and hope no one asks who appointed you steward. Xi didn’t mention the United States by name. He didn’t need to. The symphony he’s proposing to conduct is still, first and last, a solo written for one country’s benefit — it just comes with better PR about sharing the sheet music.
