Parts 94 and 95 covered what happened inside the industry after Jacob Coxon’s resignation and Dario Amodei’s essay — the investigation, the rhetoric, the chorus of executives agreeing with each other in terms too vague to hold anyone to. This entry covers what happened outside the industry, in the branch of government that is supposed to decide whether any of it matters, and the answer is almost nothing, delivered with an unusual amount of noise.

The mismatch stated as plainly as this series has seen it stated

As The New York Times’ David E. Sanger and Dustin Volz reported on September 13, 2026, the gap between the warnings and the response has become genuinely stark: on one side, a researcher’s own employer suggesting something like a 10 percent chance of civilizational catastrophe; on the other, a federal government that “barely could agree on whether there was much of anything to worry about.” Ben Buchanan, a Georgetown professor, gave the dynamic its cleanest phrasing: “We are in a period of exponential development, and there are only two ways to respond: too early or too late. The world has missed its chance to be too early.” Sanger and Volz draw the historical parallel this series has been reaching for since Part 94’s Atoms for Peace comparison, and sharpen it: Dick Cheney’s post-9/11 “1 percent doctrine” held that a risk needed no more than a 1 percent chance of catastrophe to justify acting on it. Evan Hubinger’s own estimate for AI-driven mass extinction — more than 10 percent — clears that bar by an order of magnitude, and nothing resembling a 1-percent-doctrine response has followed.

President Trump’s own position is the clearest illustration of why. Asked directly by a reporter whether he had “any concerns about A.I. leading to human extinction,” he said: “No, I don’t have any. I have concerns that if we don’t win A.I., we’re going to be put in a very bad position.” Sean Cairncross, the White House’s national cyber director, offered the administration’s closest thing to an official response at a cybersecurity conference the same week, describing a balance between “the innovation side” and “handling this technology responsibly” without once addressing the specific incidents that prompted the question. David Sacks, Trump’s former AI czar, went further, dismissing Coxon’s warnings on Fox News as a “coordinated media operation” designed to manufacture support for regulation — an accusation this series has no way to verify, but one that matches almost exactly Jensen Huang’s own dismissal, which Part 98 covers, that the entire wave of alarm exists to sell cybersecurity products. Two people who worked in the administration told Sanger and Volz that officials who do want a more proactive safety posture are reluctant to get ahead of the president, since tech executives who dislike an emerging policy have a documented habit of calling Trump directly and getting it reversed — a specific, named mechanism for regulatory capture this series hasn’t previously had on the record in this much detail.

A docket of bills that all arrived at once, and none of them close to passing

Congress’s response, once it finally came, arrived in a genuine flurry rather than a trickle, which makes its complete lack of momentum more notable rather than less. The earliest bipartisan signals actually predate the Amodei essay this section is otherwise built around: as The New York Times’ Kate Conger reported on September 9, 2026, in the immediate aftermath of Coxon’s resignation, Representative Anna Paulina Luna, a Florida Republican, called on social media for Congress to convene a special session on AI regulation, writing that “not enough of Congress is focusing on this,” while Representative Lori Trahan, a Massachusetts Democrat, wrote in nearly identical terms that “it’s past time for Congress to get off the sidelines and do its job” — two lawmakers from opposite parties reaching for the same verb, “sidelines,” within the same news cycle, three days before Amodei’s essay gave the moment its main character. As The Atlantic’s Toluse Olorunnipa reported the same week, Senator Ted Cruz confirmed he is co-authoring legislation with Democrat Amy Klobuchar and Republican Majority Leader John Thune specifically targeting AI-driven biological and nuclear catastrophe risk. Representatives Josh Gottheimer and Mike Lawler introduced a separate bipartisan bill aimed at preventing AI agents from going rogue in the specific way the Hugging Face incident demonstrated. Ro Khanna called for an entirely new AI safety agency. Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed shifted, in real time, from a June proposal for regulated AI development to endorsing “an all-out pause.” None of this, per Nate Persily of Stanford’s Law AI Initiative, is likely to move fast enough to matter: “The developments are coming so fast and furious that it is very difficult to keep up with what the harms are and how to address them,” he told Olorunnipa, adding that the underlying statutory design questions are “genuinely hard” even before politics enters the picture. Senator Bernie Sanders’s own bill with Representative Greg Casar, which would ban superintelligence development outright, has no Republican co-sponsors and, by Cruz’s own account of the current mood — “it ain’t easy, this is a very divided time” — is not close to viable.

The China framing this series has tracked since Part 86 shows up here in its clearest form yet as the argument against acting at all: Sanders told Olorunnipa his stated goal is to pressure Trump, ahead of a planned meeting with Xi Jinping, toward something resembling an arms-control negotiation rather than domestic legislation alone — an acknowledgment that a purely American regulatory response is close to pointless if the underlying competitive dynamic with China isn’t addressed in parallel. Persily’s academic framing of the same problem is blunter: this is a prisoner’s dilemma in which both countries would be better off cooperating on limits, neither trusts the other to comply, and — unlike nuclear arms control, which depends on physically countable warheads — AI compliance would be close to unverifiable by any external party.

The fuller catalogue, and the single sentence that summarises all of it

The bills named above turn out to be a fraction of what’s actually pending. As Tony Romm, Dustin Volz, and Michael Gold reported for The New York Times on September 15, 2026, Representative Ted Lieu supplied the sentence that should anchor this entire section: “There is essentially zero federal laws on A.I.” The Frontier Act, from Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan, would require the largest AI companies to publish safety-incident reports and submit to independent audits — the same “embedded evaluators” concept Amodei proposed in his September 12 essay, which Part 97 covers — in exchange for blocking states from issuing their own AI regulations, a federal preemption trade that has made the bill contentious because many states believe Washington has taken too long to act. Separately, Lieu has co-sponsored a kill-switch bill with Representative Nathaniel Moran that would let the government shut down or throttle a model posing an imminent threat; Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana sought unanimous consent for a similar measure on September 16, and, as Just the News reported, Rand Paul’s objection killed it. On the national-security side, the bipartisan Advanced AI Security Readiness Act would direct the NSA to draft a data-centre security plan, while the AI Cyber Defense Act would give water and electric utilities free access to top-tier models for their own protection — a government-mediated version of the industry’s own cyberdefense letter this series covered in Part 90. Senator Kirsten Gillibrand’s bill would bar AI from nuclear-weapons deployment decisions entirely, and a related measure requiring human oversight before the military uses AI-enabled weapons has already cleared the Senate Armed Services Committee as part of the annual defence bill.

The economic bills round out the catalogue and matter most directly to this series’ Capex Ledger sibling: the Ratepayer Protection Act, which the House passed 417 to 3 on September 16, as the Deseret News reported, would nudge state regulators to prevent A.I.’s power demand from raising electricity prices, and it is the clearest test in this catalogue of whether a near-unanimous House vote means anything. It does not. Senator Jon Husted’s unanimous-consent request was objected to within the week, and on September 30, as Roll Call reported, a cloture vote on the motion to proceed failed 57 to 43, six short of the sixty needed, with four Democrats — Maggie Hassan, Amy Klobuchar, Jon Ossoff and Raphael Warnock — crossing over. The objection was to the method rather than the goal: the bill directs states to consider federal standards without requiring any, which Chuck Schumer called “toothless,” saying Democrats “want to make it mandatory for data centers to cover their own costs.” John Thune called it “common sense” and accused Democrats of playing politics. A bill 417 members of the House wanted, killed in the Senate for asking too little, is this essay’s argument in a single vote. The rest of the catalogue got no vote at all: Sanders’s own proposal to capture a share of AI-company earnings for a public wealth fund has been covered at length in Capex Ledger’s Part IV; Hawley and Democrat Richard Blumenthal have a bill making it easier to sue AI companies over copyrighted training data; and Senator Elizabeth Warren’s AI Bubble Transparency Act would force disclosure aimed specifically at helping financial regulators prepare for the possibility that the AI investment boom is itself a bubble. Senators Josh Hawley and Mark Warner, from opposite parties, have separately focused on simply tracking AI-related layoffs — the most modest ask in this entire catalogue, and still unpassed.

The clock actually running out, and the ocean where the same story is playing out

The bill flurry above has an expiration date this series hasn’t previously had reason to name. When both chambers returned on September 14, 2026, it was for a brief session before the campaign trail: one week of House business and three of Senate business, as Jennifer Shutt reported for States Newsroom, which is the whole legislative runway any A.I. bill introduced this year had left. The Senate used part of it to hold hearings on rogue A.I. agents on September 30, and the rest to block the one bill the House had actually passed. As of early October 2026, none of the bills catalogued above had become law.

The oversight gap this series has documented in Washington has a close cousin across the Atlantic, and it takes a more concrete form. A UK government spokesperson told the BBC the country “takes the risks from advanced AI extremely seriously” and pointed to its AI Security Institute, or AISI, as the body responsible for building “a rigorous, scientific understanding” of frontier model capabilities — a mission statement that reads almost identically to the “embedded evaluators” framework Amodei proposed. Less than a week before that statement, the Financial Times reported that Anthropic had withheld its latest model from AISI entirely. Anthropic declined to comment on the withholding at the time; the UK’s Cabinet Office, asked directly about it, did not address it either, offering only that it “continues to collaborate closely with industry partners, including Anthropic, to make models safer.” A national AI safety institute that cannot confirm it has actually seen the model it exists to evaluate is a fairly precise illustration of the gap between the language of oversight and its practice — the same gap this series has now documented in the METR investigation’s constrained scope, in Amodei’s evaluator proposal drawing scepticism over conflicts of interest, and now in a sovereign government’s own flagship safety body.

Obama’s intervention, and the party still finding its footing

The most consequential political development of the week came from someone no longer in office. As The New York Times’ Evan Gorelick reported, Barack Obama told Democratic House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, at a private fundraiser, that AI could be “dangerous” without urgent management, and urged Jeffries to build “a very clear plan” on AI safety and economic disruption should Democrats retake the House — explicitly positioning the issue as central to the 2028 presidential field as well. Obama was careful to place himself in the middle of the industry’s own binary, describing himself as neither an “accelerationist” nor a “doomer,” and drew a specific, concrete contrast to nuclear proliferation that this series’ own Atoms for Peace comparison in Part 94 should sit alongside: unlike nuclear weapons, he noted, powerful AI doesn’t require “a bunch of highly enriched uranium” to build, meaning the traditional non-proliferation model of controlling physical material has no equivalent here at all.

That framing lands directly on the fault line already running through Democratic politics on this issue. Jeffries responded with a statement calling Republicans derelict on AI, but the party itself remains split between members who want aggressive regulation and members who have taken substantial industry money, a tension Olorunnipa’s reporting documents in granular electoral detail: political advertising specifically about data centres has spiked nationally, with Wisconsin’s gubernatorial race turning on ads branding the Democratic candidate “Data Center David Crowley” and Ohio’s Senate race running the identical attack in reverse, against the Republican incumbent Jon Husted, as “the face of data centres.” The Data Center Coalition — whose membership includes Amazon, Google, and Microsoft — has meanwhile stepped up lobbying specifically against moratoriums at every level of government, with the group’s Virginia director, Nicole Riley, arguing that “sweeping restrictions... risk stalling economic opportunity” — the identical argument this series’ Capex Ledger sibling has already tracked from Virginia’s own legislature to Pennsylvania’s.

The number that should worry both parties equally

A Pew poll Olorunnipa cites gives the electoral stakes a hard figure this series hasn’t had before: just over half of Americans now say they feel more concerned than excited about AI’s growing role in daily life, up from 37 percent in 2021 — a fifteen-point swing in five years, on an issue that Senator Bernie Moreno of Ohio, no ally of Sanders on almost anything else, described in nearly identical terms: the AI industry has run “the worst sales pitch in the history of sales pitches,” Moreno said, campaigning on a proposed 100 percent federal tax on state and local data-centre incentives. That a Trump-aligned Ohio Republican and an independent Vermont socialist have converged on the same rhetorical register to describe the same industry, while their own party leaderships remain paralysed on what to actually do about it, is close to a precise summary of where this essay leaves Washington: aware, alarmed in public statements from both flanks, and legislatively exactly where it started.

The poll that shows neither party actually owns this issue

A separate New York Times/Siena poll, conducted September 8-13 and reported by Tim Balk and Caroline Soler, adds precision to the Pew figure above and complicates the partisan story this essay has otherwise been telling. Sixty-one percent of voters oppose data-centre construction outright — but the partisan split is stark: 75 percent of Democrats versus 47 percent of Republicans, with independents at 60 percent. Neither party has claimed an advantage on the underlying AI question itself: 42 percent of voters trust Republicans more, 40 percent trust Democrats more, a two-point margin inside the poll’s own noise on the one issue where Democrats otherwise led on every single other category tested, including the economy and immigration. Opposition to data centres skews sharply by age — nearly 80 percent of voters under 30 oppose construction, against a bare 53 percent majority of voters over 65 — which tracks this series’ own account, in Capex Ledger, of youth-led organising against specific data-centre projects. The poll was completed the Sunday before Trump’s Truth Social escalation and Trump’s September 14 phone call to Jensen Huang; Balk and Soler note that most opponents, 56 percent, favour limits rather than an outright ban, and that AI itself registers as a decisive voting issue for under 1 percent of respondents — a reminder that everything this series has catalogued as an elite-level reckoning has not yet become a mass electoral one, whatever the Wisconsin and Ohio ad wars might suggest.

The revolt on his own platform

The clearest evidence that this reckoning has reached beyond elite commentary came from a source this series hadn’t expected to check: Trump’s own supporters, replying directly to him. As The New York Times‘ Stuart A. Thompson reported on September 15, 2026, Trump posted on Truth Social that AI and its data centres would be “the Greatest Economic Development Engine in History — Bigger than Oil, Gold, Diamonds, or even the Internet.” Within the same day, his posts escalated further: AI safety concerns were “the Golden Goose” that shouldn’t be killed, and, finally, that fears of AI “taking over the World, destroying Humanity, and all other things bad” were nothing more than “a HOAX” — comparable, in his own framing, to the two impeachment efforts against him and the Russia investigation into his 2016 campaign. The Times analysed thousands of replies to these posts and found comments critical of Trump’s AI stance outnumbered supportive ones by nearly four to one, on a platform Trump himself founded specifically to give his most loyal supporters an uncontested space. Real, named users — several identifying as repeat Trump voters — told him plainly that he’d lost them on this one: “I voted for you three times, but am very disappointed that you feel the need to shove AI and data centres down our throats,” one wrote. Another suggested, more pointedly, that he “build them data centres at Mar-a-Lago” to experience the disruption himself.

The Times/Siena poll already covered above turns out to have a sharper Republican-specific split than this essay’s earlier summary captured: 49 percent of Republicans support data-centre construction against 47 percent opposed — a near-even coin flip inside Trump’s own coalition, compared to 75 percent opposition among Democrats and 60 percent among independents. Separate Pew polling from last November found Republican concern about AI generally has grown to half the party, up from 45 percent in 2021. Raymond La Raja, a University of Massachusetts Amherst political scientist, offered the plainest reading of why the base is breaking here specifically: “It really doesn’t play well to his base because a lot of those folks are probably worried about A.I. taking their jobs.” His prediction for what happens if that worry is validated is worth sitting with, given how often this series has documented Trump’s own positions shifting without warning: “If things start to unravel with A.I. and we start seeing major social disruptions, you’re going to see him do a quick reversal.” Thompson draws the same comparison this series would: the pattern echoes the backlash Trump faced on his own platform after starting a war with Iran earlier this year, when supporters who’d valued him as “the peace president” flooded his replies with the same kind of disillusioned pushback now visible on AI. Pennsylvania’s governor’s race adds a concrete electoral data point to the trend: Republican challenger Stacy Garrity has now pledged to pause data-centre development if elected, directly competing with Democratic incumbent Josh Shapiro’s own executive order — the same reversal this series’ Capex Ledger sibling already tracked from Shapiro’s side, now matched from the other.

A stage nobody expected to share

The starkest illustration that this reckoning is not purely elite-driven arrived the same week, in a Washington conference room. As The New York Times’ Jeremy W. Peters and Cecilia Kang reported, Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon shared a stage at the Pro-Human Assembly, an event hosted by the Future of Life Institute, delivering back-to-back speeches to an audience that included evangelical, Catholic, and Mormon religious leaders, the actor and SAG-AFTRA negotiator Ashley Judd, and parents of teenagers who died by suicide after forming attachments to chatbots. Attendees received hats reading “Keep the future human.” Representative Greg Casar offered the flattest version of the policy this series has yet recorded: “The answer is simple: We ban A.I. systems that are too powerful for humans to control.” Representative Lori Trahan’s framing captured the room’s own sense of how strange the moment had become: “I can’t believe I have to say this. The future we all want is human. It’s nonnegotiable.” Archbishop Salvatore Cordileone of San Francisco urged attendees to “be the masters of A.I.” while it was still possible; Representative Chip Roy, a Texas Republican, described constituents who felt powerless watching data centres rise with no consultation. An Elon University poll released the same week found seven in ten Americans fear AI will weaken human bonds, and six in ten believe its capacity to take over human tasks will erode people’s sense of purpose — numbers that sit uneasily next to the Times/Siena finding, two paragraphs above, that almost none of these same voters consider AI decisive enough to vote on.

Bannon’s own contribution complicates the tidy bipartisan-populist story this series has told since Part 88: he does not believe Congress is capable of acting at all, and instead called for Trump himself to slow AI development by executive order — the same mechanism this series has repeatedly documented Trump refusing to use, from the voluntary review framework in Part 73 to his dismissal of Amodei’s essay. Trump’s own response to the conference, delivered the same week, moved past rhetoric entirely: Peters and Kang report he warned he could pursue criminal action against AI executives for harms caused by their technology — a new escalation, arriving from the same president who, days earlier, called the entire premise of AI danger “a hoax.” Whether those two positions are reconcilable, or simply two moods experienced by the same man in the same week, is a question this essay is no better equipped to answer than any other in this series has been. When Trump did act, on September 29, it was to sign a voluntary one-page accord with AI chief executives that he called “morally binding,” and to order federal agencies to call the technology “Super Intelligence.”
