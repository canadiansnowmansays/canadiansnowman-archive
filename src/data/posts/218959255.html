On the morning of October 5, 1970, armed men came to the door of 1297 Redpath Crescent, on the slope of Mount Royal in Montreal’s Golden Square Mile, and took away the British trade commissioner, James Cross. They belonged to the Liberation cell of the Front de libération du Québec. Their price for his life included the release of jailed FLQ members, a ransom in gold, and the broadcast of their manifesto on Radio-Canada. Within twelve days a Quebec cabinet minister would be dead, soldiers would be standing guard on Montreal street corners, and Canada would be living under the War Measures Act, the only time the law has been used in peacetime.

That was fifty-six years to the day before Quebecers went to the polls on October 5, 2026. We will come to that October. But the guns are the wrong place to begin, because they were never where Quebec’s real power came from.

There is a second habit, in English Canada, of telling the Quebec story as though it began with a referendum. It did not. By the time René Lévesque put a question to Quebecers on May 20, 1980, the province had already been turned inside out: its electricity nationalized, its schools taken from the Church, its pension money pooled in its own vault, its language made the law of the land and the language of work. The referendum was not the revolution. It was the bill arriving after the meal.

This essay is about the meal. It runs from a June night in 1960 to a summer afternoon in 1977, and it is the story of how Quebec nationalism became the most successful political lever in Canadian history, without anyone leaving anything.

The Great Darkness, and the Lights Coming On

Begin with the man the Quiet Revolution defined itself against. Maurice Duplessis governed Quebec from 1936 to 1939 and again from 1944 until he died in office at Schefferville on September 7, 1959, the province’s longest-serving premier. His Union Nationale ran a rural, clerical, patronage-oiled Quebec in which the Catholic Church administered the schools and hospitals, unions were treated as a public nuisance, and the 1937 Padlock Law let the government shutter premises accused of spreading communism, a term applied with some creativity. The 1949 Asbestos strike, put down by provincial police, became the founding grievance of a generation of young intellectuals, among them a lawyer named Pierre Elliott Trudeau.

Later admirers of the Quiet Revolution called the Duplessis years la Grande Noirceur, the Great Darkness. Historians since the 1990s have argued, with some justice, that the darkness has been exaggerated by the people who claimed credit for switching on the lights. But even the revisionists concede the fundamentals. French Canadians were a majority in Quebec and a minority in its boardrooms. The Royal Commission on Bilingualism and Biculturalism, which sat from 1963 to 1971, documented what everyone already knew: in 1965, Quebec francophones earned on average 35 per cent less than anglophones, and more than 80 per cent of employers were anglophone, as summarized in The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s entry on “Bill 101” by Michael D. Behiels and R. Hudon. In their own province, they took orders in someone else’s language.

Jean Lesage’s Liberals won the provincial election of June 22, 1960, taking 51 seats on the slogan “It’s time for a change.” It was. Over the next six years, the Lesage government did what it called rattrapage, catching up, at a pace that still astonishes. The Parent Commission on education, whose report began appearing in 1963, led to the creation in 1964 of a provincial Ministry of Education, the first since the nineteenth century, prising schooling out of clerical hands. The Caisse de dépôt et placement du Québec was created in 1965 to manage the money of a new Quebec Pension Plan, which Lesage negotiated as a separate plan rather than joining Ottawa’s. That last point deserves underlining. Quebec did not get its own pension plan by seceding. It got it by saying no to Ottawa across a conference table, and Ottawa, under Lester Pearson’s “co-operative federalism,” saying fine.

Maîtres Chez Nous

The centrepiece was electricity. Lesage’s minister of natural resources was a former Radio-Canada broadcaster, René Lévesque, whose weekly programme Point de mire had made him the most recognizable explainer in French Canada. Lévesque argued that the private power companies, overwhelmingly English-owned, should be bought out and folded into Hydro-Québec, a Crown corporation created in 1944 with a far narrower reach.

Lesage, not a radical by temperament, called a snap election on the question. On November 14, 1962, under the slogan Maîtres chez nous, masters in our own house, the Liberals won with 56.6 per cent of the vote, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Quiet Revolution” article by René Durocher. On May 1, 1963, Hydro-Québec was authorized to begin acquiring the private distributors, among them Shawinigan Water and Power and Gatineau Power. The total cost, by Hydro-Québec‘s own account in its history timeline “1960–1979: The Second Nationalization”, was $604 million, about $300 million of it raised by selling bonds on the American market. The new provincial nation was financed, in part, on Wall Street, a detail that tends to be omitted from both the nationalist and federalist versions of the story.

A people told for a century that they were good at farming, praying and having large families had just bought the biggest utilities in the province and set about building the dams at Manicouagan in French. The slogan outlived the government that coined it, and it remains the cleanest summary of what Quebec nationalism actually wanted. Not necessarily a country. A house, with its own keys.

A Balcony in Montreal

Lesage lost the 1966 election to Daniel Johnson’s revived Union Nationale, and the reforms continued anyway, which tells you how deep they had gone. Then, on July 24, 1967, during Canada’s centennial year and the triumph of Expo 67, the President of France stepped onto the balcony of Montreal City Hall.

Charles de Gaulle had travelled up the old Chemin du Roy from Quebec City to cheering crowds. From the balcony he ended his remarks with four words, Vive le Québec libre, leaning hard on the last one. It was the slogan of the separatist Rassemblement pour l’indépendance nationale, and the general knew it. Lester Pearson’s government answered that the remarks were unacceptable, and Pearson’s rebuke contained the best line of the affair: “Canadians do not need to be liberated.” De Gaulle cancelled the Ottawa leg of his visit and flew home on July 26, as the Wikipedia article “Vive le Québec libre” recounts.

The episode gave Quebec independence an international echo for the first time, and it infuriated English Canada, which had spent the summer congratulating itself on being a hundred years old and grown-up. The pattern would recur: a foreign power taking a flattering interest in breaking Canada up. Readers following the traffic between Alberta separatists and Washington in 2026 will find the shape familiar. Part VII returns to it.

The Journalist Builds a Party

Lévesque, meanwhile, had concluded that the house needed more than keys. In the autumn of 1967 he brought a proposal for sovereignty paired with an economic association with Canada to the Quebec Liberal Party’s policy convention. It was rejected, and he left the party. In November 1967 he founded the Mouvement souveraineté-association, and on October 14, 1968, it merged with the Ralliement national to create the Parti Québécois, as recorded in The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Parti Québécois” article by Clinton Archibald and Maude-Emmanuelle Lambert. The RIN dissolved and its members largely followed.

Note the hyphen in “sovereignty-association.” From the beginning, the mainstream of Quebec independence was not asking for a clean exit. It was asking for a new deal: political sovereignty, a shared currency and market, a partnership with the country it was leaving. Critics called this having your cake and eating it. Lévesque called it realism. Either way, it was a negotiating position before it was a destination, which is exactly the argument of this series.

The PQ’s early electoral record was a lesson in first-past-the-post. In 1970 it won 23.5 per cent of the vote and seven seats. In 1973 it rose to 30.2 per cent and won six, while Robert Bourassa’s Liberals took 102 of 110. A party could be the choice of nearly a third of the electorate and still fit its caucus around a kitchen table.

The Bombs

Not everyone was prepared to wait for elections. The Front de libération du Québec was founded in March 1963 and, between then and 1970, carried out more than 200 bombings and dozens of robberies, killing six people, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Front de libération du Québec (FLQ)” by Marc Laurendeau and Andrew McIntosh.

The first to die was Wilfrid O’Neil, a night watchman at a Canadian Army recruiting centre on Sherbrooke Street West in Montreal, killed by a bomb on April 20, 1963. He was not a symbol of anything; he was a man at work. In February 1969 a bomb tore through the Montreal Stock Exchange and injured 27 people. The targets were chosen for what they represented, English money, federal authority, the army, but the casualties were people, and they should be named as people rather than folded into a narrative about the ripening of a national consciousness.

Proportion matters. The FLQ was a small network of cells, never a mass movement, and Lévesque’s democratic nationalists regarded it with something between embarrassment and horror. That distinction mattered enormously in October 1970, and was very nearly erased.

October

Which brings us back to Redpath Crescent. Five days after James Cross was taken, on October 10, the Chénier cell abducted Pierre Laporte, Quebec’s minister of labour and deputy premier, from the front lawn of his home in Saint-Lambert, where he had been playing football with his nephew.

Soldiers began appearing in Ottawa. On October 13, a CBC reporter, Tim Ralfe, pressed Prime Minister Trudeau on how far he would go. “Just watch me,” Trudeau replied, and the phrase entered the language. On October 15, at the request of the Quebec government, troops deployed in Quebec. In the early hours of October 16, Ottawa proclaimed the War Measures Act, the only time it was ever invoked in peacetime, suspending ordinary civil liberties and making membership in the FLQ a crime retroactively.

On October 17, Pierre Laporte’s body was found in the trunk of a car near the Saint-Hubert airbase. He had been strangled.

Under the War Measures Act and the Public Order Act that succeeded it, police arrested 497 people. Of those, 435 were released without charge and 62 were charged, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “October Crisis” by Andrew McIntosh and Celine Cooper. The detainees included the poet Gaston Miron, the singer Pauline Julien and a good many union organizers and PQ supporters whose offence was holding opinions in the vicinity of the crisis. James Cross was found and freed on December 3, 1970, after 59 days in captivity, in exchange for safe passage to Cuba for his kidnappers. Laporte’s killers were caught later that month; Paul Rose and Francis Simard received life sentences.

Two things are true here, and the honest account holds both. A cabinet minister was murdered, a diplomat was held for two months, and a government facing a terrorist group of unknown size had real reason for alarm. And the response swept up hundreds of people who had done nothing, nearly nine in ten of whom were never charged, under an emergency law whose justification, an “apprehended insurrection,” has never been convincingly demonstrated. Public opinion at the time, in Quebec as well as English Canada, overwhelmingly supported Ottawa. History has been less generous.

If there was ever a week when the Tim Hortons shrug was impossible, it was that one. Nobody looked up at the armoured vehicles on the streets of Montreal and said, “Again? Must be Tuesday.” What is remarkable is what came after. The FLQ, which had hoped to provoke a popular uprising, provoked instead a collective revulsion that finished it as a force. Quebec independence did not die in October 1970. It went into the ballot box and stayed there.

November 15

Six years later, it came out of the ballot box with a majority. On November 15, 1976, the Parti Québécois won 71 of 110 seats with 41.4 per cent of the vote. Bourassa lost his own riding. On election night at the Paul Sauvé Arena, Lévesque told the crowd he had never thought he could be so proud to be Québécois. English Canada reacted with something between alarm and disbelief.

The PQ had won partly by promising not to do the thing it existed to do. Its étapisme, or step-by-step strategy, separated the question of good government from the question of sovereignty: elect us, and we will hold a referendum later before taking any step toward independence. It was a two-stage rocket, and the first stage was simply competence after a scandal-plagued Liberal term.

Bill 101

The first stage did not wait for the referendum. Camille Laurin, a psychiatrist and the PQ’s minister of cultural development, drafted the Charter of the French Language, Bill 101, which received royal assent on August 26, 1977. It made French the official language of the government and the courts, the normal language of work, and the language of commercial signs, initially to the exclusion of others. It also required the children of immigrants, including those moving from other provinces, to attend French schools, with narrow exceptions.

No piece of Quebec legislation has been more resented in English Canada, and none has more clearly done what it set out to do. Within a generation, the children of Italian, Portuguese, Greek and Haitian immigrants who in 1970 would have been educated in English were growing up francophone. The language of a Montreal boardroom changed. A minority that had taken orders in someone else’s language now gave them in its own.

The cost was real, and it was paid largely in departures. In January 1978, Sun Life Assurance announced it would move its head office from Montreal to Toronto, citing the new language rules; its policyholders approved the transfer by roughly five to one, as Time reported in “Business: Adieu, Montreal” on May 8, 1978. Its Montreal tower, 26 storeys of granite, had once been the largest office building in the British Empire. Over the following decades, a long procession of anglophones and their children made the drive west along the 401. Toronto, which had been gaining on Montreal since the 1950s, finished the job.

Whether this was a price or a purpose depended on whom you asked. For many nationalists, a smaller, less dominant English community was not a side effect of Bill 101. It was the point.

What Was Won Before Anyone Voted

Now add up the ledger as it stood in the spring of 1980, before a single ballot had been cast on sovereignty. Quebec owned its electricity. It ran its own schools, its own pension plan and its own investment fund. French was the legal language of work and commerce in the province. Ottawa, under the Official Languages Act of 1969, had made French equal in federal institutions, and francophone Quebecers, among them Trudeau himself, now ran the federal government. Quebec had secured a substantial role in selecting its own immigrants.

All of it was achieved inside Canada. Some of it was achieved by Ottawa, partly to prove that Quebec did not need to leave. And all of it was achieved under the steadily growing pressure of the possibility that Quebec might go.

That is the lever this series is about. The threat of exit, credible enough to frighten and never quite exercised, bought the Québécois most of the house before they ever voted on the deed. When the referendum finally came, on May 20, 1980, a great many Quebecers had to ask themselves what, exactly, independence would add to what nationalism had already won. Their answer is the subject of Part III.
