There is an old rule of dinner parties that the most discussed guest is usually the one who did not come. By that rule, the guest of honour at the 2026 DealBook Summit was Jensen Huang.

He was not on the programme. Six days before the summit, on Thursday, November 12, he had resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, the company he co-founded in 1993 and built into the most valuable business on earth. The board had met for several hours the evening before, after reporting on two extramarital relationships: one with an executive who reported to him, and one with Alice Huang, a woman American intelligence officials believe to be an asset of Beijing, as Tripp Mickle and Maggie Haberman reported in The New York Times. Beijing has called the spy allegations smears and slanders. Colette Kress, the finance chief, became interim chief executive, and the board named its first chairman, Harvey Jones.

So when Andrew Ross Sorkin put the question to his guests on November 18, he was asking about an empty chair. Can anyone replace Jensen Huang? Should anyone have tried? Nearly everyone who sat on that stage was asked some version of it, and the answers fell into a pattern that says more about the people answering than about Huang.

The full story of his fall belongs to another series on this site, The Shovel Seller. This essay is about the room.

I. “Kicking Michael Jordan off your team”

Elon Musk, speaking by video from China, where he was travelling with President Trump, was the most emphatic. Removing Huang, he said, was “like kicking Michael Jordan off your team” in the middle of a winning streak.

He did not pretend nothing had happened. “He’s certainly made some mistakes here,” Musk said, “but I don’t think he’s disloyal to the United States.” Would he hire Huang himself? “Who wouldn’t?” Huang, he said, was “somewhat stunned.” Then he added a detail that ought to have made more news than it did: “the president has voiced publicly that he thinks Jensen should have stayed as CEO.” The decision, Musk conceded, belonged to Nvidia’s board, and if shareholders disagreed, they would have to vote the board out.

The president had indeed voiced it. Two days before the summit, Trump told reporters that Huang had “learned a big lesson” and that he “would not mind” if Huang were restored, as Ana Swanson and Tony Romm reported in The New York Times. Earlier, he had said he always thought of Huang as “a fine man,” and that he did not recall being briefed on the intelligence about Alice Huang, though Marco Rubio had briefed him on Air Force One in May, according to Katie Rogers in The Times. That same week he had telephoned Huang and urged him to keep fighting for his job.

Musk’s analogy is revealing in what it leaves out. In sport, nobody asks whether a star player has been close to a foreign intelligence service. The Jordan comparison turns a question of national security and corporate governance into a question of talent, and on talent, Huang wins every time. That is precisely why his defenders prefer to argue on that ground.

II. “China wants Jensen out, so I want him in”

Palmer Luckey, the founder of the defence company Anduril, gave the shortest answer of the day, and the most telling. “China wants Jensen out,” he said, “so I want him in.”

It is a line built for applause, and it got some. As policy, it is a strange principle: decide what Beijing wants and do the opposite. If Chinese intelligence had in fact cultivated Huang, then by Luckey’s logic its success would oblige Washington to keep him in place. There is no evidence that China engineered his fall. The reporting points the other way, to an American intelligence finding, a Times investigation and a board responding to both. But the reflex matters more than the logic. In the circles closest to this administration, almost every question about the technology industry is now answered by asking which side China is on.

That reflex has a cost. The Huang affair raised the most serious question yet about how the world’s most strategically important chip company was being run, and who had access to the man running it. Luckey’s answer, like Musk’s, made that question disappear.

III. “They’re not gods”

Satya Nadella, Microsoft’s chief executive, took the opposite view, and took it carefully.

It was “pretty tragic,” he said, and it was “the Nvidia board’s decision.” He praised Kress, who had worked at Microsoft before Nvidia. Then he said the most interesting thing anyone said about Huang all day. Founders, he said, “are singular,” but “they’re not gods.” Founders also die. And if a leader leaves and the institution he built collapses without him, “then what did you build?”

It is easy to hear that as a quiet dig, from one of the most successful successors in corporate history. Nadella took over Microsoft from Steve Ballmer, who had taken over from Bill Gates, and is now in his thirteenth year running the company. Microsoft is the standing proof that a technology giant can outlive its founder and grow larger after he leaves. But Nadella’s question is also the right test of a company, and it is the test Nvidia now faces in public.

The early answer is that the institution held. On November 17, the day before the summit, Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of $115.8 billion, more than double a year earlier, and forecast about $130 billion for the next quarter, according to its quarterly results. Those numbers cover months Huang ran the company, so they prove less about his absence than the headlines suggested. More telling was what the board did that same evening. Huang had said in a statement that if the board decided his return was in the best interest of the company and America, he would be honoured to come back. On the earnings call, the new chairman said there was no plan to bring him back, as CNBC reported. The shares rose about two per cent after hours.

In January, Huang had described his own position on a podcast with unusual candour. He held no super-voting shares, he said, and if the chief executive does not do his job in the view of the board, “the C.E.O. is let go.” He also said: “there will never be one like me.” Both statements turned out to be true.

IV. “Another set of random opinions”

Sam Altman of OpenAI chose not to have a view at all. “It’s hard to imagine the AI industry without Jensen,” he said. Beyond that: “I don’t think the world needs another set of random opinions on what’s going to happen.” His co-founder, Greg Brockman, offered a single word for Huang: “singular.”

Silence is a position too, and in Altman’s case an understandable one. OpenAI depends on Nvidia’s chips more than almost any company on earth. Whoever ends up running Nvidia will decide, among other things, how much of its scarce supply goes to OpenAI and on what terms. There is no upside in offering an opinion on the succession of your most important supplier. Altman, alone among the speakers, behaved like a customer.

V. Who decides

Set side by side, the four answers divide neatly.

Musk and Luckey, the guests closest to the president, framed Huang’s departure as a loss to the country and a win for China, and placed their trust in the man. Nadella, who has spent two years staying out of Washington, placed his trust in the institution and its board. Altman, the biggest customer, placed his trust in saying nothing.

What none of them said out loud is the most unusual part of the story. The President of the United States had made it publicly known that he wanted a particular person to run a private company, and the company’s board declined. Boards are meant to answer to shareholders. They are not meant to answer to presidents, and for most of American history the question would not have arisen. It arose in November 2026 because this administration treats Nvidia as a national asset. Trump has spoken of the company as central to the stock market and to the race with China, and his own Treasury secretary, Scott Bessent, said publicly that Nvidia needed a “dramatic shake-up.” The administration was arguing with itself about what it wanted from a company it does not own.

Meanwhile, the policy question that the scandal raised was being settled elsewhere, without any of the speakers. On November 16 the administration moved to close the loophole that let Chinese companies rent advanced American chips through data centres in third countries, a rule written by Treasury and the National Security Council over the resistance of the Commerce Department, Swanson and Romm reported. Nvidia’s shares fell nearly four per cent that day. Huang and the commerce secretary, Howard Lutnick, have been asked to testify before the Senate Banking Committee in December. On November 16, the DealBook newsletter itself asked in a headline whether Huang was plotting a comeback. By the time the summit opened, the board had answered.

VI. The empty chair

There is a reason this question hung over every interview of the day, and it is not gossip.

The A.I. boom rests on a remarkably narrow foundation. Nearly every model discussed at Lincoln Center, from OpenAI’s to Anthropic’s to Musk’s own, is trained on Nvidia hardware. For three years, the industry’s answer to the question of who keeps that foundation sound has been, in effect, Jensen Huang: his judgement about whom to sell to, his relationships with Washington and Beijing, his personal management of a company with more than sixty people reporting directly to him. When that judgement came into question, the people with the most at stake split three ways, between loyalty to the man, faith in the board and discreet silence.

Nadella’s question, “then what did you build?”, is the one that will be answered over the next year, and not only at Nvidia. Every company on that stage is led by someone whose name is treated as a strategy. Musk is Tesla and SpaceX. Altman nearly lost OpenAI in 2023 and got it back within a week, because the staff threatened to follow him out the door. The ones who built institutions will survive their founders. The ones who built followings will find out what Nvidia found out in November: that a following is not a succession plan.
