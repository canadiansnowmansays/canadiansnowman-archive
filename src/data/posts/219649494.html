In Pahoa, on the Big Island of Hawaii, Mick and Ann Kalber received a registered letter in the first week of October 2026. It told them their Medicare Advantage plan was being discontinued.

Mr. Kalber is 78. He has a history of throat cancer and jaw surgeries, and he now lives on a feeding tube. He was hospitalised twice this year in Hilo. The couple pay less than $300 a month in premiums and expect to pay hundreds more next year. Their first priority is keeping access to the Mayo Clinic surgeon in Florida who has operated on his jaw. Christopher Rowland and Reed Abelson of the New York Times asked Ms. Kalber on October 9, 2026, whether the couple could afford an increase. Her answer: “No — but we can’t afford not to.”

That sentence is the American healthcare system in eight words. It is also the reason this series exists.

The Numbers

Rowland and Abelson’s reporting puts scale on the Kalbers’ letter. The health technology firm Duos estimates that roughly five million people will be forced to switch Medicare Advantage plans for 2027, because their policy has been cancelled or withdrawn from their county. Other estimates are lower. But analysts told the Times that cancellations will probably exceed this year’s total, which was itself a record.

That record was set only months ago. As the Boston Globe reported on March 28, 2026, a study published in JAMA found that nearly three million people had to find new plans for 2026. That was about one in ten people in individual Medicare Advantage plans. In Vermont, Blue Cross Blue Shield withdrew its Advantage product entirely, citing millions of dollars in losses.

For 2027, the map is worse. According to KFF figures cited by the Times, 187 of the nation’s 3,069 counties will have no Medicare Advantage plan at all, up from 118 this year. New Hampshire, North Dakota and Oklahoma join that list for the first time. UnitedHealth’s cutbacks alone are moving about 390,000 people. Health Care Service Corporation, which runs Blue Cross plans in several states, is leaving about half a dozen states. Humana told investors in July 2026 that its exits would affect roughly 600,000 members, as Newsweek reported.

Even people who keep their plan will pay more. By one estimate in the Times piece, the average out-of-pocket limit on a typical plan will rise to $6,600, from about $5,000 in 2024. Michele Harris-Padron, 74, of Santa Barbara, keeps her UnitedHealthcare plan but will pay $30 more a month, and her specialist co-pay rises from $45 to $60 a visit.

Here’s What Washington Says. Here’s What the Market Says.

What the administration says: Medicare Advantage is stable. Premiums are falling. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services data, reported by Healthcare Finance News, project the weighted average monthly premium falling from $14.37 in 2026 to $12 in 2027, a 16.5 per cent decline. CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz told the Times that insurers’ decisions about where to sell plans “should not be confused with M.A. instability.” He also credited the government with “slashing handouts to big insurance companies.”

What the market says: Jenn Kerfoot, president of Duos, told the Times that in a decade in the industry she had never seen an official announcement so out of step with conditions on the ground. Roshan Desai of the Rebellis Group, which advises health plans, said insurers once never shrank their service areas, and now it is routine.

What the money says: The administration did not slash handouts. As Axios reported on September 10, 2026, the government abandoned a proposal to hold 2027 payment rates flat. The final rule instead gave the plans a $13 billion pay increase. Axios also cited a survey in which nearly 70 per cent of health plan leaders expected 2027 benefits to be less generous anyway.

Translation: The insurers received a raise, then cut benefits, raised cost-sharing and walked out of the counties where they couldn’t make the numbers work. A falling average premium is entirely compatible with five million people losing their plans. It is what you get when the expensive rural plans are deleted from the average.

The Pattern

None of this is new. It is a rerun.

  • 1997: The Balanced Budget Act creates Medicare+Choice, the first large-scale invitation for private insurers to run Medicare benefits. The theory is that competition will lower costs.

  • 2000–2001: The insurers leave. Plans covering almost a million seniors exit before 2001. Another 536,000 are dropped for 2002, as KFF Health News recorded at the time. PacifiCare’s chief executive explains that the gap between what Medicare costs and what the government is willing to pay has become unsustainable. Washington responds by paying the plans more.

  • 2003: The Medicare Modernization Act renames the programme Medicare Advantage and, as the Boston Globe noted, increases what private insurers receive for each enrollee. Annual reports from the government’s own advisers have since found that the promised savings never arrived.

  • 2023: More than half of eligible Americans are now in a private plan. The insurers market zero-premium policies, dental and vision coverage, gym memberships and grocery cards.

  • 2024–2025: Regulators push back on overbilling and reduce reimbursements. Medical costs rise. Profits shrink.

  • 2026: Nearly three million people lose their plans. It is a record.

  • 2027: Up to five million more. 187 counties with nothing at all. The industry’s lobbying arm, the Better Medicare Alliance, says the fix begins with “fully funding Medicare Advantage.”

The script hasn’t changed in twenty-five years. Insurers enter when the subsidy is generous. They leave when it isn’t. Each exit is followed by a request for more money to prevent the next one. The patients are not part of the negotiation. They receive the registered letter.

The Sentence Readers Couldn’t Let Pass

The most revealing part of the Times story wasn’t in the story. It was in the comments, where one of the reporters, Reed Abelson, summarised the hard choices ahead and added that in some places people may even have to switch to traditional Medicare.

Even. As though it were the last lifeboat.

The readers objected, by the hundreds. A commenter from New York, DJS, took Abelson to task for writing as if the public programme were the inferior option. A retired physician and administrator from Washington State, posting as JtheJ, said that after forty years in healthcare he saw nothing good in what he and many others called the “Disadvantage” plans. A reader from Chicago, David Cort, described traditional Medicare with a supplement as a “Gold Card” and Medicare Advantage as Basic Economy: restricted, conditional and non-refundable. A reader from Oklahoma laid out his own arithmetic. His overnight hospital stay was billed at $26,000, and he paid nothing, because he had chosen traditional Medicare with a Plan G supplement. He estimated that an Advantage plan would have left him owing thousands.

A reader from Virginia, Lisa, asked the question that should embarrass Congress. If Medicare Advantage is private insurance, why were the insurers allowed to put the word Medicare in the name? Many enrollees, she wrote, never understood that they had left the public programme for a for-profit company.

Comment sections are not data. But a few hundred people sharing their own bills reveal something no CMS press release will. The people who know the system best, including doctors, insurance administrators and a retired insurance-systems architect who said he couldn’t navigate the choices himself, overwhelmingly chose the government plan.

The Trap Nobody Advertises

The readers also identified the mechanism that makes Advantage plans so hard to leave. That mechanism is the reason the exit door matters so much.

Traditional Medicare covers about 80 per cent of approved costs. Most people fill the gap with a private Medigap supplement. When you first enrol at 65, Medigap insurers must sell you a policy regardless of your health. But a person who joins an Advantage plan and later tries to return can, in most states, be medically underwritten. That means higher premiums, waiting periods or a flat refusal for the pre-existing conditions that nearly every older American has. A handful of states, including New York and Connecticut, forbid this.

So the business model works like this. Recruit people at 65, while they’re healthy and attracted by a $0 premium and a dental allowance. Keep them as they become ill, because leaving has become expensive. As a Tampa commenter put it, the effect is to pool the healthy in the private plans and leave the sick to the public one.

One detail offers some relief, and a California reader corrected another commenter about it. When an insurer terminates a plan, the people dropped receive a guaranteed right to buy a Medigap policy without underwriting, for a limited window. The exit door, in other words, is the one moment the law makes it easy to escape. That right matters only if people know it exists and can afford what’s on the other side. In Minnesota’s Crow Wing County, where every Advantage plan has disappeared, broker Nicole Converse told the Times that the Medigap alternative will cost about $330 a month, plus a separate Part D drug plan. About four million people in stand-alone Part D plans who paid no premium will now pay one, because the administration is ending temporary subsidies. The number of stand-alone drug plans has fallen by two-thirds since 2021, to 316.

The escape route exists. The toll is about $4,000 a year, charged to people living on Social Security.

The $90 Tell

One more detail from the Times story deserves attention.

Shortly before the midterms, the administration began sending payments of at least $90 to 21 million beneficiaries of traditional Medicare. Not Advantage enrollees. Democrats called it a desperate move. Several readers said they planned to donate the money to free clinics or to Democratic candidates.

Set aside the politics of a pre-election cheque. Look at whom it was sent to. When an administration that has spent its term praising private Medicare wanted to reward older voters, it reached for the public programme. Traditional Medicare is the one with the address list, the payment system and the political goodwill. It is the one the government can actually administer.

That proves the point of Part 7 of this series better than I did.

What We Said in December

In Part 7, published in December 2025, I called traditional Medicare “America’s Best-Kept Secret”: America already has a single-payer system, and the people in it like it. That remains true. Medicare Advantage was never a replacement for that system. It was a privately run layer on top of it, subsidised by the same taxpayers and dependent on the same public guarantee.

That is why its failure has a soft landing. When an Advantage plan closes, nobody is left with no Medicare at all. They are returned, by default, to the public programme that was there all along. The private market can leave. Original Medicare cannot, and does not.

There is no better description of what single payer is for. It is the insurer that cannot walk out of Crow Wing County because the margins disappointed a quarterly earnings call. As 24/7 Wall St. reported on September 5, 2026, UnitedHealth told analysts on July 16 that it expected its Medicare Advantage enrollment to fall by about 1.1 million. Wall Street was told in July. The Kalbers were told in October.

A View from North of the Border

Several Canadians joined the Times discussion, and their comments were almost apologetic in their ordinariness. A reader from Vancouver said that he pays more tax and sometimes waits his turn, but never hesitates to get treatment, and that his biggest medical expense is hospital parking. Another, identified only as Peter J, said the only bureaucracy he deals with is a new photo for his health card every five years. When he needed a heart stent, he had it within five days of a diagnosis made on a long weekend.

I write from Toronto. I would not claim that Canadian Medicare is without problems. Finding a family doctor is harder than it should be, and some waits are a disgrace. But I have never received a registered letter telling me my coverage has been withdrawn from my county, and no Canadian ever will. That isn’t because Canadians are wiser. It’s because no private company here has the right to decide that our province is unprofitable.

The Bottom Line

The private Medicare experiment has run for nearly three decades under four names and two parties. Each time, it has promised that competition would save money. Each time, the government’s own advisers have found that it costs more. Each time, the insurers have left when the subsidy disappointed them and returned with a request for more.

The 2027 enrolment period runs from October 15 to December 7, 2026. For millions of older Americans, it will be a scramble through fine print, with fewer choices, higher costs and doctors missing from networks. They will be making these decisions weeks before a midterm election in which, according to a KFF poll cited by the Times, nearly twice as many likely voters trust Democrats as Republicans to handle healthcare costs.

At a minimum, Congress could do three things this session:

  • Require real notice. Insurers knew in June which plans they were cutting and told investors in July. Members found out in October. A year’s notice of a plan exit should be the price of admission.

  • Make guaranteed-issue Medigap permanent. Anyone who leaves an Advantage plan, at any age and for any reason, should be able to return to traditional Medicare with a supplement and without medical underwriting.

  • Take the word out of the brand. If it isn’t Medicare, it shouldn’t be allowed to say it is.

Beyond that minimum is the larger answer this series proposed in Part 10: the American Health Security Act, now revised into a smaller, honestly costed bill that builds on what already works instead of subsidising what keeps leaving. It is set out section by section in the Code Blue Ledger.

Senator Hawley, the bill is still waiting. The Kalbers can’t afford what’s coming, and they can’t afford not to face it. Neither can you.
