On the afternoon of November 9, 2026, a Kentucky Republican who has already lost his seat stood on the floor of the House of Representatives and read aloud for more than an hour. House custom calls for this when a member offers a privileged resolution, and it is a rare thing to watch a parliamentary formality work as a punishment. Thomas Massie read all eight articles of impeachment against Pete Hegseth. When he sat down, a clock had started. As Elizabeth Elkind reported for Fox News on November 9, the House has two legislative days to deal with the matter, which puts the deadline at November 11.

Speaker Mike Johnson stopped this clock once. In September he sent the House home a day early, and the articles expired on the floor like milk left on a porch. Massie refiled them the first day Congress came back. The Speaker has now run out of calendar.

The Secretary is relaxed about all of this, or would like to be seen that way. On the morning of November 10, he held his first Pentagon news conference since May. As The New York Times reported, he opened by lavishing praise on the President before turning to “one disgruntled and defeated congressman.” He called the articles “a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment,” dismissed the charge that American forces had targeted civilians as “slander,” and did not mention the school in Minab. “If they want to come for the warriors,” he said, “they’re going to have to come through me.” Within the hour, Trump had graded the performance “A PLUS PLUS PLUS.” Before he spoke, a press officer warned the assembled reporters that anyone who failed to behave with decorum would be thrown off the premises, a fitting welcome from a man whose treatment of the Pentagon press corps turns up, as it happens, in Article IV.

The day before, he had spent the morning at the Naval War College in Newport, Rhode Island. As Tom Nichols reported for The Atlantic the same day, what he delivered there was less a speech than a Festivus “Airing of Grievances.” He accused the faculty of trying to “wait out President Trump” and demanded a case study on the failures of Mark Milley, the former chairman of the Joint Chiefs. “Just because some of you know Mark Milley,” he told them, “does not mean you should not study his failures.” Readers of Part IX will recognise the target. By mid-afternoon, a congressman was reading eight case studies of the Secretary’s own into the record.

What the Articles Leave Out

Part X of this series argued that Hegseth had only ever needed one review, and that the reviewer sat in the Oval Office. This instalment concerns a different kind of document, which is the 34-page resolution itself, and it is worth reading for what it does not contain.

There is nothing in it about the drinking allegations or the veterans’ charities. Testosterone goes unmentioned, as do the makeup studio and the push-ups. Signal appears only once, and not as a security breach: Massie uses the leaked chat as evidence that Hegseth knew there was no emergency in Yemen, because a man facing an emergency does not type “we can easily pause.”

A reader who has followed this series through ten instalments of spectacle may feel slightly cheated. The Massie articles treat the Secretary as a serious person who did serious things, which is more respect than he has shown most of the institutions named in them. Even the title is handled with a lawyer’s tweezers. The resolution impeaches “Peter Brian Hegseth, Secretary of Defense, also referred to as Secretary of War by the Administration.”

The charges fall into three groups.

The wars. Articles I, II and III concern Iran. Article VII concerns the seizure of Nicolás Maduro in Caracas on January 3, 2026, which the resolution calls a kidnapping and which it says killed at least 83 people. Article VIII concerns Operation Rough Rider in Yemen, in 2025. The legal core is the War Powers Resolution of 1973, passed over Richard Nixon’s veto, and the Iran counts are unusually tidy. The hostilities began on February 28, 2026, without a declaration, an authorization or an attack on the United States. The President’s letter to Congress started a 60-day clock that ran out on May 1. No 30-day extension was certified, and even if one had been, it would have ended on June 1.

Then comes Article II, which ought to be better known. On June 3, 2026, the House agreed to a concurrent resolution directing the removal of American forces from hostilities in Iran. The Senate agreed on June 23. According to the articles, it was the first time both chambers had ever passed such a measure. On June 26, the resolution says, Hegseth directed forces into hostilities with Iran again. Congress had used the emergency brake that it installed in 1973 and spent half a century declining to touch, and the car kept going.

The dead. Article IV is the one I would hand to anyone who thinks this is a procedural quarrel. It concerns the Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School in Minab, struck by three Tomahawk missiles within the first hour of the war. The resolution puts the toll at no fewer than 156 killed, at least 120 of them schoolchildren, and says more than 200 people were killed or wounded in all. It alleges that after the first missile hit, students were sheltering in a prayer room deeper in the building when the second and third arrived.

The article does not claim Hegseth picked the target. Its claim is more patient and, to my mind, more damaging. It says he asked Congress on May 29, 2025 to repeal the statutory requirement for civilian protection systems, and that those systems were halted anyway after Congress declined. It says he removed more than 90 percent of the civilian-harm staff assigned to combatant commands, cut funding for the Army’s casualty-tracking database, and fired the senior-most Judge Advocates General of the Army, Navy and Air Force. It also quotes him, generously, in his own best verse: “Maximum lethality, not tepid legality.”

Article V follows the same logic out to sea: at least 63 attacks on 67 vessels and at least 221 people killed, without a court or a declared war. It includes the September 2, 2025 strike in which two survivors clinging to wreckage were killed about 45 minutes after the first blast. It also includes the attack of December 30, 2025, after which eight shipwrecked survivors waited about 45 hours for rescuers and died before any arrived.

The senator. Article VI is the shortest route to the man himself. Senator Mark Kelly, a retired Navy captain, told American troops on video on November 18, 2025: “Our laws are clear. You can refuse illegal orders.” The resolution says the Department responded by floating his recall to active duty for court-martial. It says Hegseth directed the Secretary of the Navy to review him, then issued a Secretarial Letter of Censure on January 5, 2026, and then argued in court that retired officers have diminished First Amendment rights. The article notes that nearly a fifth of Congress could have been swept under that theory.

The Best Argument Against

The strongest objection to these articles comes from an unexpected direction, and it deserves a straight hearing. Representative Warren Davidson of Ohio is a Republican critic of the Iran war who told Fox News he will not support impeachment. His case is that Hegseth is being made a scapegoat: Congress has dodged its own responsibility for decades, the decision to go to war was the President’s, and punishing a defence secretary for following his commander-in-chief would be the truly unprecedented step.

That argument has real force, particularly against Articles I, II, III, VII and VIII. Secretaries of defence have served presidents who stretched the war power since Korea. If the unauthorized use of force were an impeachable offence, the portrait gallery at the Pentagon would look rather like a police lineup. And Davidson is right about Congress, which has been an accessory to its own diminishment for most of my adult life.

From another flank, Representative Don Bacon of Nebraska told CNN that he is “not very sympathetic” to the articles as drafted. As Ellie Quinlan Houghtaling reported for The New Republic on November 9, the things that trouble him most, the purging of generals and the withholding of money appropriated for Ukraine, are not in them. Johnson, for his part, has said that “we don’t impeach Cabinet secretaries for political gain or for cheap tricks.”

The difficulty with the scapegoat defence is that it is a defence of the wars. It says much less about the rest. Nobody ordered Hegseth to propose repealing the civilian-protection law, so far as the record shows. The Letter of Censure bears his name. Firing the military’s senior lawyers and then telling reporters, as Article V recounts, that he did not want them acting as “roadblocks” is a management philosophy of his own. Davidson’s argument covers the orders Hegseth received. It covers far less of the apparatus he took apart beforehand, which is the part of the indictment that belongs to him alone.

Massie has answered the other common objection in a sentence that is the best thing written about this affair. Some colleagues, he posted on X, say that Hegseth has been effective and that they agree with the war. “That’s not the question though,” he wrote, as Hafiz Rashid reported for The New Republic. “The question is whether he committed impeachable acts, and the answer is yes, eight times.”

The Motion to Avoid Reading

Here is what the arithmetic looks like. CNBC puts the House at 218 Republicans to 214 Democrats. ABC News reports that no Democrat appears ready to oppose the resolution, and that three Republicans voting with every Democrat would carry it. Massie himself is the first of the three. Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has declared himself a “hard yes.”

Then there are the absentees. A lame-duck session is a peculiar instrument, full of members who have been fired by their own voters and who may not care to fly in for the formalities. Republicans told Fox News that such absences, combined with anger at Hegseth over the midterm rout, could tip the outcome, though most of their conference is still expected to oppose impeachment. A senior Hill staffer put the mood to Nick Turse of The Intercept on November 5 as senior Republicans asking themselves what harm a yes vote could even do. It is hard to threaten a man with a primary after he has already lost his general.

But the vote most members are hoping to cast is not on impeachment at all. Leaders can move to table the resolution or refer it to committee, and either motion ends the matter without anyone having to say whether a defence secretary may keep a war going after both chambers have told him to stop. Massie predicted this in September, as Stars and Stripes reported: a tabling vote would serve “to insulate the members of this body from any accountability.”

Watch that first roll call, then, and not the second. If the motion to table carries, the record will show that the House of Representatives, one week after an election widely read as a verdict on the Iran war, voted not to discuss it. If it fails, Republican leaders will have lost control of the floor to a man their own President drove out of Congress, and the Secretary of War will be one vote from a distinction he has not sought.

The Other Secretary of War

The United States has impeached exactly two Cabinet secretaries. The more recent was Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024. The first held Hegseth’s preferred title. William Belknap served as Secretary of War under Ulysses Grant until 1876, when it emerged that money had been flowing to him from a trading post at Fort Sill.

As the House historian records, Belknap resigned on the morning of March 2, 1876, hoping to spare himself. The House impeached him anyway that same day, without a dissenting vote. The Senate tried him. It acquitted him on August 1, falling short of two-thirds, with several senators doubting they had jurisdiction over a man who had already left.

Belknap took money, and nothing in Massie’s 34 pages accuses Hegseth of that. But the old case holds a lesson for the Secretary and for the people around him. Massie has said that there was one contingency in which he would not refile: if Hegseth resigned. That door has been open for days. Donald Rumsfeld walked through a similar one on November 8, 2006, the day after a midterm rout over a different war, and at least had the grace to be pushed promptly. Hegseth has chosen instead to make videos. Belknap’s example suggests that once the House has made up its mind, leaving does not necessarily end the matter.

Conviction is another story. Removal needs two-thirds of the Senate, and that is out of reach in this Congress and very likely in the next. Nobody should pretend otherwise. Hegseth would remain at his desk, drawing a salary as the third Cabinet secretary ever impeached, and the Justice Department under Todd Blanche has already said it will represent him.

Does impeachment without removal matter? Nancy Youssef, who covers the Pentagon, argued on Radio Atlantic that an impeached secretary of defence, convicted or not, "puts a spotlight on the war itself." Her colleague Tom Nichols went further. "It's a huge deal because Pete Hegseth's gonna make it a huge deal," he said, predicting that the Secretary would try to drag the troops into his defence, as his "Stand and fight" videos already do. "And I think that, strangely enough, that's a good reason to do it. Because that kind of secretary of defense should be impeached and removed."

One Review Left

Which brings the series back to its audience of one, whose notices have lately turned mixed. Trump has said he has every intention of keeping Hegseth. He also told Fox News on November 8 that Hegseth was “one of the guys that was most responsible” for the Iran war. Asked on 60 Minutes whether he credited Hegseth for the Venezuela operation, he replied, as Nick Turse reported for The Intercept on November 9: “No, but he was a part of it.”

Anyone who has been told by an employer that he “was a part of it” can tell you to start clearing out the desk. Yet Hegseth’s position may have improved in one respect. Stephen Miller, reported to be close to him, has just replaced Susie Wiles as chief of staff, and Wiles had reportedly soured on the Secretary.

From up here in Toronto, I am struck by how little of this turns on Pete Hegseth. He has been the most theatrical defence secretary in American history, and this series has enjoyed the theatre more than was decent. But the document that Thomas Massie read into the record on November 9 is not about the performer. It asks whether a statute means what it says when a president’s man decides otherwise, and whether Congress, having finally pulled its brake in June, cares that nothing happened.

The House has until November 11 to answer. It can answer the question, or it can vote to table it. Either way, the roll will be called, and the names will be written down.
