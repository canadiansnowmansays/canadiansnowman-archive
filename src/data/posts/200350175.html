Governor Jeff Landry signs Louisiana’s new congressional map into law on May 29, 2025, eliminating the majority-Black 6th District.

On May 29 — two days before the legislative deadline — Governor Jeff Landry signed Louisiana’s new congressional map into law. The Senate had passed it 28-10, on a party-line vote, hours earlier. The House had passed its version the day before, as angry members of the public shouted from the gallery.

The map eliminates Cleo Fields’s majority-Black 6th District. It clusters Black communities from New Orleans to Baton Rouge into a single remaining Democratic-leaning seat held by Troy Carter. The delegation shifts from 4R-2D to a projected 5R-1D. Jay Morris, the bill’s sponsor, said on the record what the map was for: “I purposely put more Democrats into District 2 to make the remaining districts better performing for Republicans.”

Four Black members of Congress — the only four Louisiana has sent to Washington since Reconstruction — testified against the maps on May 9. Marc Morial, president of the National Urban League and former mayor of New Orleans, testified against them. So did former Mayor Sidney Barthelemy and former Clerk of Criminal Court Arthur Morrell. Dozens of voters testified in opposition during a ten-hour House committee hearing. One person testified in favour.

Louisiana’s new congressional map (SB 121). It dismantles Cleo Fields’s majority-Black 6th District and packs Black voters into a single remaining Democratic-leaning seat.

The Republican supermajority passed them anyway.

The morning after the House primaries were supposed to have taken place, Yvonne Wingett Sanchez of The Atlantic was at Mount Zion First Baptist Church in Baton Rouge — the church once led by T.J. Jemison, who in 1953 led a boycott of segregated buses that became the model for Montgomery two years later. From the pulpit, Reverend Renè Brown read a passage from the Book of Numbers about the allotment of land and told his congregation he wanted to talk about “biblical redistricting.” Two giant screens displayed the words BIBLICAL REDISTRICTING alongside the U.S., Confederate, and Christian flags. The congregation gasped. Some burst out laughing. “Oh Lordy,” one man said under his breath.

Brown closed the service by asking congregants to sign a petition to recall Landry. The idea is far-fetched in a state where Republicans dominate every lever of government. The lines for signatures crept up the aisles and jammed the foyer anyway.

Laura Bradley, 72, remembers being forced as a child to enter a malt shop through the back door because the front was reserved for white people. “It feels like we’re in James Crow Jr.,” she said after signing. “All these feelings that you thought you had allayed and set aside, now they are back in the forefront again.” The gains from decades of struggle for equal representation, she said, had been wiped out. “It’s almost starting again from ground zero.”

Cleo Fields was in the back pew, nodding and clapping. Later, in his pastor’s office, a heaviness seemed to hang over the congressman. “It pushes us back to 60 years ago,” he said. “We thought we had fought these battles.” He described what the map meant in terms that did not require a legal brief to understand: “I can play football on a Saturday and have a whole stadium cheering for me. But I can’t go govern on Monday?”

Fields will not run against Troy Carter in the single remaining majority-Black district. When asked what the new map means for his future, he said he was figuring it out. “Within the next week or two, everybody’s going to know what I’m doing.”

Congressman Cleo Fields. One of only four Black representatives Louisiana has sent to Congress since Reconstruction. He called the new map a return to battles fought 60 years ago.

He recalled a conversation with Jesse Jackson during the 1988 presidential campaign, when Fields bemoaned political disengagement. Jackson told him: “There are no apathetic people. There are only uninspired people.”

Perhaps this moment will inspire the uninspired. But across the Mississippi River, in West Baton Rouge Parish, Joseph Hopkins, a 42-year-old fast-food manager who used to vote Democrat, was fuelling his SUV. “I stopped paying attention,” he said. “There’s a lot of things they say but never follow through on. I don’t trust no man.” Outside a nearby auto-parts shop, a man in camouflage pants peered at his truck’s engine. “These people swing the vote however they want,” he said. Whether he votes or not, “ain’t gonna make much of a difference.”

The redistricting is designed to produce exactly these voices — the disengaged, the exhausted, the people who have concluded that participation is futile. The machinery does not need to prevent you from voting. It only needs to convince you that voting does not matter.

The map will face legal challenges from both sides. Black elected officials say it violates redistricting laws. The original white plaintiffs in the Callais case say the new map still relies too much on race. The same ruling that opened the door for Louisiana to eliminate a majority-Black district may now be used to challenge the map that walked through it.

The rescheduled House primaries — postponed from May 16, when 45,000 absentee ballots were discarded — are now set for November 3. Louisiana has reverted to a jungle primary for its House races, all candidates on a single ballot regardless of party. The closed-primary system that shaped Cassidy’s defeat will not apply to the House elections.

Louisiana’s chapter in this series is not closed — the litigation will continue for months, and the Letlow-Fleming Senate runoff on June 27 will determine who fills the seat Cassidy lost. But the map is signed. The machinery delivered.

Alabama: The Court That Would Not Stay Blocked

While Louisiana was finishing, Alabama was caught in a loop.

On May 11, the Supreme Court cleared the way — in a 6-3 decision — for Alabama to use its 2023 congressional map, vacating the lower court’s injunction and sending the cases back for reconsideration under the new Callais standard. Alabama Republicans moved quickly, scheduling special primaries for August 11 in four congressional districts, with 21 qualified candidates awaiting a map to run under.

The three-judge panel came back on May 26 and blocked the map again. This time on constitutional grounds — intentional racial discrimination under the Fourteenth Amendment — a finding the Callais ruling cannot touch. Two of the three judges were Trump appointees. The ruling was unanimous. “We do not find the issue particularly complex or close,” they wrote.

Alabama’s attorney general, Steve Marshall, filed another emergency application to Justice Clarence Thomas the following day, requesting a ruling by June 1. Alabama’s own election director had testified that voter rolls lock on June 2 for the June 16 runoff — leaving at most a single business day to complete a voter reassignment process that normally takes months. As of publication, the Court had not acted. The machinery of Alabama’s election calendar may have already decided what the Supreme Court has not — and Shomari Figures may keep the seat he won in 2024 simply because the clock ran out.

Marchers cross the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama — the site of Bloody Sunday in 1965, where state troopers attacked voting rights demonstrators. Sixty years later, the bridge remains a symbol of the ongoing fight against voter suppression and racial gerrymandering in the South

South Carolina: The Pause That May Not Hold

South Carolina’s Senate adjourned on May 26 without acting on a new map. Shane Massey’s stand held. Forty-five thousand early votes were cast on the first day. Clyburn’s district survived.

But the fight may not be over.

As the June 9 primary approached, Republican leaders signalled that the current elections may be the last to use the existing maps. Attorney General Alan Wilson, a Republican gubernatorial candidate, promised that “this fight is not over.” Some Republicans discussed overturning the June 9 results in favour of new primaries in August under a redrawn map.

The threat hung over the June 9 vote like a conditional clause: your ballot counts, unless we decide it doesn’t. South Carolina voters have seen this before. Louisiana discarded 45,000 ballots when it postponed its primaries. The precedent was fresh.

South Carolina voted on June 9 under the maps that Massey’s stand preserved. The threat to void the results and redraw the districts before August did not materialize. The conditional clause went uninvoked.

James Clyburn, whose majority-Black 6th District had been the explicit target of the redistricting effort, easily won his Democratic primary. He did not celebrate quietly. “The current administration in Washington, the current leadership of the Congress and a majority of the Supreme Court seem to be hellbent on turning the clock back,” he said in a statement. “If re-elected, I promise to fight against the forces of retrogression.”

Gloria Winkler, 79, had voted in Orangeburg — Clyburn’s own turf — earlier that day. “That’s racist,” she said of the redistricting attempt. “That’s a nice, political, quiet way to get rid of certain people.”

On the Senate side, Lindsey Graham survived the night that John Cornyn did not. Graham cleared 50 percent outright — 56.8 percent to Mark Lynch’s 28.9 — and avoided the runoff that ended Cornyn’s career. The difference was Trump’s endorsement. Where Cornyn faced a Trump-backed challenger, Graham had the endorsement himself. The loyalty test, for now, he passed.

The Republican governor’s race will extend to a June 23 runoff between Trump-endorsed Lt. Gov. Pamela Evette and Attorney General Alan Wilson. Trump’s blessing was not enough for an outright win in a crowded field — Evette finished at 28.9 percent, Wilson at 26.1. Representative Nancy Mace, who had courted Trump’s support and lost it after pushing for release of the Epstein files, was eliminated. On her way out she endorsed Wilson. “I chose to expose the abusers of children and, apparently, I chose wrong if the goal was winning an election,” she told supporters. “I’m at peace with that.”

The June 23 runoff will determine who leads the state that almost redistricted itself out of a congressional seat. Massey’s stand holds. But the stand deferred the battle. It did not end it.

The Scorecard at Five Weeks

Five weeks after Callais, this is where the South stands.

Tennessee eliminated its lone majority-Black district within a week. Steve Cohen, the only Democrat in the delegation, abandoned his re-election bid. Mayor Chaz Molder of Columbia, who had spent months running for Congress, found his home drawn into a different district. Tennessee’s Speaker, Cameron Sexton, said: “Just because that you may not have voted for that person doesn’t mean you’re not represented.” The projected delegation: 9R-0D.

Louisiana signed its new map on May 29. Cleo Fields’s district is gone. Morris admitted on the record the map was designed for partisan advantage. Delegation shifts from 4R-2D to 5R-1D. Legal challenges pending from both sides.

Florida’s DeSantis-appointed judge upheld the state’s new map on May 26. Democratic seats cut from eight to four. The DeSantis administration is arguing that the voter-approved Fair Districts amendments should themselves be invalidated. Plaintiffs appealing.

Alabama is in limbo. The three-judge panel blocked the new map. Marshall has appealed to the Supreme Court. Special primaries are scheduled for August under a map that no one yet knows the identity of.

South Carolina adjourned without acting. Clyburn’s district survives for June 9. Whether it survives beyond that is an open question.

Georgia’s special session begins June 17. The day before, Republicans will vote in the gubernatorial runoff between Burt Jones — the fake elector who went to the Willard Hotel on January 5, 2021 — and Rick Jackson, the billionaire. Part XXXII of this series documented what Jones did in 2020. What Georgia does in 2026 will determine who represents four million Black Georgians through 2028.

Mississippi’s governor has signalled redistricting for 2028. The clock is slower. The intent is the same.

Across the South, the New York Times reported, about 60 percent of Black Americans now live in the region — a share that has grown in recent years, reversing the Great Migration. The redistricting is hitting the part of the country where Black Americans are increasingly choosing to live, not leaving. The Voting Rights Act was written for the South. Its weakening is being felt there first.

What Comes Next

“If it didn’t matter, they wouldn’t be trying so hard to keep you from being able to vote.”

On June 9, South Carolina votes. The question is not just who wins — it is whether the results will be allowed to stand.

On June 16, Georgia holds its runoff. The question is whether the man who tried to steal the 2020 election will become the state’s next governor.

On June 17, Georgia’s special session begins. The question is whether the maps drawn in that session will survive the courts — and with an 8-1 Republican-appointed state Supreme Court, the answer is almost certainly yes.

On June 27, Louisiana holds its Senate runoff. The question is who fills the seat Cassidy lost for voting his conscience.

And somewhere between now and then, the Supreme Court may decide whether Alabama can use a map that two Trump-appointed judges called “tainted by intentional race-based discrimination.”

Janet Tobias, 70, sat outside a Louisiana State Capitol hearing room while lawmakers debated the maps that would determine her representation. “They’re trying to bring us back,” she said. She feared that “we are not going to have anyone behind those doors speaking up on our behalf.”

Nettie Ramsey, 58, cast her primary vote during her lunch break in Montgomery. “You wonder if there’s anything that can be done at this point,” she said. “But actually, I don’t ever want to see us stop fighting for our rights.”

Laura Bradley in Baton Rouge signed the recall petition and said it felt like James Crow Jr. But she also had hope — that aggressive gerrymandering could backfire by galvanising the very voters it was designed to silence.

And Terry Jackson, a 55-year-old truck driver picking up plates of BBQ at a Cajun diner in Port Allen, Louisiana, said what the redistricting maps cannot obscure and the legal briefs do not contain.

“They’re showing that, actually, your vote matters,” he said. “If it didn’t matter, they wouldn’t be trying so hard to keep you from being able to vote.”

The machinery has delivered. The maps are signed. The districts are redrawn. The ballots are discarded. The question this series keeps asking is where democracy fights back.

Terry Jackson answered it from a diner in Port Allen. The fight is in the proof. They would not be trying this hard if it were not working.
