Parts I through III of this series have been about roads, regulators, and the people navigating both. This one is about the money underneath all of it — specifically, the awkward fact that the company at the center of Europe’s best AV story had to raise most of its capital from outside Europe to get there.

The raise, in full

On February 25, 2026, Wayve announced a $1.2 billion Series D round, valuing the London-based company at $8.6 billion. The round was led by Eclipse, Balderton, and SoftBank Vision Fund 2, with new participation from Ontario Teachers’ Pension Plan, Baillie Gifford, the British Business Bank, Icehouse Ventures, and Schroders Capital. Sitting alongside those financial investors was a list that reads like a cross-section of the entire global AV supply chain: Microsoft, Nvidia, and Uber all put in money directly, while Mercedes-Benz, Nissan, and Stellantis — three separate automakers who will eventually compete against each other selling cars built on the same underlying software — joined as strategic investors. Uber separately committed up to $300 million in additional milestone-based capital tied to the rollout this series has already covered in Part II.

Two months later, in April 2026, Wayve added a further $60 million from AMD, Qualcomm, and Arm — three chipmakers who don’t typically appear in the same funding round, brought in specifically to give Wayve’s software a choice of silicon platform rather than a single dependency. Alex Kendall framed the logic to CNBC in blunt commercial terms: giving customers a choice of chip platform lets Wayve meet automakers where they already are, rather than asking every manufacturer to standardize on one vendor. That extension brought total Series D funding to roughly $1.5 billion, and Wayve’s lifetime fundraising, across eleven rounds since a 2017 Cambridge founding by Alex Kendall and fellow PhD student Amar Shah, to approximately $2.5 billion — a trajectory that included a $1.05 billion SoftBank-led Series C in 2024 before this year’s larger round.

What that money is actually betting on

The technical claim underneath all of this capital is worth stating precisely, because it’s the thing every investor in that round is underwriting. Wayve says it became the first autonomous-vehicle developer to achieve “zero-shot” driving — operating competently in a city without any city-specific fine-tuning — across more than 500 cities in Europe, North America, and Japan within a single year, as reported by Zag Daily, attributing the feat to training its foundation model on globally diverse driving data rather than the meticulously mapped, sensor-calibrated approach this series covered in Part II’s comparison with Waymo. If that claim holds up commercially rather than just in a demo, it’s the difference between an AV company that has to rebuild its system city by city and one that can plausibly license a single product into any market with a willing regulator and a willing automaker. That is, in miniature, the entire bet the Series D round represents: not that Wayve will win London specifically, but that its underlying approach generalizes fast enough to be worth funding at global scale before the London result is even fully known.

The number this essay actually exists to sit with

Here is the harder fact underneath Wayve’s success story. According to PitchBook data, European start-ups raised approximately $69 billion in venture funding last year, against $320 billion raised by U.S. start-ups over the same period — roughly a 4.6-to-1 gap, in an era when AI and AV companies are absorbing an outsized share of venture capital on both sides of the Atlantic. Wayve’s $1.5 billion round is easily one of the largest fundraises any European start-up has completed in that entire window, which makes it simultaneously a genuine European success story and a fairly precise illustration of the imbalance it’s an exception to. A continent that produced roughly a fifth of the venture capital America did last year is being asked, by its own companies’ ambitions, to compete directly against Chinese state-backed capital of the kind Part I documented flowing into Baidu, Pony.ai, and WeRide, and against the depth of American venture and sovereign-adjacent capital chasing Waymo and its domestic rivals. Wayve got its money regardless — but a meaningful share of that money, from SoftBank to Nvidia to three separate American chipmakers, came from outside Europe entirely. The round proves a European AV company can attract global capital. It doesn’t prove Europe’s own capital base is deep enough to have funded that round on its own.

Wayve isn’t alone, which is both encouraging and part of the problem

Wayve’s raise sits alongside a real cluster of large 2025 and 2026 European AI fundraises that complicate any simple “Europe can’t compete” narrative. Mistral, the French AI lab positioning itself as Europe’s answer to OpenAI and Anthropic, raised roughly $2 billion. Nscale, a London-based AI data-center developer, raised more than $1 billion the same month. Helsing, a German defense-AI start-up building AI into weapons systems, raised approximately $700 million. Isomorphic Labs, the Google-affiliated London drug-discovery company applying AI to molecular design, raised around $600 million. Taken together, this is a genuine cluster of European ambition — not a single outlier, but a small handful of companies each attempting to hold a serious position in a specific vertical of the AI economy, from foundation models to infrastructure to defense to biotech. What none of them individually solve is the structural gap PitchBook’s numbers describe: a handful of large, well-covered raises at the very top of the European market, sitting on top of a much thinner base of mid-stage funding than either the American or Chinese ecosystems can draw on beneath their own respective flagship companies.

Where Wayve sits among its actual peers

It’s worth placing Wayve inside its real competitive set rather than treating it as sui generis. Industry tracking puts autonomous-driving start-ups’ total funding since 2009 at roughly $37 billion globally, according to S&P Global’s AutoTechInsight, with Wayve now grouped among a peer set that includes SenseTime, Nuro, TuSimple, Horizon Robotics, Momenta — and Pony.ai, the same Chinese operator covered at length in Part I. That’s a useful corrective to any instinct to read Wayve purely as a British national champion: it is, more accurately, one node in a global AV-funding ecosystem in which Chinese, American, and European capital are all chasing variations of the same underlying bet, with China’s variant backed by the kind of coordinated state investment documented in Quiet War and this series’ Part I, and Europe’s variant assembled, round by round, out of whichever global investors are willing to write the check.

What this means for the rest of the race

None of this changes the fact that Wayve is, on the evidence of Parts I through III, currently Europe’s best answer to a global race being run simultaneously in Wuhan, Phoenix, and now London itself. But the shape of its cap table is itself a data point this series should keep tracking: a European AV champion whose largest backers are a Japanese conglomerate, three American chipmakers, a Canadian pension fund, and three separate multinational automakers is not quite the same story as a Chinese operator whose expansion is coordinated with its own government’s industrial policy, or an American operator drawing on the deepest venture and public-market capital base in the world. Wayve found the money it needed. Whether the next European AV company can say the same, without borrowing quite so much of it from everywhere else, is the open question this series will keep an eye on as the race keeps running.
