Between Ngo Quyen’s victory in 938 and the arrival of French gunboats on the Red River in the 1850s sit roughly nine hundred years of independent Vietnamese dynastic rule — the Ly, the Tran, the Le, and eventually the Nguyen. It’s tempting, given how much of this series has focused on foreign domination and resistance, to treat this stretch as a kind of historical intermission: the “normal” centuries between the two great foreign incursions everyone actually wants to read about. That would be a mistake. This is the period when Vietnam built the institutions, the territorial shape, and the cultural self-conception that everything after 1850 would be fighting to preserve or transform. It’s also the period that produced Vietnam’s second and third great river battles at Bach Dang, a fifteenth-century Chinese reoccupation that very nearly succeeded in erasing Vietnamese distinctiveness for good, and the beginning of Vietnam’s own expansion southward into territory that would eventually become the Mekong Delta.

The Ly Dynasty and the Shape of a State

The century following Ngo Quyen’s death was, as I noted in Part II, unstable — the Anarchy of the Twelve Warlords, resolved only when Dinh Bo Linh unified the country under the Dinh dynasty in 968. The Dinh dynasty itself was short-lived, succeeded by the Early Le dynasty, whose founder Le Hoan won the second great battle of Bach Dang against a Song Chinese invasion force in 981, using a version of Ngo Quyen’s stake-and-tide tactic that I described in Part II. But the dynasty that truly established Vietnam’s medieval state structure was the Ly, founded in 1009 by Ly Cong Uan (posthumously Ly Thai To), who moved the capital to what is now Hanoi and gave it the name Thang Long — “Ascending Dragon.”

The Ly dynasty, which ruled for roughly two centuries, built the first genuinely durable centralized Vietnamese state: a civil service examination system modeled on, but distinct from, the Chinese model; a standing military; codified law; and — significantly for later Vietnamese religious and cultural identity — state patronage of Buddhism alongside Confucian administrative structures, producing the syncretic blend of religious and philosophical influence that still characterizes Vietnamese spiritual life today. The Ly also fought and won wars against the Song dynasty to the north and expanded southward against the Champa kingdom, establishing a pattern of southward expansion — Vietnamese historians call it Nam tiến, the “southward march” — that would continue for the next several centuries and eventually bring the Mekong Delta, then under Khmer and Cham control, into Vietnamese territory.

The Ly period also produced the foundational text of Vietnamese Buddhism’s court relationship — successive Ly emperors patronized Buddhist monks as advisors, and several of the dynasty’s early rulers were themselves devout practitioners, a pattern of close ties between throne and monastery that would ebb under the more Confucian-leaning Tran and Le dynasties but never fully disappear. The dynasty’s wars against Champa, the Hindu-Indic kingdom occupying much of what is now central Vietnam, were the opening moves in what would become a centuries-long, intermittent campaign of Vietnamese expansion at Champa’s expense — campaigns that weren’t continuous conquest so much as a recurring cycle of Vietnamese incursion, Cham counterattack, and gradual territorial erosion that would only conclude, definitively, in the nineteenth century, by which point Champa had been reduced from a major regional power to a small, absorbed minority population within Vietnam’s borders. It’s worth noting, in the interest of the same historiographical honesty I’ve tried to maintain throughout this series, that Vietnam’s own historical relationship to Champa complicates any simple narrative of Vietnam as perpetual victim of larger powers — from Champa’s perspective, and from the perspective of the Khmer kingdoms whose territory would later be absorbed into the Mekong Delta, Vietnam was very much the expanding, absorbing power, not the resisting one.

The Tran Dynasty and the Mongols

The Ly gave way to the Tran dynasty in 1225, and it was the Tran who faced the single greatest external military threat Vietnam confronted in this entire nine-hundred-year stretch: the Mongol Empire, at the height of its power, under Kublai Khan.

The Mongols invaded Vietnam three times — in 1258, 1285, and 1287-88 — and each time, despite commanding a military force that had conquered Song China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and pushed into Europe, they were ultimately repelled. The architect of Vietnamese resistance was Tran Hung Dao, a member of the royal Tran family and one of the most celebrated military commanders in Vietnamese history, whose reputation in national memory rivals or exceeds Ngo Quyen’s. Tran Hung Dao’s strategy against the third Mongol invasion drew directly and consciously on Ngo Quyen’s precedent: in 1288, facing a large Mongol fleet on the Bach Dang River — the same river, the same tidal geography — Tran Hung Dao replicated the iron-tipped stake trap, timed again to the tide, and destroyed the Mongol fleet in a battle that Vietnamese historical memory treats as the direct successor and validation of Ngo Quyen’s original tactic three and a half centuries earlier.

The three invasions were not identical in character or scale. The 1258 campaign was comparatively small, a probing expedition that seized the Tran capital briefly before being forced to withdraw amid disease and supply difficulties. The 1285 invasion was far larger, involving several hundred thousand Mongol and allied Chinese troops under Prince Toghan, one of Kublai Khan’s sons, and came close to succeeding — Tran forces were pushed back repeatedly, the capital fell again, and the Tran court was forced into temporary flight. But Tran Hung Dao’s strategy in this campaign, as in the others, was to trade space for time: avoid decisive engagement with the main Mongol force, allow the invaders to overextend their supply lines in unfamiliar and often disease-prone terrain, and strike back once Mongol strength had been sapped by exhaustion, illness, and the difficulty of provisioning a large army in a country whose population had been organized, in part deliberately, to deny invaders easy access to food and local support. By mid-1285, the Mongol position had deteriorated badly enough that Tran forces were able to go on the offensive and force a costly withdrawal. The 1287-88 invasion, launched partly to punish Vietnam for this defeat and partly to try again with better preparation, ended even more decisively with Tran Hung Dao’s second Bach Dang victory.

The significance of defeating the Mongols specifically is hard to overstate. This was an empire that, at its peak, stretched from the Pacific to Eastern Europe, and Vietnam is one of a very small number of societies that met Mongol invasion and simply was not conquered — not through isolation or luck, but through direct, repeated, successful military resistance. Along with Japan’s famous defense against Mongol invasion fleets around the same period (aided, in Japan’s case, by the typhoons remembered as kamikaze), Vietnam’s three successful defenses against Mongol invasion stand as among the most significant military achievements of any medieval Southeast or East Asian state, and Tran Hung Dao’s tactical writings on guerrilla warfare, strategic patience, and asymmetric resistance against a numerically and technologically superior invading force would be studied — explicitly, by name — by Vietnamese military leadership again in the twentieth century, including Vo Nguyen Giap.

The Le Dynasty and the Ming Occupation

The Tran dynasty eventually weakened and was displaced, and in the resulting instability, Ming China saw an opportunity that echoed the Southern Han’s opportunism four centuries earlier. In 1407, Ming forces invaded and, this time, succeeded in reestablishing direct Chinese administrative rule over Vietnam — the only successful reimposition of the kind of full administrative control that had characterized the original thousand years of Bắc thuộc, and one that Ming administrators pursued with an explicit, stated goal of eliminating Vietnamese cultural distinctiveness for good: destroying Vietnamese historical records, imposing Chinese customs, and attempting a more thorough cultural assimilation than earlier periods of Chinese rule had pursued.

This Ming occupation lasted only twenty years — considerably shorter than the original millennium of Bắc thuộc — but it produced one of the most celebrated resistance movements in Vietnamese history: the Lam Son uprising, led by a wealthy landowner named Le Loi, beginning in 1418. Le Loi’s ten-year campaign against Ming occupation is a genuinely remarkable story of patient, grinding guerrilla resistance against superior imperial resources — retreat into mountainous terrain, gradual accumulation of local support, and eventual military victories that forced a full Ming withdrawal by 1427. Le Loi founded the Later Le dynasty, which would go on to become the longest-ruling dynasty in Vietnamese history, and Le Loi himself joined Ngo Quyen and Tran Hung Dao in the pantheon of foundational Vietnamese resistance figures — a third data point, alongside the Trung Sisters and Bach Dang, in the accumulating national mythology of small, patient Vietnamese resistance eventually outlasting larger imperial powers.

One detail from the Lam Son uprising is worth flagging specifically because of how directly it echoes forward into the twentieth century: Le Loi’s campaign relied heavily on guerrilla tactics — avoiding direct confrontation with superior Ming forces, retreating into terrain the Ming forces didn’t know as well as the Vietnamese did, wearing down occupying forces through patience and attrition rather than decisive set-piece battles. This is, functionally, the same operational logic that would define Viet Minh resistance against the French and, later, North Vietnamese and Viet Cong strategy against the United States. The specific tactics differed across five centuries of changing military technology, but the underlying doctrine — geographic knowledge and patience defeating superior resources — is a continuous thread running from Ngo Quyen through Tran Hung Dao through Le Loi and into the twentieth century.

Le Loi’s chief strategist and closest advisor during the uprising, Nguyen Trai, deserves particular mention, since his writings would become foundational to later Vietnamese political and military thought in a way that goes beyond the immediate Ming campaign. Nguyen Trai’s Great Proclamation upon the Pacification of the Wu (referring to the Ming, whom Vietnamese sources of the period sometimes called by the older dynastic name), composed after the successful expulsion of Ming forces, is frequently cited by later Vietnamese nationalists as an early articulation of Vietnamese national distinctiveness and sovereignty — asserting that Vietnam possessed its own mountains and rivers, its own customs, and its own history distinct from China’s, and that this distinctiveness justified independent rule regardless of China’s greater size and power. Ho Chi Minh, centuries later, would draw on precisely this rhetorical tradition in his own declarations of independence, and Nguyen Trai’s proclamation is sometimes described by Vietnamese historians as an early Vietnamese equivalent to a declaration of independence in its own right, predating the concept in Western political thought by several centuries in the specific context of asserting sovereign distinctiveness from a much larger neighbouring civilization.

Southward Expansion and the Nguyen Lords

The Later Le dynasty’s long reign, from 1428 into the eighteenth century, was also the period of Vietnam’s most significant territorial expansion — the continuation of Nam tiến, the southward march, which gradually brought the former Champa kingdom’s territory and eventually the Mekong Delta, previously under Khmer control, into Vietnamese hands. This expansion is important for understanding the Vietnam that would eventually confront France and the United States: the country’s elongated, S-shaped territorial form — stretching roughly a thousand miles from the Red River Delta in the north to the Mekong Delta in the south — is a product of centuries of gradual southward conquest and settlement, not a natural or ancient boundary. The cultural, dialectal, and to some degree political differences between north and south Vietnam that would matter enormously during the twentieth-century partition and war have roots in this longer history of a northern-originated state gradually absorbing southern territory that had, for centuries before, belonged to entirely different civilizations.

By the sixteenth century, the nominal authority of the Later Le emperors had become largely ceremonial, with real power divided between two rival noble families — the Trinh lords controlling the north and the Nguyen lords controlling the south — in an arrangement that functioned, for roughly two centuries, as a de facto partition of Vietnam under a single nominal dynasty, complete with periodic wars between the two power centers. This division is worth noting because it establishes that the north-south split that would define twentieth-century Vietnam wasn’t a novel Cold War imposition — it echoed a much older pattern of the country’s two halves developing distinct centers of power and periodically finding themselves at odds.

The Trinh-Nguyen wars, fought intermittently across most of the seventeenth century, were fought along a militarized border roughly in the vicinity of the modern 17th parallel — the same latitude, by no means coincidentally given the region’s geography, that would later become the Cold War-era partition line between North and South Vietnam. Neither side succeeded in permanently defeating the other, and the wars eventually settled into a long, uneasy truce that persisted for most of the eighteenth century, with the Nguyen lords using the relative peace to continue southward expansion into former Champa territory and, eventually, into the Khmer-controlled Mekong Delta, gradually absorbing what is now southern Vietnam through a combination of military pressure, negotiated settlement, and Vietnamese peasant migration into the newly available agricultural land.

The Tay Son Rebellion and the Rise of the Nguyen Dynasty

The Trinh-Nguyen division was finally ended, though not in the way either side expected, by the Tay Son Rebellion — a peasant uprising beginning in 1771, led by three brothers from the Tay Son village, which overthrew both the Trinh and Nguyen ruling families and briefly unified the country under Tay Son rule. The Tay Son period is a fascinating and often underexplored chapter of Vietnamese history — a genuine peasant-led revolutionary movement centuries before the Communist Party would claim that mantle, achieving military victories against internal rivals, a Siamese invasion in the south, and a massive Qing Chinese invasion in the north (defeated at the Battle of Ngoc Hoi-Dong Da in 1789, in yet another instance of Vietnam repelling a numerically superior Chinese invading force).

But the Tay Son dynasty didn’t last. A surviving member of the defeated Nguyen lords, Nguyen Anh, gradually rebuilt his power base in the south — notably with military and material assistance from French Catholic missionaries and mercenaries, a detail that matters enormously for what follows — and by 1802 had defeated the Tay Son forces and unified the country under his own rule as Emperor Gia Long, founding the Nguyen dynasty that would rule, in increasingly diminished and eventually purely ceremonial form, until 1945.

That French assistance to Nguyen Anh’s rise is where this series’ next chapter properly begins. The Nguyen dynasty Gia Long founded would, within a few decades of its establishment, find itself facing French pressure that started as missionary and commercial influence and ended, by the 1880s, as full colonial conquest — a debt of assistance called in in the cruelest possible way. The nine hundred years of independent dynastic rule this essay has covered — the Ly, Tran, Le, and the Nguyen’s own violent rise — end not with another Bach Dang-style repelled invasion, but with the one foreign power Vietnam’s medieval military doctrine hadn’t been built to face: an industrial European empire arriving by sea with technology no amount of tidal knowledge could counter.
