Elsinore, Ontario. Population 33,000. The castle has always been here. Gerald has copies of everything.

I grew up in London, Ontario, which named itself after London, England, and its river after the Thames, and has spent a hundred and fifty years doing its level best to inhabit the comparison. This tells you something about the ambitions of southwestern Ontario and the cheerful stubbornness with which the landscape receives them.

I have been writing about American politics for the better part of a year now, at some volume and with considerable feeling, over at CanadianSnowmanSays. It is work I believe in. It is also, on certain Sunday mornings, work that leaves you staring at the river and wondering whether the river has the right idea.

The river, for its part, has no idea. It simply runs through Perth County at whatever depth the season provides, pausing for no committee and acknowledging no tariff. It has been doing this since before Perth County was Perth County. It will be doing it long after the current news cycle has resolved itself one way or another.

This is a different project.

Sketches of Elsinore is a series of weekly sketches about a fictional city of thirty-three thousand people on the Avon River in southwestern Ontario, forty-five minutes east of London on the 401. The city is named after Hamlet’s castle, because its founders in 1854 chose that name and left no record of their reasoning, and because something is always, gently, rotten, and because the name has proved accurate in ways the founders could not have anticipated.

The sketches are written in the tradition of Stephen Leacock, who in 1912 published Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town — a serial portrait of a fictional Ontario municipality called Mariposa, based on Orillia, populated by characters whose ruling passions and magnificent self-delusions made them simultaneously ridiculous and beloved. The book is still in print. Mariposa is still there. Josh Smith still runs his hotel.

I am not Stephen Leacock. I want to be clear about this before we proceed. I am Stephen Gordon, which — given that Leacock's own first name was also Stephen — is either a coincidence or a qualification, and I have never been entirely sure which. What I share with him is a Canadian outsider's eye for the gap between aspiration and reality, a conviction that small towns are not small subjects, and a belief that comedy and seriousness are not opposites but the same thing seen from different angles.

Each sketch follows a local matter in Elsinore — a committee meeting, a civic event, a question about who owns the swans — and finds in it a mirror of something larger happening to the south, which is where the larger things are currently happening. The mirror is not always flattering to either reflection. This is, I think, the point.

You will meet, in the weeks ahead: Gerald Fitch, who has been volunteering for the municipality for twenty-one years and has copies of everything and moves at his own pace. Darlene Potts, the Swan Warden, who speaks rarely and precisely and has been on the river at five forty-five every morning for twenty-two years with her dark green notebook. Mayor Doug, who has served four terms on a platform of benign continuity and owns a good coat that his wife told him made him look like he meant it, and who has meant it, more or less, ever since. Alistair, the Festival’s Artistic Director, in his farewell season, staging plays in a town on a river in southwestern Ontario and believing, as he has believed for thirty-one years, that this is among the most important things a person can do. Wayne, who connects all of Elsinore’s assets for visitors with the smooth professional conviction of a man who has found his niche and occupied it completely, and who is currently learning Korean. Reg Plimpton, whose positions on all significant matters of public concern have evolved consistently over twelve years, with a latency of approximately six weeks. Bradley Fenn, who is bald and thorough and has excellent shoes and uses the phrase “unelected influence” the way other people use punctuation.

And the Perth County farmer, who has farmed this land since before most of the people in these sketches were born, and who speaks once per sketch, and whose one line is always the truest thing said.

The column is called the Moonbeam Chronicle because Elsinore sits under Ontario skies and I was thinking about the moon when I named it, and because Leacock got the sunshine and this seemed fair, and because the best observations about small towns are made at night, when the committees have gone home and the river is running in the dark and things are, for the moment, as they are.

The sketches publish every Sunday morning. They run approximately two thousand five hundred words each, which is the length Leacock’s chapters ran, which is the length of a Sunday morning with a good cup of coffee and no particular obligation to be anywhere.

The first two Sundays will be the Introduction — the town and then the people — before the sketches proper begin. Think of it as arriving in Elsinore before anything happens. Every good visit starts that way.

Free subscribers receive every sketch. There is, at present, no paid tier, because the work is new and the town is still finding its feet and Gerald has not yet been asked to set one up, though he would, at his own pace, if asked.

Come for the swans. Come for a play — or a play on words. Stay for Gerald's filing system.

Something is always, gently, rotten.

The Avon runs through it regardless.

— Stephen Gordon
