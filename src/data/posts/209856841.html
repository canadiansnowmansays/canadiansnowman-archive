Postmark: 16 Wigmore Street, London. A pair of robotic arms glide along an overhead track, plating a crab bisque. A price tag on the wall reads six figures. The floor beside the counter has a small, unattended stain.

Jane Jetson pushed a button on a keypad and asked for pizza, and seconds later it appeared, fully cooked, no human hand involved anywhere in the process. Part VII of this series, Where Is Rosie?, catalogued the long hierarchy of domestic tasks robotics has tried to solve — vacuuming solved decades ago, laundry still the hardest problem in the field — and placed cooking somewhere in the honest middle: partially addressed, genuinely complex, blocked less by mechanics than by judgment. A robot arm can chop a vegetable more precisely than a human hand. Knowing that the onion has gone soft, that the guest arriving tonight cannot eat gluten, that the sauce needs another five minutes though the recipe card says otherwise — these are not motion problems. They are the same reasoning problems this series keeps finding at the bottom of every task that looks simple from the outside.

This postcard is about the machine that has, more than any other, tried to answer that challenge anyway — by teaching a robot not to reason about cooking, but to copy it, motion for motion, from a professional chef’s own hands.

The arms that learned by watching

Moley Robotics, founded in London in 2015 by the Russian-British mathematician Mark Oleynik, built what the company still calls the world’s first fully robotic kitchen — a pair of articulated arms suspended from an overhead track, gliding above a custom cooktop, sink, and set of bespoke pans, cooking meals with no human hand anywhere in the process. As one 2026 industry survey describes it, the arms carry five-fingered hands built in collaboration with the industrial robotics firm Universal Robots and the German manipulator specialist SCHUNK, equipped with tactile sensors and refined across eleven separate development cycles and a hundred thousand test cycles before the system was considered ready to cook in a real kitchen rather than a laboratory.

The technique behind the arms is the part of this story that belongs most naturally in this series, because it is not conventional programming at all. As the company’s own engineering history records, Moley’s hands were designed by the Shadow Robot Company — a London firm that ordinarily builds dexterous manipulators for nuclear power plants and NASA, the exact high-hazard, precision-above-all pedigree this series traced through Part XIX’s disaster robots and Part XX’s surgical needle — and the cooking motions themselves were captured directly from professional chefs wearing sensor-laden gloves, their hand movements recorded stirring, seasoning, plating, then mapped onto the robot’s own joints until the machine could reproduce the exact motion, temperature, and timing of the human original.

This is not a robot that has been told the abstract concept of sautéing. It is a robot that watched a specific chef sauté a specific dish many times, and learned to move exactly as that chef’s hands moved. The company’s in-house chef, James Taylor, adapts each new recipe specifically so a robotic arm can execute it, and the library has grown, across the system’s various generations, to several thousand professionally developed dishes.

The honest price of not lifting a finger

What Moley has never hidden, to its credit, is what this costs — and the honesty is itself worth reporting, because it tells you exactly who this technology currently serves. As one reviewer’s account of a home visit to the company’s showroom put it, with a headline this series appreciates for its candour, the full two-armed installation retails at roughly $335,000 once professional installation, custom cabinetry, and the overhead track system are included — a construction project, not an appliance purchase, requiring certified technicians and, in the reviewer’s own account, several days of work before a single meal gets cooked. A scaled-down, single-armed version called the X-AiR, intended to be installable in an ordinary domestic kitchen rather than a purpose-built showroom, arrived at a comparatively modest $105,000 to $248,000 depending on configuration — still, by any reasonable measure, a price that places the machine firmly in the same early-adopter territory this series found the 1X NEO humanoid occupying in Part XVII, a demonstration of what the technology can do rather than a product aimed at the household budget most readers of this series actually have.

And the honest reviewer’s headline captured something else worth sitting with: a hundred-thousand-dollar robot arm that nobody, in the ordinary sense of household need, actually requires. This is not a criticism so much as an accurate description of where the technology currently sits on the curve this series has traced through every other domestic robot it has examined — the Roomba took two decades to go from a curiosity to fifty million homes; Moley’s robotic kitchen, twenty-two years after the company’s first prototype appeared at a German trade fair, is still closer to the curiosity end of that same curve than the ubiquity end.

What it cannot yet do

The honest limits of the system are worth stating plainly, because they map so precisely onto the boundaries this series has found in every other domestic robot. As one 2026 review of the technology explained, Moley’s kitchen includes a genuine self-cleaning cycle — the arms will wash the utensils, pans, and cooking surfaces they themselves used, using an integrated sink and dishwasher built into the unit — but the cleaning stops exactly at the boundary of what the robot touched. It will not wipe a splash of sauce that lands on the floor. It will not put away the ingredient containers a human filled before cooking began. The robot’s competence ends precisely where its own recorded motions end, and everything outside that boundary — the mess a human kitchen actually generates around the edges of a meal, rather than within the strict choreography of preparing it — remains entirely a human responsibility.

There is a candid, almost wistful note in the same review worth repeating for what it reveals about who this machine is and is not for: if you genuinely love the process of cooking — the feel of the knife, the smell of garlic hitting the pan, the creative improvisation of a good cook working without a recipe — a robot chef is not for you, because it automates precisely the part of the experience you enjoy. Moley was never built to replace a cook who wants to cook. It was built for the household that wants dinner without anyone cooking it at all, replicated exactly, every time, from a professional’s own hands.

The cheaper cousin, and what it gives up

Moley is not the only bet on robotic cooking, and the alternatives reveal exactly which trade-offs a lower price demands. A smaller company called Sereneti Kitchen has pursued a far more modest architecture: a single robotic arm working from pre-portioned ingredient pods, assembling a limited menu of dishes rather than replicating a chef’s full repertoire of free-form technique. The price falls accordingly, into a range closer to a premium kitchen appliance than a home-renovation project — but so does the ambition. A Sereneti unit cannot chop, cannot select an unfamiliar ingredient from an open refrigerator, cannot adapt to what is actually in the pantry that evening. It is closer, in spirit, to an automated vending machine for a curated set of recipes than to Moley’s attempt at a general-purpose robotic cook. The trade-off is consistent with everything this series has found elsewhere on the domestic frontier: capability and price move in the same direction, and the gap between a five-thousand-dollar humanoid’s limited chores in Part XI and a full-scale industrial arm’s true dexterity is the same gap separating Sereneti’s pod-based simplicity from Moley’s chef-motion fidelity.

There is a third layer to this story that has nothing to do with robotic arms at all, and it may turn out to matter more than either of the physical systems this postcard has described. AI recipe-generation platforms — software that proposes a dish based on whatever ingredients a person actually has on hand, their stated dietary restrictions, and their historical preferences — are advancing considerably faster than the robotic hardware meant to execute them, for the obvious reason that generating a recipe requires no dexterous manipulation at all, only the same kind of pattern-matching and generation that has driven the broader AI boom this series’ parent publication tracks closely. It is entirely plausible that the contextual-judgment problem this postcard opened with — knowing the onion has gone soft, that a guest cannot eat gluten — gets solved by software well before any robotic arm is dexterous or affordable enough to act on that judgment in an ordinary kitchen. The reasoning and the manipulation may simply arrive on two different timelines, with a human hand serving as the bridge between them for years after the thinking part is solved.

The commercial cousin already at scale

While Moley’s domestic ambition remains a luxury curiosity, a parallel branch of kitchen robotics has already reached genuine commercial scale, in the unglamorous way this series has learned to expect real adoption to arrive. As industry coverage of the sector notes, Miso Robotics’ Flippy — a robotic arm purpose-built for commercial fry stations — has moved well past demonstration, with successor units now turning out thousands of servings a month across fast-food and quick-service locations, doing the single, narrow, repetitive, hot-oil-adjacent task that most closely resembles the factory-floor automation this series traced through Part XIV’s China deployment order rather than the general-purpose home-chef ambition Moley has pursued.

The commercial kitchen, like the commercial warehouse before it, has proven a far friendlier environment for robotic cooking than the home — a single task, repeated identically thousands of times, with none of the contextual judgment a home cook’s varied, improvisational evening actually demands.

What Jane Jetson actually got right

From the balcony, the robotic kitchen postcard closes a loop this series opened in its very first dispatch about the home. Jane Jetson’s punch-card pizza machine was a fantasy of instant abstraction — press a button, food appears, no visible mechanism, no visible cost. What has actually arrived, sixty-plus years later, is the opposite kind of machine entirely: intensely, visibly mechanical, built from a specific chef’s captured motions, priced at a level that makes its abstraction available to almost nobody, and bounded so precisely by what it was taught to do that a stray splash of sauce on the floor defeats it completely.

There is a version of this story that reads as disappointment — sixty years, and dinner still does not simply appear. But there is a truer reading available, the same one this series found in the laundry-folding robots of Part VII and the five-thousand-dollar humanoid of Part XI: the technology does not arrive all at once, fully formed and instantly affordable. It arrives first as an extraordinary, expensive demonstration in a London showroom, proving the motion-capture technique actually works; then, over years or decades, the price falls, the capability broadens, and the machine that once cost as much as a house becomes, eventually, a machine that costs as much as a very good stove. Moley’s own trajectory — from a two-hundred-thousand-dollar prototype at a 2015 trade fair to a “mere” hundred-thousand-dollar single-arm unit a decade later — is exactly that curve, still early, still steep, moving in the only direction this series has ever found these curves to move.

A Canadian reads this postcard with a particular fondness for the specific absurdity at its centre — a machine that has memorized the exact hand motions of a Michelin-adapted chef, precise to the millisecond, and still cannot manage the one thing every parent teaches a child within their first few years: wipe up what you spill. It is a reminder that home is not, in the end, a sequence of choreographed tasks but a continuous, unscripted negotiation with mess, and that the gap between executing a recipe and actually running a kitchen is the same gap this series has found, again and again, between performance and competence — a marathon-winning robot that falls at an unmapped barricade, a gallbladder removed from a pig that has not yet been removed from a person, a dinner cooked to perfection beside a stain nobody told the arms to notice.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived plated, on time, and left a small stain on the floor that nobody had told it to clean.
