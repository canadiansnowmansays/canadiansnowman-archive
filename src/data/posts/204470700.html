Melat Kiros, 29 — the democratic socialist and first-time candidate who unseated a 15-term incumbent in Denver.

There is a particular kind of political defeat that ought to be impossible, and Diana DeGette just suffered it.

DeGette was not a centrist caught napping to the left of her district. She was, by any conventional measure, one of the most progressive members of the entire Colorado delegation — a fifteen-term congresswoman who backed Medicare for All, called for abolishing Immigration and Customs Enforcement, ran a television ad featuring praise from Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and served as an impeachment manager in Trump’s second Senate trial. She had the endorsements, the record, and a nearly three-decade head start. She had, in the home stretch, a last-minute flood of outside money rushing to her defense.

And on Tuesday she lost her primary by roughly five points to Melat Kiros — a twenty-nine-year-old democratic socialist, first-time candidate, and lawyer who immigrated from Ethiopia as an infant and who was, quite literally, born four months after DeGette first took the oath of office. The Associated Press called the race early Wednesday.

When a genuine progressive with a Medicare-for-All record and an AOC endorsement can lose a Democratic primary from the left, something more interesting than an ordinary upset is underway. The floor of what counts as acceptably progressive has not shifted. It has been picked up and relocated.

Not an Accident, a Pattern

The temptation with any single primary is to explain it locally — a stale incumbent, a demographic shift, a well-run insurgent campaign in a district that had quietly moved underneath its congresswoman. All of that is true of Denver, which is younger and more diverse than the district DeGette first won in 1996. But the local explanation collapses the moment you widen the frame, because DeGette is not an isolated casualty. She is, by one count, the seventh House member to lose renomination this cycle — and the third in a single week.

One week before Denver, New York City delivered the same verdict three times over: two incumbents dethroned in their primaries and a third progressive winning a key open seat, all three winners endorsed by Zohran Mamdani, whose own victory last year kick-started the whole sequence. Kiros now joins a cohort of democratic socialists heading toward Congress — Claire Valdez and Darializa Avila Chevalier in New York, Chris Rabb in Pennsylvania — with Bernie Sanders, the Democratic Socialists of America, and Justice Democrats stitched through nearly every one of their campaigns. As MS Now has reported, the movement’s ambitions now reach well beyond deep-blue cities.

This is the tell. A single insurgent victory is a story about a candidate. Seven of them, clustered in a matter of weeks and running on a shared program with shared endorsers, is a story about a party changing shape.

The Program

A shared program, shared endorsers: single-payer health care, a ban on corporate donations, and an end to U.S. military aid to Israel.

It is worth being precise about what these candidates actually share, because “socialist” has spent a century as an American insult emptied of specific content. The Kiros cohort is not vague. The program is legible: universal single-payer health care, a ban on corporate donations to campaigns, and — the throughline that has become the movement’s connective tissue — an end to American military support for Israel.

That last plank is doing more organizing work than any other, and it is the one establishment Democrats have least figured out how to answer. Kiros made her opposition to the war in Gaza central to her identity; she was fired from a Manhattan law firm in 2023 after refusing to retract a letter questioning Israel’s historical legitimacy. She has drawn sustained criticism for declining to call a fatal firebombing of pro-Israel marchers in Boulder antisemitic. DeGette, who once called herself a “strong supporter” of Israel and later opposed the sale of offensive weapons, found herself on precisely the wrong side of the issue that now animates the base she needed.

The same fault line runs straight through the general-election map. Abdul El-Sayed carries a version of this politics into a Michigan Senate race; Graham Platner carries a populist cousin of it into Maine. Francesca Hong will test in a Wisconsin governor’s race whether any of it survives contact with a genuine swing-state electorate. The movement’s ambitions, in other words, no longer stop at the borders of deep-blue cities — which is exactly what its opponents inside the party most fear.

The Money Doesn’t Work Anymore

Readers of my other running series will feel a flicker of recognition here, and it is worth naming rather than pretending these are separate stories. When Democracy Fights Back has already walked this same New York ground once — Lander, Valdez, Avila Chevalier — but from a different doorway. There, the argument was about the money: three candidates who ran explicitly against a ninety-six-million-dollar lobby and beat it, proof that dark money loses its spell the moment someone turns on the lights. That was a story about what these insurgents defeated. This is a story about what they are. The same victories that embarrassed AIPAC’s super PAC are also, viewed from the other side, the raw material of a democratic-socialist bloc assembling itself in real time — and the Kiros win in Denver is the sign the pattern has outgrown a single city.

For readers who followed that fight, the most striking feature of the Kiros victory is what failed to stop it.

The old machine still deployed the way it always has. DeGette held a fundraising advantage. Outside groups — some tied to pro-Israel PACs — rushed in with late spending. Kiros, for her part, built her entire case partly on that fact, arguing the donations left her opponent beholden to interests rather than constituents. And the money lost anyway, by ten points on the night’s early count.

We watched the same dynamic play out, at far greater expense, in the NY-12 contest that closed out The New Camelot — a primary that drew tens of millions in industry spending and still turned on which candidate voters actually recognized as one of their own. There is a lesson accumulating across these races that the party establishment has been slow to absorb: in a low-turnout primary powered by young, motivated volunteers, a late money bomb is not a firewall. Sometimes it is an accelerant, handed to the insurgent as proof of exactly the corruption she is running against.

The Establishment Isn’t Dead — It’s Learning

Sen. John Hickenlooper read the moment — moving left, consolidating labor, outspending his challenger eight to one — and survived where DeGette didn't.

None of this means the old guard is finished, and the honest version of this story requires saying so.

On the very same Colorado night, Senator John Hickenlooper — a seventy-four-year-old moderate with twenty years in state politics, precisely the profile that has been losing all year — turned back his own progressive challenger. As the New York Times reported, he did it by reading the moment correctly: moving smartly to the left on immigration and ICE, consolidating labor and activist support before his opponent could, and outspending her by something like eight to one. Toppling a sitting senator in a statewide race, it turns out, remains far harder than knocking off a House member in a compact urban district.

So the picture is not a rout. It is a party sorting itself in real time into those who see the shift coming and adapt to it, and those who assume their credentials will protect them and discover, like DeGette, that credentials are exactly what no longer protects anyone.

What a Canadian Notices

From north of the border, the thing that stands out is not the leftward drift itself — every Western democracy has its insurgent flank — but the velocity, and the fact that the American left has stopped treating its own ascent as improbable.

A year ago, a democratic socialist beating a fifteen-term progressive incumbent would have been written up as a stunner, a bolt from the blue, an aberration demanding explanation. On Tuesday it was the third such result in seven days, and Kiros herself framed it not as a surprise but as a schedule: “This is a movement, and we are just getting started.” The insurgents have stopped asking the party’s permission to exist. They are simply winning, cataloguing the wins, and treating each one as confirmation of a timetable already set.

Whether that timetable runs into a wall in November — in Michigan, in Maine, in a Wisconsin governor’s mansion where a swing electorate gets the deciding vote — is the genuinely open question, and this series will follow it there. But inside the Democratic Party, in the primaries the party runs against itself, the argument is starting to look settled. The remarkable thing about Melat Kiros is no longer that she won. It is that nobody who has been paying attention was truly surprised.
