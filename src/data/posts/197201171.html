Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays

His name is Alejandro. He weighed two pounds when US Congressmembers Pramila Jayapal and Jonathan Jackson met him in April at Havana’s Eusebio Hernández Pérez maternity hospital. He was premature, lying in one of the few functioning incubators in the building. The others had been damaged by the voltage surges that follow nationwide blackouts — surges that fry the delicate electronics in medical equipment the way they fry everything else. US sanctions make importing replacement parts for the broken incubators nearly impossible.

In the same hospital, Jayapal and Jackson watched pregnant women in their final days trudging up flights of stairs, the elevators inoperable without power. They saw doctors manually pumping ventilators to keep babies breathing during blackouts, nurses planning for a fully powerless hospital by preparing to swaddle newborns in blankets and put them back in dead incubators, hoping the infants would stay warm enough to survive. From 2018 to 2025, as US sanctions grew progressively more punitive, Cuba’s once-impressive infant mortality rate increased by 148 percent.

Jayapal and Jackson are members of the House Foreign Affairs Committee. They spent five days in Cuba in April — the same weeks that the most significant American diplomatic visit since Barack Obama touched down in Havana in 2016 was taking place. They came back and wrote, in the New York Times, what they had seen. “We returned from our trip certain,” they said, “that if the American people knew the full extent of what is happening on the ground in Cuba, they would demand an end to the blockade immediately.”

Baby Alejandro is two pounds and has no idea that he is the current endgame of sixty-seven years of American Cuba policy. He would like his incubator to work. The men deciding his country’s fate are arguing about something else entirely.

Aircraft at José Martí International Airport in Havana. On April 10, 2026, a U.S. government plane landed here for the first high-level talks since the Obama era.

The Plane

On April 10, 2026, a United States government aircraft landed at José Martí International Airport in Havana for the first time since Obama’s visit a decade ago. The visit had not been publicly announced. It was confirmed, eventually, by the State Department — and by the Cuban Foreign Ministry, which described the exchanges as “respectful and professional” and “devoid of threats.”

American assistant secretaries of state sat across from Cuban deputy foreign minister-level officials. Separately, a senior US official held a meeting with Raúl Guillermo Rodríguez Castro — El Cangrejo, the Crab — Raúl Castro's grandson and the back-channel negotiator whose role this series documented in Part 13. Rubio himself has spoken directly to Rodríguez Castro. The meetings confirmed what the series argued then: Raúlito is the real interlocutor, regardless of whatever title he does or does not hold.

What made this different from the months of back-channel communication that preceded it was the plane. A US government aircraft landing in Havana is not a routine diplomatic gesture. It is a statement — that Washington is taking the negotiations seriously enough to put officials physically in the room, in the country, at a moment when the administration is simultaneously maintaining a fuel blockade that has pushed the island to the edge of systemic collapse. The State Department's message, delivered publicly alongside the visit, was characteristically direct: "The Cuban regime should stop playing games as direct talks are occurring. They have a small window to make a deal."

One detail that did not make most of the official readouts: a US Navy MQ-4C Triton surveillance drone was flying near Havana during the meetings. The reconnaissance flights have since intensified and are part of what is expected to be a larger American military buildup around the island. Diplomacy, in the current American mode, is conducted under the eye of the surveillance aircraft. The message being sent is not subtle.

What’s On the Table

The specific proposals that emerged from the April 10 meetings are the most concrete picture of what a US-Cuba deal might actually contain — and they are revealing in what they prioritize.

On the American side: the release of political prisoners, including specifically named dissidents and artists from the San Isidro Movement. Compensation for Americans and US corporations whose assets were confiscated after the 1959 Revolution — a claim valued by some estimates at over nine billion dollars, representing one of the oldest and most intractable grievances in the entire bilateral relationship. Economic and governance reforms to attract foreign investment. A curbing of what Washington describes as foreign intelligence, military, and terrorist groups operating from Cuban territory — meaning, primarily, the Russian signals intelligence facilities and whatever Cuban relationships with Iran, China, and Hezbollah that American intelligence has catalogued.

And Starlink.

Starlink satellites being deployed — technically operational in Cuba but currently prohibited by the government.

The offer to bring Elon Musk's satellite internet service to Cuba is the most unexpected item on the American list — and potentially the most consequential. Internet access in Cuba is currently monopolized by the state telecommunications company ETECSA, whose contract runs until 2036. The Cuban government has previously described satellite internet services as a form of "digital aggression," recognizing that unrestricted internet access represents a direct threat to information control. Musk noted on his X account in March that Starlink is already technically operational in Cuba — it cannot be marketed there because the regime prohibits it. The American proposal to bring Starlink to the island would effectively break the state's monopoly on information, which is not a marginal concession. It is a structural challenge to one of the pillars of the regime's control over its population.

Cuba’s response to the Starlink proposal has not been made public. The silence is itself informative.

The $9 Billion Problem

The property compensation demand deserves particular attention because it has been a fixture of American Cuba policy since 1959 and has never been resolved — and because the mechanism Cuba has now proposed for addressing it reveals how far the negotiations have moved toward the realm of practical deal-making.

Earlier reporting indicated that Cuba had signalled willingness to pay a lump sum in compensation for the confiscations, to be distributed by Washington to roughly six thousand American claimants. Nine billion dollars is the upper bound of various estimates of the claim's value; the actual settlement figure, if negotiations reach that point, would almost certainly be lower and would involve complex questions about interest, depreciation, and the offset of American economic damage caused by the embargo itself.

But the significance is not the number. It is that this conversation is happening at all. For sixty-seven years, the property question was one of the declared non-starters of any normalization — the Cuban position being that American economic damage through the embargo far exceeded any legitimate claim for confiscated property, and the American position being that compensation was a precondition for any meaningful change in relations. That both sides are now apparently discussing mechanisms — not just principles — represents movement of a kind that the previous six decades of standoff had not produced.

Whether the movement is genuine or tactical — whether Cuba is signalling seriousness or buying time — is the question this series has been asking since Part 13. The answer remains unclear. But the conversation itself is new.

New Dawn, New Sanctions

Three weeks after the April 10 Havana talks, on May 2, 2026, Donald Trump signed an executive order imposing new sanctions targeting Cuban officials responsible for repression and threatening foreign financial institutions dealing with sanctioned sectors of the Cuban economy.

The timing is worth dwelling on. The most significant face-to-face diplomatic meeting between Washington and Havana in a decade took place on April 10. Cuba confirmed the talks as “respectful and professional” on April 21. On May 2 — eleven days later — Trump signed new sanctions escalating pressure on the island.

This is not contradictory within the Trump administration’s framework. It is the framework. Talking and squeezing simultaneously. Offering Starlink with one hand and tightening the sanctions vise with the other. The administration has been transparent about this approach since The Atlantic reported in March that officials described the Venezuela negotiations as a “dry run” and noted explicitly that talks could function as “a form of subterfuge” — a way to construct a record of Cuban intransigence that would justify more forceful action if the diplomatic track failed to produce results on Washington’s timeline.

Trump at a Turning Point USA rally in late April: "And very soon, this great strength will also bring about a day 70 years in waiting — it's called a new dawn in Cuba." The phrase "new dawn" has now appeared in multiple administration contexts. It is doing the work that "liberation" and "regime change" used to do, while committing to less — a new dawn can mean many things, from a genuine democratic transition to a cosmetic reshuffling of faces while the underlying system persists. The administration has not explicitly stated what political or economic changes it requires. Rubio has at various points suggested that broad economic reforms might suffice — and, in the next breath, declared that nothing will change as long as the current regime holds power. Both things, simultaneously, from the same official. The ambiguity is not accidental. It is the negotiating posture.

Reports emerged simultaneously that the Pentagon was ramping up military action plans against Cuba, should the diplomatic track fail. Asked about this on Air Force One, Trump said: “Well, it depends on what your definition of military action is.” He did not elaborate.

What Two Congress Members Saw

Jayapal (in red) and Jackson walking in Havana. They witnessed the human impact of the ongoing crisis firsthand.

The Jayapal-Jackson essay in the New York Times, published May 11, 2026, is worth reading in full — but its most important contribution to the public record is the eyewitness testimony from inside Cuban institutions that official delegations typically do not see.

They met political dissidents and their families. They met religious leaders and entrepreneurs and members of civil society organizations. They met families of political prisoners. "Everywhere," they wrote, "there was agreement: America's blockade must end, and a US invasion must not take place." That consensus — spanning people with very different views of the Cuban government — reflects something this series has argued throughout: the blockade's civilian damage and the threat of military intervention are not popular with the Cuban people it supposedly aims to free, even among those who oppose the government.

They also documented something the negotiations have not yet addressed: the economic reforms Washington has been demanding are already partially underway. Cuban American entrepreneurs are being allowed to invest in private businesses. Small and medium-size enterprises now account for large parts of the economy and workforce. The Obama-style economic opening that Rubio spent years denouncing as naive is happening — not because Washington forced it, but because the Cuban government has been implementing it under economic duress.

The question is whether Washington will recognize these reforms as sufficient, or whether the political demands — leadership change, free elections, curbing of Russian intelligence operations — will remain as preconditions that Cuba cannot meet without the government effectively negotiating its own dissolution. That is still the core deadlock. Cuba will reform. Cuba will not reform itself out of existence. Washington wants both.

Where Things Stand

Cuba’s economy has contracted 23 percent since 2019. Analysts project a further GDP decline of 7.2 percent for 2026. On May 14, the crisis crossed a threshold: Cuba’s energy minister, Vicente de la O Levy, announced that the country had run out of oil entirely. “We have absolutely no fuel oil, absolutely no diesel,” he said. “In Havana, the blackouts today exceed 20 or 22 hours.” The single Russian tanker — the Kolodkin’s delivery that this series noted as the only significant fuel arrival in four months — has been exhausted. Jorge Piñón, the Cuban energy specialist at the University of Texas, confirmed that the supply chain is now empty. When electricity returns, it lasts ninety minutes. Cubans are sleeping on rooftops to escape the heat, cooking with charcoal, banging pots and pans in the streets of Havana. The energy minister warned that the crisis will deepen as summer temperatures rise and demand on the grid increases — and that even solar power is no substitute, because the grid is too fragile to handle the current from the panel parks Cuba has built.

Then, on Thursday, the Cuban government announced that CIA Director John Ratcliffe had visited the island — the highest-ranking Trump administration official to do so since the pressure campaign began. He met with Cuba’s interior minister — not the foreign minister, the interior minister, the official responsible for internal security and intelligence. The Cuban government said the meeting addressed “the current situation” between the two countries, and that Cuban officials had used it to make the case that their country does not constitute a threat to American national security. The April 10 talks sent assistant secretaries of state. Now Washington has sent the head of its intelligence agency to sit across from the head of Cuba’s internal security apparatus. The escalation in both seniority and subject matter is unmistakable. Whatever is being discussed now is being discussed at the level where decisions get made.

The Polymarket probability of a formal US-Cuba deal by June 30 sits well below 25 percent. Mexico, Spain, and Brazil’s leaders have urged “sincere and respectful dialogue.” Germany’s chancellor has said there is no evident justification for a US attack.

Inside Cuba, the gestures continue. The 2,010 prisoner release in April. The FBI investigation into the February boat shooting. The Díaz-Canel government announcing reforms while also warning, publicly, that Cuba is “prepared to fight” if the United States moves to military action. Both things simultaneously — negotiate and prepare to resist — because the government has no other viable option.

The negotiations are in what Díaz-Canel called, in March, their “first phase.” The agenda is still being established. No deal has been announced. No breakdown has been declared. The Triton drone circles. The Starlink offer sits on the table, unanswered. Rubio, asked in March about the role of the oil blockade, dismissed it: “The reason why they don’t have oil is because they don’t have any money to pay for oil.” On Wednesday — the night before Ratcliffe landed in Havana, the same night the energy minister announced Cuba had no fuel left — Rubio told Fox News that he doubted it was possible “to change the trajectory of Cuba as long as these people are in charge in that regime.” He added: “I hope I’m wrong. We’ll give them a chance. But I don’t think it’s going to happen.” That is the Secretary of State publicly pre-announcing the failure of his own diplomatic track, the night before the CIA director arrives to continue it. As of tonight, Cuba has no oil at all — and the man who said that is not the one sleeping on a rooftop in Havana.

Ricardo Zúniga, who negotiated Cuba policy for Obama and has followed these talks closely, offered the assessment that matters most: the Castros are shrewd negotiators who have extracted themselves from tough spots before. The Obama talks took eighteen months. This administration is less patient, more prone to escalation, and has demonstrated in Venezuela and Iran that it will move from negotiation to action when it decides the time is right.

“They heard that Cuba’s turn is next,” one official told a reporter after the April 10 talks. The message was delivered without threats, the Cubans said. Perhaps. But it was delivered under a surveillance drone, by a delegation from an administration that has now conducted two regime changes in the hemisphere in four months, while simultaneously preparing indictments of Cuban leaders in South Florida and signing new sanctions packages every few weeks. And now that delegation has been followed by the director of the Central Intelligence Agency.

The island that has refused to kneel for five hundred and thirty years is still refusing. It is doing so in the dark — twenty-two hours of darkness per day now, in a capital city of two million people — with a CIA director on its soil and no oil in its reserves and a two-pound baby in a broken incubator down the road, waiting for someone to find a way to fix what has been broken. Not just the incubator. Not just the grid. Everything that led to both of them failing at once.

That has always been the question. As of tonight, it is more urgent than it has ever been.

Postscript — May 15, 2026

Soon after this was published, the New York Times reported that the Ratcliffe visit went further than the Cuban government’s initial announcement indicated. Ratcliffe met not only with the interior minister but directly with Raúl Guillermo Rodríguez Castro — the Crab — and delivered a specific demand: shut down the Russian and Chinese intelligence listening posts on the island and take steps to open the economy. It is the first time the administration has publicly articulated concrete terms.

Then came the second development. Federal prosecutors in Miami are working on an indictment of Raúl Castro. He is ninety-four years old, frail, with poor hearing and difficulty speaking. The possible charges include drug trafficking and the 1996 Brothers to the Rescue shootdown — the episode that produced the Helms-Burton Act and has shaped American Cuba policy for thirty years.

The Venezuela playbook is now explicitly on the table. Senior officials familiar with the administration’s deliberations told the Times that they want the option of running the Maduro seizure operation again — the same federal indictment-to-military raid sequence that removed Venezuela’s leader in January. A large-scale operation is probably not imminent; American Special Operations Forces remain deployed in the Middle East. But people briefed on the administration’s thinking say senior officials want the option available.

Frank Mora, the former ambassador to the Organization of American States and now a professor at Florida International University, called the Ratcliffe visit an ultimatum: “The president is frustrated that he is not getting the results he wanted, or maybe he was promised, in Cuba. They are tightening the screws.” The indictment, he said, is a “psychological operation” — aimed as much at the Cuban American community in Miami as at Havana.

William LeoGrande, of American University, offered the assessment that this series has been making for seventeen parts: “The Cubans are not good at backing down.”

The optics of sending an elite military team to seize a ninety-four-year-old man who can barely speak are, as the Times noted, “likely to be poor.” That may not matter to the White House. What matters is whether it matters to Havana — and whether the men negotiating Cuba’s future understand that the country they are trying to break has been refusing to break since before the United States existed.

The incubator is still running. The lights are still off. The story is not over.
