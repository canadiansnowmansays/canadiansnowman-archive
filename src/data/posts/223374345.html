There are worse places to be arrested than the Baur au Lac. The hotel has stood at the head of Lake Zurich since the 1840s, set in its own park, discreet in the Swiss manner. That was its appeal to the men who ran world football, who lodged there during FIFA’s annual gatherings, a short drive from headquarters and a long way from anyone who might ask questions.

On the morning of May 27, 2015, two days before FIFA’s Congress was to re-elect Sepp Blatter to a fifth term, someone asked questions. Swiss police officers in plain clothes arrived without warning and went upstairs to the officials’ rooms. As Le News reported that day, the Swiss Federal Office of Justice confirmed the arrests had been made at the request of United States authorities in New York, and that extradition procedures would follow. The Federal Office of Justice initially spoke of six arrests; by the Justice Department’s count, seven officials were taken into custody in Zurich that morning.

The Brooklyn indictment

Hours later, in Brooklyn, the American case was unsealed. A Justice Department press release of May 27, 2015 described a 47-count indictment filed in federal court in Brooklyn against 14 defendants. The charges were racketeering conspiracy, wire fraud conspiracy and money laundering conspiracy, among others, and covered a scheme prosecutors said had run for 24 years. Officials, the government alleged, had solicited and received well over $150 million in bribes and kickbacks from sports marketing executives in exchange for media and marketing rights to tournaments in the Americas.

The defendants included Jeffrey Webb, then a FIFA vice-president and head of CONCACAF, the confederation for North and Central America and the Caribbean; his predecessor Jack Warner; Eugenio Figueredo, another FIFA vice-president; José Maria Marin, the former head of Brazil’s football federation; Nicolás Leoz, long-time president of South America’s CONMEBOL; and the Argentine marketing executives Hugo and Mariano Jinkis of Full Play. All were presumed innocent, as the Justice Department noted.

Attorney General Loretta Lynch, who had built the case as the US Attorney in Brooklyn before her promotion, described corruption she called “rampant, systemic, and deep-rooted both abroad and here in the United States.”

Why Brooklyn? Because, on the American theory, bribes negotiated abroad had moved through American banks or concerned American markets, which engaged US wire fraud and money laundering statutes and the racketeering law built for the Mafia. Whether “honest services” wire fraud could be stretched to cover commercial bribery of foreign sports officials was a question the defendants raised from the start. It would come back, a decade later, to haunt the last prosecution standing.

The man with the microphone

The case began with Chuck Blazer, the American who had served as CONCACAF’s general secretary and sat on FIFA’s executive committee for years. The same press release disclosed that Blazer had pleaded guilty on November 25, 2013 to a ten-count information covering racketeering conspiracy, wire fraud conspiracy, money laundering conspiracy, income tax evasion and failing to report a foreign bank account. He forfeited more than $1.9 million at the time of his plea.

What made Blazer valuable was not his plea but what preceded and followed it. As Al Jazeera reported on July 13, 2017, he had secretly agreed to cooperate with investigators and carried recording devices into meetings with fellow officials. FIFA banned him for life in 2015. He died in July 2017, aged 72.

Stephen Leacock would have enjoyed the irony. The man who exposed the system had profited from it for years. Football’s great reckoning arrived not as a moral awakening but as a plea bargain.

Jack Warner and the eleven-year fight

Jack Warner, the Trinidadian who ran CONCACAF before Webb and served as a FIFA vice-president, was not in Zurich that morning. He was at home in Trinidad and Tobago, where a provisional warrant was issued on May 27, 2015. He surrendered the same day and was granted bail the next. Trinidad’s then Attorney General issued an Authority to Proceed on September 21, 2015.

Warner’s judicial review was dismissed by Trinidad’s High Court in 2017 and by the Court of Appeal in 2019. On November 17, 2022, the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London dismissed his further appeal, and the extradition hearing was free to resume.

It did not end there. On July 17, 2026, as The Gleaner reported that day, citing the Caribbean Media Corporation, Justice Karen Reid of the Trinidad and Tobago High Court permanently stayed the extradition proceedings. In a 71-page judgment, she found that the State had allowed the courts to proceed as though a special arrangement with the United States guaranteed Warner “specialty” protection, the safeguard that a person can be tried only for the offences for which they were surrendered, when no such arrangement existed. That, she held, breached his constitutional right to liberty. As Ryan Bachoo reported for Guardian Media on July 18, 2026, she called the State’s conduct serious and an abuse of the court’s process, while rejecting Warner’s claim of a deliberate conspiracy against him from the outset. The Attorney General was ordered to pay his costs, and Anna Ramdass of the Trinidad Express noted on July 18, 2026 that damages were to be assessed on September 30, 2026, unless the parties agreed a figure first.

Warner, now in his eighties, still faces 29 US charges alleging fraud, racketeering and illegal wire transfers between 1990 and 2011. He has always denied wrongdoing, and he has never been tried, let alone convicted, on any of them. He remains in Trinidad, and I have found no public report that the State has appealed Justice Reid’s ruling or of the sum awarded in damages.

Blatter wins, then leaves

FIFA’s Congress went ahead as scheduled, which tells you a good deal about FIFA. On May 29, 2015, two days after the raid, Blatter took 133 votes in the first round to 73 for Prince Ali bin al-Hussein of Jordan. That was short of the two-thirds needed for an outright win, but Prince Ali withdrew before the second round. As CNBC reported that day, the victor offered a philosophy of oversight: “We, or I, cannot monitor everyone all of the time.”

He did not have to monitor them much longer. On June 2, 2015, four days after his re-election, Blatter announced he would lay down his mandate and called an extraordinary congress to elect a successor. Blatter himself was never charged in the American case, and he has consistently denied wrongdoing.

The Swiss track

The Americans were not alone. On the same morning as the hotel arrests, the Office of the Attorney General of Switzerland announced that it had opened criminal proceedings against persons unknown on suspicion of criminal mismanagement and money laundering in connection with the awarding of the 2018 and 2022 World Cups, to Russia and Qatar. Investigators seized documents at FIFA’s headquarters.

Eight years later, the reckoning was, to put it kindly, modest. Federico Franchini, writing for swissinfo.ch on June 13, 2023, counted 25 criminal investigations opened, three convictions by summary penalty order, at least eight investigations dropped, and a case against German officials over the 2006 World Cup that collapsed in 2020 when the statute of limitations ran out. A probe into Russia’s hosting contracts was closed in August 2022; Qatar was described as not having cooperated with Swiss requests for legal assistance.

The one Swiss trial producing a lasting conviction concerned broadcast rights, not World Cup votes. On December 19, 2025, as the Associated Press report carried by Africanews set out, Switzerland’s Federal Supreme Court upheld the conviction of FIFA’s former secretary general Jérôme Valcke for bribery and forgery over World Cup television rights deals in Italy and Greece, with a suspended sentence of 11 months. The same ruling upheld his acquittal on criminal mismanagement and the acquittal of Paris Saint-Germain president Nasser al-Khelaifi, his third in five years, on all charges.

The December sequel

Lynch had warned the defendants’ colleagues that patience would not save them. On December 3, 2015, in remarks published by the Justice Department, she announced a 92-count superseding indictment charging 16 additional defendants, all current or former football officials, and said eight more had agreed to plead guilty. “You will not wait us out,” she said. “You will not escape our focus.”

The setting that morning was familiar. Two more FIFA vice-presidents, Alfredo Hawit of Honduras, who had replaced Webb at CONCACAF, and Juan Ángel Napout of Paraguay, who had become CONMEBOL president, were arrested at the Baur au Lac. A dawn visit from the police at the same Zurich hotel was, it seemed, becoming a FIFA tradition.

The ethics committee acts

FIFA’s own machinery stirred in the autumn. On October 8, 2015, its ethics committee provisionally suspended Blatter, UEFA president Michel Platini and Valcke for 90 days. On December 21, 2015, as the PBS NewsHour reported that day, the committee banned Blatter and Platini for eight years over a payment of about two million Swiss francs that Blatter had approved to Platini in 2011 for work said to have been done a decade earlier. The committee found a conflict of interest. Blatter promised to fight; Platini called it a masquerade.

FIFA’s appeal committee cut both bans to six years in February 2016, and the Court of Arbitration for Sport reduced Platini’s to four in May 2016 while upholding Blatter’s in December 2016. These were sporting sanctions under FIFA’s ethics code, not criminal findings.

The criminal case over the same payment went very differently. Switzerland’s Federal Criminal Court acquitted both men of fraud, disloyal management, misappropriation and forgery on July 8, 2022. An appeals chamber of the same court acquitted them again on March 25, 2025, and in August 2025 the Swiss prosecutor announced it would not appeal further. The acquittals are final. No Swiss court has found that either Blatter or Platini committed a crime.

The Brooklyn verdicts

Most of the American defendants pleaded guilty. Three chose to fight. After a six-week trial before US District Judge Pamela K. Chen, a Brooklyn jury on December 22, 2017 convicted Napout of racketeering conspiracy and two counts of wire fraud conspiracy, and Marin of racketeering conspiracy, three counts of wire fraud conspiracy and two counts of money laundering conspiracy. On December 26, 2017, it acquitted Manuel Burga, former head of Peru’s federation, of racketeering conspiracy, the only charge on which he had been extradited. The US Attorney’s Office for the Eastern District of New York set out those verdicts on December 26, 2017.

Judge Chen sentenced Marin to four years on August 22, 2018, and Napout to nine years on August 29, 2018. Neither served the full term. As Law360 reported on March 30, 2020, Chen granted Marin, then 87, compassionate release because of his age, his health and the pandemic, with the prosecutors’ agreement. In June 2023, Chen granted Napout compassionate release as well; Inside World Football reported on July 3, 2023 that he would go home to Paraguay after about five and a half years in prison.

The case that vanished

The last trial ought to trouble anyone who believes the 2015 raid settled anything. In March 2023, a Brooklyn jury convicted Hernán López, a former head of Fox International Channels, and the Argentine marketing company Full Play Group of charges including honest services wire fraud conspiracy and money laundering conspiracy, over bribes prosecutors said were paid for South American broadcast rights. A third defendant, former Fox executive Carlos Martínez, was acquitted by the same jury. The Twenty-First Century Fox company itself was not accused of wrongdoing.

On September 1, 2023, Judge Chen set the convictions aside and acquitted López and Full Play. Relying in part on the Supreme Court’s decision earlier that year in Percoco v. United States, she found no precedent for applying the honest services statute to commercial bribery of foreign sports officials.

The government appealed and won. On July 2, 2025, as Daniel Seiden reported for Bloomberg Law, the Second Circuit held that such bribery schemes fall within the statute, vacated Chen’s judgments and sent the case back.

Then the government changed its mind. In December 2025, the Solicitor General told the Supreme Court that the United States would no longer defend the convictions, and the Brooklyn US Attorney, Joseph Nocella Jr., told Judge Chen he would seek dismissal “in the interests of justice.” On January 12, 2026, in López v. United States, the Supreme Court vacated the Second Circuit’s decision and sent the case back. And on May 27, 2026, the eleventh anniversary of the Baur au Lac raid, Judge Chen granted the dismissal. As Larry Neumeister of the Associated Press reported that day, Nocella told the court the case did not fit the administration’s priorities, which he listed as terrorism, national security, narcotics, human trafficking and violent gangs. Kara Scannell’s report for CNN put it more bluntly in its headline: bribery, the Justice Department had said, was not a priority.

López said he was relieved that a case that should never have started was over. Neither he nor Full Play stands convicted of anything. Nocella said the decision should not extend to anyone else convicted in the FIFA case, and FIFA told the court that the government had turned over $201 million to FIFA and other bodies for community football projects.

The others noticed all the same. As Patricia Hurtado reported for Bloomberg News on May 27, 2026, Marin, Napout and two men who had pleaded guilty, Hawit and Costa Rica’s Eduardo Li, were already asking to have their own convictions wiped away. In August 2026, as the Costa Rican daily La Teja reported on August 13, the Justice Department opposed those petitions, filed by Li and four others. I have found no report that Judge Chen has ruled on them.

What the raid was worth

Here is the scorecard. Numerous guilty pleas in Brooklyn, several of them now the subject of requests to undo them. Two trial convictions, both men released early. One jury acquittal, and one jury verdict set aside, reinstated, then dissolved when the government walked away. A Swiss investigation of 25 cases with three summary convictions and one trial conviction upheld by the country’s highest court. Blatter and Platini, the two men the scandal drove from office, twice acquitted of every criminal charge. Jack Warner, the case’s most colourful defendant, permanently beyond its reach.

In my view, the raid at the Baur au Lac did one thing superbly: it ended Sepp Blatter’s reign and cleared the board for a new president. What it did not do, and was perhaps never designed to do, was change the incentives of the institution itself. It removed a cohort of men, many of whom admitted their guilt. It did not remove the conditions that made them rich.

That distinction mattered enormously in February 2016, when FIFA chose the man who would promise to finish the job. Part IV asks what he did with the chance.
