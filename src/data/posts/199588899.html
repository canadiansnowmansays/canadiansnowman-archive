Canada calls itself a mosaic. It is a polite metaphor — the idea that different cultures retain their distinct identities within a unified national frame, as opposed to the American melting pot that supposedly dissolves differences into a common alloy. Canadians are proud of this metaphor. We put it in our civics textbooks and repeat it at citizenship ceremonies.

India is the country that actually is one.

This essay steps back from the chronological narrative to examine what makes India’s diversity not merely remarkable but structurally unprecedented among the world’s democracies. I do this because every political argument about India — about Partition, about the Emergency, about Modi, about Kashmir — is ultimately an argument about whether this diversity can be governed democratically, and if so, how. You cannot understand the politics without understanding the mosaic.

And you cannot understand the mosaic from a textbook. You can understand it from a kitchen.

I. Language

India has no national language. This is a fact that surprises most people outside the subcontinent. Hindi, written in the Devanagari script, is the official language of the central government — but the Constitution lists 22 “scheduled languages,” each with its own literary tradition, each spoken as a mother tongue by millions. The actual number of languages spoken in India, depending on how one draws the line between a language and a dialect, exceeds 19,000.

The political significance of this becomes clear the moment you try to impose uniformity. In 1965, the Indian government attempted to make Hindi the sole official language, as the Constitution had originally envisioned after a fifteen-year transition period. Southern India erupted. Tamil Nadu — where Tamil, a Dravidian language unrelated to Hindi and possessing a literary tradition older than most European languages — led massive protests. Students burned themselves alive. The government backed down and amended the Official Languages Act to continue English as an associate official language indefinitely.

This is the pattern. India’s diversity is not decorative. It is structural. It limits the power of the central government not through constitutional checks alone but through the sheer impossibility of imposing a single framework on a population that speaks in twenty-two official languages and thinks in nineteen thousand.

In my kitchen, this plays out at a human scale. Rikesh speaks Gujarati. Gurneet speaks Punjabi. They communicate in Hindi — a language neither of them learned at home but that both acquired as the lingua franca of a country that needed one. When they speak to me, they switch to English — the colonial language that became the professional passport. When Ugyen joins the conversation, he adds Dzongkha to the rotation and speaks Hindi with an accent that Rikesh and Gurneet find charming. Five languages, four men, and a set of code-switching protocols that none of them consciously learned but all of them execute fluently.

This is India. Not the country on the map. The country in the conversation.

II. Caste

Caste is not a class system. Class systems are economic — you can, in theory, move between them. Caste is birth. You are born into it. You marry within it. You eat with it. You die in it. And although the Indian Constitution abolished untouchability in 1950, and although affirmative action policies have created a significant Dalit middle class, and although urban India increasingly operates on meritocratic principles that soften caste boundaries, the system remains the most persistent social architecture in Indian life.

The numbers tell part of the story. The Scheduled Castes — the administrative term for Dalits — constitute approximately 16.6 percent of India’s population, roughly 200 million people. The Scheduled Tribes — indigenous and tribal communities, many of them geographically isolated and historically marginalized — constitute another 8.6 percent. The Other Backward Classes — a vast administrative category encompassing thousands of castes considered socially and economically disadvantaged — account for an estimated 41 percent. Together, these groups constitute a majority of India’s population, and the constitutional reservation system ensures that they are represented in government employment, educational institutions, and legislative bodies.

The politics of reservation are fiercely contested. Upper-caste Indians often resent what they see as reverse discrimination. Lower-caste Indians see reservation as the minimum necessary correction for millennia of exclusion. The Mandal Commission report of 1980, which recommended extending reservations to the Other Backward Classes, triggered nationwide protests when implemented in 1990 — upper-caste students burned themselves alive in opposition. The politics of who gets reserved status and how much reservation is enough has consumed more political energy in independent India than arguably any other domestic issue.

What the West misses about caste is its granularity. It is not a four-tier system — Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra — as the textbooks describe. It is a system of thousands of jatis (sub-castes), each with its own hierarchy, its own endogamy rules, its own dietary codes, its own social status relative to the jatis around it. Two Brahmins from different regions may not intermarry. Two Shudras from the same village may occupy different rungs on a local hierarchy invisible to outsiders. The system is fractal — zoom in and it becomes more complex, not less.

Ambedkar understood this. He did not merely want to abolish untouchability. He wanted to annihilate caste itself — to destroy the system that assigned human worth on the basis of birth. He failed. The system bent but did not break. India has a Dalit president. India has Dalit millionaires. India also has villages where Dalits cannot draw water from the same well as upper-caste Hindus, where Dalit grooms riding horses to their weddings are attacked by mobs who consider the display of dignity an act of insubordination.

Both things are true. India contains them both.

III. Religion

India is approximately 80 percent Hindu, 14 percent Muslim, 2.3 percent Christian, 1.7 percent Sikh, and less than 1 percent each Buddhist, Jain, and other faiths. These numbers, while accurate, are misleading in every direction.

Hinduism is not a religion in the way Christianity or Islam is a religion. It has no founder, no single scripture, no central authority, no uniform creed. It is a family of traditions, practices, philosophies, and devotional movements that span the spectrum from rigorous philosophical monism to exuberant popular polytheism to village folk practices that a Brahmin priest in Varanasi would not recognize as Hindu. The category “Hindu” is itself partly a colonial invention — the British needed a box for the census, and “Hindu” became the default label for everyone who wasn’t Muslim, Christian, Sikh, or Parsi. The internal diversity of Hinduism is so vast that scholars debate whether it constitutes a single religion at all.

Islam in India is equally complex. Indian Muslims are Sunni and Shia, Sufi and Salafi, Deobandi and Barelvi. They speak Urdu, Malayalam, Bengali, Tamil, Kashmiri, and dozens of other languages. The Muslim experience in Kerala — where Islam arrived through Arab traders and coexists with a largely tolerant Hindu and Christian majority — is entirely different from the Muslim experience in Uttar Pradesh, where communal tensions are a fixture of political life. The Muslim experience in Kashmir — where Muslims are the majority but live under what amounts to military occupation — is different from both.

The Sikh community — roughly 20 million people, concentrated in Punjab but dispersed globally through one of the world’s great diasporas — occupies a unique position. Sikhism is a distinct religion, founded in the fifteenth century by Guru Nanak, with its own scripture, its own houses of worship, its own code of conduct, and its own traumatic political history within independent India. The attack on the Golden Temple in 1984, the assassination of Indira Gandhi by her Sikh bodyguards, the anti-Sikh pogroms that followed in Delhi — these events are not distant history. They are living wounds. The Khalistan movement — the demand for an independent Sikh homeland — has been suppressed within India but persists in the diaspora, including in Canada, where it has become a source of significant diplomatic tension.

Gurneet’s faith is not incidental to his identity. It is his identity. The turban, the uncut hair, the steel bracelet — these are not cultural accessories. They are articles of faith, worn by a man who lives in my house in Toronto and who carries, in his daily appearance, the visible markers of a community that has been alternatively celebrated and persecuted within the Indian state.

IV. Region

The difference between Indian states is not the difference between, say, Ontario and Quebec — though that comparison is closer than most. It is the difference between nations that happen to share a constitution.

Kerala, in the southwest, has a literacy rate of 94 percent, a life expectancy comparable to developed nations, and a communist government that has been alternating in power with the Congress Party since 1957. It is also majority Hindu but with a large Muslim and Christian minority, and its political culture is defined by social reform movements that predate independence.

Bihar, in the northeast, has a literacy rate below 65 percent, some of the worst poverty indicators in Asia, and a political history defined by caste-based mobilization, corruption, and feudal power structures that the constitutional order has barely dented.

Tamil Nadu operates in Tamil, maintains a fierce cultural and linguistic independence from the Hindi-speaking north, has produced a film industry that rivals Bollywood, and has generated political dynasties — the DMK and AIADMK — rooted in Dravidian identity politics that explicitly reject the Brahminical Hinduism of the northern heartland.

Gujarat — Rikesh’s home state — is Modi’s home state. It is the laboratory of Hindutva governance, the site of the 2002 anti-Muslim riots that remain the most contested event in Modi’s political biography, and also one of India’s most economically dynamic states, with a business culture that has produced some of the country’s most successful entrepreneurs. Gujaratis have a reputation within India as traders, businessmen, and migrants — the community’s global diaspora stretches from East Africa to Leicester to the corner stores of Toronto.

Punjab — Gurneet’s home state — is the Sikh heartland, the breadbasket of India, the state that was partitioned in 1947, that lost half its territory and its most sacred site, that bore the brunt of Partition violence, that suffered Operation Blue Star and the subsequent insurgency, and that now faces an agricultural crisis and a drug epidemic that are driving its young people abroad.

These are not provinces within a uniform nation. They are distinct civilizational units that have chosen — or been incorporated into — a shared political framework. The framework holds because the constitution allows enormous regional autonomy, because the federal system distributes power, and because Indian politicians have learned, through decades of trial and catastrophic error, that the centre cannot hold if it squeezes too tight.

V. The Binding Agents

If India is this diverse — if the differences of language, caste, religion, and region are this profound — what holds it together?

The constitution, obviously. Ambedkar’s architecture creates a legal framework within which diversity can coexist. But constitutions are words on paper. Something else is needed.

Cricket is one answer. The Indian Premier League is the most valuable cricket league in the world, and its teams cut across every regional, linguistic, and religious divide. When India plays Pakistan, the country experiences a unity that no political speech has ever achieved.

Bollywood is another. Hindi cinema is the one cultural product that is consumed across the entire country and across the diaspora. A Bollywood film can feature a Punjabi hero, a Tamil heroine, a Muslim villain (or, increasingly and controversially, a Muslim hero), and songs in three languages, and the audience in a single theatre in Mumbai will contain representatives of castes, religions, and regions that do not otherwise interact.

The railways, for all their colonial origins, continue to be the physical spine of Indian unity. A train journey from Delhi to Chennai crosses linguistic, culinary, and cultural boundaries every few hundred kilometres. The Indian middle class was built, in part, on the railway examination system and the transferable government jobs that sent Tamil-speaking bureaucrats to Punjabi-speaking states and vice versa.

And there is the democracy itself. The simple act of voting — of standing in a queue with your neighbours, regardless of caste or creed, and exercising the franchise — is a ritual of belonging. India’s Election Commission is one of the most competent institutions in the country, and it conducts elections of a scale and complexity that no other democracy has ever attempted. The 2024 general election involved nearly a billion eligible voters. Electronic voting machines were transported by elephant to remote Himalayan villages. The process took six weeks. It worked.

The binding agents are imperfect. Cricket excludes those who don’t watch it. Bollywood reinforces stereotypes as often as it dissolves them. The railways are overcrowded and underfunded. The elections can be — and have been — manipulated by communal appeals that divide as much as the act of voting unites. But together, they constitute a national life that is real, if fragile. India holds together not because its people are the same but because they have developed, over seventy-nine years of democratic practice, the habits and institutions that allow difference to coexist.

Whether those habits and institutions can survive the current political moment — whether the mosaic can endure a government that wants to replace it with a monochrome — is the question that haunts the remainder of this series.
