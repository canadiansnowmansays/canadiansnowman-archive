by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. Readers of this series know that by now, and know that every so often the count lands on a number that was already a year before I got to it, and I stop whatever I was doing and write about it.

This is essay 1,776, and you have seen it coming for eighteen hundred numbers, and so have I. Let me declare the register at the top, as this series does: this is one of the serious ones, though not one of the grave ones — a distinction the year itself insists on, because 1776 is simultaneously the most inflated number in the modern world and one of the few that deserves its inflation. The loudest consequences of this year get argued elsewhere, endlessly, and this essay is not that argument. This essay asks only what the Numbers Game always asks: what kind of counting happened here, and does the arithmetic hold?

Begin, because this series must, with the dates, which are wrong in the most instructive way. The thing everyone celebrates on July 4 — independence — was actually voted on July 2, 1776, when the Continental Congress adopted the Lee Resolution severing the colonies from the Crown. John Adams, who knew exactly what had happened, wrote to Abigail in famous certainty that the second of July would be commemorated by succeeding generations as the great anniversary festival, with pomp, parade, guns, bells, and illuminations from one end of the continent to the other. He was precisely right about the festival and off by two days on the date, because what July 4 marks is the adoption of the announcement — the text, the Declaration — and the signing came later still; most delegates put their names to the parchment on August 2. So the most celebrated date on the American calendar commemorates neither the decision nor the signatures but the publication, and I want to salute the accidental honesty of that. A country founded by press release, celebrating the press release, is a country that understood from the first hour what this series took fifteen hundred essays to establish: the count that is not posted is not a count. Adams predicted the right festival for the wrong reason. The people, without consulting him, chose the day the number went public.

Now the document, and here is where essay 1,776 earns its seat in this series rather than in everyone else’s, because the Declaration of Independence is not, structurally, a cry of the heart. It is a proof. Jefferson — a man with Euclid on his shelf and geometry in his prose rhythms — built it exactly as the mathematicians of essays 1,543 and 1,687 built their books. It opens with axioms, and says so: we hold these truths to be self-evident, which is the eighteenth century’s plain term of art for the postulates you do not argue but from which all argument proceeds — that all men are created equal, endowed with unalienable rights, that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. It then announces its evidentiary standard in a single clause that could serve as this series’ masthead: let facts be submitted to a candid world. And then it submits them: the grievances against the King, twenty-seven of them by the standard count, itemized, He has after He has after He has, a numbered indictment in everything but the numerals. Readers of essay 1,517 will recognize the form at a glance, because it is the form: the itemized complaint, nailed to the world’s door, each count answerable on its own line, daring the institution to dispute the items rather than demand the list back. Luther posted ninety-five theses against an arrangement of salvation; the Congress posted twenty-seven against an arrangement of sovereignty; and both understood that the list format is itself the argument — it says we have done the books line by line, and here is the audit, in public, over our names. The Declaration is the 95 Theses of political arithmetic, and it worked for the same reason: you can hang an angry man, but item fourteen remains on the table.

And I must, as a Canadian, direct your attention to one of the twenty-seven items, because my country is in the indictment, and almost nobody on either side of the border remembers it. Among the grievances stands the complaint that the King had abolished the free system of English laws in a neighbouring province, establishing therein an arbitrary government — the neighbouring province being Quebec, and the offence being the Quebec Act of 1774, which readers of essay 1,759 met two entries ago as the instrument by which the conquered Canadiens kept their church, their civil law, and their language. Sit with the parallax. The arrangement that Canada regards as its founding accommodation — the first entry in the long ledger of protected subsidiaries that became my country — appears in the American founding document as an act of tyranny. The same clause, read from Philadelphia, was despotism; read from the St. Lawrence, it was survival. I know of no cleaner demonstration in eighteen centuries of entries that a number’s meaning depends on which side of the ledger you keep — and it explains, better than any battle, why the invitation the Congress extended to Canada went unanswered, and why there is a border for me to write from. The Americans indicted the very accommodation that made Canada possible. We declined, politely, to co-sign the indictment of ourselves, and have been declining, politely, ever since. The current management to the south has lately renewed the invitation in cruder terms; the answer has not changed; some accounts stay open for two hundred and fifty years.

Now the entry this essay cannot avoid and will not soften, because this series does not round men. The proof had a suppressed figure. In 1776, the thirteen colonies held roughly half a million human beings in slavery — about one person in five of the population whose equality the axiom declared self-evident. Jefferson’s own draft had contained a furious passage on the slave trade — blaming it, with breathtaking convenience, on the King — and the Congress cut it, along with a quarter of his text, to hold the southern delegations, and the man who wrote all men are created equal held, at that hour and until his death, hundreds of the men in question as property. There is no arithmetic that reconciles this and I will not pretend to any. What I will say is what the Numbers Game is uniquely built to say: the axiom was published anyway. And a published axiom, unlike a private conviction, is a standing liability on the books of everyone who claims the document — checkable, citable, undeletable, accruing. The suppressed figure did not disappear; suppressed figures never do, as this series has shown from the Bills of Mortality to the fire of 1666; it sat in the ledger as the discrepancy that every honest audit of the republic would strike, generation after generation.

And the audits came, and they used the document against its authors, which is precisely what a posted proof permits. Eighty-seven years on, at the dedication of a cemetery, a president opened with an act of explicit arithmetic — four score and seven years ago — and I ask you to notice what Lincoln was doing with that count: he was dating the nation not from its Constitution, the working charter full of compromises with the suppressed figure, but from the Declaration, the proof with the axiom — re-founding the country, in two hundred and seventy-two words, on the entry that shamed it. And a century after that, on the steps of a memorial to that president, a preacher from Atlanta reached for the most Numbers Game metaphor in American oratory: the architects of the republic, he said, had signed a promissory note to which every American was to fall heir — a note guaranteeing the unalienable rights — and America had defaulted on it insofar as her citizens of colour were concerned; and his movement had come to the capital, he said, to cash the cheque. A promissory note. A default. A cheque presented for payment. Dr. King read the Declaration exactly as this series reads everything, as an instrument — and the deepest fact about the American founding is that the instrument supports the reading. You cannot cash a cry of the heart. You can cash a proof with your name in the axiom. That is the difference between 1776 and every merely glorious revolution in the files: the document was drafted, perhaps half-knowingly, as a debt the future could collect.

The Semiquincentennial arrived on schedule, which is more than can be said for the celebration. Washington spent the fourth of July 2026 under a heat index reported at a hundred and ten to a hundred and fifteen degrees, and then under a thunderstorm; the National Mall was evacuated, the checkpoints reopened at a quarter to ten at night, and the President’s address — which he had promised in advance would be long — went out well after the hour at which the republic’s birthday has historically been considered concluded. The fireworks, when they came, were billed as record-breaking. The speech was not apolitical and did not attempt to be: it made the case for a bill requiring documentary proof of citizenship to vote, and it described a faction of the opposition party as a cancer requiring excision. Several Democratic-led states declined to send delegations. A number of booked performers withdrew. A white-nationalist group attended, and went home by Metro. I enter these as items, not as a verdict. The reader may keep whichever set of books they prefer.

Then the coins, which this series cannot let pass. The Mint’s anniversary programme struck a quarter for the Declaration of Independence — Jefferson, the Liberty Bell — and another for the Gettysburg Address, Lincoln and a pair of linked hands: which is to say the republic minted, in the same year and the same series, both the proof and the most famous audit ever performed against it. Four score and seven years, in metal, at twenty-five cents. And beside them the Treasury issued a one-dollar coin bearing the profile of the living president who signed the redesign act into law, the Great Seal on its back — permissible, the department’s lawyers determined, because the governing statute forbids a living person only on the reverse, and tradition, which forbade considerably more, is not a statute. The designs prepared under the previous Treasury Secretary, built around abolition, suffrage and civil rights, were not the ones used. I add no editorial to that sequence. I note only, in the manner of this series, which entries were struck, and which were struck out.

So: axiom, or merchandise? The number has been priced before — this is the management that once retailed its own goods at $1,776 — and it has now been struck in metal with a living face on it, which is as complete an answer as that question is ever going to receive from the people it was addressed to. But it is the wrong question to end on, because the document does not require the assent of its current custodians to remain in force. O Say Can You See keeps the running tally of the specific defaults, weekly, and will go on keeping it; what belongs here is only the narrow finding this entry was built to make. The note is still outstanding. It was presented by a president at a cemetery and by a preacher at a memorial, and it is being presented this year by people whose names will appear in none of the coverage. The teller’s window, as of this writing, is open — grudgingly, with a queue, under a management that would rather it were a gift shop. It is open because the axiom is posted, and a posted axiom cannot be un-posted. That is the entire security. It has been enough before.

Here, then, is the idea at the centre of this essay: a nation founded on a proof is permanently auditable against its own axiom — and that is the founders’ one unrevokable gift, including the founders who failed it. Most countries rest on a story: blood, soil, saga, a marriage of crowns, fifteen minutes on a field. Stories can be retold to fit the management. An axiom cannot; it can only be honoured or defaulted on, and the default is computable, by anyone, forever — by Lincoln at the cemetery, by King at the memorial, by the candid world to which the facts were originally submitted, a world in which I, two hundred and fifty years on and one border north, am a paid-up member. That is why this number, alone among the inflated numbers I cover, survives its own merchandising. The souvenir stand sells the parchment at $17.76; the parchment, unimpressed, goes on stating the axiom; and every abuse committed under its flag is not merely wrong but arithmetically falsifiable against the founding entry. The men of 1776 posted a standard they could not meet. Posting it was still the most consequential act of accounting since Wittenberg, and the ledger it opened is the one their successors are audited in — weekly, in my columns, and nightly, I suspect, in their own dreams.

Dior’s regard for declarations of independence is documented and total: she issues one every morning at the moment the leash comes off at the dog beach, and it contains a single self-evident truth, unwritten, unsigned, and unanimously ratified by her entire legislature, which is her. But observe — as I do, every morning — that she comes back. That is the part the fireworks never mention. Independence is declared in a sentence; the coming back, the choosing of the household again and again, daily, with the whole lakeshore available — that is the union, and it is renewed at the whistle, and no parchment on earth has ever secured anything half so well. The Americans wrote the best first sentence in political history. The rest of the document, as Dior demonstrates each morning and their own history keeps proving, is the walk home.

The count moves on, as it does, and the next landmark arrives in a hurry: essay 1,789, the year the axiom crossed the Atlantic and detonated — one revolution consolidating into a constitution in New York while another opened, in Paris, the decade that would teach Europe what the guillotine’s arithmetic looks like. Two revolutions, one year, opposite ledgers. The Numbers Game will keep both sets of books, as it always does.

Twenty-seven items, one axiom, half a million suppressed entries, and a cheque still at the window. The proof stands. The audit continues. That is not the failure of 1776. That is 1776, working.

See you at 1,789, when the axiom reaches Paris.
