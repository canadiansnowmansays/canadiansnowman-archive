On a recent TikTok, Senator Ted Cruz was asked a simple yes-or-no question: should violent January 6th rioters who were convicted and then pardoned be eligible for taxpayer compensation? He never answered it. He answered a different question entirely — one about Joe Biden — and seemed to find that perfectly sufficient. This essay is about why that move fails, and why it fails in both directions.

A CanadianSnowmanSays Essay | Stephen Gordon

In mathematics, two negatives make a positive. Multiply negative one by negative one and you get positive one — clean, reliable, the kind of certainty that makes mathematicians sleep soundly. The double negative is not merely permitted in arithmetic; it is required. The universe of numbers demands it.

Politics is not mathematics.

In politics, two wrongs produce a sum that is reliably worse than either wrong alone — not better, not neutral, but compounded. The second wrong does not cancel the first. It validates it, normalizes it, raises the floor for what the next administration will feel licensed to attempt. Two wrongs, in the political ledger, make a catastrophe on an instalment plan.

I raise this because there is a rhetorical move so common in American political discourse that it has its own Latin name — tu quoque, “you too” — and it is almost always deployed as a weapon rather than an argument. Point out that a powerful man has corrupted an institution he was entrusted to protect, and within seconds someone will remind you that the other powerful man did it too.

Sometimes that reminder is honest. Sometimes it is deflection. The trick — and this is the trick that most partisan commentators refuse to perform — is to hold both possibilities simultaneously without letting either one off the hook.

I want to try that today. Not as a defender of Joe Biden. Not as a defender of Donald Trump. As someone who believes that the Justice Department of the United States is worth defending as an institution, and that when presidents — any president — treat it as a personal instrument, the damage accumulates regardless of the party affiliation of the person holding the pen or auto-pen.

The trouble with applying the standard honestly is that it requires you to first establish what each wrong actually was — and that requires a level of evidentiary care that the loudest voices on both sides have largely abandoned.

So let’s do the work.

What Biden’s Justice Department Actually Did Wrong

The case against the Biden DOJ is real. It is not a fabrication of right-wing media. And it deserves to be stated plainly.

The Hunter Biden investigation was handled abnormally. The most damaging evidence on this point did not come from Republican attack ads. It came from two career IRS investigators — Gary Shapley and Joseph Ziegler — who testified under oath before Congress that the investigation into Hunter Biden’s tax and business affairs was impeded in ways they had never previously encountered. Shapley, a supervisory special agent with over a decade at the IRS, testified that DOJ prosecutors blocked certain investigative steps, restricted interviews, and allowed statutes of limitations to expire on the most serious charges. Ziegler, who himself voted for Biden, corroborated the core account. Neither has been credibly impeached.

The initial plea agreement that emerged from years of investigation was so narrowly constructed — a pair of misdemeanor tax charges and a diverted felony gun charge — that the federal judge overseeing it rejected it in open court, a rare and pointed rebuke. The Justice Department eventually appointed David Weiss as special counsel. Weiss had been the original prosecutor whose office was alleged to have constrained the investigation in the first place. The optics of that appointment were, to put it charitably, self-defeating.

The classified documents investigation produced a two-tiered outcome without an adequate explanation. Robert Hur, the special counsel appointed to investigate Biden’s handling of classified materials, declined to recommend prosecution. His reasoning — that Biden was an “elderly man with a poor memory” whose jury presentation would be sympathetic — was legally unusual and, for many observers across the political spectrum, unsatisfying. The underlying conduct (retaining classified documents from his Senate years, leaving them in a Delaware garage) was not trivial. The decision not to prosecute may have been defensible. The explanation offered for it was not the Justice Department’s finest hour.

The timing and optics of the Trump federal indictments created legitimate institutional questions. This one requires the most careful handling, because it is the most frequently abused. The underlying cases — the documents case in Florida, the January 6th conspiracy case in Washington — were built by career prosecutors and supervised by a special counsel appointed under longstanding DOJ regulations. The evidence in both was substantial. None of that is in serious dispute.

But the fact that the indictments landed during the 2024 presidential campaign, against Biden’s most likely opponent, created a perception problem that the DOJ — whose institutional legitimacy depends in part on public confidence — had an obligation to manage with exceptional care. Whether it did is a question reasonable people answer differently. What is not reasonable is pretending the perception problem did not exist.

Taken together, the case against the Biden DOJ is not a fantasy. It centres on preferential treatment toward the president’s family and an inconsistency in prosecutorial energy that eroded confidence in equal application of the law. That is a serious problem. It deserves serious accounting.

What Trump’s Justice Department Actually Did — Both Times

Now for the harder conversation. Because the Trump record is not symmetric, and pretending it is would be its own form of dishonesty.

In the first term, Trump treated the Justice Department as a personal grievance machine. He fired FBI Director James Comey and subsequently told NBC’s Lester Holt, on camera, that the Russia investigation was on his mind when he did it — a statement that made obstruction of justice arguments nearly inescapable as a matter of logic, whatever one concludes legally. He publicly demanded that the DOJ investigate his political opponents. He intervened in sentencing recommendations for Roger Stone and Michael Flynn, prompting the resignation of the career prosecutor in the Stone case and the public rebuke of Attorney General Barr by over 2,000 former DOJ employees. He attempted to appoint Jeffrey Clark — a junior environmental lawyer with no criminal law experience and a willingness to send false letters to state legislatures — as Acting Attorney General, a plan abandoned only when his senior DOJ leadership threatened mass resignation.

These were not optics problems. They were documented attempts to use the federal prosecutorial power of the United States to protect allies, punish perceived enemies, and subvert an election result.

In the second term, the stated agenda has been retribution, and the actions have matched the statement. Trump entered his second term having promised, explicitly and repeatedly, to use the Justice Department to pursue those he regarded as enemies. The appointment of Kash Patel as FBI Director — a man who had published a book identifying specific journalists and political figures as targets — signalled plainly that the firewall between presidential politics and federal law enforcement was not merely being weakened but being deliberately demolished. Career prosecutors handling cases Trump found inconvenient were removed or reassigned. The pardoning of January 6th defendants, including those convicted of violent felonies, was undertaken not as an exercise of mercy but as a political signal about whose violence the state would and would not punish.

The second Trump DOJ has not simply underperformed on institutional independence. It has been explicitly redesigned to eliminate it.

The Asymmetry You Are Not Supposed to Name

Here is where the “both sides” framework breaks down, and where honesty requires some precision.

The Biden DOJ failures were real, serious, and primarily directional toward protection of the president’s family. They represent the corruption of prosecutorial discretion through favoritism — a wrong that has occurred, in various forms, under administrations of both parties, and which warrants the full weight of oversight and accountability.

The Trump DOJ failures were real, serious, and primarily directional toward targeting political opponents and insulating the president from legal accountability. They represent a deliberate attempt to redesign the institution itself — its leadership culture, its norms of independence, its relationship to political power — in ways that will outlast any individual administration if not reversed.

These are both wrongful exercises of presidential influence over an institution that is supposed to be independent. They are not the same exercise. One is corruption by favoritism. The other is corruption by weaponization. The first undermines the principle of equal treatment. The second undermines the principle that the law constrains the powerful. Both matter. The second matters more, not because Democrats are better people than Republicans, but because the scale and the stated intent are categorically different.

I recognize that naming the asymmetry will irritate people on both sides. Those who have spent four years pointing at Hunter Biden’s laptop will accuse me of minimizing. Those who regard the Trump prosecutions as straightforwardly heroic will accuse me of false equivalence. Both accusations would miss the point.

The point is this: accountability is not a tool. It is a standard. The moment you apply it selectively — defending your team’s misconduct while prosecuting the other side’s — you have not defended accountability. You have destroyed it, and replaced it with something that merely wears its clothes.

A Note From Outside the Arena

I watch American politics as a Canadian, which means I watch it with the particular clarity of someone who has no team in the game but a great deal invested in the outcome. What I see, from this vantage point, is a country in which the institutional infrastructure of the rule of law is being stress-tested in ways it was not designed to handle, and in which the response from a significant portion of the citizenry is to cheer for the stress rather than to resist it.

The Justice Department of the United States is not a perfect institution. It never has been. Its history includes the persecution of civil rights leaders, the internment of Japanese Americans with legal blessing, and a long list of other episodes that should humble anyone inclined to reverence. But it has also, at its best, been the instrument through which the country has held its most powerful actors to account. That capacity — the capacity of a society to say no, not even you — is not a small thing.

When presidents corrupt it, in whatever direction, they are not simply committing a political wrong. They are spending down the institutional capital that makes self-governance possible.

Joe Biden spent some of that capital protecting his son. Donald Trump has been spending it by the truckload, and has brought a refinery.

Two wrongs. Neither cancelled by the other. Both requiring honest accounting.

That is not a partisan statement.

That is the minimum the standard demands.
