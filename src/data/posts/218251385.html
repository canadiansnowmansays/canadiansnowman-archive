Every technology race needs a villain, and for the past several years American AI executives have found theirs conveniently located across the Pacific, conveniently opaque to independent verification, and conveniently useful for justifying whatever the executive in question already wanted: fewer regulations, more chips, faster data-centre permitting, looser export controls when it suits them and tighter ones when it doesn’t. The China AI race is real. Whether it is the race its loudest narrators describe is a different question entirely, and one worth taking apart piece by piece.

The Catalogue of Horrors

As Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic in August 2026, the worst-case scenarios circulating among American AI and national-security researchers read like a checklist assembled for maximum cinematic effect: missile-defence systems so complete they render the American nuclear arsenal obsolete, endless drone armies overwhelming every conventional defence, bioweapons engineered to target specific ethnic groups, even the prospect of genetically engineered “super soldiers.” Rama Elluru, of the Schmidt-founded Special Competitive Studies Project, offered Wong a comparison to Marvel’s Captain America as a plausible outcome of Chinese AI research. This is not measured threat assessment. This is a pitch deck wearing the costume of a national security briefing, and the tell is that nearly every named source with a financial or institutional stake in the AI industry’s growth is also the source describing the most apocalyptic version of what happens if a rival gets there first.

The scenarios share a structural feature this series has flagged before, in the earliest Asimov Gap parts on the gap between speculative capability and demonstrated capability: they all assume the prior arrival of something close to superintelligence — a system smart enough to design smarter systems recursively, achieving what David Lin of the SCSP called an escape velocity from which rivals cannot recover. Wong’s reporting is careful, and correctly so, to note that whether such a system can or will be built at all remains genuinely unresolved. Building an entire policy framework on a technology that may not exist yet, may never exist, and has no agreed technical roadmap toward existing, is not caution. It is speculation wearing caution’s clothes.

Software Doesn’t Win Wars, and Neither Does Fear

The most useful corrective in Wong’s piece is also the most mundane: military capability does not translate cleanly or quickly from an AI model’s cleverness into a battlefield outcome. The Pentagon has reportedly used AI to accelerate target selection in the war with Iran, and that war has not gone well for the United States despite the assistance. A ceasefire agreed on April 8, 2026 has been repeatedly violated and extended; as Mohamad Ali Harisi reported for The National on October 3, 2026, talks in New York had failed to produce a breakthrough, a third American carrier strike group was heading for the region, and a source in Tehran said that “assessments in Iran are that the war is resuming.” The attacks continued into the week this part was posted: on October 12, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said they had struck a supertanker in the Strait of Hormuz, and Riyadh’s main airport was closed until October 17 after renewed Houthi attacks. That record undercuts the theory that smarter software delivers decisive advantage on its own. R. David Edelman, the MIT AI-policy researcher who served on Obama’s National Security Council, put the sceptic’s case plainly to Wong: it strains credibility that a six-month AI lead could be the difference between winning and losing a war against any serious adversary. Translating a theoretical AI breakthrough into physical capability — missile interceptors, biological super-soldiers, an actual functioning drone army — still requires the boring, slow, capital-intensive work of manufacturing, testing, and deployment that no chatbot accelerates by being clever.

This is where Wong’s reporting arrives at the argument this series finds most persuasive: if there is a race worth taking seriously, it is not a military one but an economic and industrial one, and on that front the picture is considerably less flattering to American confidence. Chinese frontier models from Moonshot AI, Z.ai, DeepSeek, and Alibaba are, by most independent accounts, close in capability to the top American offerings and substantially cheaper to run — a point this series has made before in the context of open-weight model releases, but worth restating because cost, not benchmark supremacy, is what actually determines adoption by the businesses and governments deciding which AI stack to build their future on. Grace Shao, the analyst behind the newsletter AI Proem, told Wong she has never heard Chinese AI executives or researchers describe their own work as part of a national contest against the United States. The race, as they experience it, is about diffusing the technology into the real economy — not about beating anyone. Americans have narrated an arms race. China appears, by Shao’s account, to be running an industrial policy.

The Advantage That Has Nothing to Do With AI

The most important paragraph in Wong’s reporting is the one about solar panels and humanoid robots, because it identifies China’s actual structural advantage and it has almost nothing to do with the AI models themselves.

China manufactures roughly ninety percent of the world’s crucial solar panel components and, last year, produced nearly ninety percent of all humanoid robots sold globally. This is not an AI advantage. It is a decades-old manufacturing and industrial-policy advantage, the fruit of choices made long before generative AI existed, now positioned to compound with AI adoption because a country that already builds the physical hardware AI needs — robots, chips, renewable energy infrastructure to power data centres — has a structural head start no amount of Silicon Valley model-training compute can offset on its own. Meanwhile, as this series’ Capex Ledger sibling has tracked in detail, the United States is straining to meet AI’s electricity demands even now, and struggled to resupply missiles during the same Iran conflict its own AI-accelerated targeting systems were meant to help win.

This reframes the entire “race” question in a way the missile-defence and bioweapon scenarios obscure. If the contest is genuinely about which country’s industrial base can absorb and deploy AI at scale, the United States is not obviously ahead, and the remedy is not a tighter export-control regime or a louder warning about Chinese superintelligence. It is investment in the unglamorous physical capacity — power generation, component manufacturing, robotics production — that Wong’s sources agree actually determines who benefits from AI diffusion, as distinct from who wins a hypothetical algorithmic arms race.

Belt, Road, and Server Rack

Where the China threat becomes genuinely concrete in Wong’s reporting, rather than speculative, is in the export of AI infrastructure alongside the Belt and Road Initiative’s existing footprint of highways, ports, and fibre-optic cable across Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

Daniel Remler of the Center for a New American Security frames the real risk as a technology sphere of influence congealing into political alignment — nations dependent on Chinese-built data centres and AI services becoming more likely to vote with Beijing at the United Nations, several already having done so on the question of Taiwan. This is a slower, less cinematic threat than an engineered pathogen, and precisely because it is slower and less cinematic it receives a fraction of the attention devoted to the missile-defence scenario. It is also, unlike the missile-defence scenario, already happening, verifiably, right now, which ought to make it the more urgent of the two concerns rather than the more neglected one.

The authoritarian-export dimension compounds this. China is already using AI to strengthen domestic surveillance and suppress dissent; the same infrastructure exported abroad extends that capability to whichever governments host it. Wong’s sources raise the additional possibility that, in a conflict scenario, China could threaten to interrupt partner nations’ digital communications or power grids — including in countries hosting American military installations that have come to depend on the same infrastructure. The threat here is not a single decisive blow. It is what Wong calls a hastening of an ongoing decline in American influence, and the freedoms that influence has, at its best, extended abroad. That is a genuinely serious concern. It simply is not the same concern as an AI system inventing an unstoppable weapon, and treating the two as interchangeable lets the more urgent, more mundane threat go comparatively unaddressed while everyone argues about superintelligence.

Applying TACO to a Government Arguing With Itself

This series’ TACO framework usually tracks how one actor responds to external pressure. The China AI race offers a stranger case: an administration Threatening, Accommodating, and Obfuscating simultaneously, sometimes within the same week. Wong catalogues the contradictions without much need for editorial embellishment — the White House loosened export restrictions to allow more advanced AI chip sales to China while separately debating whether the Pentagon and intelligence agencies should even be allowed to use Anthropic’s most advanced models; Defense Secretary Hegseth warned of China’s military buildup mere weeks after President Trump described a “fantastic future together” with Beijing following a visit there. Shyam Sankar of Palantir invokes a “hot Cold War II.” Meanwhile the industry’s own major players — OpenAI, Anthropic, Nvidia — cannot agree among themselves what they are lobbying for, because their commercial interests in the Chinese market, in chip exports, and in domestic regulatory freedom all pull in different directions depending on the week.

The whiplash continued after Wong’s reporting. When Xi Jinping made his state visit to Washington from September 23 to 25, 2026, the two governments did not announce any agreement on chip export controls; what they did announce, according to a Chinese Foreign Ministry readout reported by U.S. News & World Report on September 26, 2026, was a $30 billion reciprocal tariff reduction and a new bilateral dialogue on artificial-intelligence risks, with the next round scheduled for November. UPI reported the same day that the White House was describing the arrangement as a “super intelligence” dialogue. The White House fact sheet went further. As Mike Pearl reported for Gizmodo on September 26, 2026, it stated that “the two leaders agreed to use the term ‘super intelligence’ rather than ‘artificial intelligence’ to describe the applicable emerging technologies,” under a heading reading “MAKING THE WORLD SAFER FOR AMERICANS,” and set up a new channel for reporting “SI incidents” without saying what would count as one. Sebastian Gorka, the White House counterterrorism director, corrected a television anchor on air: “It’s SI. As of this week, it’s Super Intelligence.” Gizmodo also cited the Associated Press verdict that the meeting “produced no major breakthroughs.” Michael Kratsios, director of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, set out the limits of the new dialogue, according to The Next Web: “International dialogue cannot be allowed to drift toward global governance.” An administration whose officials had spent the year casting Chinese superintelligence as an existential threat was now scheduling regular conversations about it with Beijing, which is either sensible diplomacy or a fresh demonstration that the threat’s severity depends on who is in the room.

This is not a coordinated Threaten posture aimed at China. It is a Threaten posture aimed, mostly, at Congress and the American regulatory apparatus, with China serving as the justification rather than the actual target. Sarah Myers West of the AI Now Institute makes this explicit in Wong’s reporting: the China arms-race framing has been deliberately wielded for years to head off domestic regulation of the tech sector, and more recently to win favourable industrial policy for data-centre construction.

Mark Zuckerberg ran this exact play in 2018 congressional testimony, warning that restricting facial recognition would simply hand the advantage to Chinese firms. A dozen former national-security officials made the identical move against antitrust proposals shortly after. The pattern long predates the current AI boom; the AI boom has simply given it a new and more urgent-sounding vocabulary.

The Kissinger Line That Still Fits

Wong’s closest historical parallel — a 1958 Kissinger-directed report warning that Soviet and Chinese strategic superiority would produce brutal consequences, paired with the “fervent belief in unending scientific progress” that justified DARPA’s founding — belongs permanently in this series’ historical toolkit, because it demonstrates that the current rhetorical structure is not new, adapted, or specific to AI at all. It is the same Cold War grammar, redeployed, with “superintelligence” standing in for “strategic superiority” and Sam Altman and Dario Amodei standing in for the scientist-advocates who justified the original arms-race spending fifty years ago. That earlier belief did produce genuine breakthroughs — nuclear deterrence architecture, the space programme, ARPA’s own precursor to the internet — which is precisely why the analogy cuts both ways. Fear-based technology races have sometimes produced real capability. They have also, reliably, produced enormous, difficult-to-reverse spending commitments justified by threats that were exaggerated in the moment and only clearly overstated in hindsight, decades later, by which point the spending had already reshaped the economy that authorised it.

What the Question Should Have Been

Wong’s closing argument is the one this series wants to end on, because it reframes the entire debate away from a binary the industry finds convenient. The question is not whether America or China wins an undefined race to an undefined finish line. It is what kind of society either country is building along the way, and who actually benefits from the outcome — a question Myers West poses directly, and one the loudest China-hawk voices in Wong’s reporting conspicuously decline to engage. If Silicon Valley’s executives genuinely believed in free-market competition, antitrust enforcement separating model providers from chip and data-centre providers would follow naturally from that belief. If they were genuinely alarmed about AI-enabled surveillance, federal digital-privacy legislation — stalled in Congress for years, for reasons entirely unrelated to China — would already exist. The China threat is real enough to justify serious industrial and infrastructure policy. It is not, on the evidence Wong assembles, real enough to justify treating every domestic regulatory question as a national-security emergency to be waved away, which is the actual function the boogeyman has been asked to perform since long before anyone called it artificial intelligence.
