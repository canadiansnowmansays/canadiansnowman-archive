Three days after the midterm elections, Elon Musk killed his truck.

Early on Friday, November 6, 2026, he announced on X that Tesla would wind down Cybertruck production by the middle of 2027, to free up factory space for the Cybercab, the steering-wheel-less robotaxi the company is trying to put on American streets. There was no ceremony and no tribute. The vehicle Musk once called the best product he had ever made was simply reassigned, like a floor of an office building, to a newer tenant.

The timing invites one reading, and it is the obvious one. On Tuesday, November 3, Musk watched roughly half a billion dollars of his money buy almost nothing. On Friday he retired the product most closely associated with the politics he had paid for. Saahil Desai, writing in The Atlantic on the day of the announcement, suggests the decision may be seen in part as political triage: a way of washing his hands of a vehicle liberals had nicknamed the “Deplorean.”

That reading is not wrong. But this series has spent twenty-two instalments arguing that Musk’s empire does not run on politics, or even on products. It runs on promises, stacked one on top of the next, each new one large enough to make the market forget the last. Read that way, the Cybertruck was not punished for its politics. It was cannibalised for the next promise. That is how the Stack has always worked, and the truck is simply the first casualty you can kick the tires of.

The promise in 2019

It is worth remembering how the Cybertruck began, because almost nobody does any more.

On November 21, 2019, Musk walked onto a stage in Hawthorne, California, beside a vehicle that looked like a doorstop drawn by a child with a ruler. It was stainless steel. It was angular to the point of rudeness. And it was, Musk said, going to start at about $40,000. Tesla had already shown that an electric car could be fast and desirable. The Cybertruck was meant to show something harder: that an electric pickup, the most American of vehicles, could be cheap enough for the people who actually buy pickups.

Then his designer, Franz von Holzhausen, threw a steel ball at the “armoured glass” window, and it cracked. He threw another at the rear window. That one cracked too. Musk stood in front of the damage and said there was room for improvement.

It did not matter. Within a week Musk said 250,000 people had put down refundable deposits. In the language of Part XIV, the market was not pricing the truck. It was pricing the belief in the truck, and in 2019 that belief was very cheap to hold: $100, refundable, for a pickup that would change the industry.

The product in 2023

The truck that arrived was not that truck.

Deliveries began at Giga Texas on November 30, 2023, four years after the unveiling and about two years later than first promised. The launch edition came in at roughly $100,000, two and a half times the promised starting price. The cheaper versions arrived later, with less range than first advertised, and never got anywhere near $40,000. The vehicle weighed close to 7,000 pounds. Its single windshield wiper, which Musk likened to a katana, became a running joke. Its stainless-steel panels turned out to be hard to shape and harder to fix.

Then came the recalls. In April 2024, Tesla recalled trucks because a piece of trim on the accelerator pedal could slip loose and wedge the pedal down. In March 2025, it recalled more than 46,000 trucks, nearly every one it had then built, because an exterior panel held on with adhesive could detach at speed. Desai summarises the record without mercy: panels that can fall off, and a pedal that could turn the truck into a cruise missile.

The arithmetic of belief caught up with the arithmetic of sales. Against 250,000 week-one reservations, Tesla has sold about 70,000 Cybertrucks in three years, according to Desai. Tesla said in 2024 that it had installed capacity to build 125,000 a year at Giga Texas. The line was built for a market that existed only in the deposit book.

That gap, between what a Musk product is said to be and what it turns out to be, is the subject of most of this series. The Roadster that still has not shipped. The Optimus robot nobody has independently verified (Part XVII). The robotaxi fleet whose growth curve cannot yet be read (Part XVI). The Cybertruck is different only in one respect. It actually shipped, so the gap could be measured in steel.

The hat in 2024

Somewhere between the promise and the product, the truck acquired a second job.

In August 2024, the streamer Adin Ross gave Donald Trump a Cybertruck wrapped in a giant image of his bloodied face from the assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania, that July. Trump circled it on the driveway at Mar-a-Lago during a livestream and said, approvingly, that it was “an Elon.” Desai calls it probably the most important Cybertruck ever built, and he is right. From that moment the truck stopped being merely a vehicle and became what he calls a MAGA hat on wheels.

It is hard to think of another consumer product that changed political meaning so completely, so fast. In 2019 the Cybertruck was a nerd’s fantasy, bought on deposit by tech enthusiasts who were mostly not Republicans. By 2025, after Musk spent more than $250 million helping elect Trump and then went to Washington to run the Department of Government Efficiency, it was a culture-war totem. Owners on one side bolted on flags. Owners on the other side bought bumper stickers apologising for their purchase. In the spring of 2025, Tesla showrooms drew protesters, and some Cybertrucks were vandalised in parking lots. A car company had become a political party’s merchandise table.

A product can survive being ugly. It can survive being late and overpriced. It is much harder for a product to survive being half of a political argument, because it loses the other half of its buyers outright, and the half it keeps buy it as a statement rather than a truck. Statements do not need replacing every few years. Trucks do.

The bill in 2026

Which brings us to Tuesday.

Charlie Warzel laid out the numbers in The Atlantic the day after the vote. Musk spent about $474 million on the 2026 midterms, making him the largest non-candidate donor in any American election cycle, and accounting for roughly a quarter of all the money Republicans raised. More than $168 million went to super PACs backing Ken Paxton in Texas, the state where Tesla has its headquarters and where the Cybertruck is built. Paxton lost to James Talarico by twelve points. Republicans lost the House and at least seven Senate seats.

Musk’s response came at 1:12 a.m. on November 4, in a post on X that has since drawn more than 12 million views: data centres in space, he wrote, were looking pretty appealing right now. It was a joke. It was also, as Desai notes in passing, one of the promises that now does the work the Cybertruck used to do. When one bet fails, point at a larger one further away.

There was a quieter irony on the ground. In South Texas, the voters who swung hardest against the Republicans this year were the working men of what the strategist Chuck Rocha calls the “big-ass-truck economy,” the ones Nick Miroff found for The Atlantic in October with pickups parked in their yards because they could no longer afford the diesel. Those were real trucks, bought for work, idled by prices. The $100,000 truck that had become the emblem of the movement those voters abandoned was never built for them, and never priced for them. It was a hat. They needed a truck.

The Stack reading

So which is it, political triage or something else?

The case for triage is the timing, and the timing is real. But consider what Tesla actually said it was doing with the space. It is not closing a line to save face. It is converting the line to build Cybercabs, and that decision was coming regardless of how Texas voted.

Part XVI of this series argued that the Cybercab’s real constraint was never demand. When the robotaxi launched with paying passengers in Texas on September 3, 2026, there were 45 of them registered in the state. The bottleneck was manufacturing capacity, with a second bottleneck in federal limits on how many vehicles without steering wheels and pedals can be sold to the public. Musk cannot do much about the second. He can do a great deal about the first, and the fastest way to find factory floor in Austin is to stop building the vehicle that is not selling.

And there is a precedent, which tells you this is a habit rather than a reaction. At the start of 2026, Tesla announced it would end production of the Model S and Model X at its Fremont plant, reported Fox 7 Austin, to turn that space over to the Optimus robot. The Model S was the car that made Tesla. It was retired in favour of a robot that, as Part XVII noted, still has no independently verified production numbers. Now the Cybertruck is retired in favour of a robotaxi whose growth curve, as Part XVI noted, nobody can yet read.

The pattern is the point. In the Stack, a shipped product is a liability. It has a sales figure. It has recalls. It can be measured, and measurement is where belief goes to die. An unshipped product has no such problems. It can be priced at whatever the market is willing to imagine. Every time a real product disappoints, Musk replaces it on the factory floor with a hypothetical one, and the stock is priced on the hypothetical. That is the discount rate on belief, Part XIV’s argument, run as an operations plan.

The political reading and the Stack reading are not really rivals. The midterms told Musk the hat was worth nothing. The Stack told him what to put in its place.

What it could have been

It would be unfair to end without the case for the defence, and Desai makes it well.

The Cybertruck was absurd, but it was different. You could take a video call from the driver’s seat. It made rude noises on command. In a market where American carmakers mostly built electric versions of the petrol cars everyone already owned, here was someone treating an electric vehicle as a chance to start again from a blank sheet. Desai points to China, where buyers can now get electric cars with built-in fridges and headlights that project films on a wall, and asks how many more electric vehicles might be on American roads if the Cybertruck had kept its original promise.

From Canada, the country that gave Musk his first passport, it is tempting to agree. A $40,000 electric pickup in 2021 would have been a genuinely important thing. It might have done more for the transition away from petrol than any subsidy Washington has offered or withdrawn since. The tragedy of the Cybertruck is not that Musk tried something strange. It is that he promised the important version, built the expensive version, and then lent the expensive version to a political movement that spent the next two years dismantling the policies that might have helped electric vehicles sell.

That is the Musk pattern in miniature: a real insight, an impossible promise, an expensive compromise, a political detour, and an exit through the door marked “next.” The Cybertruck went through every stage in seven years.

The limit

Desai closes his piece with the observation that the Cybertruck is a reminder, nearly 7,000 pounds of it, that the influence of the world’s richest man has limits. This week supplied two such reminders at once. Half a billion dollars could not buy Texas. A stainless-steel truck could not buy a market.

What neither reminder has yet touched is the thing underneath them both. The Stack absorbed the Cybertruck’s failure the way it has absorbed everything else: by turning the failure into floor space for a larger promise. The robotaxi now gets the factory. Space-based data centres now get the punchline. The market, so far, keeps paying.

The truck that was a hat is going away. The habit that made it is not.
