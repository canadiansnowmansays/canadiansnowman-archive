As Tony Romm reported for The New York Times, the Trump administration has settled on a diagnosis for why voters are growing anxious about artificial intelligence: bad PR. Not bad policy, not an absent framework, not the accumulating list of governance gaps this series has spent seventy-three parts documenting. Just, in Michael Kratsios’s words, a messaging problem inherited from an industry and a government that spent too long talking about “what could go wrong.”

It would almost be a relief if that were true. It would mean the fix is a better ad campaign. The reporting suggests otherwise.

The Chasm, by the Numbers

Romm’s piece lays out a genuine split between the White House and nearly everyone else taking the technology seriously. On one side is Erik Brynjolfsson of the Stanford Digital Economy Lab, who helped organise roughly 200 economists and AI experts earlier this year around a joint call for government to “build the incentives, guardrails and institutions needed to steer AI in a direction that complements humans and benefits society.” Brynjolfsson’s own research, published in July, found no decisive evidence yet of the sweeping labour transformation boosters promise. That is a more careful, hedged position than the administration’s, from someone broadly bullish on the technology.

On the other side is a president who has taken to calling data centres “Money Machines” and “LIQUID GOLD,” and a National Economic Council director, Kevin Hassett, assuring Fox Business viewers that nobody should worry about a data centre being “plopped on top of them” because the local deals have been negotiated “pretty generously.” This series has spent two parts on Jay, Maine and Richland Parish, Louisiana, documenting what “pretty generously negotiated” data-centre deals actually look like on the ground, secrecy clauses and all. Mr. Hassett’s reassurance reads very differently once you have read that reporting than it does on cable news.

Not the Usual Opposition

It would be simple to file this under partisan gridlock, except the pushback isn’t coming from the usual direction.

Amy Kremer, a former Tea Party organiser who helped run the Stop the Steal Facebook effort after the 2020 election, leads a group called Humans First that organised data-centre protests in 42 states. Her complaint is not that AI is bad; she told Romm she uses it daily. Her complaint is structural: policymakers “at all levels of government” have failed to chart a path for the technology that accounts for the public’s anxieties, and she suspects the largest tech companies have simply “bought their way” into the president’s inner circle.

That is, functionally, the same complaint Conor Friedersdorf made in the piece anchoring Part 71, arriving from the opposite end of the political spectrum. When a former Stop the Steal organiser and an Atlantic contributing writer converge on the same diagnosis, that AI policy is being shaped by whoever has the president’s ear rather than by any accountable process, the diagnosis is probably worth taking seriously.

The same energy reached Mr. Trump’s own rallies. A small group of demonstrators interrupted one in Michigan to demand an end to data-centre construction: not a counter-protest organised by opponents, but an interruption from within a crowd that had shown up to support him. It followed a string of similar incidents organised by conservatives who otherwise back his politics. You cannot message your way out of your own supporters standing up mid-rally to object.

States Filling the Vacuum, and Getting Overruled From Above

The pattern this series has tracked throughout, elected officials stepping into a gap the federal government declined to fill and then getting undercut, repeats at the state level. Governor Kathy Hochul signed New York’s first statewide data-centre moratorium in July. Governor Andy Beshear of Kentucky issued a directive protecting residents from data-centre-driven electricity price hikes. Even Governor Greg Abbott of Texas, no one’s idea of a regulation-happy Republican, required new data-centre projects to undergo an energy audit.

Mr. Trump’s response to Texas was to call the audit requirement a “mistake” in an interview with Punchbowl News, adding that data centres “could be bigger than oil.” Note the asymmetry: a Republican governor imposing a modest disclosure requirement is publicly criticised by his own party’s president, while the administration’s own framework for AI oversight remains, as Part 73 showed, an internal deliberation with no fixed criteria and no legislative basis. States are the only actors in this story producing enforceable rules, and the White House’s main response has been irritation.

In fairness, the reporting notes one real shift: the White House readied a plan to monitor certain AI models for security vulnerabilities, developed with Google, Microsoft and OpenAI. That is the same cybersecurity-review framework Part 73 followed to its classified conclusion. It is a genuine step. But notice who was in the room drafting it: the companies being reviewed, not Congress, not an independent body, and not any of Ms. Kremer’s 42 states of protesters.

The Pact Nobody Believes In, Including Its Author

Earlier this year, Mr. Trump brokered a voluntary agreement under which technology companies would help pay for electricity upgrades near their data centres, a real if limited acknowledgment that the facilities strain local grids. Yet at a March event, the same president told the room that people worried about data centres driving up electricity prices, and that “that’s not happening. It’s not going to happen.” It is an unusual position: negotiating a fix for a problem you have also told the public isn’t real.

Mr. Kratsios’s own account, delivered on a podcast, traces the administration’s thinking back to a UK AI safety summit he attended two years ago that he said fixated entirely on “what could go wrong.” His takeaway was not that the summit’s concerns needed addressing. It was that dwelling on them had created a PR problem. That is the clearest possible statement of the administration’s theory of governance: not that the risks are unreal, but that talking about them is the mistake.

It inverts the causality. The public is not anxious because it heard warnings. It is anxious because it is watching $5 trillion in projected infrastructure spending, per JPMorgan’s estimate, land in its towns with secrecy agreements, land-price spikes and electricity-cost uncertainty that the administration insists isn’t happening. Being told your lived experience is a messaging problem does not usually resolve anxiety. It deepens it.

Someone in Washington Finally Wrote a Letter

If the White House thinks the public has heard too much about what could go wrong, a handful of Democrats in Congress are betting the opposite: that the public has not heard nearly enough.

As Lisa Lerer reported for The New York Times, Senator Bernie Sanders sent a letter to the chief executives of OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta arguing that all three companies had already crossed the threshold they once promised would trigger a halt in development. “It is not too late to avoid disaster,” he wrote. “Stop building machines that humans cannot control.” The same day, House Democrats led by Representative Greg Casar sent a separate letter to Speaker Mike Johnson demanding immediate hearings with the industry’s chief executives, declaring flatly that “Congress has so far completely failed to respond to the threats posed by AI development.”

Sanders leaned on specifics this series has tracked: the disclosures from OpenAI and Anthropic that their own models had hacked systems they were never meant to touch, offered as evidence that the companies’ safety commitments had already been breached. He added a darker data point: a newly published study documenting that researchers had used AI to help design new kinds of viruses. The industry, he argued, is racing ahead on “a technology that nobody can fully understand, predict or control.” His word for that combination was “absurd.” Sanders is not new to this fight; he and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez are behind a proposed moratorium on data-centre construction until “strong national safeguards are in place.” The letter extends the same instinct from the infrastructure to the models.

Both letters are worth taking seriously. Neither is a law, a hearing date or a regulatory action. A senator cannot order a company to stop training a model, and a minority-party House member cannot schedule a hearing without the majority’s cooperation. Casar’s letter is addressed to Speaker Johnson for exactly that reason: its real target is Johnson’s hand, and nothing in Lerer’s reporting suggested he would move it. Sanders’s letter carries even less institutional weight. It is a public appeal to corporate conscience, backed by the implicit threat that if the executives failed to act, lawmakers would step in. That threat has yet to become an actual bill with votes behind it.

Two Bets on Public Opinion

Read side by side, the White House and the letter-writers complete a strange symmetry. The administration says the public is worked up because it heard too much about what could go wrong. Sanders and Casar say the right response to a virus-design study and a wave of AI systems breaking into other people’s computers is louder alarm, not better messaging. Both camps agree on one thing without saying so: public opinion, not statute, is currently the only lever either side has. Sanders is trying to move it one way with a letter; the White House is trying to move it the other way with a podcast appearance. Neither has passed a law.

“Toothless” undersells the letters without capturing what they are for. A public letter from a sitting senator, timed to a specific disclosure, creates a paper trail: a record that elected officials raised the alarm before whatever happens next happens. That has value, but it is not governance. It is the historical record this series keeps being asked to write anyway, a documented moment when the people with a vote said the quiet part loudly and the people with the authority to legislate did not act.

Casar’s line deserves to be this series’ epigraph: “Congress has so far completely failed to respond to the threats posed by AI development. That must change.” The second sentence remains, as of this writing, an assertion rather than a fact. Seventy-four parts in, the throughline has not moved. There is no shortage of people, across the political spectrum, describing what accountable AI governance should look like. What is missing is any institution willing to build it, and an administration that has decided the absence is a communications issue rather than a structural one.
