Top Performer #2

Denmark is, by most measures, the happiest country on Earth. This is not a coincidence, and it is not merely a cultural trait or an accident of Scandinavian temperament. It is the product of deliberate choices — about governance, about social investment, about environmental stewardship, and about how a nation of fewer than six million people chooses to relate to the rest of the world.

What distinguishes Denmark from its Nordic neighbours — all of whom score exceptionally in this series — is not any single policy but a national orientation toward practical idealism. Denmark does not merely build good systems. It builds good systems and then exports the technology that makes them possible. Danish wind turbines now generate electricity on every continent. Danish urban planning principles shape cities from Melbourne to Medellín. Danish welfare state design is studied in policy schools worldwide. Denmark’s contribution is not just domestic excellence. It is domestic excellence packaged for global application.

The Scorecard

The Analysis

Human Development: 45 out of 50

Denmark’s human development mirrors the Nordic pattern but with its own distinctive character. The flexicurity model — combining flexible labour markets with strong social safety nets — has produced an economy that adapts to change while protecting citizens from its worst effects. Workers can be hired and fired with relative ease (the flexibility), but generous unemployment insurance, retraining programmes, and social support ensure that job loss does not mean financial catastrophe (the security). The result is an economy that is simultaneously dynamic and humane — a combination that most nations believe is impossible and that Denmark has maintained for decades.

Danes cycling en masse in Copenhagen — vibrant streets, people of all ages.

Life expectancy is approximately 82 years. Healthcare is universal, funded through taxation, and characterised by high-quality primary care. Education is free through university, with students receiving a monthly stipend rather than accumulating debt — a policy that treats education as an investment in the nation’s future rather than a market transaction. Democratic governance earns a 9 — Denmark’s parliamentary system, proportional representation, and high levels of civic engagement produce governments that are responsive and accountable.

Gender equality earns a 9. Denmark’s parental leave policies, childcare infrastructure, and workplace culture have produced one of the most gender-equal labour markets in the world. Women’s representation in parliament and corporate leadership is among the highest globally.

Poverty reduction earns a 9. Denmark’s Gini coefficient is among the lowest in the world, reflecting a society where the distance between the wealthiest and poorest citizens is narrow by any international comparison. The social safety net — comprehensive, well-funded, and designed to prevent poverty rather than merely ameliorate it — means that extreme deprivation is essentially nonexistent among Danish citizens.

Environmental Stewardship: 40 out of 50

Aerial of a Danish wind farm on a sunny day with export cables or neighboring country hint (if available).

Denmark is a global leader in wind energy — and not merely for domestic consumption. This distinction matters for the Contribution Index because it represents intentional contribution rather than incidental spillover. Denmark did not merely develop wind technology for itself. It built an industry designed to export clean energy solutions to the world.

Danish company Ørsted — formerly DONG Energy, an oil and gas company that pivoted entirely to renewable energy — has become the world’s largest developer of offshore wind power. Danish wind technology expertise has been exported to every continent. Denmark generates over 50 percent of its electricity from wind, with periods of 100 percent coverage becoming routine. On particularly windy days, Denmark exports surplus clean electricity to its neighbours.

Renewable energy earns a 9. The trajectory score is strong but nuanced — the EPI noted that Denmark dropped from first to tenth in the 2024 rankings as its pace of decarbonization slowed in hard-to-abate industrial sectors. This is the honest challenge facing every frontrunner in the energy transition: the easy wins have been captured — coal plants closed, wind farms built — and the remaining emissions reductions require structural changes in heavy industry, agriculture, and transportation that are more technically difficult and politically fraught. The 9 reflects both the extraordinary achievement and the slowing momentum that honesty requires acknowledging.

Emissions per capita earn a 7 — similar to other Nordic nations, reflecting the structural challenges of wealth, cold climate, and industrial activity. Denmark’s per capita emissions are roughly 5 metric tons — well below the EU average and dramatically below the United States or Australia.

Pristine Danish Island

Biodiversity and habitat protection earn an 8. Denmark’s relatively flat, intensively farmed landscape presents different conservation challenges than Norway’s mountains or Finland’s boreal forests, but the nation has invested significantly in marine protected areas, rewilding projects, and sustainable agricultural practices.

Danish private philanthropy amplifies the public investment in ways that are, even by Nordic standards, extraordinary. The Novo Nordisk Foundation — one of the world’s largest charitable foundations, with assets exceeding $100 billion — funds biomedical research, education, and humanitarian projects globally. The Carlsberg Foundation funds science and the arts. The Lundbeck Foundation supports neuroscience research. Denmark’s philanthropic sector punches far above its population weight, but unlike in nations where philanthropy substitutes for government action, Danish giving adds to an already comprehensive public commitment. This is what philanthropy looks like when it is not filling gaps but extending reach — when the baseline is already excellent and the giving makes it extraordinary.

Systemic Behaviour: 37 out of 50

Denmark’s systemic behaviour is strong across the board, with two notable qualifications.

Development aid consistently meets the 0.7 percent of GNI target — a commitment that Denmark has maintained for decades, making it one of only a handful of nations that actually deliver on the international development promise that most wealthy countries endorse at summit after summit and then ignore in their budgets.

Corruption earns a 9 — Denmark consistently ranks at or near the top of Transparency International’s index. Adherence to international law earns a 9 — Danish engagement with international legal frameworks is constructive and genuine.

Arms exports earn a 6 — Denmark has a modest defence industry, and its military contributions to NATO operations — including in Afghanistan and Iraq — place it in a more complex position than nations with no military engagement. The score reflects a nation that participates in collective security (which the Contribution Index does not penalise) while also exporting weapons at levels that a pacifist reading of Danish values would find uncomfortable.

Refugee contribution earns a 7, reflecting a genuine and difficult tension. Denmark’s immigration and refugee policies have tightened significantly under successive governments, moving away from the more open posture of earlier decades. Laws designed to discourage asylum seekers — including provisions to process claims in third countries — have drawn criticism from UNHCR and human rights organisations. The 7 reflects the historical contribution and the current trajectory, which are moving in different directions.

The Neighbour Test: 41 out of 50

Denmark’s Neighbour Test ties Sweden at 41 — joint highest in the series alongside Norway’s 42. The consistency score of 8 edges Sweden’s 7, reflecting Denmark’s less conflicted relationship with arms exports and a social model that has been more stable under political pressure.

Multilateral orientation earns a 9. Denmark’s engagement with the EU, NATO, and the UN is deep and constructive. Its development diplomacy — channelling aid through multilateral institutions while maintaining bilateral programmes — reflects a sophisticated understanding of how to maximise impact at scale.

Response to global crises earns an 8. Denmark has contributed to international humanitarian responses consistently, though at levels slightly below Sweden’s extraordinary 2015 refugee intake. Its contributions to peacekeeping, disaster relief, and pandemic response are reliable and well-funded.

Treatment of allies earns an 8. Denmark’s relationships within the Nordic Council, the EU, and NATO are characterised by genuine partnership. Its engagement with Greenland and the Faroe Islands — self-governing territories within the Danish realm — is the most complex and occasionally contentious aspect of its alliance management, particularly as Arctic geopolitics intensifies and the current US administration has expressed interest in Greenland’s strategic position.

Consistency of values versus interests earns an 8. Denmark’s social model, its development aid, its environmental policies, and its multilateral engagement are all broadly aligned with its stated values. The tightening of immigration policy represents the most significant area of value-interest tension, but in the broader context of Danish governance, the alignment between rhetoric and action remains strong.

Long-term stewardship earns an 8. The wind technology that Denmark developed and exported is not merely a domestic achievement — it is a deliberate gift to the global energy transition. When Ørsted builds offshore wind farms across Europe, North America, and Asia, it is Danish stewardship in action: the choice to invest in technology that benefits the world, not just the nation that developed it. This is intentional contribution — not the incidental spillover that the Innovation Question addresses, but a deliberate strategy to build something the world needs and then make it available. It is, in miniature, what the Contribution Index exists to measure.

The Editorial Conclusion

Denmark scores 163 out of 200 — third in the top performers, one point behind Sweden and five behind Norway. The margins at the top of the Contribution Index are narrow — the difference between third and first is less than 3 percent — and the ordering among the top three Nordics could reasonably be argued in any direction.

The wind at our backs. Denmark has proven that a small nation can lead the world in renewable energy, not just for itself but for everyone. The Ørsted transformation — from fossil fuel company to the world’s largest offshore wind developer — is itself a metaphor for the choice this series measures: a nation that looked at its existing assets, acknowledged that they were part of the problem, and rebuilt them into part of the solution.

That is what contribution looks like at its best. Not perfection — Denmark’s immigration tightening and its arms exports prevent that claim — but the consistent, deliberate, exportable pursuit of a world that works better for everyone. Denmark built the wind turbines. The rest of the world can decide whether to install them.
