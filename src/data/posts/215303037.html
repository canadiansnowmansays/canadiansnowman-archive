Stephen Gordon Sep 16, 2026

On Thursday morning, Brianna Lennon, the clerk of Boone County, Missouri, arrived at her office expecting to spend the day proofreading ballots. It is September, and in ordinary election years, that is what county clerks do in September: they check the text of the ballot, verify the candidate names, make sure the instructions are clear, and prepare the physical documents that voters will use to choose the people who represent them. It is technical work, necessary work, and work that requires a person to sit still and pay attention.

Instead, on Thursday morning, Lennon spent the day refreshing web browsers.

As the New York Times reported this week, Lennon had loaded the online dockets of the Missouri Supreme Court, the U.S. Supreme Court, and three separate federal district courts onto her personal device, her work computer, and her home laptop. She checked them repeatedly, throughout the day, waiting to see which court had issued the next order that would tell her which congressional map her county was supposed to be using, which candidates would appear on the ballot, and whether the work she had already done preparing for the election would have to be redone. “I have these on my personal device, I have them on my computer at home, and I have just refreshed them throughout the day,” Lennon said on Thursday at her office in downtown Columbia, Missouri. It was not a metaphorical description of her anxiety. It was a literal account of how she spent her time.

This is what happens when courts close doors, but close them late.

The Map, the Primary, and the General

When we last counted Missouri in this series, it was listed as +1R in the 2026 redistricting arms race, pending a referendum. The state’s Republicans had drawn a new congressional map in 2025, following Trump’s push in Texas and the national mid-decade redistricting frenzy. The state’s Democrats had sued. The Missouri Supreme Court had ordered a return to the old map. Republicans had pushed back. By August, voters in Missouri had already cast a primary election under the new map — had nominated candidates under those district lines, had chosen the people they wanted to send to Congress under those boundaries. And then, in September, the courts intervened again.

The sequence is different from the other major redistricting fights the series has covered. In Texas, the new Republican map survived the courts and was used for the general election. In Florida, the new map survived because the state Supreme Court refused to enforce the voter-written constitutional ban in time to stop it. In Missouri, the new map won the primary but lost the general. The referendum that froze the old map in place — a device written into the state constitution to prevent exactly this kind of mid-decade chaos — stayed in force. SCOTUS, on Thursday, refused to lift it. The primary voters, who cast their ballots under the new map and chose their nominees under those lines, would now have to vote in a general election under the old map. The people they nominated would not appear on the ballot in their own districts.

This is not disenfranchisement in the sense the courts usually measure it. No one was prevented from voting. But it is a form of electoral confusion so profound that the word “disenfranchisement” barely captures it. Voters were asked to nominate representatives in one congressional district and then asked to choose between those nominees in a different congressional district. The meaning of “you chose the nominees” became a question without an answer.

The Tuesday That Would Not Stay Settled

The chaos reached its apex on Tuesday, September 10th, the state deadline for finalizing the ballots. Denny Hoskins, Missouri’s Secretary of State, had spent the preceding weeks trying to navigate the contradictory court orders. The state Supreme Court said to use the old map. The federal district court said to use the new map. The Trump administration had filed briefs saying the new map was constitutional and that the state should be allowed to use it. By Tuesday morning, Hoskins still did not know which map he was supposed to be administering. He was in Dallas for the Republican National Convention when the final court orders began to arrive.

Lennon received an email from the state at 4:44 p.m. citing a Missouri Supreme Court order. The instructions were clear: use the old map. Hoskins had decided. The state was moving forward with the 2022 district lines. Clerks across Missouri began adjusting their ballot preparation according to these instructions.

Then, at 6:20 p.m. the same day, a second email arrived. This one cited a federal district court decision. Use the new map, it said. The order was the opposite of the order from ninety-six minutes earlier. Lennon had already begun implementing the first instruction. Now she and every other county clerk in Missouri had to stop, reverse course, and begin implementing the second one. And then, by the end of the week, Hoskins reversed again. SCOTUS had cleared the way for the old map. The new map was out. The old boundaries would be used in November.

What this sequence did to the actual machinery of elections is what the series has been building toward in the past week of mail-voting reporting. It is not just that voters are confused. It is not just that candidates have to scramble. It is that election officials lose the ability to administer the election on time.

Rachel Lightfoot, the Republican clerk in Polk County in southwest Missouri, described the experience in three words: “just a lot of whiplash.” She added: “everybody is at least three weeks behind now.” Three weeks behind means sample ballots do not get posted. Three weeks behind means voters do not get their early voting materials in time. Three weeks behind means the entire precinct infrastructure for November shifts from preparation to panic.

Kurt Bahr, the director of elections in St. Charles County near St. Louis, had been planning to post sample ballots online in mid-September. Instead, he spent Tuesday and Wednesday refreshing court dockets. By the end of the week, when SCOTUS finally settled the question, he and his staff had nine days to do the work they normally did in three weeks. “Now every county in the state can begin the process of building their elections under that map — with the little asterisk that is over our heads that it’s still subject to change,” Bahr said.

The Courts Close Doors Late

This is the moment where the guardrails work, but work too late to matter. The Missouri Supreme Court was right. The referendum was constitutional. The voters had indeed chosen a mechanism to freeze the map. The courts honored it. SCOTUS refused to override it. The judicial system functioned. The rule of law prevailed. And the price of all of that virtue was that county clerks across the state now have to administer an election in a state of controlled chaos, three weeks behind schedule, with the constant fear that another court order might change the rules one more time.

The question at the end of the mail-voting series — Part XLVI-XLVII — was whether the courts could close the doors that the administration kept opening. They did. The judges said no. The administration filed again. The judges said no again. And by the time the courts were done saying no, the uncertainty had already done its work. Voters were already confused about whether their mail ballots would be delivered. Election officials were already behind.

Missouri shows the same pattern, but with the guardrail actually functioning. The courts closed the door. They used the referendum mechanism, the state constitution, and the full weight of judicial authority to say: no, you cannot redraw this map in time for the election. The answer was right. The timing was everything else. By the time the door closed, the primary had already happened under the wrong map. By the time the question was settled, the clerks had lost three weeks they could not get back. By the time the courts finished their work, the election was already in the machinery, already scrambled, already three weeks behind the deadline that makes elections administrable.

This is what it looks like when the courts, confronted with emergency applications and chaos, finally say yes to their role as guardrails and no to the chaos. It looks like Brianna Lennon refreshing three court dockets on her personal phone instead of proofreading ballots. It looks like clerks “at least three weeks behind,” with “an asterisk over our heads that it’s still subject to change.” It looks like the guardrail working and producing a ballot that the election officials cannot administer on time.

What Happens to the Referendum?

One more question, which the series has not faced yet, now sits on the November ballot. Proposition A asks Missouri voters whether they want to amend the state constitution to give the legislature — rather than the voters — the power to redraw the map without a referendum. If voters approve it, the 2025 redistricting fight becomes permanent. If they reject it, the referendum mechanism stays in place, and mid-decade redistricting stays constrained by the requirement that voters approve it.

This is the irony that no court can resolve: the guardrail that worked in September — the referendum mechanism — is itself on the ballot in November, as a question that the voters will have to answer under the very map that the guardrail produced. Voters will choose whether to keep the guardrail the same week they vote under the map it protected. If the referendum passes, they will have voluntarily surrendered the mechanism that just saved them from a mid-decade gerrymander.

Correction and addendum, September 16, 2026: A more detailed account of the September court sequence, reported by Madeleine Carlisle for The Atlantic, shows the chronology above was incomplete in one important respect. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled unanimously for the old map on September 3rd — not as part of the single-day whiplash described above, but nearly a week earlier. Hoskins then appealed that ruling to the U.S. Supreme Court, which rejected his appeal the following Tuesday. It was only thirty minutes after that rejection that a federal district judge, ruling on a separate lawsuit, ordered the opposite: that the 2025 map be used. Hoskins seized on that ruling to announce Missouri would use the new map after all — at which point the Missouri Supreme Court threatened him with contempt of court for defying its own order. The U.S. Supreme Court then stepped in again to restore the old map, and Hoskins was briefly held in contempt before reversing his position to match. That contempt finding, however brief, is a genuine escalation this piece did not capture: a sitting secretary of state held in contempt of his own state’s highest court in the final weeks of an election, for continuing to insist a map the courts had already rejected was still valid.

One additional note for readers: a federal appeals court hearing was scheduled for September 17th, one day before Missouri’s deadline to make absentee ballots available to overseas voters. Legal experts told Carlisle a further change to the map is unlikely at this stage, but as the past two weeks have shown, that is not the same thing as certain.
