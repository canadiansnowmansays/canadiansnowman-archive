Postmark: A garage, anywhere with a 3D printer. A spool of filament. A parts list. Twenty-five hundred dollars. Some assembly required — a great deal of assembly required.

There is a moment, in the life of every transformative technology, when it slips the leash of the institutions that birthed it and lands in the hands of amateurs. The mainframe became the personal computer. The telephone network became the modem, and the modem became the bedroom-coded website. The gene sequencer, once a room-sized instrument tended by white coats, became a mail-order kit. And the moment always arrives the same way — not with a corporate announcement, but with a parts list and a price that a determined hobbyist can just barely justify to a skeptical spouse.

For the humanoid robot, that moment arrived this spring, and it arrived — fittingly, for a series that keeps insisting the future has a human anchor — from an unexpected quarter. In May, as Hugging Face announced on its own blog, the company best known for hosting the open-source machine-learning models this publication’s parent series spends its days examining released something called the LeRobot Humanoid: an open, low-cost, bipedal robot that costs roughly twenty-five hundred US dollars in parts, built from 3D-printed components, off-the-shelf hardware, and affordable electronics, with the complete stack — hardware files, assembly documentation, simulation tools, training environments — released free on GitHub for anyone to build, break, repair, and improve.

Twenty-five hundred dollars. A pair of legs, for now — the release is candid that the current platform is a lower body learning to stand and walk, with the torso and arms still to come. But a pair of legs you can print in your garage, assemble on your kitchen table, and program from a laptop. Part VII of this series went looking for Rosie the robot maid and found her missing. This postcard is about the workshop where somebody is quietly trying to print her, one 3D-printed femur at a time.

The ladder of prices

To understand why twenty-five hundred dollars matters, you have to see the ladder it sits at the bottom of — and the ladder has been collapsing downward at a speed that ought to frighten and thrill in equal measure.

At the top, the machines Part VIII catalogued: Tesla’s Optimus, Boston Dynamics’ Atlas, Figure’s humanoids — priced for Fortune 500 budgets and, in most cases, not really for sale at all. Just below them, the industrial workhorses: Unitree’s flagship H2, a hundred and eighty centimetres of bipedal capability with a bionic face, at forty to sixty-nine thousand dollars. Then the machine that has become the field’s default research platform, Unitree’s G1, starting around thirteen and a half thousand dollars for the base unit and climbing past seventy for the fully-equipped developer model — roughly, as one reviewer put it, twenty-six times cheaper than a Boston Dynamics Atlas. Then Unitree’s newest and smallest, the R1, a modular humanoid starting at forty-two hundred dollars. And now, beneath all of it, the Hugging Face machine at twenty-five hundred, built from parts, owing nobody a licence fee.

Watch what that ladder has done in eighteen months. Part VIII, when I first drafted it, quoted a humanoid price of sixteen thousand dollars and called it startling. By the time the New York Times updated me in June, Unitree machines were selling below five thousand. Now the entry point is a printable parts list at half that again. This is the price curve of the early personal computer compressed into a fifth of the time, and it is happening because — as Part VIII and Part XI both documented — the Chinese electric-vehicle supply chain already mass-produces every component a humanoid needs: the motors, the actuators, the batteries, the sensors. The garage tinkerer of 2026 is buying, at hobbyist scale, from the same catalogue that supplies the state-owned enterprises Beijing just ordered to deploy ten thousand robots. The supply chain does not care whether the buyer is a ministry or a maker. It ships either way.

The Raspberry Pi moment

There is a phrase the robotics press keeps reaching for, and it is the right one: this is robotics’ Raspberry Pi moment.

The Raspberry Pi — the credit-card-sized computer that sold for thirty-five dollars and taught a generation to code — mattered not because it was powerful but because it was cheap enough to not be precious. You could let a child break it. You could try something stupid and only lose thirty-five dollars. That permission to fail, distributed at scale, produced more working programmers than any expensive machine ever could, because learning happens in the space between attempts, and expensive machines make you afraid to attempt.

The Hugging Face humanoid is an explicit bid to do the same thing for embodied AI. Its makers say so plainly, as the project’s own release explained: this is not the most advanced humanoid; it is the humanoid you can build, understand, repair, instrument, simulate, and use for learning experiments. The comparison the coverage kept drawing was to exactly this Raspberry Pi lineage — just as the Pi transformed computing education, an open, reproducible, sub-three-thousand-dollar humanoid could bring robotics into classrooms, makerspaces, and independent workshops that could never justify a fifteen-thousand-dollar research platform, let alone a hundred-thousand-dollar one.

And critically, the release goes beyond dumping CAD files onto the internet — a practice the field has learned to call “performative open-source,” where a company posts files that no outsider can actually build from. Hugging Face released the full stack: the bill of materials, the wiring, the motor setup, the simulation environments where you can test a walking policy before you risk the physical hardware, the pipelines that close the persistent gap between what works in simulation and what works on a real machine standing on a real floor. The company has spent the last year assembling this ecosystem deliberately — it acquired the French open-source robotics startup Pollen Robotics, built the LeRobot software library that now standardizes control across everything from a fifty-dollar arm to a full humanoid, and partnered on a sub-three-thousand-dollar upper-body humanoid called HopeJR. This is not a stunt. It is an infrastructure play, aimed at owning the open layer of physical AI the way Linux owns the server.

The university in the loop

The garage is not building alone, and this is the part of the story that turns a hobbyist curiosity into something with real gravity. The same open platforms that a teenager can print are the platforms the world’s leading robotics labs have standardized on — which means the amateur and the frontier researcher are now, for the first time, working on the same machine.

As NVIDIA announced in the spring, the company released an open humanoid reference design built around its Isaac GR00T platform — and extended support for it explicitly to Unitree’s G1, the very machine sitting in university labs everywhere. The institutions signing on read like a roster of the field’s frontier: Stanford’s robotics centre, ETH Zurich, UC San Diego, the Allen Institute. The director of Stanford’s robotics centre made the logic plain, in words that could serve as this whole postcard’s thesis: robotics moves fastest when researchers can build on open platforms, share code, and test ideas on real machines. An open reference design, he said, lets students and collaborators create, compare, and share robot behaviours on physical hardware — not in a proprietary silo, but in a commons where a breakthrough at one lab can be reproduced at the next by Tuesday.

Consider what that shared substrate means. When a graduate student at ETH Zurich trains a new walking policy on a G1, and a hobbyist in a Canadian garage is running the same open LeRobot software on a printed platform, the improvement one of them discovers can flow to the other. The datasets are hosted on the same public hub. The simulation environments are the same open code. This is the pattern that made open-source software eat the world — not because open code is always better, but because open code compounds, each contribution building on the last, across every workshop and lab at once, with no corporate gatekeeper deciding whose improvement is allowed to matter. The humanoid field has just adopted that pattern wholesale, and the compounding has begun.

The company town and the commons

Here is where the postcard finds its real subject, because the twenty-five-hundred-dollar humanoid is not just a cheap machine. It is one side of a philosophical argument about who the robotics century will belong to — and the other side is visible in the same reporting, if you know where to look.

Consider the two open-source stories running side by side this spring. Hugging Face, an American company built on the open-source ethos, released a humanoid whose entire value proposition is that you own it, modify it, understand it, repair it. And Unitree, the Chinese champion, also went open-source in March — releasing UnifoLM-VLA-0, a vision-language-action model that lets its robots perform household tasks from plain spoken commands, free on GitHub. Two companies, two continents, both embracing openness. But the openness means different things. Hugging Face is opening the whole machine — hardware and software, the right to build the thing yourself. Unitree is opening the software layer that runs on hardware only it manufactures at scale. One is giving you the printing press. The other is giving you free ink for a press only it can build.

This distinction is not academic, and a Canadian — heir to a long argument about whether the important industries should be owned in the commons or in the company town — feels its weight immediately. The dream the Hugging Face project embodies is the maker’s dream, the same one that animated the personal-computer revolution: that a transformative technology is safest and most creative when the largest possible number of ordinary people can get their hands inside it. The reality the Unitree ladder embodies is the platform dream: that the technology will be cheap and abundant and genuinely useful, but rented rather than owned, its deepest workings sealed inside a machine that ships from one factory in Hangzhou in four to eight weeks, on a credit card. Both will exist. The question the garage answers, one printed femur at a time, is how large the commons gets to be before the company town paves it over.

The flaw in the machine

And then there is the detail that keeps this postcard honest, because a series pledged to wonder-first must also report the splinter under the fingernail.

As security researchers disclosed, every Unitree humanoid and quadruped in the field was found to carry a wormable Bluetooth vulnerability — nicknamed UniPwn — that could, in principle, let an attacker hop from one compromised machine to the next, the way a biological contagion jumps between hosts. Fixes have been rolling out. But sit with the implication, because it is the shadow side of the whole democratization story. When humanoid robots were hundred-thousand-dollar rarities in guarded labs, their security was a narrow, containable problem. When they are five-thousand-dollar appliances in tens of thousands of homes and workshops and warehouses — some printed in garages from open files, some shipped by the pallet from Hangzhou — they become something the computer-security world knows and dreads: a vast, uneven, under-patched attack surface, walking around on two legs, within arm’s reach of children and kitchen knives.

The personal computer’s democratization gave us the creative explosion of the modern internet. It also gave us the botnet, the ransomware epidemic, the surveillance economy — because cheap, abundant, connected machines in untrained hands are a gift to the builder and the attacker alike, and the same openness that lets a hobbyist improve a robot lets a bad actor study it. There is no version of the five-thousand-dollar humanoid that arrives without this bill attached. The garage that can print a walking machine is the same garage, in a different hand, that can modify one. This is not an argument against the democratization. It is the democratization’s true price, and it should be named at the counter rather than discovered later.

What the workshop knows

So where does the balcony come down on the garage?

On the side of the garage, mostly — with the splinter acknowledged. The history this series keeps consulting is nearly unanimous on the point: technologies that stayed locked in institutions stayed small, and technologies that reached the workshop grew strange and wonderful in ways no institution would have authorized. The personal computer was a toy for hobbyists that the mainframe companies dismissed, right up until the hobbyists ate the mainframe companies. The people printing legs in their garages this year are doing something that looks, from the boardroom, like play — and play, as any student of innovation knows, is what serious progress looks like before anyone has figured out how to bill for it.

There is a particular Canadian pleasure in this that I will confess to. We are a nation of tinkerers and dark-garage inventors — the country that produced the snowmobile, the electric wheelchair, the pacemaker, insulin, and a robotic arm that outlived every program it was built for. The democratized humanoid is an invitation extended to exactly the kind of person this country keeps producing: the one in the cold garage, past midnight, with a parts list and a stubborn conviction that the thing can be made to work if you just adjust one more joint. That person has never needed permission, and now, for twenty-five hundred dollars and a spool of filament, they do not need a corporation either.

What the workshop knows, that the boardroom forgets, is that the future of a technology is not decided by the people who can afford the expensive version. It is decided by the people who can afford to break the cheap one, and to keep breaking it until it does something nobody expected. The ten thousand robots Beijing ordered will make the headlines. But the machine that changes everything — the one that finally crosses the last mile from almost-working to actually-useful — may well be assembled, first, on somebody’s kitchen table, from parts, by a person who could never have afforded to be told they weren’t allowed.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one came with a bill of materials.
