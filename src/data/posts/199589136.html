I began this series in a kitchen in Toronto, watching five languages negotiate the coffee maker. I end it in the same kitchen, having spent twelve essays and five thousand years trying to answer a simple question: How?

How does a country with more internal diversity than all of Europe combined sustain the largest democratic experiment in human history? How did a civilization that was plundered by a corporation, starved by an empire, partitioned by a lawyer with a pencil, and suspended by a prime minister’s daughter manage to build — and so far, maintain — a constitutional democracy that gives the vote to nearly a billion people?

The honest answer is: I don’t entirely know. And I suspect that nobody does — not the political scientists who study it, not the Indian politicians who operate within it, not the Indian citizens who sustain it with their votes, their patience, and their stubborn insistence on being heard.

But I know more than I did when I started. And what I know has changed the way I think about democracy itself.

I. What the Civilization Teaches

The first thing India teaches is that democracy is not a Western invention transplanted onto foreign soil. It is a human impulse — the impulse toward self-governance, toward the resolution of disputes through deliberation rather than violence, toward the inclusion of the governed in the act of governing — and India’s version of it draws on civilizational resources that predate the Enlightenment by millennia.

Ashoka’s rock edicts — carved in the third century BCE, proclaiming religious tolerance and the duty of rulers to serve the welfare of all people — are not democracy in the modern institutional sense. But they are the moral architecture on which democracy rests: the idea that power has obligations, that governance serves the governed, and that diversity is not a problem to be solved but a reality to be respected.

Ambedkar understood this when he wrote the constitution. He was not copying Westminster. He was building something that had no exact precedent anywhere — a constitutional framework designed from the ground up for a society of incomprehensible diversity, a society in which the very concept of “the people” encompassed Brahmins and Dalits, Hindus and Muslims, Tamil speakers and Hindi speakers, village farmers and Bombay industrialists, all of whom had to be persuaded that the same document could protect all of their interests simultaneously.

The fact that this has worked — imperfectly, unevenly, with regular crises that threaten to end the experiment — is not a vindication of Western liberal theory. It is a vindication of Indian civilizational practice. India holds together because the civilization has been holding contradictions together for five thousand years. The democracy is not imposed on the civilization. It is an expression of it.

II. What the History Warns

The second thing India teaches is that democracy is never safe. It is not a destination. It is a practice — daily, effortful, vulnerable to interruption and decay.

The Emergency demonstrated that the constitution contains the tools for its own subversion. Partition demonstrated that democracy cannot prevent catastrophe when the forces of division are stronger than the habits of coexistence. The Babri Masjid demonstrated that democratic mobilization can be directed toward the destruction of the democratic compact. Gujarat 2002 demonstrated that elected governments can preside over communal violence and be rewarded at the ballot box.

The lesson is not that Indian democracy is a failure. The lesson is that democracy is not self-executing. It requires institutions — courts, election commissions, free press, independent civil services — that are strong enough to resist the encroachment of majoritarian power. It requires a political culture that values pluralism not as an abstract principle but as a practical necessity in a society where no single group can legitimately claim to represent the whole. And it requires citizens who are willing to defend it — not with slogans, but with votes.

The 1977 election remains the most important single event in Indian democratic history. Not because it produced a good government — the Janata Party collapsed within three years. But because it demonstrated that Indian voters could remove a leader who had suspended their rights. The correction came from below. Not from the courts, which capitulated. Not from the military, which stayed neutral. Not from the international community, which was largely indifferent. From the people. Three hundred million of them, standing in queues, marking ballots, and saying: no.

I watched the aftermath of that correction from a sidewalk in London in June 1977, without knowing what I was watching. Morarji Desai — the man the voters chose, the man Indira had imprisoned — rode past me in a limousine at the Silver Jubilee. Pierre Trudeau, the only leader with his window down, acknowledged our yelling and our mad waving. Two democracies, represented by two men in a procession, one of them a former prisoner who had just inherited a restored democracy, the other the prime minister of a country that would not fully understand India for another fifty years. We still don’t. But we are trying. This series is part of the trying.

Whether Indian voters will perform that correction again — whether the mechanisms of democratic self-repair are still functional in an era of media capture, communal polarization, and institutional erosion — is the question that hangs over Indian democracy today.

III. What the Present Demands

I have applied the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — to Modi throughout this series, and I want to be precise about what I am and am not saying.

I am not saying that Modi is a dictator. He is an elected leader, returned to power by the largest electorate in human history, governing through a parliament, subject to a constitution, and operating within a system that — however strained — retains the essential features of a democracy: contested elections, an independent (if pressured) judiciary, a federal structure that distributes power, and an opposition that is weakened but not eliminated.

I am saying that the Hindutva project — the redefinition of India as a Hindu nation, the systematic marginalization of Muslim citizens, the erosion of institutional independence, the capture of media, and the use of state power against political opponents — represents the most sustained challenge to the Indian constitutional order since the Emergency. And I am saying that the parallels to other democracies that have degraded — Hungary, Turkey, Poland, and, in a different register, the United States — are real enough to warrant the comparison and the concern.

The TACO pattern is visible: the threat of communal violence and legal sanction against dissenters; the accommodation of international partners whose strategic interests outweigh their concern for Indian democracy; the rare capitulation when popular pressure becomes overwhelming (the farm laws); and the relentless obfuscation — the management of narrative, the rebranding of authoritarian measures as development or national security, the construction of a personality cult that transcends and obscures the institutional damage beneath.

Whether India is experiencing a slow-motion Emergency — an Emergency by a thousand cuts, without the formal declaration but with similar consequences for civil liberties, press freedom, judicial independence, and minority rights — is a question I pose to the reader, not a verdict I pronounce from a kitchen in Toronto.

IV. What Canada Learns

I am Canadian. I have written this series from the outside, looking in. But the outside is not as far from the inside as it used to be.

Canada is home to 1.8 million people of Indian origin. The Indian diaspora is our largest visible minority. The politics of the subcontinent — the Hindu-Muslim divide, the Khalistan question, the caste system, the BJP’s transnational influence — are no longer foreign policy. They are domestic reality. They are in our parliamentary caucuses, our university campuses, our newsrooms, and our neighbourhoods.

The Nijjar affair demonstrated that the geopolitics of the subcontinent can produce political assassinations on Canadian soil. The farm protests demonstrated that Punjabi-Canadian solidarity can influence Indian domestic policy. The rise of Hindutva-aligned organizations in the Canadian diaspora demonstrates that democratic backsliding in one country can export its ideology to another.

Canada’s mosaic — our polite, self-congratulatory metaphor for multiculturalism — is being tested by the realities of a world in which the countries our immigrants come from do not stay behind when the immigrants arrive. The impossible democracy is not just India’s challenge. It is Canada’s. It is any country that believes that diversity and democracy can coexist.

And the testing goes in both directions. This week — as I write the final words of this series — U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio concluded a four-day visit to India aimed at repairing a relationship frayed by Trump’s tariffs and Washington’s courtship of Pakistan. At the same moment, Indian Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal led a hundred-member trade delegation to Canada — the largest India has ever sent abroad — and sat down with Prime Minister Mark Carney to discuss a future trade agreement. India, in other words, is simultaneously managing its most important strategic relationship (with the United States), rebuilding its most damaged one (with Canada), and doing both on its own terms, on its own schedule, with its own interests firmly in the centre of the frame.

The diplomatic choreography is pure India. Accommodate everyone. Commit to no one. And never, under any circumstances, let anyone else set the tempo. Nehru would recognize it instantly. So would Chanakya, who wrote the manual twenty-three centuries ago.

And so the impossible democracy is no longer only India’s burden to carry.

V. The Impossible Democracy

I have been sharing a kitchen with Indian men for the better part of two decades. The lesson has been consistent and unavoidable, and it is the lesson this series has tried to expand into history, politics, and geopolitics:

India is not one thing. It is a civilization of a billion worlds — each with its own language, its own gods, its own food, its own history, its own grievances, its own dignity. The miracle is not that these worlds sometimes collide. The miracle is that they share a constitution, a flag, a cricket team, and a railway system, and that they do so voluntarily — noisily, imperfectly, sometimes violently, but voluntarily.

India should not work. Every model of political science, every precedent of post-colonial governance, every theory of national development says that a country this diverse, this poor, this fractured by caste and religion and language, cannot sustain democratic governance. And yet it does. Not perfectly. Not without failure. Not without the Emergency, not without Partition, not without Gujarat, not without Kashmir. But it does.

The word “impossible” in this series’ title is not a prediction. It is a description — a description of what India has done and continues to do, every day, against the odds, against the models, and against the forces within it that want to replace the democratic experiment with something narrower and more controllable.

The impossible democracy is impossible because it shouldn’t exist. It is a democracy because, so far, it does.

Whether it will continue to exist — whether the contradictions that the civilization has contained for five thousand years can survive the ideology that wants to resolve them by force — is not a question I can answer from my kitchen. It is a question that 1.4 billion people will answer with their votes, their voices, their choices, and their stubborn, magnificent, impossible refusal to be reduced to one thing.

I began this series by saying that India is not one thing. I end it by saying that this is not India’s weakness. It is India’s genius. And if the rest of us — Canadians, Americans, Europeans, all of us who are struggling with diversity and democracy and the question of whether the two are compatible — can learn anything from the Indian experiment, it is this:

The mosaic holds. Not because the tiles are the same. Because they are not.

This series was written over the course of several weeks in May 2026, in a townhouse in Toronto where five languages are spoken every morning and the spice shelf remains a site of ongoing negotiation.

I owe debts I cannot fully repay.

To Rikesh, who has lived in my house for ten years, who works in IT, who speaks Gujarati and Hindi and English, who introduced me to artificial intelligence and thereby made himself complicit in everything that followed. He has been patient with my questions about India, generous with his knowledge, and diplomatically silent about the volume of essays emerging from my office. His mother Asmita, a retired school principal visiting from Gujarat, questioned whether AI was a legitimate writing partner. She was asking the right question. She usually does.

To Gurneet, who has lived in my house for two years, who is Sikh, who speaks Punjabi and Hindi and English, who carries in his turban and his steel bracelet the visible markers of a faith and a community that have survived persecution, partition, and the ongoing complication of being a minority within a democracy that is redefining itself around a majority it does not include him in. His presence in this house — and in Canada — is part of the story this series tells, whether he signed up for that or not.

To Ugyen, my husband, who is from Bhutan, who speaks Dzongkha and Hindi and English, who is not Indian but who is South Asian, and who has shared his life with a man who writes about other countries’ problems with more energy than he sometimes applies to the dishes. His quiet good humour is the foundation on which this household — and therefore this series — rests.

To Vinod Gupta, my Camera Department Manager at the Woolco in Dufferin Mall in 1975, the first Indian man I knew well, whose home I visited as a young man and where I saw, for the first time, the domestic architecture of a patriarchy I did not yet have the vocabulary to name — a story I tell more fully in the addendum on women. I hope his wife’s life grew easier with the years. I never forgot her.

To the many Indian men who have passed through this house before Rikesh and Gurneet — roommates, boarders, temporary residents whose names I have not all retained but whose conversations, whose food, whose willingness to explain their country to a Canadian who kept asking questions, gave me the education that no university offers and no textbook replicates.

To Dior, the husky, who has slept through all of it.

And to the reader. If you have come this far — through 42,000 words, through five thousand years of civilizational history, through the Indus Valley and the East India Company and Partition and the Emergency and the saffron turn and the Rubio visit and the kitchen — then you have done what I hoped you would do. You have taken India seriously. Not as exotica, not as a problem, not as a market, but as a civilization that has been asking the hardest questions about human diversity and self-governance for longer than most of us knew there were questions to ask.

The impossible democracy continues. The mosaic holds. The kitchen is open.
