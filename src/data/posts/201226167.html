In 2025, the historian Johann Neem stood before the American Historical Association and delivered an argument that he knew would make his colleagues uncomfortable.

Neem had spent years watching the academy’s retreat from national narrative — the suspicion of any single American story, the fracturing of the discipline into subfields, the widespread conviction among serious historians that telling the story of the United States as a unified national story was, at best, naive and, at worst, complicit in the ideological project of a state whose record on its stated ideals was, to put it charitably, mixed. He shared many of these concerns. He understood the arguments. He had written seriously about the exclusions and distortions of the old patriotic histories.

But he had also watched what filled the vacuum. And what he saw alarmed him more than the partial stories the academy had replaced.

His lecture argued that Americans now lived with three competing versions of their national story — none of them dominant, none of them adequate, and all of them producing, in their interaction, something far more dangerous than any of them individually: the collapse of the shared narrative infrastructure that a creedal nation needs to hold itself together. The response was, by the standards of academic conferences, heated. One scholar accused him of “retreat, if not outright capitulation” to the logic of nationalism. Others were more sympathetic. The debate itself was the point: that a room full of professional historians could not agree on whether the American story was worth telling was, in miniature, a version of the same problem Neem was describing.

This part of the series examines his taxonomy in detail — because understanding what the three schools actually argue, and where each goes wrong, is essential to understanding why the mainstream is losing and what it would take to recover it.

I. The Mainstream School: The Hardest Position to Hold

The mainstream school is, Neem argues, how most Americans still think about their country, even though it has largely lost its institutional defenders.

Its core argument is deceptively simple: America is a work in progress. The founding ideals were genuine — the Declaration’s claim that all are created equal was not merely rhetoric, even if the men who wrote it were unable or unwilling to live up to it. The history of the republic is substantially the history of people demanding that the ideals mean what they claim to mean — abolitionists, suffragists, labor organizers, civil rights marchers, and countless others who held the creed up against the practice and insisted on closing the gap. The progress has been real. The distance remaining is also real. The work is unfinished.

This is the tradition of Lincoln at Gettysburg — the “unfinished work” of those who died, the “new birth of freedom” still to be achieved. It is the tradition of King’s “promissory note” — the Declaration as a check written to all Americans, still waiting to be honored in full. It is the tradition of Obama at Selma — “America is not yet finished,” the belief in the ongoing project as “the highest form of patriotism.” It is the tradition, more recently, of the “No Kings” rallies that looked back to the founding era for the vocabulary of resistance.

The mainstream school requires something cognitively and morally demanding: the simultaneous holding of two propositions that appear to be in tension. The ideals are real. The failure to live up to them is also real. Both things are true. Neither cancels the other. The story is neither the flattering version that smooths over the failures nor the prosecutorial version that denies the ideals. It is the actual version — complex, contradictory, and still unfolding.

This is hard. It is considerably harder than either of the alternatives. The post-American school relieves you of the burden of defending the ideals. The hyper-American school relieves you of the burden of confronting the failures. The mainstream school relieves you of neither burden. It asks you to carry both, simultaneously, indefinitely.

That demand is, I think, a significant part of why the mainstream school has lost institutional ground to its competitors. Not because Americans have become less capable of moral complexity — the survey data, which we will examine in Part IX, suggests they haven’t. But because the institutions responsible for transmitting the mainstream school’s framework — the schools, the universities, the civic organizations, the political parties — have largely stopped trying to make the case for it.

II. The Post-American School: The Necessary Overcorrection

The post-American school begins with an observation that is largely correct and arrives at a conclusion that is largely counterproductive.

The observation: the history of the United States has been substantially shaped by racism, genocide, and the systematic exclusion of non-white, non-male, non-propertied people from the political and economic life of the republic. The consensus historians glossed over this. The patriotic narrative deployed in schools and popular culture suppressed it. A honest account of the American past requires centering it — not as a footnote to the main story of expanding liberty, but as a structural feature of how the republic was actually built and maintained.

This is true. The scholarship that established it is important. Anyone who wants to understand American history honestly has to engage with it.

The conclusion: because the national story has been so thoroughly shaped by white supremacy and settler colonialism, the project of telling a single, inclusive national story is itself suspect. Any such story will necessarily reproduce the hierarchies it claims to transcend. The nation-state is not an appropriate organizing framework for historical understanding. The correct response is to decenter the nation — to tell the histories of groups, communities, and social formations rather than the history of a political entity whose organizing myth has always served the interests of power.

Here is where the post-American school produces consequences its proponents did not intend.

First, it cedes the national story to its worst interpreters. A serious scholar can decline to write national history on principle. The Bill O’Reillys and Newt Gingriches of the world have no such scruples. The vacuum left by the academy’s principled retreat has been filled by popular historians whose relationship to evidence is, to put it diplomatically, flexible. The people who decided the story was too compromised to tell gave it to people whose interest in telling it had nothing to do with truth.

Second, it leaves the people who most need the story — the immigrants, the newly naturalized citizens, the communities whose histories the old narrative suppressed — without a place in the national conversation. Telling the histories of groups rather than the history of the nation does not necessarily produce a more inclusive result. It can produce a more fragmented one — a collection of parallel histories that share a geography but not a project, communities that have their own stories but no shared story, citizens of a creedal nation who have been given every reason to doubt the creed but no alternative framework for understanding what they share.

Third — and this is the strange convergence Neem identifies — the post-American school and the hyper-American school agree on more than either would like to admit. Both have concluded that the history of the United States is, above all, a history of whiteness. The post-American school says this means the national story is tainted and should be decentred. The hyper-American school says this means the national story belongs to white Americans and should be protected. The mainstream school — which says the story belongs to everyone who has ever fought to make the creed mean what it says — is squeezed between two versions of the same essentialism.

III. The Hyper-American School: The Political Weapon

The hyper-American school is, in some ways, the easiest to describe and the most dangerous to engage with seriously — because it has largely ceased to be a historical argument and become a political instrument.

Its historical roots are real enough. Throughout the nineteenth century, a simplified patriotic narrative served the function of binding together a diverse and geographically scattered nation — giving Americans from Massachusetts to Mississippi a common set of heroes, a common set of founding myths, a common sense of providential purpose. The narrative was incomplete, often dishonest, and regularly deployed to justify expansion, exclusion, and violence. But it was, in its way, a genuine attempt to construct a shared identity for a creedal nation.

The modern hyper-American school revives this tradition but strips out whatever intellectual honesty it originally possessed. In its current political form — embodied in Trump’s 1776 Commission, in the executive orders on “restoring truth and sanity to American history,” in the removal of displays about George Washington’s enslaved people from Independence Hall and the stripping of passages about Indigenous displacement from Grand Canyon exhibits — it is not history at all. It is the deliberate suppression of historical fact in the service of a political mythology.

As Appelbaum notes, the executive order instructed the National Park Service to remove signs that “inappropriately disparage” historical figures rather than focusing on the “greatness” of American achievements. This is not a historical position. It is a censorship directive. It does not argue that the removed information is false — it argues that true information is inappropriate when it complicates the preferred narrative. The distinction is significant: the hyper-American school, in its current political expression, is not making a historical argument. It is making an authoritarian one.

But the hyper-American school has also, in recent years, made a more intellectually serious argument that deserves to be taken seriously before being rejected. This is the argument, associated with thinkers like Patrick Deneen and others in the national conservative movement, that the consensus liberals got something right: America does have a particular tradition, a particular set of cultural inheritances, a particular way of organizing social and political life that is not reducible to a set of abstract propositions. The creedal nation model, in this critique, is itself a kind of liberalism — a universalism that dissolves particular communities, particular loyalties, particular forms of life into the acid of abstract principle.

There is something to this. A nation cannot be held together entirely by propositions. It needs culture, custom, habit, shared memory — the kind of thing that propositions alone cannot generate. The hyper-American school is right that the creedal model, taken to an extreme, can become an excuse for ignoring the concrete communities and traditions that give the creed its actual content.

Where it goes wrong — fatally wrong — is in concluding from this that the creed should be narrowed rather than deepened. That the “particular people” who get to define American identity should be defined by ancestry rather than subscription. That Vance is right and Appelbaum is wrong — that America is a place with a people, rather than an idea with an argument.

This conclusion does not merely fail on its merits. It fails on its own terms. The “heritage Americans” whose traditions the hyper-American school seeks to protect are themselves the product of immigration, of subscription, of the creedal bargain. The Irish, the Italians, the Eastern Europeans who are now claimed as the core of “real America” were, within living memory, the suspicious outsiders whom the previous generation of heritage Americans regarded with exactly the contempt now directed at more recent arrivals. The hyper-American school wants to close the door behind the last group that walked through it. This is not a tradition. It is a pattern.

IV. The Strange Convergence

Neem’s most important observation — the one that generated the most controversy at the American Historical Association — is also the one that deserves the most attention: the far right and the far left have arrived, by entirely different routes, at the same conclusion about American history.

Both now agree that the story of the United States is, fundamentally, a story about whiteness.

The post-American school says this means the national story is contaminated by white supremacy and must be decentred or abandoned. The hyper-American school says this means the national story belongs to white Americans and must be protected from those who would dilute or critique it. The logical consequence of both positions, pursued to their ends, is the same: a country in which the national story is understood as the property of one racial group, to be contested for or surrendered rather than shared.

This convergence is not accidental. It is the product of decades of history wars in which both sides have accepted, at some level, the framing that race is the organizing category of American history — that the central question is whether white supremacy is the country’s defining legacy (the post-American answer) or its aberrant exception (the hyper-American answer), rather than asking whether a creedal nation’s story can be organized around something other than racial identity at all.

The mainstream school says it can. It says the organizing category of American history is the argument about the creed — who gets included in the proposition “all,” what it costs to demand inclusion, what it costs to deny it, and what the ongoing extension of the creed has made possible. Race is central to that story, but as a fact to be confronted and a wrong to be addressed, not as the story’s organizing principle or its ultimate meaning.

This is a harder position to hold, politically, than either alternative. It cannot be reduced to a bumper sticker. It requires engagement with complexity that neither the culture warriors on the right nor the scholars in retreat on the left have been particularly interested in providing.

V. What the Mainstream Requires

The mainstream school is losing not because it is wrong but because it is hard, and because the institutions that should be making the case for it have largely stopped trying.

What would it take to recover it?

It would require, first, historians willing to write national histories — to attempt the kind of single-volume, accessible, honest account of the American story that Lepore produced in 2018 and that had been missing for a generation before her. This is happening, slowly. But it is swimming against the institutional current of a discipline that still regards national narrative with suspicion.

It would require, second, a political class willing to make the affirmative case for the mainstream school — to argue, in public, that America is a work in progress and that the progress is real and worth defending. This is rarer. Most politicians who might make this argument are afraid of being accused, by one side, of insufficient patriotism, or, by the other, of insufficient critique. The political incentive structure rewards the extremes and punishes the middle.

It would require, third, an education system capable of transmitting the mainstream school’s framework to students who are currently receiving either nothing at all — fourteen percent proficiency, the football coach in the history classroom — or competing mythologies from culture warriors on both sides.

None of these requirements is being met, in 2026, at anything like the scale the crisis demands.

But the hunger is there. The readers who made These Truths a best-seller. The millions who read Letters from an American every morning. The people who show up at “No Kings” rallies carrying copies of the Constitution. They are not asking for the flattering version. They are not asking for the prosecutorial version. They are asking for the true version, told with enough craft and courage to hold together as a story about a country they still, despite everything, want to believe in.

The mainstream school exists for them. The question is whether anyone is going to teach it.
