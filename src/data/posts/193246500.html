Every year, the Elsinore Festival applies to the Canada Council for the Arts for operational funding. Every year, the application is completed by the end of January, reviewed in the spring, and approved in June — at which point Alistair receives the confirmation with the expression of a man who had not genuinely doubted the outcome but had spent five months preparing to be devastated by it.

This has been the rhythm of things for as long as anyone can remember. Gerald Fitch has filed the supporting documentation for nineteen consecutive applications. He has his own folder system for it. The folder system works. These two facts are related.

This year, the Canada Council revised the application form.

Section 7, which had previously asked for a general statement of artistic mandate, now required, in the language of the revised form, “a clear articulation of measurable cultural outcomes for each proposed production, including but not limited to audience reach, community engagement metrics, and demonstrable contribution to the creative economy of the applicant’s region.”

Section 7 requires a clear articulation of measurable cultural outcomes for each proposed production. Gerald is working on it.

Alistair read Section 7 on a Tuesday morning in January. He set the form down. He picked it up. He read it again, in the way that a person reads something they have understood perfectly and are hoping to have misunderstood.

He had not misunderstood it.

The Festival’s 2026 season — Alistair’s farewell season, the one he had titled This Rough Magic with the particular care of a man who knows which words he wants on his last marquee — included, among other productions, King Lear.

Alistair was required to provide measurable cultural outcomes for King Lear.

He sat with this for two days. He made notes. He crossed the notes out. He called a colleague in Toronto who had completed a similar application for a smaller company and asked how she had managed Section 7. She said she had written “increased civic dialogue around themes of power and succession” and left it at that.

He thanked her and hung up and stared at the wall.

He could not write “increased civic dialogue around themes of power and succession” for King Lear. King Lear was not about civic dialogue around themes of power and succession. King Lear was about what happens when the centre fails. It was about fathers and children and the specific madness of a man who gives everything away and then cannot understand why he has nothing. It was about standing on a heath in a storm and discovering that the storm is not the worst thing happening.

He had been thinking about King Lear for thirty-one years. He did not know how to put this in a form field.

Gerald Fitch became aware of the Section 7 situation on a Thursday, when Alistair appeared at the municipal hall to ask, with the careful composure of a man who has decided to ask for help before the alternative becomes necessary, whether Gerald might have any thoughts on completing grant applications.

Gerald had nineteen years of thoughts on completing grant applications. He said he was happy to help.

He read Section 7. He read the season schedule. He read Alistair’s notes, which covered four pages and included the phrase “the inadequacy of metric systems to measure the irreducible” twice, underlined. He removed his reading glasses. He put them back on. He asked Alistair what he wanted Section 7 to say.

Alistair said he wanted it to say that audiences would leave changed in ways that could not be predicted or quantified and that this was the entire point and always had been.

Gerald said he would see what he could do.

He typed for eleven minutes. He showed Alistair the result.

It read: “Audiences will leave changed. We cannot say how. This is, we submit, the point.”

Alistair read it. He looked at Gerald. He looked at it again.

“That’s it,” he said.

Gerald printed it. He filed a copy. He paper-clipped it to the application with the efficiency of a man who has been doing this for nineteen years and sees no reason to make it more complicated than it is.

Wayne had submitted his tourism development application in the first week of January. It was four pages. He had included photographs. The photographs were well-lit and clearly labelled. He had received an acknowledgment within seventy-two hours. He did not mention this to Alistair. There are things Wayne understands about the management of information.

The Swan Warden submitted the annual swan habitat maintenance grant on behalf of the Swan Habitat Preservation Fund of Perth County without apparent difficulty. She noted at the February committee meeting that the swan habitat application had always included a clear statement of measurable outcomes, because swans were countable and their habitat was measurable and their contribution to regional tourism was documented in Wayne’s own quarterly figures. She said this without looking at Alistair. She moved on to the next item.

Alistair did not respond. He was thinking about King Lear.

Also at the February committee meeting, Gerald noted that he had received a public comment submission regarding the federal arts funding framework and had filed it in the appropriate folder. He distributed copies.

The submission was from Bradley Fenn, who had moved to Elsinore from Mississauga seven years earlier and served on the planning committee. It was three pages, organised, clearly written. It used the phrase “ideological capture of publicly funded cultural institutions” on page one and again on page two. It argued that the Canada Council’s funding criteria should include an assessment of whether recipient organisations reflected a diversity of perspectives rather than, in Bradley’s phrasing, “a narrow progressive consensus that does not represent the full range of Canadian values.” It did not specify what the full range of Canadian values was, or who had determined it, or who would assess compliance.

Gerald had filed it without editorial comment, because filing things without editorial comment is what Gerald does. He had, however, placed it in a sub-folder he labelled, with characteristic precision, Comments — External — Unsolicited.

Carly Meadows, who had been covering the committee meeting for the Beacon, asked Gerald afterward whether she could see the submission. Gerald gave her the folder number. She found it, read it, and called Bradley Fenn for comment. Bradley said he stood by the submission and believed arts funding should reflect the values of all Canadians. She called Alistair. Alistair gave her four minutes in the parking lot, which produced two quotable sentences and one paragraph she had to summarise because it ran to the end of her recording. Norman read her draft, removed two adjectives he considered redundant, and ran it on page two below a photograph of the Festival Theatre marquee. He told her it was solid. This was, in Norman’s vocabulary, a considerable compliment.

The Swan Warden had read the submission before the meeting ended. She put it in her bag. She had said nothing about it for three weeks, as of this writing. She is watching.

Jim walked past the Festival Theatre on a Thursday morning in February with the labrador, the vizsla, and the corgi from Waterloo Street. The sandwich board outside the box office read: This Season: King Lear. Measurable Outcomes: TBD.

He looked at it for a moment. The labrador looked at a squirrel. Jim moved on.

He mentioned the sandwich board to Maggie at Sunday dinner. Maggie said she’d heard about the Section 7 situation — Alistair’s colleague had brought the rescue greyhound in for a follow-up, and had mentioned it in the way that people mention things in waiting rooms when they are waiting and the thing is on their mind.

Jim said that for what it was worth, the outcomes of King Lear seemed measurable enough to him: you went in and something happened and then you were different. He said this was how most things worked if you were paying attention. He did not elaborate. He passed the bread.

Maggie said she thought Gerald had probably got it right.

Jim said he thought so too.

I did not speak at the committee meeting when the grant application came up for discussion. It was not my place.

But I will say here that my family has a connection to this stage going back to its early years. My great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside was a Canadian diplomat of considerable distinction — a man who operated at the level of nations, who advised prime ministers, who understood the relationship between countries as a set of daily decisions made by specific people in specific rooms. His grandson Eric apprenticed at the Festival in its early years and went on to a career of forty years across three countries, in theatres and on screens, playing fathers and detectives and men of complicated histories. He started where Alistair’s actors start: on the stage at the south end of Ontario Street, learning what the work requires.

None of this appears in Section 7 of any grant application. You cannot measure what a stage produces in the people who stand on it and go out into the world. You can only note, eventually, where they went.

Gerald put it better, in eleven minutes. I acknowledge this.

The Perth County farmer drove past the Festival box office on his way to the feed supply in late January. He read the sandwich board. He drove on. He was asked, at the feed store, what he thought about the measurable outcomes business.

He said it seemed like a lot of paperwork for a play.

He paid. He drove home. This was, in its way, a more complete assessment of the situation than anything in Alistair’s four pages of notes, which Alistair would have acknowledged if anyone had asked him, which no one did.

The Canada Council approved the grant in June. It always does. The approval letter described the Festival’s application as “compelling and clearly articulated.” Gerald filed the letter. He filed a copy of Section 7 in a second folder, separately, because he thought it might be useful someday. The season opened on April twenty-first. The Tempest first. Then Godot. Then, in July, King Lear.

The audiences left changed.

We cannot say how. That was, all things considered, the point.

A Note for the Record

The Canada Council for the Arts wants measurable cultural outcomes. The form is bureaucratic and the question reductive and Alistair spent four pages explaining why before Gerald compressed it to two sentences. But Section 7 contains, underneath its form-field language, a fundamental assumption: that culture produces something worth measuring. That the attempt to quantify it, however inadequate, is made in good faith by people who believe the activity has value.

To the south, PBS and NPR are being defunded. Not because they failed to demonstrate outcomes — their audiences number in the tens of millions. They are being defunded because someone has decided the content fails an ideological test. Not: prove your value. But: prove your loyalty. Section 7 assumes culture is worth measuring. The southern version doesn’t grant the premise.

Bradley Fenn’s submission used the phrase “ideological capture.” It is in Gerald’s sub-folder. The Swan Warden has read it. Carly has written about it. Norman ran it on page two.

Gerald’s answer to Section 7 — audiences will leave changed, we cannot say how, this is the point — was written to satisfy a bureaucratic requirement. In the current climate it reads, increasingly, as a statement of principle worth keeping.

The storm, as Lear told us, is not the worst thing happening.

The Moonbeam Column appears in the Elsinore Beacon every Sunday morning. The Canada Council for the Arts is real. Section 7 is real. Gerald’s sub-folder is real in every way that matters.
