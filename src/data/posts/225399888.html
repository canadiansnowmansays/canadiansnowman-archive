On Thursday evening, 12 November 2026, Senator Raphael Warnock landed in Atlanta and went straight from the airport to the National Center for Civil and Human Rights, where Adam Harris was waiting to interview him in front of a live audience. “Didn’t pass go,” Warnock said, “didn’t collect $200.” The conversation went out four days later as an episode of the Radio Atlantic podcast, and The Atlantic published the transcript on 16 November.

Warnock had reason to be in a good mood. A month earlier he had been one of the few national Democrats to campaign in Mississippi for Scott Colom, raising hundreds of thousands of dollars for a campaign most of his party had written off. “We lose elections by the margins of our disengagement,” he said at the time. “Mississippi is a blue state; it just doesn’t know it yet.” On 11 November, Colom was declared the winner. The last part of this series told the story of that race and of the blue slip that kept Colom off the federal bench. This part is about what Warnock thinks should happen next, and whether any of it can.

He began with a question he wanted the audience to ask themselves. Everyone had spent the previous week wondering when Mississippi would finish counting. The better question, he said, was why it was taking so long.

Checking the senator’s answer

Warnock’s answer came in three steps. People had been purged from the rolls, so they had to cast provisional ballots. They had to cast them on Election Day, because “you don’t vote early in Mississippi without an excuse.” And Election Day itself was a working Tuesday, which made the whole system, in his words, “a poll tax on working people because the only time you can vote is on Tuesday during work hours.”

The first step is well documented. Toluse Olorunnipa and Yvonne Wingett Sanchez reported in The Atlantic on 9 November that Mississippi’s inactive list had swollen to about 230,000 by Election Day, roughly one registered voter in nine. Inactive voters were sent to affidavit ballots. One of them was Virginia Gordon-Lang, a Marine veteran with 15 years of active duty, who had moved in April and filed her change of address. She was told her ballot would be thrown out. “I want my vote back,” she said. The secretary of state, Michael Watson, called the inconvenience “well worth the election integrity added by having cleaner voter rolls!”

The third step is where the senator went further than the facts. One reader of the Atlantic transcript, a former elections worker, pointed out that Mississippi does let people vote before Election Day if their job keeps them from the polls. He is right. The list of excuses published by the Mississippi Secretary of State includes anyone “required to be at work on election day during the times at which the polls will be open,” and anyone on call during those hours. Such voters can cast an absentee ballot in person at the circuit clerk’s office.

So Tuesday is not literally the only time a working Mississippian can vote. But look at when that office is open. This year in-person absentee voting ran from 19 September to 31 October, Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., with two Saturday mornings added at the end. A shift worker who cannot get to a polling place between 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. on a Tuesday is told to visit a government office during the ordinary working day instead, and to state a reason. The excuse system doesn’t remove the problem Warnock described. It moves it to a different day of the week and adds paperwork.

Notice, too, who doesn’t need an excuse at all. Mississippians aged 65 and older qualify for absentee voting by age alone. The state has decided that retirees may vote at their convenience and that people still working must explain themselves. That is not a poll tax in the legal sense; the Twenty-fourth Amendment settled that. But it is a cost, it is unevenly spread, and it falls on the voters with the least time to spare. Warnock overstated the rule. He did not overstate its effect.

A right, on paper

“Don’t ever say voting is a privilege,” Warnock told the audience. “Driving is a privilege. Voting is a right.” He reminded them that Black Americans had the right to vote on paper for nearly a century, from the Fifteenth Amendment in 1870 to the Voting Rights Act in 1965, before the country made any serious attempt to let them use it. Mississippi, he said, “embodies that, but it’s not alone.”

The paper right had protection for 48 years. Section 5 of the Voting Rights Act required states and counties with a history of discrimination to get federal approval before changing their voting rules. In 2013, in Shelby County v. Holder, the Supreme Court struck down the formula that decided who was covered, which switched preclearance off. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that the country had changed. Ruth Bader Ginsburg, in dissent, wrote that throwing out preclearance when it was still working was “like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet.” Warnock paraphrased the line, then added that her words “proved to be tragically correct.”

He also cited a number, and this one checks out exactly. In March 2024 the Brennan Center for Justice published a study of nearly a billion voter-file records from 2008 to 2022. It found that the racial turnout gap, the difference in turnout between white and nonwhite voters, has been growing almost everywhere. It also found that the gap grew almost twice as quickly in the places that used to be covered by preclearance as in comparable places that never were. Shelby County didn’t create the gap. It made it grow faster in exactly the places Congress had singled out for protection.

That finding matters for how Democrats read 3 November. Black turnout in the South surged this year, and it carried Colom over the line. Much of it was anger at the maps themselves. In North Carolina, early votes cast by Black voters were running 57 percent ahead of 2022 by late October, according to The Atlantic, and voters there kept bringing up the redrawn lines. But a surge measures what people can overcome when they are angry enough. The turnout gap measures what happens in an ordinary year, when they aren’t. Policy has to be written for the ordinary year. A party that treats this autumn as proof the barriers no longer matter will be making Roberts’s mistake from the other side.

The agenda, and the arithmetic

Asked what strengthening the right to vote would look like, Warnock gave a list. Pass the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act. Reverse Citizens United, “even if we have to do it by a constitutional amendment.” Get rid of partisan gerrymandering. And make sure “the so-called SAVE Act never sees the light of day.”

Each of those has a history, and the history is mostly of defeat.

The John Lewis Act would rebuild preclearance on a new footing. Instead of the old formula, which the Court said rested on decades-old data, coverage would depend on a recent record of voting-rights violations. As Warnock described it, the bill would also deal with dark money. Strictly speaking, the money and gerrymandering provisions belonged to its companion, the Freedom to Vote Act. The two were combined into a single bill in January 2022, when Democrats last held both chambers. On 19 January 2022 it fell short of the 60 votes needed to end a filibuster, and a vote to change the filibuster rules for this one bill failed too, because Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema would not go along.

The new Senate will have a Democratic majority, but not one of 60. When Mary Peltola was declared the winner in Alaska on 17 November, The New York Times reported, Democrats reached 56 seats, the party’s largest tally in more than a decade and still four short. And even a bill that passed both chambers would land on the desk of a president who has spent two years trying to tighten voting rules, not loosen them. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of each chamber. A constitutional amendment to undo Citizens United needs two-thirds of Congress and then 38 state legislatures. Warnock knows all of this. He has been in the Senate since January 2021 and watched the 2022 bill die.

The SAVE Act is the only item on his list that he wants to stop rather than pass. It would require documentary proof of citizenship, such as a passport or birth certificate, to register to vote, and it passed the House in April 2025. Stopping a bill is the one thing a Senate minority, or a narrow majority, can reliably do. With Democrats running the Senate in January, that part of his agenda looks secure.

So the honest reading of the big list is that it is a statement of what Democrats will fight for, not a forecast of what they will get. Which is why the most interesting thing Warnock said came next.

Why skinny

“In the meantime,” he said, “I would like to offer in the new Congress a slender and skinny bill to deal with gerrymandering. And I have that bill.”

The reasoning was a pastor’s and a politician’s at once. Republican legislatures had been redrawing congressional maps since 2025, and after the Supreme Court decided Louisiana v. Callais last spring, more of them rushed to do it a few months before the election. This series followed the process through Florida, North Carolina and Missouri, where, as The Atlantic reported in September, candidates spent months unsure where their own districts’ boundaries were. “Well, as we say in church, God works in mysterious ways,” Warnock said. “And maybe, maybe after some of my Republican friends got crushed in their own gerrymandered districts, there might be some takers who will join me in this effort to get rid of gerrymandering altogether.”

There is a real argument buried in that joke. A partisan gerrymander works by spreading your own voters thinly enough to win as many seats as possible, by modest margins. In a normal year that is efficient. In a wave year it is dangerous, because a seat drawn to be won by five points is lost when the national environment moves fifteen. Political scientists call the result a "dummymander." The House popular vote this year went to Democrats by about 13 points, the largest margin since 1976, and in North Carolina the freshly drawn Republican seats fell with it. There, the new map didn't protect the people it was drawn for. It exposed them. On Sunday 22 November the chair of the House Rules Committee, Virginia Foxx, became the latest, losing her North Carolina seat by about 300 votes after a recount. Her defeat gave Democrats 258 seats, as Tim Balk reported for The New York Times, the largest House majority either party has won in a national election since 1992.

That gives Warnock his pitch. A ban on partisan gerrymandering, or a requirement that states use independent commissions, can be sold to Republicans as insurance. If maps are drawn fairly, a bad year costs you the seats you should lose, not the extra ones you stretched to win.

The weight on the ankle

The weakness in the pitch is that North Carolina was the exception. A preliminary estimate by The New York Times found that Republicans still came out four to seven seats ahead because of the maps they redrew in 2025 and 2026. North Carolina was the one state where the effort appears to have backfired, and only narrowly. Everywhere else, the new lines either helped Republicans or made no difference. Democrats won the House anyway, by margins big enough to overcome the lines in most of the targeted districts. That is not the same as the lines failing.

The two parties read the results in opposite ways. Kevin McCarthy, the former Republican speaker, told The Times that the redraws had failed, and that in some cases the new lines "probably played against us." The Democrats' campaign committee said in a memo that every Democrat who ran for re-election in a district redrawn by Republicans had won, among them Don Davis in North Carolina's First, the seat this series followed in Part LXII. But the Republican pickups that came straight from the new maps tell the other half of the story. In Tennessee, Steve Cohen chose not to run again in his carved-up Memphis district. In Texas, Julie Johnson chose not to defend her redrawn seat in the Dallas suburbs. Both seats went Republican. The maps did not have to beat those incumbents. They persuaded them not to try.

Adam Serwer set out what the lines did in The Atlantic on 20 November, cautioning that votes are still being counted. In Texas, Democrats won more than half the popular vote and came away with 14 seats to the Republicans’ 24. In Florida, about 48 percent of the vote is set to give Democrats eight of 28 seats. In Tennessee, Democrats won about 44 percent and Republicans are set to hold eight of nine. The gerrymander that ran the other way, California’s, leaves Republicans four seats out of 52 on roughly 30 percent of the vote. Political geography explains some of this, since Democrats crowd into cities and suburbs while Republicans spread across the countryside, but not all of it. The Times estimated that party-blind maps might have taken Democrats close to a veto-proof two-thirds of the House, with nearly 20 more seats in Texas, Florida and Georgia alone.

Justin Levitt of Loyola Law School gave Serwer the right image for this. A runner who wins a 100-yard dash with a 75-pound weight tied to one ankle has done something remarkable, but the race was still rigged. And the people who tied on the weight expect it to work next time. Destin Hall, the Republican speaker of the North Carolina House, has said no map can withstand a swing of 15 or 20 points and that he expects his party to take the lost seats back. Nobody in Raleigh is talking about redrawing. The lines were built for an ordinary year, and an ordinary year will come.

That is the real case for the skinny bill, and Levitt made it in a sentence: voting rights belong at the front of the agenda so that "it doesn't take an extraordinary election with extraordinary turnout" to produce results that should be routine. It is also why some conservative commentators have drawn exactly the wrong lesson. The Wall Street Journal editorial board held up the year's minority winners, Colom among them, as proof that fears about structural racism had been overblown. But Colom won a Senate seat, decided by the popular vote of the whole state. It is the one race no line-drawer can touch. So did Annie Andrews, South Carolina's first Democratic senator in nearly three decades, as Emily Davies reported for The New York Times, and so did James Talarico in Texas, the same state where Democrats won more than half the House vote and came away with 14 seats out of 38. Colom's victory says a great deal about Black voters in Mississippi. It says nothing about maps.

The other trouble with the pitch is the audience. The Republicans who were “crushed in their own gerrymandered districts” will not be in the next Congress to vote for anything. The ones who survived are disproportionately the ones in the safest seats, the people for whom the current system works best. The lesson of the dummymander is clearest to those who no longer have a vote. Persuading the survivors will mean persuading them that the next wave could reach them, too, and that the next map might be drawn by the other party.

Then there is the president. Donald Trump pushed Texas into its mid-decade redraw in 2025 and cheered the rest. A bill that ended the practice would need his signature or a veto-proof majority, and it is hard to see either. Days before the election he told The New York Post he would become "Mr. Veto" if Democrats took Congress. Even 258 seats is 32 short of the two-thirds needed to override him in the House. Warnock's own advice to the other side is the best summary of the problem. "When you get a result that you don't want in an election," he said, "what you're supposed to do is course-correct. Don't start playing with the lines."

From Toronto, all of this has a familiar answer. Canada took the drawing of federal ridings away from politicians in 1964, when Parliament handed the job to independent commissions, one per province, chaired by a judge. Nobody here pretends the commissions are perfect, but nobody campaigns to bring back the old way either. It is the sort of reform that looks impossible right up until it is done, and then looks obvious.

Even if the skinny bill dies, offering it does some work. It puts every senator on the record, including the newest one from Mississippi. It gives state reformers, who have had more success than Congress, a federal standard to point to. And it tests whether the Republicans who spent November watching some of their maps fail have learned anything, which is worth knowing before 2028.

The pastor in the Senate

Harris asked how Warnock keeps his faith at the centre of his politics. “I am not a senator who used to be a pastor,” Warnock replied. “I’m a pastor who serves in the Senate.” He still returns to the pulpit at Ebenezer Baptist Church every Sunday, partly, he said, because “I don’t wanna spend all my time talking to politicians. I’m afraid I might accidentally become one.”

The line got a laugh. The thought behind it connects to everything above. Warnock called empathy “the first step of good public policy and good politics,” and asked: “If you can’t see your neighbor, how are you going to represent your neighbor?”

Put that question to the Mississippi system. A Marine veteran who did everything the state asked of her when she moved was still pushed onto an affidavit ballot. A worker who can’t make it to the polls on a Tuesday is told to come in on a weekday instead. A state official looks at the inconvenience and calls it well worth it. None of that requires anyone to dislike the people involved. It only requires that nobody making the rules is thinking about them.

January 5 and January 6

Warnock’s book, The Crooked Places Made Straight, argues that America “is both January 5 and January 6.” On 5 January 2021, Georgia elected a Black pastor from public housing and the Jewish son of an immigrant to the Senate on the same night. The next morning Warnock was doing television interviews, feeling, as he put it, that he had arrived. By lunchtime the Capitol was under attack. When people said January 6 was not who Americans are, he told the audience, he understood the impulse, but it wasn’t true. “It’s a part of who we are, and when we’re in denial about that, we can’t fix it.”

Harris asked the obvious question: if this election was a January 5, how do you prevent the next January 6? Warnock answered with a story rather than a programme. His father, a Second World War-era veteran, was once made to give up his bus seat to a white teenager while wearing his soldier’s uniform. “And decades later,” Warnock said, “the man who had to give up his bus seat has a son who sits in a Senate seat. We’re both of those things.”

That is the right way to read 3 November. Colom’s win is a January 5. The purged rolls, the excuse list, the rushed maps and a turnout gap that grows faster where the protections were removed are the conditions that make the next January 6 more likely, because they teach people the rules can be bent when the other side might win. The skinny bill will probably fail. But it is aimed at the right thing: the lines themselves, drawn by the people who benefit from them. Course-correct, Warnock told the Republicans. Don’t play with the lines. It is advice the country has never quite taken, and Mississippi has just shown what it costs to keep ignoring it.
