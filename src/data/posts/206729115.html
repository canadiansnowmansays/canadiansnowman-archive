Every monarchy examined across the previous twenty parts of this series lost power, kept power, or negotiated power away under some form of pressure, whether domestic reform movements, foreign colonial ambition, military defeat, or simple financial exhaustion. Bhutan’s Wangchuck dynasty offers this series something this entire project has not encountered once in twenty prior parts: a king holding genuinely secure, unthreatened, and functionally absolute authority, facing no meaningful domestic uprising, no foreign colonial pressure comparable to what toppled Hawaii’s crown in Part XIX, and no financial crisis of the kind that forced Louis XVI’s hand in Part XIV, who nonetheless decided, entirely on his own initiative and against the wishes of much of his own population, that his country should become a democracy.

Bhutan’s monarchy is, by the standards of nearly every case examined in this series, remarkably young. The country had been governed for centuries under a dual system, established in the seventeenth century by the Tibetan lama and military leader Ngawang Namgyal, dividing spiritual authority between a Je Khenpo, the head of Bhutan’s Buddhist monastic establishment, and temporal authority between a series of regional governors called penlops, an arrangement that, much like the divided sacred and secular authority this series traced through Tonga’s Tuʻi Tonga line in Part XX and shogunal Japan in Part XVI, proved chronically unstable and prone to internal rivalry among competing regional strongmen. Ngawang Namgyal himself, known to Bhutanese history and religious tradition as the Zhabdrung Rinpoche, deserves a moment’s attention as the figure who first gave Bhutan its distinct national identity at all, since without his own unifying work there would have been no coherent Bhutanese state for the later Wangchuck dynasty to eventually rule. A Tibetan lama who fled to Bhutan in 1616 following a succession dispute within his own religious lineage in Tibet, Ngawang Namgyal united Bhutan’s previously fragmented valley communities under a single religious and administrative system, successfully repelled several Tibetan military invasions aimed at unseating him, and codified a legal and monastic governance structure, the dual system this series has just described, that gave Bhutan its own distinct cultural and political identity separate from Tibet for the first time, laying the institutional groundwork three centuries before Ugyen Wangchuck’s own unification of the office of Druk Gyalpo itself.

This instability was finally resolved in 1907, when Ugyen Wangchuck, the penlop of Tongsa who had already emerged as Bhutan’s most powerful regional governor through a combination of military success and skillful diplomacy with British colonial authorities in neighboring India, was unanimously elected as Bhutan’s first hereditary Druk Gyalpo, or Dragon King, by an assembly of the country’s religious leaders, government officials, and regional chiefs, unifying temporal and spiritual legitimacy under a single new royal office for the first time in Bhutanese history.

The dynasty’s early reigns focused substantially on the delicate diplomatic task of preserving Bhutanese sovereignty in the shadow of British India and, later, an increasingly assertive independent India and an expansionist China, a positioning problem this series recognizes as broadly similar to the pressures facing several of the smaller monarchies examined in earlier parts, though Bhutan’s particular geographic position, wedged directly between two of the twentieth century’s most populous nations along the sensitive Himalayan frontier, gave this challenge unusual and enduring strategic weight. Bhutan signed a treaty with independent India in 1949 that granted India guidance over Bhutan’s foreign affairs in exchange for Indian recognition of full Bhutanese internal sovereignty, a relationship considerably more favorable to Bhutan’s own independence than the outright absorption this series documented befalling India’s own five hundred princely states in Part X, and one that has proven durable and largely uncontroversial within Bhutan itself across the following decades, later updated through a revised 2007 treaty that removed India’s formal guidance role over Bhutanese foreign policy entirely while preserving the close security and economic partnership underlying the original arrangement, a modernization that occurred, notably, in the very same period as Bhutan’s own domestic democratic transition, suggesting a broader and evidently deliberate pattern of the fourth king updating nearly every major structural relationship the kingdom depended on before handing full authority to his successor.

It was the third Druk Gyalpo, Jigme Dorji Wangchuck, reigning from 1952 until his death in 1972, who began the slow process of modernizing Bhutan’s governing institutions, abolishing serfdom, introducing Bhutan’s first National Assembly in 1953 with a meaningful, if still limited, degree of elected representation, and beginning the country’s cautious opening to the outside world after centuries of deliberate isolation comparable in spirit, though considerably less rigidly enforced, to the isolation this series traced through Tokugawa Japan in Part XVI. But it is his son, the fourth Druk Gyalpo, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, who ascended the throne in 1972 at only sixteen years of age following his father’s sudden death, whose reign produced the single most extraordinary act of voluntary self-limitation examined anywhere in this entire series.

Jigme Singye Wangchuck governed, for the great majority of his thirty-four-year reign, with genuinely absolute formal authority, commanding real personal popularity and legitimacy among his own population that gave him no obvious external or internal reason to surrender any of it. It was during this same reign that he articulated the philosophy for which Bhutan has since become internationally known, Gross National Happiness, first raised publicly in 1972 in response to a journalist’s question about Bhutan’s low gross national product, when the young king reportedly replied that Gross National Happiness was more important than Gross National Product, a remark that developed over subsequent decades into a formal governing framework built around four specific pillars, sustainable and equitable socioeconomic development, preservation and promotion of Bhutanese cultural heritage, conservation of the natural environment, in a country that remains one of the very few carbon-negative nations on earth, and good governance. This framework, whatever its practical limitations and the genuine skepticism some development economists have directed at it as a governing metric, gave Bhutan’s monarchy a distinctive and internationally admired legitimating story considerably more sophisticated than the simple resource-wealth bargain this series examined in the Gulf states in Part XVII, one grounded in an explicit and publicly articulated theory of what a state’s power is actually meant to be used for.

The decision that makes this reign genuinely singular within this entire series, however, is not Gross National Happiness itself but what Jigme Singye Wangchuck did with the concentrated authority that philosophy operated under. Beginning in earnest in the 1990s and accelerating through the early 2000s, the king began systematically transferring his own executive authority to a newly empowered Council of Ministers and, eventually, to a parliamentary system entirely of his own design, culminating in Bhutan’s first written constitution, drafted under his direct personal supervision and circulated for public consultation across the entire country in a series of town hall meetings the king himself attended, in which, by numerous documented and widely reported accounts, ordinary Bhutanese citizens repeatedly urged their own king not to give up his absolute authority, expressing genuine confusion and even distress at a monarch who appeared to be voluntarily diminishing an office they had no complaint against and saw no need to see reformed.

The town hall consultation process itself is worth describing in a bit more detail, since it shows a monarch treating public buy-in as genuinely important even though he held more than sufficient formal authority to simply impose the new constitution by decree without any consultation whatsoever. Over the course of several years, drafts of the proposed constitution were distributed to every household in Bhutan, and the king and crown prince personally traveled to remote villages across the kingdom’s difficult mountainous terrain to explain the proposed changes directly to citizens, many of whom, according to numerous journalistic accounts of these sessions, responded with visible confusion or open reluctance, having known no other system of governance in their own lifetimes or their parents’ lifetimes and seeing no particular grievance against a monarchy whose recent stewardship of the country they broadly trusted. This is, this series should note directly, close to the exact inverse of every other popular pressure for reform examined across the previous twenty parts, a population actively resisting a reduction in royal authority that its own king was determined to impose upon them for their own long-term benefit as he understood it, rather than a population demanding constraints on a reluctant crown.

Jigme Singye Wangchuck proceeded regardless, and in December of 2006, at the height of his own personal popularity and with no domestic crisis, foreign pressure, or financial emergency of any kind forcing his hand, he abdicated the throne entirely in favor of his own son, Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck, then only twenty-six years old, specifically in order to ensure that Bhutan’s promised transition to full constitutional democracy would occur under a young monarch who would grow into his own reign as a genuinely constitutional figure from its very outset, rather than asking an older king, himself raised and legitimated under the older absolute system, to preside uneasily over his own authority’s dismantlement. Bhutan’s first fully democratic parliamentary elections followed in March of 2008, and the new constitution took formal effect that same year, transforming Bhutan within a single royal transition from one of the last genuinely absolute monarchies on earth into a constitutional democracy with an elected Prime Minister and National Assembly holding the great majority of substantive governing power, a transformation this series has not seen accomplished anywhere else through such an entirely voluntary and unforced royal initiative.

The young king who inherited this newly constrained office, Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck, has since built a considerable independent public standing of his own within the new system, presiding as a genuinely popular ceremonial and cultural figure rather than a diminished or resentful one, a distinction that mattered enormously for whether his father’s entire project would actually succeed rather than curdle into quiet royal dissatisfaction with the new arrangement. His marriage in 2011 to Jetsun Pema, a commoner considerably younger than himself, was celebrated across Bhutan with genuine popular enthusiasm and international media attention that further cemented the monarchy’s continued cultural centrality even as its formal political power had been deliberately reduced, and the couple’s public visibility, including extensive travel across Bhutan’s own remote rural districts to maintain direct personal contact with ordinary citizens in a manner reminiscent of the Ashanti and Zulu kings’ own emphasis on visible, demonstrated engagement examined in Part XII, has helped ensure that Bhutan’s monarchy retains deep genuine affection despite holding only a fraction of the authority his father once wielded without question.

The reasoning Jigme Singye Wangchuck himself offered publicly for this decision deserves to be quoted at some length, because it stands in such stark contrast to the reasoning, or absence of reasoning, offered by nearly every other monarch examined across this series’ previous twenty parts. He argued, across numerous public addresses during the transition period, that a country’s long-term stability and prosperity could not be guaranteed to rest indefinitely on the wisdom or character of any single individual monarch, however well-intentioned, and that Bhutan’s own future security depended on building durable democratic institutions while the country still had the luxury of doing so gradually, deliberately, and peacefully, rather than waiting for a crisis, a succession dispute, or a less capable future king to force the issue under considerably less favorable circumstances. This is, in effect, the exact argument this series has traced Britain arriving at only gradually and reactively across three centuries in Part XVIII, and Tonga’s own Tupou V arriving at only under the direct pressure of the 2006 riots in Part XX, articulated instead as a fully proactive first principle by a king facing no comparable pressure whatsoever.

It is worth being honest about the genuine complications and limits of this otherwise remarkable story, since an uncritical account would do this series’ broader project a disservice. Bhutan’s modernization under the Wangchuck dynasty has not been free of serious controversy: the government’s citizenship and cultural assimilation policies during the 1980s and 1990s, requiring adherence to Bhutanese dress and cultural norms drawn substantially from the dominant Ngalop ethnic group, led directly to the forced expulsion or flight of a substantial portion of Bhutan’s ethnic Nepali Hindu minority population, the Lhotshampa, beginning in the early 1990s, with well over one hundred thousand people ultimately displaced into refugee camps in Nepal for years or, in many documented cases, decades, an episode Bhutan’s own international reputation as a uniquely enlightened kingdom rarely accounts for and one this series notes here rather than passing over in silence. Bhutan’s celebrated Gross National Happiness framework and its voluntary democratic transition, in other words, coexisted with a genuinely serious human rights failure affecting a substantial ethnic minority within the same kingdom during broadly the same period, a reminder this series has offered before and will offer again that no monarchy’s legitimating story, however genuinely admirable in one dimension, should be accepted as the complete account of that monarchy’s actual conduct.

What Jigme Singye Wangchuck’s voluntary abdication and democratic transition adds to this series’ broader argument, set against every other case examined across the preceding twenty parts, is a genuinely rare demonstration that the various pressures this series has traced compelling monarchies toward reform, popular uprising, foreign intervention, financial collapse, military defeat, are not, in fact, the only route by which a monarchy’s power can be voluntarily and durably reduced. A sufficiently secure, popular, and far-sighted monarch, facing none of those pressures at all, can simply choose to give the bulk of his own authority away, on the theory that his country’s long-term interest is better served by durable institutions than by his own family’s continued concentration of power, a choice this series has not documented being made anywhere else, under anywhere near comparably favorable and unforced circumstances, across its entire survey of monarchy from ancient Mesopotamia to the present day.

Part XXII turns from Bhutan’s voluntary transition to a monarchy that chose the opposite strategy from nearly every neighbor discussed in this part of the series, preserving its full traditional authority intact into the present day specifically by cultivating an aura of such total, untouchable reverence around the crown that even discussing its limits remains, to this day, a criminal offense.
