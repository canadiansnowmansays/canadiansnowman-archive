In the autumn of 1961, a young historian named Howard Zinn was teaching at Spelman College in Atlanta — a historically Black institution whose students were, at that moment, sitting at lunch counters and marching in the streets as part of the civil rights movement. Zinn marched with them. He was arrested with them. He wrote about them. And he began to understand, with a clarity that would eventually produce one of the most widely read works of American history ever published, that the story being told in American classrooms bore almost no resemblance to the America his students were living in.

A People’s History of the United States, published in 1980, argued that conventional American history had been written from the perspective of the powerful — the Founders, the presidents, the industrialists, the generals — and that an honest account of the American past required telling it instead from the perspective of those on whom power had been exercised: the enslaved, the colonized, the exploited, the dispossessed. The book sold more than two million copies. It is still in print. It has shaped the political education of several generations of Americans, particularly on the left.

It is also, in ways Zinn himself might have acknowledged, a profoundly partial book — a counter-mythology rather than a complete history, as likely to mislead in the direction of total condemnation as the consensus historians’ books were likely to mislead in the direction of total celebration. But its success tells you something important about the hunger it was feeding. Millions of Americans wanted a history that did not require them to suppress what they knew about their country’s actual past. They wanted a history that included them — all of them, not just the ones who happened to have won.

The academic revolution that Zinn’s popular work reflected produced scholarship of extraordinary value and introduced a generation of readers to histories they had never been taught. It also, as a structural matter, consumed the national narrative without replacing it. This is the paradox at the heart of the history the history wars produced: a revolution that was necessary, largely correct in its critique, and deeply consequential in its unintended effects.

I. What the Revolution Got Right

Let the record show, before anything else, that the historians who dismantled the consensus school were correct in their essential argument.

The American story that prevailed in classrooms and popular culture through the mid-twentieth century was not merely incomplete. It was, in its key structural assumptions, dishonest in ways that had real political consequences. It treated the exclusion of Black Americans, women, Indigenous peoples, and immigrant communities as peripheral footnotes to a central story of expanding liberty — rather than recognizing that those exclusions were not incidental to the story but constitutive of it. The wealth that financed the Revolution was substantially built on enslaved labor. The land on which the republic was established was taken, by force and by fraud, from peoples whose civilizations had existed there for millennia. The democracy that the consensus historians celebrated as exceptional was exceptional in ways that depended, structurally, on the non-participation of the majority.

These are not left-wing talking points. They are historical facts, documented in primary sources, established through the kind of rigorous archival scholarship that the academy is designed to produce. The historians who produced this scholarship — C. Vann Woodward on the South, John Hope Franklin on African American history, Gerda Lerner on women’s history, Vine Deloria on Indigenous experience, Herbert Gutman on labor — were doing what historians are supposed to do: following the evidence where it led, regardless of whether the destination was comfortable.

The result was an enrichment of American historical understanding that was, and remains, genuine and valuable. We know more about the actual American past than any previous generation. We have more tools for understanding it, more voices from its margins, more honest accounts of its contradictions. The revolution was necessary. It was largely right. And it produced, as a byproduct, a structural problem that its architects did not adequately address.

II. The Structure of the Problem

The problem was not that the new historians told the wrong story. The problem was that they stopped believing a national story was worth telling at all.

This is a subtle distinction that matters enormously. The critique of the consensus school was a critique of content — the consensus historians had gotten the story wrong by getting the cast of characters wrong, the power dynamics wrong, the moral weight of exclusion wrong. A reasonable response to that critique would have been to tell a better story: more honest, more inclusive, more willing to sit with the contradictions rather than smoothing them over. And some historians attempted exactly this.

But the dominant response in the academy was different. It was a critique not merely of the content of the old story but of the form — of national narrative itself. The very act of telling the American story as a single story, with a single through-line and a shared set of protagonists, came to be seen as inherently suspect. Any such story, it was argued, necessarily privileged some perspectives over others. Any attempt to find a common thread through the American past was an attempt to impose a false unity on a genuinely diverse and conflicted history. The nation-state, in this framing, was not an appropriate organizing category for historical understanding. It was a political construction that served the interests of those already in power.

As Jill Lepore has observed, this intellectual position, whatever its merits as a theoretical proposition, had a practical consequence that its proponents did not fully reckon with: it ceded the terrain of national narrative to people with far fewer scruples about evidence and far stronger motivations for mobilization. The historians retreated from the national story at precisely the moment when the national story was most contested and most in need of serious stewardship.

What happened in the academy was, in miniature, what has happened in American democratic institutions more broadly. The people with the most sophisticated understanding of how things work — and the most legitimate claim to authority within their domain — concluded that their domain was compromised and withdrew from it. And the space they vacated was filled, as spaces always are, by people who had no such scruples about compromise.

III. The 1619 Problem

No single episode illustrates the consequences of this dynamic more clearly than the reception of The New York Times’ 1619 Project.

Published in 2019, the 1619 Project was an attempt by journalist Nikole Hannah-Jones to reframe the American founding story — to argue that the true founding of the United States should be dated not to 1776 but to 1619, when the first enslaved Africans arrived on American shores, and that the contributions and suffering of Black Americans should be placed “at the very center of our national narrative” rather than at its margins. As Hannah-Jones told Appelbaum, the project sought to chronicle “the struggle of Black Americans to perfect these ideals of liberty, freedom, equality in the law, equality in society” — which she described as “a redemptive, unifying narrative.”

The project was, in many respects, an attempt to do exactly what the academic revolution had failed to do: to tell a national story that included what had been excluded, that centered what had been marginalized, that gave Black Americans their rightful place in the story of their own country. For this ambition it deserves credit.

It was also, in key respects, historically overreaching — and the historians who said so were not all conservatives looking for a political weapon. Some of the most eminent historians of the American founding and of slavery — Sean Wilentz, Gordon Wood, James McPherson, Victoria Bynum — wrote to the Times to object to specific factual claims in the project, particularly the argument that the American Revolution had been fought, in significant part, to preserve slavery in the colonies. This claim, they argued, was not supported by the historical evidence. The Times’ response — to correct a factual error in the online version while refusing to issue a formal correction — pleased no one and illuminated the degree to which the project had become a political document as much as a historical one.

Trump, predictably, found in the 1619 Project a perfect foil. He attacked it as “toxic propaganda, ideological poison that, if not removed, will dissolve the civic bonds that tie us together.” His 1776 Commission, constituted as a direct counter, produced a document so credulous in its celebration and so thin in its scholarship that it was publicly repudiated by the American Historical Association, the Organization of American Historians, and virtually every serious professional historian in the country.

And there, in miniature, is the structure of the problem: a project that sought to expand the national narrative, overreached on key historical claims, and handed the right a political weapon; a counter-project that was not history at all but political theater dressed in academic language; and in between them, the vacuum where a serious, honest, inclusive national story should have been — and wasn’t.

IV. The Lepore Moment

In 2018, something important happened.

Jill Lepore — one of the most respected historians in the United States, a Harvard professor and New Yorker staff writer — published These Truths: A History of the United States. It was 800 pages long. It told the American story from the arrival of Columbus to the election of Donald Trump. It did not flinch from the atrocities — the genocide, the slavery, the systematic exclusion of women and minorities from the political life of the republic they had built. It did not pretend the ideals were fake. It held both things simultaneously: the genuine aspiration and the genuine failure, the promise and the betrayal, the distance between the creed and the practice.

It was the first book of its kind — a serious, single-volume history of the United States by a major historian — in a generation.

It became a best-seller.

Lepore was astonished by the reader response. People wrote to her from across the country — not historians or academics but ordinary citizens who had been waiting, without knowing it, for someone to try to tell the whole story. The woman studying for her citizenship exam who kept reading past the exam, because the story was too compelling to put down. The readers who said they had always known the dark chapters were real but had never seen them integrated into an account of the country that also took its ideals seriously.

The response to These Truths told Lepore something she had not quite expected: that the retreat from national narrative had not produced a public that was comfortable with narrative absence. It had produced a public that was hungry — ravenous, in some cases — for someone to try to tell the truth about the country they lived in. Not the flattering version. The whole thing. The journey, as that citizenship exam reader put it.

Heather Cox Richardson, a Boston College history professor, launched a newsletter in 2019 on the premise that “to understand the present, we have to understand how we got here.” Letters from an American became the most successful newsletter on Substack. By the mid-2020s she had millions of subscribers reading her daily dispatches — a historian’s account of the American present, grounded in the American past, told with the assumption that the story was coherent enough to be told and important enough to be heard.

The mainstream school was not dead. It had been abandoned by the institutions responsible for it. The public had not abandoned the hunger for it.

V. What the Revolution Should Have Built

I want to end this part with an argument rather than a verdict.

The historians who dismantled the consensus story were right. The story they dismantled was incomplete, partial, and in its consequences often harmful. The scholarship they produced has made American historical understanding richer, more honest, and more capable of accounting for the actual complexity of the American past. None of that should be conceded.

What should be conceded — what the evidence of the last fifty years makes unmistakably clear — is that dismantling a story without building a replacement is not a neutral act. In a creedal nation, the national story is not merely a cultural product. It is infrastructure. It is the mechanism by which the creed is transmitted from one generation to the next — not as dogma, but as argument. As something worth arguing about. As a shared reference point for a shared project.

The consensus historians built that infrastructure badly. They built it on a foundation that excluded most of the people the creed claimed to include. But they built it. And when the next generation tore it down — with excellent reasons and correct arguments — the failure to build something in its place was not a minor academic oversight. It was a civic catastrophe whose consequences are still unfolding.

The revolution ate the narrative. The narrative was imperfect and needed to be eaten. But a creedal nation that has consumed its own story has to build a new one, or stop being a creedal nation.

That is the unfinished work. Not of the Founders, who left plenty of their own. But of the historians, the teachers, the journalists, the public intellectuals who dismantled a story they knew was wrong and then, for reasons that made sense within the academy, declined to build a better one.

The hunger is there. The Lepore reader proved it. The Richardson millions proved it. The people are not, most of them, asking for the flattering version. They are asking for the true one, told with enough coherence to hold together as a story.

That is what a creedal nation requires. That is what this series is about.
