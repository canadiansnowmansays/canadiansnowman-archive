CanadianSnowmanSays | Sunday Edition

A 79-year-old president, in possession of the most powerful office on earth, generated this image and posted it. The author presents it without further comment, on the grounds that further comment would be redundant.

Having established, in the preceding essay, the full weight of the evidence — the identical photograph, the birtherism, the racial psychology, the comparative record across approval ratings and economic performance and global standing and legal conduct and physical condition — I find myself returned to a simpler question.

Why?

Why the identical photograph, posted without apparent awareness that it was the same photograph? Why “Hussein,” deployed in 2026 as though it retains the power it was meant to have in 2011? Why the Nobel Prize grievance, nursed across seventeen years like a splinter that has worked its way too deep to extract? Why, on his 80th birthday, with the presidency of the United States in his hands and a cage fight being constructed on the South Lawn, does this man still need to be better than Barack Obama?

I have been thinking about this carefully.

I have consulted the psychological literature. I have reviewed the political record. I have considered the structural arguments about racial resentment and motivated reasoning and the politics of white grievance, all of which are real and documented and important.

And then, sometime around Tuesday, it came to me.

It is envy.

Simple, ordinary, garden-variety envy. The oldest and most undignified of the seven deadly sins. The one that makes you post the same photograph twice because you needed it to be different. The one that makes a 80-year-old president of the most powerful nation on earth lie awake thinking about a prize given to someone else in 2009.

Envy. I should have seen it sooner. I apologize for the delay.

Let us take the inventory, because envy of this magnitude deserves to be fully catalogued. Future scholars will want the complete record.

The Nobel Prize. Obama won the Nobel Peace Prize in October 2009, nine months into his first term, awarded by the Norwegian Nobel Committee for his “extraordinary efforts to strengthen international diplomacy and cooperation between peoples.” Obama accepted it with the grace of a man who understood the weight of the honor and the complexity of receiving it mid-presidency. “I do not view it as a recognition of my own accomplishments,” he said, “but rather as an affirmation of American leadership.”

Trump has been publicly agitated about this prize for sixteen years. “If I were named Obama, I would have had the Nobel Prize given to me in ten seconds,” he said. The Nobel Committee was not asked to respond to this. The Nobel Committee has also clarified, when asked, that prizes cannot be transferred. Trump was passed over in 2025. He has since noted, more than once, that “nobody else settled wars” and that “Obama got the Nobel Prize and he had no idea why.” The prize, like the man, will not go away.

He has been standing here since 2009. The coffee is cold. Oslo has not called.

The inauguration crowd. On January 20th, 2017, at approximately 12:01 PM, before the new president had issued a single executive order or made a single policy decision, the White House dispatched its press secretary to deliver a statement about crowd sizes. The statement was false. Obama’s 2009 inauguration had drawn a larger crowd. The photographic evidence was unambiguous. The statement was issued anyway. This was the first official act of communication from the new administration. Not policy. Not vision. Crowd sizes.

A psychologist might note that beginning your presidency by lying about crowd sizes suggests a preoccupation with your predecessor’s crowd sizes. I merely observe it.

The approval ratings. Trump has spent both terms selectively citing polls showing him ahead of Obama at comparable points in their presidencies, invariably from conservative-leaning outlets, invariably contradicted by broader aggregates. The FiveThirtyEight aggregate, which combines multiple polls, shows Obama consistently eight to ten points higher than Trump at equivalent stages. Trump’s response to this has been to question the methodology, the pollsters, the aggregators, and the general concept of polling when it produces results he dislikes. Obama’s response to Trump’s approval rating obsession has been, as best anyone can determine, not to have one. This asymmetry is itself informative.

The physical condition. Obama runs half-marathons. He kite-surfs. He shoots hoops with the easy fluency of a man who has played the game his whole life. He appears in public at 64 looking like a person who has treated his body as an instrument rather than a container. Trump, who has publicly stated his belief that the human body possesses a finite amount of energy that exercise depletes — a theory not endorsed by medical science at any point in recorded history — moves between golf carts and motorcades and has arranged his birthday celebration around watching other men exert themselves in an octagon while he observes from a front-row seat. The Village People, as I noted in the preceding essay, could not have choreographed the Pride Month symbolism more elegantly.

The envy of physical vitality is perhaps the most poignant item in the inventory. You cannot fake a half-marathon. You cannot post an identical photograph of a half-marathon and label one “Hussein Obama” and one “Trump” and expect the clouds to look different. The body keeps an honest record.

The wish. And here, before we proceed to the library, we must pause for a moment on something Trump said in 1989. Not last week. Not during the birther years. In 1989, before Barack Obama had entered public life, before anyone outside Chicago’s South Side had heard his name, Donald Trump sat down for an interview with NBC News and said the following:

“A well-educated black has a tremendous advantage over a well-educated white in terms of the job market. And, I think, sometimes a black may think that they don’t really have the advantage or this or that but in actuality today, currently, it’s a great... I’ve said on occasion, even about myself, if I were starting off today I would love to be a well-educated black because I really believe they do have an actual advantage today.”

He wished he was Black.

"I would love to be a well-educated black because I really believe they do have an actual advantage today."
— Donald J. Trump, NBC News, September 5, 1989
Barack Obama was 28 years old. He had not yet entered public life. The envy had no face yet.

Let that settle for a moment. Take your time. There is no rush.

Notice the architecture of the statement. The grievance comes first — the claim that educated Black men have an unfair advantage over educated white men in the job market. Then the wish that follows from it. This is not a compliment. It is a complaint dressed as an aspiration. The man was not celebrating Black achievement. He was resenting it, and then confessing that he wished he could claim it for himself.

The man who would spend the next three decades questioning whether a Black man was legitimately American — who deployed “Hussein” as a weapon, who demanded birth certificates, who built his entire political identity on the claim that Barack Obama’s achievement was somehow unearned or illegitimate — had already, privately and on camera, revealed the source code. The envy preceded the target. Obama simply gave it a face.

This is not hypocrisy in the ordinary political sense. It is something more consuming and more revealing. A man so eaten by envy that he convinced himself Black men had an unfair advantage — and then spent decades trying to strip one particular Black man of every advantage, every credential, every legitimacy. The wish curdled into resentment. The resentment curdled into birtherism. The birtherism curdled into the identical photograph posted on his 80th birthday, still needing the Washington Monument to look different.

It never did. It never will.

The presidential library. The Obama Presidential Center is being built on the South Side of Chicago, designed by the architectural firm Tod Williams Billie Tsien Architects, representing a genuine legacy institution in the community that shaped the 44th president. It is a real building, designed by real architects, grounded in a real place with real significance.

This week, Trump posted an AI-generated image depicting the Center as a tower topped with a garbage bag, surrounded by a tent city, captioned as what the library will look like “when fully matured.”

The man building a UFC cage on the South Lawn of the White House and comparing it to the Eiffel Tower generated artificial intelligence imagery to mock his predecessor’s actual architectural legacy.

Read that sentence again at your leisure.

The competence. This is the envy that underpins all the others, and the one that is hardest to satirize because it is, at bottom, genuinely sad. Obama was good at the job. Fluent, disciplined, intellectually serious, emotionally contained, capable of holding complexity without collapsing it into slogans. He read. He thought. He listened. He changed his mind when the evidence warranted. He surrounded himself with people who were smarter than him in their domains and listened to what they said. He left office with his marriage intact, his family’s dignity intact, his country’s global reputation — while battered by eight years of partisan warfare — recognizably intact.

Trump has spent a decade trying to unpick every stitch of this record. The Iran deal, withdrawn. The Paris Agreement, withdrawn. The ACA, attacked repeatedly and unsuccessfully. The environmental regulations, rolled back. The alliances, strained. The norms, shredded. The fundamental project of his presidency, as more than one observer has noted, has been less the pursuit of his own vision than the erasure of his predecessor’s.

This is not governance. It is envy with an executive order.

And here is where the envy curdles into something uglier, and where satire must briefly yield to honesty: the envy is not only about accomplishment. It is about what Trump believed, and has always believed, Obama should not have been able to accomplish. A man who built his political career on the claim that Obama was not legitimately American, who deployed the middle name as an othering device, who questioned the Harvard degree and the Nobel Prize and the inauguration crowd — that man was not only envious of Obama’s success. He was incredulous at it. He could not accept that it was earned. And the reason he could not accept that it was earned is the reason this comparison has always been about more than politics.

The identical photograph tells you everything. The man needed the Washington Monument to look different under him. He needed it so badly that he posted the same image twice and did not notice, or did not care, that it was the same. The monument looked the same. The world could see it. The man posting it could not.

That is envy. Pure, undignified, magnificent in its pettiness, seventeen years deep and showing no signs of abating.

Obama, for his part, is 64 years old, building his library, running his half-marathons, and receiving standing ovations when he enters rooms.

The Washington Monument has no comment.
