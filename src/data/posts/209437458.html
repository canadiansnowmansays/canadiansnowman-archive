On June 12, 2026, a new rule took effect in Canada. Under Telecom Regulatory Policy 2026-43 and section 27.04 of the Telecommunications Act, service providers were prohibited from charging fees to activate or modify a service plan — the “junk fees” that make it costly to sign up, switch, or leave. The stated purpose was plain: to make it easier for Canadians to change providers and chase a better deal without being punished for the privilege.

It is worth pausing on how quickly the rule was tested.

Bell Canada had moved first, even before the deadline. On May 6, Commission staff noticed a new $40 “device-handling charge” and wrote to Bell to say it did not appear to qualify for the exemption the policy allows for genuinely optional products. On June 9, staff noticed TELUS had introduced a $15 fee for a SIM card or eSIM, and wrote a similar letter. And on June 16 — four days after the ban came into force — staff learned that Rogers had begun charging a $40 device setup charge, a $25 shipping charge, and an unspecified SIM fee.

Three carriers. Three sets of brand-new fees. All appearing within a window of weeks around the very moment a rule arrived to prohibit fees of exactly that kind.

“We considered this practice to be compliant”

What happened next is the part worth dwelling on, because it tells you how this system actually behaves under pressure.

The Commission did not merely grumble. It wrote to each company and asked a direct question: have you stopped charging these fees, and if not, explain why you believe they are lawful. The answers, now on the public record, were a study in creative interpretation.

Bell confirmed it would continue charging its device-handling fee, arguing that buying a phone is an optional transaction separate from signing up for service. TELUS confirmed it had not stopped charging its SIM fee, arguing that a SIM card has many uses — replacing a damaged one, travel — and so is not really an activation fee at all. Rogers confirmed it had not stopped either, offering the fullest catalogue of explanations: the device setup charge was tied to “assisted” purchases and therefore optional; the SIM fee was old, not new; the shipping charge was “a standard charge assessed across all industries.”

Each argument is individually plausible. That is the point. Presented with a rule that says you cannot charge people to activate service, a large company with capable lawyers will always be able to describe the new charge as something else — a handling fee, a setup fee, a shipping fee, a fee for a thing that is technically optional if you squint. The Commission’s own position cut through it neatly: a phone and a SIM card are required to deliver the service. You cannot make them optional by relabelling the invoice.

None of the three backed down. So on June 30, the Commission escalated, launching a show-cause proceeding against Bell, TELUS, and Rogers together. The stakes it named are not trivial: administrative monetary penalties of up to $10 million per company, and up to $25,000 for the individual officers or directors who authorized the charges.

The tell

By mid-July, there was movement — of a particular kind. Rogers and its Fido brand quietly cut the $25 shipping charge to $9. A retreat, apparently, under scrutiny.

Then came the detail that tells you everything. Rogers noted on its website that the $9 price is a limited-time offer, and that the fee will return to $25 on September 8 — which is to say, just before the season when new phones are announced and people are most likely to be buying one. The concession was engineered to expire precisely when it would cost the company the most to keep.

This is not a story about villains twirling moustaches. It is a story about incentives. A company’s job is to earn revenue; a regulator bans one route to it; the company finds another; the regulator bans that; and so on, indefinitely. It is whack-a-mole, and the mole has shareholders and a legal department.

Why this matters for advance billing

Regular readers know I have spent the better part of a year on a narrower question: why do telecom carriers bill you thirty days in advance, for service you have not yet received, when every other essential utility bills you after the fact?

The fee saga is the same problem wearing different clothes, and it makes the argument for a structural fix better than I could on my own.

Consider what the piecemeal approach requires. The Commission must notice each new fee, write letters, demand justifications, weigh the “optional product” defence, launch a proceeding, and threaten penalties — for every fee, one at a time, forever. It is enormously resource-intensive, it is always reactive, and it is always a step behind the next relabelled charge. The carriers can invent new fees faster than any regulator can prohibit them.

Advance billing is the deeper version of the same dynamic. So long as you are charged ahead for service you have not received, there will be phantom balances when you cancel, refunds you must chase, late fees on money you never truly owed, and a permanent supply of billing disputes for the Commission and the complaints ombudsman to adjudicate one customer at a time. You can regulate the symptoms of advance billing indefinitely — mandate prorated refunds, cap this, prohibit that — and the underlying practice will keep generating new versions of the same harm.

Or you can fix the root. Bill for service actually delivered, and the phantom balance never exists. There is nothing to refund, nothing to relabel, nothing to dispute. The whole category closes.

That is the difference between playing whack-a-mole and turning off the machine.

Where this stands

I should be careful to keep two things separate, because they are separate. The fee show-cause proceeding is its own matter, distinct from the consultation on advance billing. But they belong to the same family, and together they answer two objections I hear constantly.

To those who say the CRTC is captured and will never act against the carriers: it has just launched an enforcement proceeding against all three at once, with millions in potential penalties. It acts.

And to those who say advance billing is simply “a business decision” beyond the regulator’s proper reach: the Commission has now confirmed, in a procedural ruling on the harmonization proceeding, that advance billing is within the scope of that proceeding — a legitimate question for parties to address. The public record of that ruling notes the specific questions raised: whether advance billing serves consumer protection objectives, whether billing in arrears should be required, and whether real-time balance visibility should be mandated.

The question is on the table. The deadline to add your voice is August 11. And the lesson of the fee saga is the best argument I know for why the structural answer is the right one: you can ban a fee, and watch three new ones take its place by Friday — or you can fix the practice that keeps producing them.
