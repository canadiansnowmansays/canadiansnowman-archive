In June 2020, Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong.

The law was enacted without going through Hong Kong’s legislature. It was drafted in secret, published the evening it took effect, and criminalised secession, subversion, terrorism, and collusion with foreign forces in terms broad enough to cover virtually any significant political opposition. It established mainland Chinese jurisdiction over cases that Beijing deemed sufficiently serious. It created a new security agency in Hong Kong staffed by mainland officials operating outside Hong Kong law. It applied to anyone in the world who took actions deemed to violate it — a jurisdictional claim without precedent in Hong Kong’s legal history.

Within months, the pro-democracy movement that had filled Hong Kong’s streets with millions of demonstrators in 2019 was dismantled. Its leaders were arrested, charged, and prosecuted. Its political parties dissolved. Its newspapers closed — Apple Daily, Hong Kong’s most prominent pro-democracy publication, published its final edition in June 2021 after its assets were frozen and its executives arrested. The annual Tiananmen vigil was banned. The civic institutions that had distinguished Hong Kong from the mainland — its independent judiciary, its free press, its civil society organisations — were not abolished formally but were stripped of the space and protection that had made them functional.

The “one country, two systems” framework that Deng Xiaoping had negotiated with Margaret Thatcher in 1984 — the commitment to preserve Hong Kong’s distinct systems for fifty years after the 1997 handover — expired not in 2047 but in practice in 2020, twenty-seven years early. The framework had always rested on a political commitment rather than an enforceable legal guarantee. When Xi decided the commitment was no longer convenient, the framework collapsed.

The Hong Kong episode has implications that extend beyond the city itself, and they have not been missed in Taipei. The “one country, two systems” model was originally designed with Taiwan as the ultimate audience — the arrangement that was supposed to make reunification attractive to a democratic Taiwan by demonstrating that Beijing could honour its commitments to maintain distinct systems within Chinese sovereignty. Hong Kong’s experience has rendered this demonstration counterproductive: Taiwan’s public opinion has moved consistently away from any arrangement involving Chinese sovereignty since 2020, and the political parties that favour closer ties with Beijing have lost ground in successive elections. The tightening in Hong Kong was a strategic choice that achieved its short-term objective — suppressing the pro-democracy movement — while substantially damaging China’s long-term political prospects for peaceful reunification with Taiwan. Xi’s government has not publicly grappled with this contradiction.

Xinjiang is where the tightening has been most severe, and most contested.

The Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region in China’s far northwest is home to approximately 12 million Uyghurs — a Turkic Muslim people whose language, culture, and religion are distinct from Han Chinese. Uyghur separatist movements and terrorist attacks, primarily by groups affiliated with or inspired by global jihadist ideology, had killed hundreds of people in Xinjiang and in other parts of China between the 1990s and the mid-2010s. The Chinese government’s response evolved from conventional counterterrorism and policing toward something qualitatively different beginning around 2017.

The internment of Uyghurs in what the Chinese government describes as “vocational education and training centres” — and what UN human rights bodies, Western governments, and survivor accounts describe as mass detention camps — has been documented through leaked government documents, satellite imagery, academic research, and survivor testimony. The Xinhua News Agency’s own internal documents, leaked to the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists in 2019, include operational manuals for the camps describing the use of forced political indoctrination, restrictions on religious practice, and surveillance designed to identify and suppress any expression of Uyghur cultural or religious identity.

The scale is disputed. Estimates of the number of Uyghurs detained range from several hundred thousand to more than a million, based on different interpretations of satellite imagery and government documents. The Chinese government has denied the characterisation of the camps as detention facilities and has disputed the higher estimates. Independent verification is impossible because access to Xinjiang for foreign journalists and researchers has been severely restricted since 2017.

The United States government, the European Parliament, the Canadian Parliament, and several other governments have characterised the policy as genocide or crimes against humanity. China has rejected these characterisations as interference in its internal affairs. The legal debate about which characterisation is accurate — whether the policy meets the strict definition of genocide, which requires intent to destroy a group “as such” — continues among scholars.

What is not seriously disputed is that the Chinese government has implemented a systematic programme using mass surveillance, mass detention, forced labour, restrictions on religious practice, family separation, and compulsory political indoctrination to suppress the cultural and religious identity of the Uyghur population. The surveillance infrastructure deployed in Xinjiang — facial recognition systems, DNA collection, phone monitoring, neighbourhood checkpoint systems, predictive policing algorithms — has since been rolled out in modified form across China. Xinjiang was the laboratory. The country is the experiment.

The COVID pandemic of 2020 to 2022 belongs in any account of the tightening, because it demonstrated both the capabilities and the limits of Xi’s system at its most extreme.

China’s initial response to COVID — the suppression of early warnings by Wuhan health authorities, the delayed public acknowledgement of human-to-human transmission, the management of the WHO’s access — illustrated the information dynamics that this series has documented across multiple historical episodes. Local officials did not want to report bad news upward. The system produced a delay that cost the world dearly.

The subsequent Zero COVID policy, maintained for nearly three years, demonstrated the system’s capacity for blunt-force social control at a scale no democratic government could have attempted: city-wide lockdowns enforced by QR codes and phone-based location tracking, mandatory quarantine facilities for positive cases and close contacts, apartment welding rumoured in some accounts and confirmed in others, a surveillance and compliance apparatus that would have been unthinkable in any country governed by consent rather than authority.

The policy was eventually abandoned in December 2022, abruptly and without adequate preparation, after protests in multiple cities — including a striking demonstration in Shanghai in which protesters held up blank white sheets of paper, the whiteness itself conveying what could not be said in words. The blank paper was not merely a clever protest symbol. It was an accurate description of the space that political expression occupied in Xi’s China: empty by design, available only if it remained empty. The moment protesters put words on the paper, the paper became a criminal document. The blankness was the statement.

The abandonment produced a wave of infections that caused significant mortality among an elderly population that had not been adequately vaccinated. The government’s handling of this final phase, including the suppression of excess death data, illustrated again the information dynamics that the Peng Dehuai passage in Part VI described: a system that produces false data upward when the truth is politically inconvenient.

COVID, in the long view, was a stress test of the Xi system. The system passed the authoritarian portion of the test — it could lock down a continent-sized country for three years — and failed the adaptive portion. It could not correct itself when the policy was producing worse outcomes than alternatives. The pattern is familiar.

Taiwan is where the tightening has been least visible from the outside but most strategically significant.

China’s military posture toward Taiwan has shifted measurably under Xi. The People’s Liberation Army Air Force has dramatically increased the frequency of incursions into Taiwan’s Air Defense Identification Zone — from occasional demonstrations to near-daily operations that have required Taiwanese aircraft to scramble repeatedly, depleting pilot hours and maintenance budgets. Naval exercises in the waters around Taiwan have become more frequent and more operationally realistic. The August 2022 military exercises, conducted in response to US House Speaker Nancy Pelosi’s visit to Taipei, included the first PLA rocket forces drills that simulated encirclement of the island and the first missiles fired over Taiwan’s territory in decades.

The exercises were not simply political signalling. They were operational rehearsals — demonstrations of capabilities that would be required in an actual military operation and opportunities to identify and address planning gaps. The PLA that conducted those exercises in 2022 is more capable than the PLA that conducted similar but less extensive exercises in 1996, during the last major Taiwan Strait crisis. The gap between the capabilities required to invade Taiwan and the capabilities the PLA actually has has narrowed significantly during Xi’s tenure, though how much it has narrowed and whether it has narrowed enough to make an invasion militarily feasible without catastrophic costs remains the central question of Indo-Pacific security analysis.

Xi has been more explicit than any previous Chinese leader about the timeline for Taiwan’s “reunification.” The formulation that the Taiwan question “cannot be passed down from generation to generation” — made in a speech in 2021 — has been interpreted as a deadline implied but not specified. Whether Xi intends to force the issue militarily, through economic coercion, through political pressure, or through a combination of these approaches that does not require a direct assault is the question that preoccupies the intelligence agencies of every country with a stake in the outcome. The Formosa series of this Middle Kingdom section addressed it in detail. The history series can only note that the tightening of China’s Taiwan posture is of a piece with the tightening in Hong Kong and Xinjiang: Xi’s China is pressing its territorial claims more explicitly, more insistently, and with more evident willingness to accept international costs than any previous Chinese leadership.

The Wolf Warrior diplomacy that accompanied these policies represents a change in Chinese diplomatic style that is both symptom and cause.

Chinese ambassadors and foreign ministry spokespersons who, under previous leaderships, had operated in the register of careful, measured diplomatic language began, under Xi, to respond to foreign criticism with direct, aggressive, and sometimes insulting rhetoric. Zhao Lijian, the Foreign Ministry spokesman who became the most prominent practitioner of the style, circulated a fabricated image depicting an Australian soldier murdering an Afghan child in response to Australian calls for an independent investigation of Chinese trade practices. Senior Chinese officials responded to Canadian criticism of China’s human rights record by pointing to indigenous rights abuses in Canada. The Australian ambassador was summoned repeatedly and presented with a list of grievances. Trade sanctions against Australian wine, barley, beef, and coal followed Australian calls for a COVID origins investigation.

The Zero COVID policy of 2020 to 2022 belongs in any account of the tightening, because it demonstrated both the system’s capacity and its limits at the same moment. For two years, China maintained near-total suppression of COVID transmission through a combination of mass testing, immediate quarantine of contacts, and city-wide lockdowns — including the lockdown of Shanghai, a city of 26 million people, for two months in 2022. The policy worked, in the narrow sense that China’s COVID death toll during the suppression period was dramatically lower than Western countries with more permissive approaches. It failed, in the broader sense that when Omicron made suppression impossible and Xi abruptly abandoned Zero COVID in December 2022 — reportedly after protests in multiple cities that constituted the most visible public dissent of his tenure — the unvaccinated elderly population experienced a wave of deaths that Chinese authorities have not fully disclosed. The policy’s sudden abandonment, after two years of propaganda depicting it as a triumph of Chinese governance over Western chaos, was handled by simply stopping the reporting. The deaths happened. The accounting did not.

The Wolf Warrior style has produced more international resistance to China than the quiet diplomacy of the patient decades would have — this is the consensus assessment, including among some Chinese scholars who have said so cautiously. It has contributed to the formation of new security partnerships — AUKUS, the Quad, expanded Five Eyes coordination — that would have been harder to build against a China that was still hiding its brightness. Countries that had maintained studied neutrality on China-related issues have found the combination of economic coercion, territorial assertiveness, and diplomatic aggression sufficient motivation to take positions they previously avoided.

Whether this matters strategically depends on whether the international coalition it has galvanised can sustain the semiconductor restrictions, investment screening, and technology competition that represent the West’s primary instruments of response. Xi’s calculation — that China’s economic weight, its Belt and Road relationships, and its indigenous technology development will eventually render the coalition’s restrictions ineffective — is the central wager of the 141 pages. The 15th Five-Year Plan is, among other things, Xi’s answer to the Wolf Warrior’s diplomatic costs: if China can achieve technological self-sufficiency, the costs of the tightening become manageable.

Whether that calculation is correct will be the defining strategic question of the next decade.
