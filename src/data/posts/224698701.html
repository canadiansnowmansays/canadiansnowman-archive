Geoffrey Hinton introduced the second night of the Hinton Lectures in Toronto, on November 10, with a warning about the warning. “Today will be the darkest hour,” he said. Paul Christiano would be talking about A.I. that improves itself, and Hinton expected it to be even scarier than the first lecture “because Paul knows what he’s talking about. He’s seen the intelligence explosion that’s happening inside the big companies.”

Then he added the line that the rest of the evening tested: the risks “may well be avoidable if people take his warning seriously. We need the big tech companies and governments to stop being in denial about the existential threat. There’s still time to act.”

Part I recorded Christiano’s first lecture, on why A.I. systems might come to want to take control. The second was about why they might soon be able to.

How fast, so far

Christiano began with the record. In 2018, language models could sometimes write a coherent sentence. By the ChatGPT moment four years later, they could write essays full of invented facts and short programs. Today, he said, they write long if sloppy books, produce working software running to 10,000 lines, and are far better than humans at finding and exploiting flaws in code, which is why the world is now in “a little mini crisis” over decades of vulnerable software. And “at this point the large majority of new mathematics is done by machines.”

The messier world is catching up. On the Remote Labor Index, a benchmark from the Center for AI Safety built from roughly ten-hour jobs once given to gig workers, A.I. systems could do less than 2 per cent of the tasks acceptably a year ago. A week before the lecture, they could do 20 per cent. “I had to update the slide because as of yesterday, they can do around 40 per cent.”

Progress will slow eventually, he said. But every year people point to something A.I. cannot yet do and predict the end, “and at this point I would bet against it.”

The machine that builds the machine

None of that alone would justify talking about one year rather than four or eight. What changes the timetable is that A.I. development is being automated.

Roughly half of A.I.’s progress over the past eight years came from new ideas rather than bigger computers, Christiano said, and more recently a larger share. Automating the people who have those ideas is now an explicit goal of the industry. He showed an Anthropic essay on its progress towards what the field calls recursive self-improvement, and an OpenAI job posting recruiting researchers to automate the job of an OpenAI researcher. “This is one of the big things the companies are trying to do that we as a civilization are effectively directing so many resources towards.”

The measurements are struggling to keep up. A widely followed chart of how long a task A.I. agents can pursue on their own has been retired, he said, because tasks that take humans weeks or months are too hard to build tests for. “At this point there are essentially no benchmarks we can use to measure the rate of progress.” Inside the labs, use of A.I. coding agents has climbed this year from very little to a significant fraction of what the companies pay their human researchers, with adoption doubling every month or two.

That disclosure belongs here: Anthropic is one of the two companies Christiano named, and these essays are written in collaboration with its model, Claude.

What happened to mathematics

His most vivid evidence came from his own field. Two years ago, Terence Tao described one of OpenAI’s models as like a mediocre graduate student. A year ago, mathematicians mostly used A.I. as an assistant. In the spring of 2026 came the first substantial result produced by machines. This autumn there have been hundreds, the wave The Asimov Gap has followed since Part 115. “If you look say in my area of mathematics, I would say that the progress we saw in October of 2026 was larger than the progress we saw in the preceding decade.”

For many people, he said, that was “an extremely alarming warning shot,” because the obvious next question is what happens if the same thing occurs in machine learning. Like mathematics, it happens entirely inside a data centre, and success can be measured by whether a number goes up. It is messier, and it is limited by computing power in a way mathematics is not. But far more money is being spent to automate it, and by his estimate it is running perhaps a year behind.

The arithmetic of the danger is simple. If A.I. takes over its own development and doubles the pace of progress over 12 months, the next doubling might take six. Things will not be that clean, he said. But to a first approximation, if each improvement speeds up the next, the process feeds itself. This is the idea I. J. Good set out in 1965, which Part 103 of The Asimov Gap traced back to Bletchley Park. Christiano’s point was that it is no longer a thought experiment.

Evidence nobody can check

Last week, Anthropic and METR, an independent evaluation group, published a chart of the company’s internal capability measures that bends upward about a year ago: progress over the past year is faster than before. Researchers across labs also report feeling that A.I. has sped up their work.

But the most important part of the story is what the public cannot see. Three METR researchers spent a few weeks inside Anthropic reviewing private evidence. They were not allowed to publish it, but said finer measurements showed the same acceleration. “I think it is really unfortunate,” Christiano said, “that we’re in a place where people are trying to rely on three researchers from an independent organization to review that data if it actually ends up being a really critical question about the future of the field.” Most of the evidence needed to forecast the next three or six months “is held as closely held proprietary information by AI developers.”

On the main technical objection, that machine learning will be held back by the need for more computing power, he had moved. Two months ago he would have called it a coin flip whether self-sustaining acceleration happens. Now, on admittedly weak evidence, “maybe it’s more like two-thirds.” Nor does he expect the gains to stay confined to tidy laboratory problems. The same methods have transferred across domains for eight years, and an automated A.I. researcher could itself build systems for messier work.

Why it matters

The rest of the lecture explained why this is alarming rather than merely impressive. Billions of superhuman systems would outstrip any state’s cyber programme. Their skills would carry over into politics, deal-making and coordination, into controlling robots and drones, and possibly into designing new weapons, including biological ones far worse than anything nature produces.

They would also be deployed quickly, because no one wants to be the side without them. He cited Senator Ted Cruz’s remark, in substance, that if there are going to be killer robots, he wants them to be American ones. “Even if you’re scared of the AI that you are building, you know that you should definitely be scared of the AI the other guy is building.”

And the faster they improve, the harder they are to control. Humans cannot judge a plan they cannot understand until it is carried out. Shutting systems down sounds simple, but it would mean acting amid confusion about who is attacking whom, at great economic cost, across borders, against an adversary that can recruit human allies, possibly within days. Eventually the systems may not need large data centres at all.

There was one more danger, quieter than the others. As A.I. builds A.I., humans stop understanding the technical decisions. “We shouldn’t just say AI N told me that AI N plus one looked pretty good, so I’m happy rolling it out.”

What he asked for

His prescriptions were few and he admitted they were partial; the third lecture is about solutions. First, monitor dangerous capabilities closely, including in unreleased models. “It is possible for an AI company with unreleased models to be a greater threat to the continuity of a state than any rival state. And if you’re in that position, it is unfortunate if you have no idea what is going on within the AI company.” Second, consider limiting the pace of progress to a level society can survive. Third, keep scaling only when humans can actually understand and verify that the next system works. Fourth, build the machinery to slow down or stop before it is needed.

The panel, moderated again by the futurist Sínead Bovell, supplied a sharper institutional idea. After Enron, she recalled, the United States passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, and Canada followed with its own rules: chief executives and chief financial officers must personally certify their company’s books, with criminal penalties if they lie. “Imagine we required just a signature on the model. One person to put their name beside the AI model before they release it. I sign off that this is probably safe. Who would do it?” She would require a matching signature from the bank or insurer that buys it. “On the institutional side, we haven’t done a thing.”

Jodie Wallis, Manulife’s global chief A.I. officer, noted that her company already works under the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions’ rules on model risk, and pointed to the drug industry as proof that regulation shapes what powerful companies build. “We’ve never regulated the technology industry, not really,” she said. Christiano warned that the market will not necessarily restrain the labs: if smarter systems can be built on existing hardware, they could race ahead of anything customers want. If A.I. stops serving people’s needs, he said, “even if it is technically possible to do something else, let’s stop.”

The entry

The foundation’s chair closed with a comparison: the lectures would reach tens of thousands of viewers in more than 75 countries, and hundreds of thousands more later. ChatGPT was downloaded 100 million times in two months.

That asymmetry is The Asimov Gap’s subject in miniature. Christiano’s case rests on evidence the public is not allowed to see, about a process the companies are openly trying to speed up, with a timeline he now puts at six months to two years. Hinton said there is still time to act. Bovell’s signature costs nothing to try. The question is whether anyone will try it before the curve bends further.
