There is a particular kind of American figure who exists to be misunderstood twice — once by his enemies, and once by his friends who insist they always knew him best. Lindsey Graham, who died on July 11, 2026, at the age of 71, of what his office called a "brief and sudden" illness, was exactly this kind of man. He spent more than three decades in Washington being simultaneously the most predictable and the most bewildering figure in the Republican firmament, and he managed the trick right up to the end: scheduled for his 64th appearance on "Meet the Press", its moderator, Kristen Welker, noted, on the Sunday morning he did not live to see, fresh off a trip to a Ukrainian drone factory, a man still working the room from beyond the room.

I did not know Senator Graham personally, and I write from Toronto, which is to say from a safe and slightly bemused distance — the traditional vantage point of the Canadian observing American political theatre with a mixture of alarm and grudging fascination. But you do not need to have shaken the man's hand to understand what he represented, because the record does it for you, and the record is longer and stranger than the tidy "go to hell" / "not far behind God" bookend that most of Sunday's obituaries reached for.

The full arc, briefly

In July 2015, Graham told Trump on CBS not to "be the world's biggest jackass." Trump's response was not a tweet but an act of genuine menace: as the Times chronicled in June, he read Graham's personal cellphone number aloud to a rally crowd in South Carolina and invited them to call it. By December of that year, dropping his own presidential bid, Graham told CNN that Trump's rhetoric disgusted him and that his proposed Muslim ban would amount to a death sentence for American allies in the Middle East.

Then came March 2017 — a detail worth sitting with: according to New York magazine’s account, cited by the Times, mutual friends brokered a “make-up lunch” between the two men after Trump’s inauguration. Whatever was said over that meal did more work than any subsequent golf outing. By January 2018, Mr. Trump told assembled Senate Republicans, with evident wonder at his own reversal, that Graham had gone from enemy to friend — “Can you believe it?” Graham, for his part, explained the shift to CBS News in the same month with a kind of shrugging honesty rare in politicians: he’d called Trump a bigot, run out of adjectives, and then watched the American people reject his analysis at the ballot box.

It would be tidy to end the arc there, but it doesn’t end there, and this is the part most Sunday tributes skipped. On January 6, 2021 — the day of the Capitol riot — Graham said at the time, “Trump and I, we’ve had a hell of a journey... count me out. Enough is enough.” That is a real rupture, not a rhetorical flourish. And yet weeks later, unlike Bill Cassidy and six other Republicans, Graham did not vote to convict at the impeachment trial. Rifts opened again in 2024 over a federal abortion ban, serious enough that Trump said publicly he regretted his 2020 endorsement. By this year’s primary, the two were back in lockstep — Graham and his allies spending upward of $15 million on ads promoting Trump’s endorsement, per AdImpact’s tracking, against a challenger, Mark Lynch, who spent nearly $5 million trying to paint Graham as insufficiently loyal. Graham told a local South Carolina station the same week: “If you want somebody who can go to Washington to help him, I’m your best choice.” Marjorie Taylor Greene, endorsing Lynch, wrote in her endorsement that Graham was an “America Last warmonger” — a reminder that his final act was still contested within his own coalition, not just applauded.

So: not two quotes a decade apart, but a full cardiogram — capitulation, rupture, reconciliation, rupture again, reconciliation again. The man’s relationship with Trump had a pulse.

The other Graham

It is worth remembering, too, that this was not always the profile. As Robert Draper's 2010 Times Magazine profile recounted, Graham was once a bipartisan dealmaker so far removed from what he became that the Club for Growth ranked him dead last among Senate Republicans, below a dozen Democrats and independents. Glenn Beck called him Obama Lite. Rush Limbaugh called him Lindsey Grahamnesty. He couldn't operate a coffee maker. He didn't notice a television had been stolen from his own kitchen because he never used the kitchen. "I don't have a life," he told his profiler, without apparent self-pity.

That profile is worth dwelling on a moment longer, because it shows a Graham almost unrecognizable from the man Sunday’s tributes described. Graham recalled to the Times in 2010 that he had spent months and 183 meetings on the Kerry-Lieberman climate bill — rebranded, for the benefit of conservative ears, as an “energy independence” bill, because, as he put it, “To me, it is about jobs, not polar bears!” When Harry Reid signaled he’d prioritize immigration reform over the climate legislation instead, Graham called him directly, accused him of chasing Hispanic votes for his own re-election, and by his own account the two men traded “a few F-bombs” before hanging up. This was not a man who avoided a fight; it was a man who picked his fights with his own party’s leadership as readily as with the opposition.

His method, when he described it plainly, was almost mechanical in its honesty. Graham told his profiler exactly how he intended to move public opinion on Guantánamo Bay — polling showed 88 percent of Republican primary voters opposed to closing the prison outright, but that number dropped to roughly 40 percent when the question was reframed around military commanders’ judgment, and Graham calculated he could get the number to 50-50 with the right coalition of retired generals and Bush-administration voices behind him. “Reason always prevails,” he said over almond schnapps, “if you can market it right.” He held up Ted Kennedy as he described it in that 2010 profile as his model for how the game was actually played — Kennedy would work behind the scenes with Republicans while publicly denouncing them, so that neither side paid a political price for the cooperation. Graham ran a version of the same act with the Obama White House for years, by his own description.

The McCain friendship, which shaped nearly everything else in his career, ran the same way. Graham described himself flatly as “the wingman” during McCain’s 2008 campaign — the one who kept things light when there was little to laugh about, who stayed with the McCains at their Sedona ranch the day after the loss, and who flew with McCain to Chicago a month later to meet Obama’s transition team, sitting across from Rahm Emanuel in a conversation that, by both men’s account, established a working relationship that would outlast McCain’s own involvement in it.

What held, and who said so

Where Graham was least contradictory, and most sincere by every account, was in his appetite for American military power projected abroad — and here the tributes that came in on Sunday add real specificity. As the New York Times reported Sunday, President Zelensky noted that Graham had visited Ukraine ten times since the 2022 invasion, and that the two had spoken twice in Graham’s final week alive, saying he would miss their conversations. The alliance’s secretary general said NATO’s Mark Rutte called Graham’s defense of the alliance one of his rare breaks with Trump, who has never hidden his skepticism of NATO. As the New York Times has reported, Prime Minister Netanyahu called him a beloved friend who understood the security of Israel and America as inseparable.

Perhaps the most affecting tribute came from Megan McCain, daughter of Graham’s closest friend and legislative mentor, who, as Megan McCain wrote, recalled his “acerbic quick wit” and his fondness for storytelling, saying simply: “He was literally never, ever boring.” And Donna Brazile told the Times — she who ran the Democratic National Committee, who disagreed with Graham on nearly everything he ever voted for — that he’d be the first to tell you that with a smile, and that he served the country in uniform and in the Senate regardless.

A somebody’s last performance

Governor McMaster now has a temporary appointment to make, and South Carolina will hold a special election in November, and Mitch McConnell remains hospitalized, and the Senate Republican caucus finds itself short a reliable, senior vote at a moment when it can least afford to be short one.

Stephen Leacock once wrote that in Mariposa, the town he invented and I have since borrowed for my own fictional Elsinore, everyone was a somebody, and the trick of small-town life was that the somebodies never stopped performing for each other even when no one was watching. Washington is Mariposa with worse traffic and higher stakes, and Lindsey Graham was one of its most durable somebodies — a Club for Growth pariah who became a MAGA loyalist, a dealmaker who once traded F-bombs with his own majority leader and then spent his last years as a president’s most reliable Senate vote, a man who told Trump to go to hell and meant it, who broke with him on January 6th and meant that too, and who spent his final week on earth in Kyiv anyway, doing the one thing that never seemed to change across all the reinventions. That is either the most damning thing you can say about a public man, or the most human. On July 12, 2026, I suspect it is both.
