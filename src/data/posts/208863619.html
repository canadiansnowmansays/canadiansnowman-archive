Stephen Gordon Jul 28, 2026

Twenty parts of this series have been built around a single, stubborn suspicion: that the tariffs, the annexation talk, the shifting legal justifications, none of it was really about dairy quotas or liquor shelves or wildfire smoke. It was about whether Canada would submit. That suspicion just stopped being this series’ own theory. Chrystia Freeland — the woman who sat across the table from Washington during the first Trump trade war, and negotiated the USMCA into existence — said it herself, in the New York Times today, in language considerably blunter than anything this series has used.

It’s worth taking her essay seriously on its own terms before folding it back into everything this series has already documented, because the argument doesn’t need this series’ help. It stands on its own. What this series can add is the paper trail.

I. The Argument, Stated Plainly

Freeland’s case is simple enough to state in one sentence: the tariffs’ stated justifications have changed so many times — national security, then wildfire negligence, then dairy and alcohol and auto discrimination — that the instability of the excuse is itself the evidence. A government that actually cared about any one of those grievances would have picked one and built a durable case around it. A government using trade law as a demonstration of raw power reaches for whatever justification is lying closest to hand and swaps it out the moment it stops being useful. This series has been documenting exactly that swap — IEEPA to Section 122 to Section 301 to Section 338, autos to dairy to alcohol to smoke — since October. Freeland just named what all of it was actually for.

Her framing of the stakes is the sharpest part of the whole piece: this isn’t a negotiation over trade terms, it’s a test of whether Canada will move from being a treaty-bound ally to something closer to a vassal, subject to Washington fiat — her comparison point being Venezuela, “only with snowdrifts and hockey.” That’s not hedged, academic language. That’s a former deputy prime minister of Canada telling American readers, in their own paper of record, exactly what she thinks is actually on the table.

II. Trump’s Own Confirmation

What makes Freeland’s essay land as more than opinion is that she doesn’t have to reach far for evidence — Trump keeps supplying it himself. After this month’s tariffs were announced, he told reporters, in effect, that Canada’s survival depends entirely on the United States and that it has no path forward without American support. That’s not Freeland’s interpretation of his intentions. That’s Trump narrating the dominance thesis in his own words, unprompted, in public. Stephen Miller made much the same point even more explicitly to Jake Tapper earlier this year, describing a worldview in which niceties and rules-based talk are irrelevant next to raw strength and force. Between the two of them, the administration has said the quiet part out loud often enough that “quiet part” may not be the right description anymore.

III. What This Series Already Had on the Record

Read Freeland’s essay next to this series’ own timeline and the fit is uncomfortably precise.

Part XVI covered Congresswoman Tenney’s CANADA Act — a bill demanding Canada buy more American liquor, filed the same month the actual grievance, per Freeland’s account, kept sliding: national security, then discrimination, then smoke. Part XVII made the case that the wildfire-smoke tariff threat was itself a pretext dressed in legal language because you cannot actually sue a country for its weather — which is precisely the kind of “laughably flimsy, constantly changing justification” Freeland says is the point rather than a flaw. Part XIX tracked Carney folding fast on concrete economic pressure — the electricity surcharge, the digital services tax — while holding firm rhetorically at Davos and in the Oval Office, and closed on an open question about whether that calibration was wisdom or a losing strategy. Freeland’s essay answers that question more forcefully than this series was willing to: appeasement doesn’t buy safety, she argues, it just invites the next demand, dressed as a more graceful version of the same surrender. Part XX watched two countries fail to jointly open a bridge named for their own friendship. Freeland’s essay is the sentence that explains why: you cannot hold a joint ceremony with a government that has told you, repeatedly, that the relationship it wants isn’t a partnership at all.

IV. The Number That’s Worse Than the One We Already Had

This series has already used one striking Pew number — the collapse in Canadians calling the U.S. a reliable partner, from 83 percent in 2022 to 35 percent this year, cited back in Part XIX. Freeland’s essay adds a second number that is, if anything, more damning. In a recent Pew survey across 36 countries, only 33 percent of Canadians viewed the United States favorably — compared with 44 percent who viewed China favorably. Canada is not just souring on America. By this measure, it now rates an authoritarian one-party state that jailed two Canadian citizens on transparently retaliatory grounds as more favorable than its oldest ally and neighbor. Freeland doesn’t pretend that comparison is rational — she calls the China-favorability finding “nonsense” on its face, given Beijing’s record — but she’s honest about why it’s happening anyway: superpowers get resented, and this particular superpower has spent two years actively earning the resentment rather than merely inheriting it.

V. The Playbook She’s Actually Recommending

It’s worth noting Freeland isn’t writing as a detached commentator. She ran Canada’s 2018 response to the first Trump trade war personally — the original dollar-for-dollar retaliation this series has referenced as the template Carney has only partially followed. Her prescription now is a repeat of that same instinct: meet pressure with matched pressure, because appeasement has a well-documented failure mode. That’s not a neutral historical footnote. That’s the architect of the first retaliation telling her successor, in public, that his more measured approach may be the wrong lesson to have drawn from her own playbook. Whether Carney takes that note from a former colleague and Liberal Party ally is, itself, worth watching for in whatever comes next.

VI. The Bigger Question She’s Actually Asking

Freeland’s essay doesn’t stay narrowly focused on Canada, and this is where it becomes something closer to a warning than a trade dispute retrospective. She points out that Washington is alienating its allies at precisely the moment it can least afford to — noting that the U.S. share of global GDP has fallen from roughly half in 1944 to just over a quarter today, while the combined weight of NATO, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand still adds up to well over half the world economy. An America that treats that coalition as vassals rather than partners is, by her account, spending down leverage it will need against China later, not banking a win now. The closing question she poses isn’t really about Canada at all: whether a future, post-Trump Washington could ever rebuild what this period is spending, or whether the world will conclude this was never really a departure from what Americans actually thought of their allies all along.

VII. What This Confirms, and What It Doesn’t

I want to be careful not to overclaim what one guest essay proves. Freeland is a former senior Canadian official with every institutional incentive to frame this dispute as a matter of sovereignty rather than commerce, and she says so herself — this is a woman who negotiated the USMCA and has a stake in its legacy. That doesn’t make her wrong. It does mean this series should treat her essay the way it treats every other piece of evidence: as a strong data point, not a verdict. What it confirms, at minimum, is that the “this is really just about tariffs” reading of the last twenty parts was never the only serious interpretation available — someone who sat in the actual negotiating room has now said, on the record, that she reached the same conclusion this series has been circling since October.

One more thing, from one Torontonian to another: thank you, Ms. Freeland, for the years already spent in that room, and for still showing up to say the hard part out loud now that you’re no longer required to.

VIII. The Ledger

Twenty parts in, the pattern this series has tracked — the shifting justifications, the folding on economic pressure and holding on rhetoric, the diminished bridge opening, the collapsing favorability numbers — now has a name attached to it that isn’t this newsletter’s own. Whether Ottawa or Washington drops the next piece of news, this series will be watching for whether it fits the dominance-and-submission frame Freeland just put on the record, or whether something genuinely changes the shape of the argument. Given the last twenty parts, I wouldn’t bet heavily on the second option. But I’ve been wrong about the timeline before, and I’d rather be surprised than right.
