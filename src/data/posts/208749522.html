The Introduction to this series was somewhat unkind to Marco Polo, characterizing him as a well-travelled tourist who arrived at an established destination fourteen centuries into its operating history and presented the crowd as evidence of discovery. That assessment stands. It is also incomplete, and this installment exists to fill in what the joke leaves out — because the book Polo produced, whatever its accuracy and whatever its author’s actual role, did something no other document in this series has done. It changed what Europeans believed was out there, and roughly two centuries later, that belief sent ships west across the Atlantic.

The family background matters. The Polos were Venetian merchants of the middling sort, and Marco’s father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo had already made one extraordinary journey before Marco ever left home. Departing around 1260 on a trading venture to the Black Sea, they found their return route blocked by warfare between Mongol factions, kept going east rather than risk the way back, and eventually reached the court of Kublai Khan. Kublai, according to the account, received them with interest, questioned them about European affairs and the Latin church, and sent them home in the late 1260s as envoys carrying a request to the Pope for a hundred learned men who could explain Christianity — a request that says something about Mongol religious curiosity and something else about how the European response would go. The brothers arrived back in Venice in 1269 to find the papacy vacant, waited, eventually secured two Dominican friars who abandoned the journey almost immediately upon encountering the first war zone, and set out again in 1271 with the friars gone and Niccolò’s teenaged son Marco added to the party.

They travelled overland — through Anatolia, Persia, and Central Asia, past Kashgar and around the Taklamakan by the southern route, through the Hexi Corridor — and reached Kublai’s summer capital at Shangdu around 1275, having taken between three and four years. Marco was seventeen or so when he left and in his early twenties when he arrived. He then remained in Mongol China for roughly seventeen years.

What exactly he did during those years is the central unresolved question. The book claims he served Kublai as a trusted official, was sent on missions to distant provinces, and at one point governed the city of Yangzhou for three years. This claim is difficult to sustain. Chinese and Mongol administrative records for the period are extensive, and no one convincingly identifiable as Marco Polo appears in them. The Yangzhou governorship in particular is hard to credit; a foreign appointee to a substantial city administration should have left a documentary trace. Sceptics have used this silence to argue the whole journey was fabricated from other people’s accounts. The counterargument, which most specialists now find more persuasive, is that Polo was almost certainly present but was almost certainly a rather more junior figure than the book presents — a commercial agent, perhaps a salt-administration functionary, someone attached to the enormous cosmopolitan bureaucracy Kublai staffed heavily with foreigners precisely because they had no local factional loyalties — and that the elevation of his role from useful foreigner to intimate of the Khan is exactly the sort of embellishment one would expect from a book composed years later by a professional writer of romances working from an ageing merchant’s recollections.

That professional writer is the other reason the text reads as it does. The Polos returned to Venice around 1295, having come back by sea via Sumatra, India, and Persia in a fleet escorting a Mongol princess to a marriage in the Ilkhanate. Shortly afterward Marco was captured in a naval conflict between Venice and Genoa and imprisoned in Genoa, where he shared confinement with Rustichello da Pisa, a writer known for Arthurian romances in French. The book they produced together — variously titled Le Devisement du Monde, Il Milione, or in English simply The Travels — is therefore a collaboration between a merchant recalling events from up to thirty years earlier and a romance writer with professional instincts about what makes a passage land. The result is not organized as a travelogue. It is organized more like a geographical inventory, province by province, listing what each place produces, what its people believe, what they trade, and what remarkable things happen there, interspersed with narrative set pieces about Kublai’s court. It is, in its structure, closer to a merchant’s reference manual than to an adventure story, which is a point in favour of its underlying seriousness.

Its contemporaries did not believe it. The nickname Il Milione is generally understood as a jab — the million lies, or the man who deals in millions — and there is a widely repeated deathbed anecdote in which Polo, urged by friends to retract his exaggerations before facing judgment, replied that he had not told the half of what he saw. The scepticism was not unreasonable. He described a city with twelve thousand bridges, a stone that burns, a paper currency that everyone accepts as though it were gold, a postal system whose riders cover in a day what a normal traveller manages in ten, and a court whose scale exceeded anything in European experience by an order of magnitude. Readers who knew that Venice was a large city and that the King of France was a great monarch had no framework in which any of this was plausible.

Most of it was true. The bridges of Hangzhou were fewer than twelve thousand but the city was genuinely enormous, plausibly the largest in the world at the time and vastly larger than any European city. The burning stone was coal, in general household use in China centuries before it was in Europe. The paper currency and the yam relay system are exactly as the previous installment of this series described them. Polo’s descriptions of Chinese salt administration, of specific provincial products, and of Persian and Central Asian geography are frequently accurate in ways that would be hard to fake, and some details he reported — the existence of Japan, which he never visited but described from hearsay as an island kingdom rich in gold — were unknown in Europe from any other source.

The errors and omissions are equally telling. He never mentions the Great Wall, though the structure as it exists today is largely Ming-era and the thirteenth-century remnants were in poor repair and no particular tourist attraction. He never mentions tea, which is genuinely odd. He never mentions foot-binding, or Chinese characters. He gets some geography confused and some numbers wildly wrong. Sceptics list these as evidence of fabrication; the more economical reading is that Polo spent his time in a Mongol administrative world that was substantially Turkic, Persian, and Central Asian in language and personnel rather than in Han Chinese society, that he probably never learned to read Chinese, and that he was recalling all of it, without notes, decades later, to a man who was writing it down in a third language.

The scepticism has a modern scholarly form as well, and it is worth engaging because the debate clarifies what the book actually is. Frances Wood’s 1995 study Did Marco Polo Go to China? argued the negative, resting principally on the documentary silence, the conspicuous omissions, and the possibility that Polo assembled the account from Persian and Arabic sources available in the Black Sea trading colonies without ever going further east. The response from specialists was substantial and largely unpersuaded. Igor de Rachewiltz, Stephen Haw, and others pointed out that Polo’s account contains details unlikely to have come from any available written source — the correct names and relationships of obscure Mongol officials, accurate descriptions of specific administrative practices, and terminology that reflects Persian and Turkic usage current at the Yuan court rather than the Arabic geographical vocabulary he would have picked up in a Levantine port. Haw further argued that several of the notorious omissions dissolve on inspection: the Great Wall as a continuous stone structure did not exist to be omitted, and Polo’s silence on tea is less strange when one notes that tea-drinking was much less established among the Mongol elite he moved with than among the Han population he mostly did not.

The manuscript situation compounds every question about what Polo actually said. There is no surviving original. The earliest text is a Franco-Italian version, and the surviving manuscripts diverge from one another substantially — some contain material absent from others, and one fifteenth-century Latin manuscript discovered in Toledo in the 1930s includes roughly two hundred passages found nowhere else, including material on Chinese religious practice and on the geography of northern Asia. Whether these represent authentic content dropped by other copyists or later interpolation is disputed. The practical consequence is that scholars arguing about what Marco Polo claimed are frequently arguing about different books, and any confident statement about what “the text says” requires specifying which text.

Polo was also not the only European making the trip, which is worth stating because his fame has obscured a genuine if short-lived wave of contact. Giovanni da Montecorvino, a Franciscan, reached Beijing in 1294, was appointed archbishop there, and built churches with a reported congregation in the thousands, mostly Alan mercenaries in Mongol service rather than Chinese converts. Odoric of Pordenone travelled to China in the 1320s and left an account that overlaps Polo’s substantially — which sceptics have occasionally cited as evidence of copying in one direction or the other, though independent observation of the same places would produce much the same overlap. The Florentine merchant Francesco Balducci Pegolotti compiled, around 1340, a commercial handbook that includes a matter-of-fact section on the overland journey to Cathay, advising merchants on guides, currency, provisions, and the length of each stage, and describing the road as perfectly safe by day or night. Pegolotti had never made the trip himself; he was compiling standard trade information for practitioners, which is precisely why the passage is such good evidence. By the 1340s, travelling to China was a thing a Florentine merchant could look up in a manual.

That window closed almost immediately. Within a decade of Pegolotti’s handbook, the pandemic described in Part VII of this series was moving along the same roads, and within two decades the Yuan Dynasty was collapsing and the Ming that replaced it in 1368 had no interest in a foreign presence of the kind Kublai had cultivated. Montecorvino’s church in Beijing vanished. The overland route to Cathay reverted, in European understanding, from a documented commercial itinerary to a rumour, and Polo’s book — which had been one account among several — became the account, largely because it was the one that kept being copied.

The book’s influence is where the argument about its accuracy stops mattering very much. It circulated widely in manuscript across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries — well over a hundred manuscript copies survive, in numerous languages, no two quite identical, since medieval copyists edited freely — and it established in the European imagination the proposition that the east was not merely distant but staggeringly rich, populous, and sophisticated. Cathay entered the European mental map as a real place worth reaching. And in the 1480s, a Genoese sailor working through his own copy of the book annotated it heavily in the margins, paying particular attention to the passages on the wealth of Cathay, the gold of Cipangu, and the distances involved.

Columbus’s copy survives, in Seville, with his notes in it. His entire proposal rested on a calculation that Asia extended much further east than it does and that the westward ocean crossing was therefore much shorter than it is — a calculation he built partly on Polo’s reported distances, which overstated the eastward extent of Asia considerably. The Genoese sailor was wrong about the geography, wrong about the distance, and wrong about what he found. He was wrong in a direction that Polo’s exaggerations had helped point him. It is difficult to construct a cleaner example of a book mattering more than its accuracy.

Which returns us to the assessment the Introduction offered, now with the qualification it needed. Marco Polo did not open the Silk Road, discover China, or pioneer anything. Everything he saw had been seen by Sogdian, Persian, and Arab merchants for a thousand years, and by Rabban Bar Sauma travelling the other direction at almost the same moment. What he did was write it down for an audience that had never heard any of it, in a language that audience could read, with enough narrative embellishment to make it circulate. The Silk Road’s fifteen hundred years of Central Asian merchants left ledgers, letters, and contracts, most of them recovered by archaeologists from sealed caves and abandoned watchtowers. The Venetian latecomer produced a bestseller. Posterity has treated the two contributions with a consistency that is, on reflection, entirely characteristic of how history gets written: the people who make a thing work rarely write the book, and the book is what survives.
