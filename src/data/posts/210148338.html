Postmark: Somewhere in the Bay Area. A stationary robot on a tabletop, folding a bath towel for the fortieth time that day. It pauses on a pair of jeans. A light blinks. Somewhere else, a stranger takes the wheel for six seconds.

Part VII of this series, Where Is Rosie?, spent considerable space establishing laundry as the single hardest problem in domestic robotics — harder than vacuuming, harder than lawn mowing, harder even than cooking, because fabric is a deformable object with no fixed shape, no repeatable grasp point, and no two starting configurations ever quite the same. That postcard left the question open: not whether a laundry robot would arrive, but when, and in what compromised form. This postcard reports the answer, and the honest engineering has turned out to be right about almost everything.

The lamp that isn’t quite a robot

The strangest and, in its way, most instructive entry in this year’s crop of laundry robots comes from a start-up called Syncere, whose product is not shaped like a robot at all. As the Washington Post’s own account of a Palo Alto demonstration describes, Syncere’s device is a pair of lamp-shaped units, called Lume, priced at thirty-five hundred dollars for the pair — deliberately disguised as ordinary bedroom furniture rather than presented as visible robotic hardware, on the theory that a machine which looks like something you would already own is easier to welcome into a home than one that announces itself as a robot. The company’s co-founder, defending the price against sceptics, pointed out that premium designer lamps already retail for more than two thousand dollars — an argument this series notes with interest, because it reframes an entire category of domestic robot as a furniture purchase rather than a technology purchase, the same instinct that led 1X to wrap its NEO humanoid in soft fabric rather than exposed actuators.

The reporter’s own verdict on what is actually being sold is worth repeating in full, because it captures something this series has found true of nearly every frontier technology it has examined: Syncere and its competitors are not, strictly speaking, selling a robot that folds laundry today. They are selling a promise that the robot will fold laundry adequately once enough customers have used it, generated enough correction data, and funded enough software updates — a promise, delivered now, of a product to be finished later, with the earliest buyers effectively subsidizing the company’s own training data collection in exchange for early access to an unfinished result.

The robot that admits what it can’t do yet

The more mature and more transparent entry in this year’s field comes from Weave Robotics, a Bay Area start-up not yet two years old, whose product Isaac 0 is, by design, the least ambitious possible version of a laundry robot: a stationary base, a tabletop workspace roughly six feet by five, and exactly one job. As Weave’s own product page states plainly, Isaac 0 folds t-shirts, long sleeves, sweaters, pants, and towels, taking thirty to ninety minutes per load, and does not yet handle large blankets or bed sheets at all — a limitation the company states upfront rather than burying in fine print. As one detailed industry account of the launch reports, the company’s own explanation for choosing a stationary, single-purpose design over a more capable mobile robot was refreshingly unglamorous: the ruthless paring-down of scope was precisely what let a fifteen-person team ship a working product to real customers’ homes as a young company, rather than continuing to chase a more impressive but unshippable mobile platform.

The mechanism Isaac 0 uses to handle the garments it cannot yet fold reliably is the same reverse-centaur architecture this series has now traced through the home humanoid of Part XVII and the surgical robot of Part XV. As the company’s own founder explained to industry press, when the robot’s software detects it is stuck or has made an error that has set back its progress, it summons a remote Weave specialist, who sees only the camera feeds necessary to complete the specific task — no audio, no unrelated footage of the home — and takes over the robot’s arms for an average of five to ten seconds before handing control back to the automated system. Every one of those corrections becomes training data for the next version of the model, the same flywheel this series found driving 1X’s NEO: the early, imperfect customer is quietly teaching the machine that will eventually need them less.

The graveyard this robot had to walk past

No honest account of this technology can skip the fact that Isaac 0 and Lume are not the first attempt at this exact problem, and that the attempts before them mostly failed outright. As one 2026 industry analysis notes plainly, a company called FoldiMate raised millions of dollars pursuing a consumer laundry-folding appliance and shut down without ever shipping a single unit to a paying customer — a cautionary data point this series’ own coverage of hype cycles has learned to take seriously. Samsung and LG have both shown prototype folding machines at trade shows across multiple years without committing either to a consumer release, until LG finally unveiled a humanoid called CLOiD at CES 2026 explicitly promising to help households achieve what its marketing called zero labour.

The CLOiD demonstration is worth reporting in unflattering detail, because it is exactly the kind of honest failure this series has learned to value over a cherry-picked success. As one detailed CES account recorded, the robot approached a single towel that a human assistant had already laid out for it — unable, in the demonstration, to retrieve the towel from a hamper itself — and spent thirty seconds folding it, only to fail to stack the finished fold into a neat pile, undoing much of the careful work the folding itself had just accomplished. Subsequent attempts reportedly took a full minute per towel. This is not a company hiding its limitations; it is a company whose own live demonstration, in front of the assembled technology press, revealed exactly how far the gap between demo and product still runs — the same performance-versus-competence gap this series named as its own capstone argument in Part XVIII, playing out in real time on a CES show floor.

What thirty minutes of folding actually proves

The honest verdict on where this leaves the technology, as of mid-2026, is a genuine but heavily qualified yes: laundry-folding robots exist, ship to real homes, and complete real loads — but every one of them, without exception, still depends on a human being somewhere in the loop, whether that human is a remote teleoperator correcting a difficult fold in real time or a family member pre-sorting the load and setting aside the bedsheets the robot cannot yet touch. Weave’s newer Isaac 1, a wheeled, mobile successor unveiled only weeks after Isaac 0 began shipping, extends the same architecture to making beds, tidying clutter, and putting items away — priced identically at just under eight thousand dollars, and just as candid that a remote specialist remains one difficult task away at all times.

The Washington Post’s framing of the whole category deserves the last word on this point, because it is the same conclusion this series reached about the domestic robot curve in Part VII, restated in a sharper, more current key: household robots, the columnist argued, are waiting for their own version of the moment self-driving cars went from impressive demo to genuinely trusted product — a moment that arrived for neither technology on the timeline its earliest boosters promised, and that both technologies are still, honestly, waiting for.

The privacy switch this series has been waiting to see

One design detail in Weave’s newer mobile robot deserves particular attention from this series, because it is precisely the safeguard the reverse-centaur problem in Part XVII called for and did not, at the time, find. Isaac 1, the wheeled successor to the stationary Isaac 0, reportedly includes hardware controls that physically disable its onboard cameras whenever they are not actively required for a task — not a software toggle a company could quietly revise in a future update, but a physical switch built into the machine itself. This is a direct, concrete answer to the exact anxiety this series raised about the stranger behind the home robot’s eyes: a teleoperated device that can see into your living room is only as trustworthy as the promise that it is not watching when it has no task to perform, and a hardware-level camera cutoff is a far more credible version of that promise than any privacy policy a company could later amend. Whether this becomes the industry standard, or remains a single start-up’s differentiator, is worth watching as every other company racing toward the same product — Tesla, 1X, LG, Figure — makes its own choice about how literally to take the same problem.

The pricing pattern across this entire generation of products is also worth noting as its own small data point. Isaac 0, Isaac 1, and LG’s still-unpriced CLOiD are all converging, independently, on a figure somewhere between seven and twenty thousand dollars — considerably below the hundred-thousand-dollar range Moley’s chef-motion-capture kitchen commanded in Part XXIV, and considerably above the price of an ordinary appliance. This middle band is, on the evidence of every domestic robot this series has tracked, exactly where a genuinely new category sits in the years just before it either falls toward mass-market affordability or stalls out as a permanent luxury good — the same fork in the road Part XXIV found Moley’s kitchen approaching from a higher price point, and the same fork this series expects every one of these laundry robots to reach within the next several years, one way or the other.

What the fold actually means

From the balcony, the laundry robot’s arrival — however partial, however teleoperator-dependent, however limited to t-shirts and towels while blankets and pillowcases wait for a future software update — closes a loop this series opened in its very first territory-defining postcard about the home. Rosey the Robot promised effortless, invisible competence. What has actually arrived is something more honest and, in its way, more interesting: a stationary metal frame on a kitchen table, folding real laundry for real families, while somewhere else a stranger watches through a camera feed and quietly takes the wheel for six seconds at a time, teaching the machine to eventually need them less.

A Canadian reads this with the particular patience this series has learned from every domestic-robot postcard it has filed: the Roomba took two decades to go from a curiosity to fifty million homes, and the laundry robot, arriving now at eight thousand dollars with a human specialist standing by, is somewhere near the very beginning of that same long curve rather than anywhere close to its end. The fold has finally arrived. It arrived exactly as compromised, as teleoperated, and as honestly incomplete as the engineering always said it would — which, after everything else this series has learned to expect from the frontier, counts as a genuinely good outcome.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived folded, mostly, by a machine, with a little help.
