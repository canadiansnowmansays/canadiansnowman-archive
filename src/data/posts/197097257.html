On Thursday night, Dan Helmer received a shipment of boxes containing 1,000 yard signs that read: “Dan Helmer for Congress.”

By late Friday morning, he no longer had a seat to run for.

That is the speed of what the Virginia Supreme Court did on May 8th. Not an abstraction about procedural technicalities and constitutional amendment sequencing. Not a seat count on a national scoreboard. A man with 1,000 yard signs and no district to put them in.

“There’s no seat for me,” Helmer said. His new yard signs, he noted, “are probably not as useful as they were yesterday.”

In Part XXV of this series, I examined the Virginia ruling as a structural story — the asymmetry between parties that built good-government constraints and the party that kept legislative power, the good-government trap that caught Democrats who had played by every rule. That analysis was accurate. It was also incomplete.

The complete picture includes the yard signs. It includes the food pantry. It includes Beth Macy, the best-selling author who was considering dropping out of her primary the morning the ruling came down, and by afternoon was the Democratic candidate against a Republican incumbent in a district Donald Trump carried by 25 points. It includes Tom Perriello, who woke up Friday morning in a district Kamala Harris carried by three points and by afternoon lived in a district Trump won by twelve.

Seventeen days. $70 million. Forty thousand votes. And then, with a stroke of a pen in Richmond, campaigns went poof.

The Architect Without a Building

Dan Helmer was not just a candidate in the new Virginia map. He was one of its architects.

A senior member of the Virginia House of Delegates, Helmer had been central to the redistricting push that began in October — the legislative strategy, the constitutional amendment process, the referendum campaign. His Democratic colleagues subsequently drew a new district in Northern Virginia specifically suited for him — the lobster-shaped Seventh, stretching from the Potomac River suburbs westward and southward, the district that received more attention than any other in the proposed map.

He built the house. The court tore it down. He is standing in the rubble with 1,000 yard signs.

On Monday, Virginia’s attorney general filed an emergency application to the United States Supreme Court asking the justices to overturn the Virginia ruling. The legal argument is narrower and more technical than most coverage has suggested — Virginia is arguing that under federal law, “Election Day” means the single day on which votes are counted, not the beginning of early voting. If the Supreme Court agrees, the General Assembly’s October 31st vote was valid, the amendment stands, and the map is restored.

It is a genuine legal argument. It is also being heard by a conservative supermajority that upheld the Texas map, handed down Callais, and has shown no particular appetite for outcomes that benefit Democratic gerrymanders. Helmer’s congressional campaign remains, for the moment, in suspended animation — alive in theory, dead in practice, awaiting a Court that will likely decide its fate before the August 4th primary deadline.

This is not, in the grand scheme of what this series has been documenting, the most important consequence of the Virginia ruling. The most important consequences are structural and demographic — the four seats that won’t flip, the majority that becomes harder to reach, the Black voters in Tennessee and Louisiana and Alabama who are losing representation as this essay is written.

But Helmer’s story captures something the structural analysis misses: the human cost of a system where the rules change after people have built their lives around them. He is not a sympathetic figure in some of the coverage — he was, after all, an architect of a gerrymander, and gerrymanders are not inherently sympathetic things. But he was playing within the rules as they existed. He won at the ballot box. And then the rules changed..

The Food Pantry

Former congressman Tom Perriello. Credit...Eze Amos for The New York Times

Tom Perriello’s story is different, and harder.

Perriello is a former congressman who served in the Obama era and later worked as a diplomat in Africa. He began his 2026 campaign in December, expecting the new maps and positioning himself for the district that would stitch together small Democratic-leaning cities and college towns in the Shenandoah and Blue Ridge Mountains.

When the Virginia Supreme Court ruling came down Friday morning, his district — shaped for him, friendlier to him — disappeared. He announced he would now run against Representative Ben Cline, a Republican whose district includes conservative Southside Virginia. Trump carried it by twelve points.

That is the political dimension. The human dimension arrived when Perriello walked into a food pantry in the Shenandoah Valley shortly after the ruling.

The African-American woman who runs it broke down in tears.

“She said for the first time in her life she thought she was going to have representation,” Perriello recalled. “This is what the last two months have been about, about hope for the first time for people.”

I have been writing this series for twenty-seven parts. I have examined Supreme Court rulings, legislative manoeuvres, constitutional technicalities, procedural sequencing, seat counts and structural advantages and the mathematics of how many points Republicans can lose the national popular vote by and still hold the House.

The woman in the food pantry is the reason any of it matters.

She had, for the first time in her life, believed she might have a representative who looked like her, who came from her experience, who understood what the Shenandoah Valley looks like from inside a food pantry rather than from a campaign bus passing through. The proposed map made that possible. The Virginia Supreme Court, on a 4-to-3 vote, on a procedural question about four days of early voting, took it away.

“This is what the last two months have been about,” Perriello said.

Yes. And now she knows, again, that it won’t be.

The Knife Fight

Virginia congressional candidate Beth Macy.Credit...Eze Amos for The New York Times

Beth Macy came to politics through a different door.

She is the best-selling author of Dopesick, the book about the opioid crisis in Appalachia that became an Emmy-winning television series. She started her congressional campaign in November, before the proposed maps were finalised, and ended up drawn into a district with Perriello — who was far better known, had deeper connections in their shared region, and was the more obvious primary favourite.

On Friday morning, Macy was seriously considering conceding the primary and endorsing Perriello. A few hours later, the Virginia Supreme Court ruling made that calculation irrelevant. The district they were competing in was gone. Both of them were now running in different places.

Macy announced she would remain a candidate against Representative Ben Cline, the Republican whose district includes her hometown of Roanoke — a district Trump carried by 25 points.

“I feel bad, but you know, we can’t just roll over,” she said. “Democrats have got to stop showing up to a knife fight with a spork.”

She is not short on optimism. She believes the backlash to Republican policies in Washington could lead to Virginia Democrats sweeping out incumbents in ways that achieve what the maps were supposed to guarantee. She may be right. The political environment is brutal for Republicans — Trump’s approval ratings below forty percent, the Iran war unpopular, gas prices rising, Democratic enthusiasm in special elections running at levels that suggest a genuine wave.

But a wave is not a map. A map is a guarantee. A wave has to break at exactly the right moment, in exactly the right districts, in exactly the right proportions. The map was supposed to eliminate the uncertainty. The court eliminated the map.

“We have never been to where we are in this country,” Macy said. “It’s a national emergency.”

She is not wrong. The question is whether a national emergency produces a wave, or whether the structural disadvantage absorbs the wave before it reaches the shore.

The Governor’s Problem

Gov. Abigail Spanberger speaking with Don Scott, the House Speaker, in her office in February. Mr. Scott was one of the architects of the redistricting amendment that was overturned on Friday.Credit...Erin Schaff/The New York Times

Abigail Spanberger won the Virginia governorship last November by fifteen points — the largest raw-vote margin in state history. She was, in the weeks before the redistricting ruling, one of the most popular governors in the country and a figure whose national profile was growing.

She is now dealing with the fallout from a redistricting campaign she never fully embraced.

During her election campaign, Spanberger was initially sceptical of the gerrymandering effort Democratic legislative leaders were pursuing. Once the referendum was put before voters, she urged people to vote for it — but with measured language that frustrated full-throated supporters while doing nothing to disarm opponents. She emphasised that it was temporary. She did not make it her cause.

She is now its most prominent political casualty.

“She is the leader of the party,” said L. Douglas Wilder, Virginia’s Democratic governor from 1990 to 1994. “And the new map was her party’s priority.”

The relationship between Spanberger and Democratic legislative leaders has been, in the charitable phrasing of the New York Times’s Campbell Robertson, “prickly.” Some legislators have begun referring to her derisively as “141” — as if she were an extra member of the General Assembly rather than its governor. She has proposed amendments to legislation that were largely ignored and sent back unchanged, daring her to veto or sign.

Her declining poll numbers reflect both the redistricting defeat and the broader difficulty of governing when her own caucus is pulling in multiple directions and Republicans are accusing her of betraying her bipartisan pledges.

This is the specific political peril of the moderate governor in a partisan era: you are too liberal for Republicans who wanted you to govern differently and too cautious for Democrats who needed you to lead more forcefully. The redistricting campaign produced a version of that trap in miniature — she was criticised for insufficient enthusiasm by the campaign’s architects and criticised for any support at all by its opponents.

“It’s not like taking the morally principled stance would have been without cost,” said Brian Cannon, a Democrat who opposed the redistricting amendment. “But it turned out the politically expedient way came with even steeper costs.”

Spanberger’s statement after the ruling was disciplined and forward-looking: her focus would be on ensuring voters had the information necessary to make their voices heard in November, “because in those elections we — the voters — will have the final say.”

She is right about that. The voters will have the final say. The question Part XXV raised and this essay cannot fully answer is whether the structural disadvantage being built — district by district, ruling by ruling, week by week — will make that final say harder to translate into the majority Democrats are reaching for.

Republicans hold 23 state government trifectas to Democrats’ 16 — and critically, most Republican-controlled states kept map-drawing power in their legislatures, while Democratic states handed it to independent commissions. Virginia had the trifecta. The commission requirement made it meaningless without a referendum. The referendum was voided. That is the structural trap in miniature.

What Virginia Actually Lost — And Is Still Fighting For

Representative Hakeem Jeffries said Friday that Democrats will “pick up at least two seats” in Virginia under the existing maps — targeting Representatives Jen Kiggans and Rob Wittman, whose districts Trump carried by five points or less in 2024. Shannon Taylor, a local prosecutor, and former Representative Elaine Luria are the candidates in those races, and both entered well before the redistricting push. Neither depends on the new map.

Two seats is not four seats. It is also not nothing.

Virginia Democrats hold six of eleven House districts. The goal with the referendum was ten of eleven — a near-sweep in a state that has been trending Democratic for a decade. What they are left with, if the Supreme Court declines to intervene, is the baseline they started from, plus two competitive opportunities in districts that were always going to be competitive regardless of what the Virginia Supreme Court decided.

The redistricting campaign cost $70 million, consumed months of political capital, alienated some voters who had approved the bipartisan commission in 2020, gave Republicans a gift in the form of hypocrisy arguments about Democratic gerrymanders — and now rests in the hands of a Supreme Court that has spent the last thirteen years narrowing democratic protections, not expanding them. The structural deficit facing Democrats nationally — six to twelve seats, depending on the outcome of pending Southern maps — makes every lost opportunity exponentially more costly.

Virginia’s attorney general called the state court’s ruling “judicial defiance” of the voters and argued the justices were “deeply mistaken on critical issues of federal law.” The federal question is specific: does “Election Day” under federal law mean the day votes are counted, or the day early voting begins? Virginia is arguing the former. If the Court agrees, the map is restored. If it doesn’t — or if it declines to take the case — the four seats are gone.

The legal argument is real. The political odds are not encouraging.

What Virginia lost, if this fight fails, is not just the four seats. It is the $70 million that might have been spent elsewhere. The political capital that might have been invested differently. The goodwill of the voters who approved the bipartisan commission and then were asked to undo it. And the moment in a Shenandoah Valley food pantry, when a woman who thought she was going to have representation for the first time was told, by a court ruling on a technicality about early voting, that she would not.

The Knife They Found

Beth Macy said Democrats need to stop showing up to a knife fight with a spork. In the days after the ruling, Democrats found two knives — one legitimate, one audacious.

The legitimate knife was the one they used. On Monday, Virginia’s attorney general filed an emergency application to the United States Supreme Court, arguing that the state court’s ruling revolved around a question of federal law — the definition of “Election Day.” Virginia officials called the state court’s decision “judicial defiance” of the voters and argued it had “deprived voters, candidates and the commonwealth of their right to the lawfully enacted congressional districts.” The application argues that under federal law, Election Day is the single day votes are counted — which means the General Assembly’s October 31st vote came before the election legally began, and the amendment process was valid all along.

This argument is connected to a Mississippi mail-in ballot case already before the Supreme Court — in which Republicans are arguing for a narrow definition of Election Day to block late-arriving mail ballots. The irony is exquisite: the same legal principle that Republicans are using to restrict ballot counting in Mississippi could, if the Court applies it consistently, restore the Virginia map. Virginia Democrats are asking the conservative supermajority to be consistent. That is a request with a mixed historical track record.

The audacious knife was floated but not used — at least not yet. In a private call that included Hakeem Jeffries and Virginia’s Democratic House members, a proposal circulated: lower the mandatory retirement age for Virginia Supreme Court justices from 75 to 54, force all seven off the bench, fill the vacancies through the Democratic-controlled General Assembly, and use the reconstituted court to reinstate the map. Former Representative James Moran called it “a bridge too far.” Representative Suhas Subramanyam said “everyone has got to have a strong stomach right now.”

The SCOTUS application is the path they chose. It is the cleaner, more legitimate path. It is also the path that ends at a court that handed down Callais three weeks ago.

The escalation ladder has no visible top. But for now, Virginia is climbing it through the front door rather than the back.

The View From Here

Beth Macy said Democrats have got to stop showing up to a knife fight with a spork.

She is right. She is also, in her willingness to run in a district Trump carried by 25 points rather than concede the field, demonstrating what showing up to the knife fight actually looks like. You show up. You run. You make the case to voters who have been told the system doesn’t work for them, in districts designed to ensure the system doesn’t work for them, and you make the case anyway.

That is not a strategy for winning maps. It is a strategy for winning elections despite maps. It is the longer, harder, less certain path — the one that depends on waves breaking at the right time in the right places, on enthusiasm outrunning structural disadvantage, on the woman in the food pantry turning that moment of broken hope into motivation rather than disengagement.

Democracy fights back. In Virginia, in 2026, it is fighting back with a spork.

Whether that is enough depends on what November looks like when it arrives.

Postscript

Twelve hours after this essay was published, the Supreme Court rejected Virginia’s emergency application. The order was one sentence. No vote count. No reasoning. No noted dissents. The map is gone. The four seats are gone. The front door closed. Beth Macy’s spork just got shorter.

Scott Surovell, the Democratic majority leader in the Virginia state senate: “I’m not surprised the U.S. Supreme Court chose not to apply the U.S. Constitution or federal law when it does not benefit the interest of President Trump.” Ryan McDougle, the Republican who brought the suit: the Court had “affirmed what we always knew: you cannot violate the Constitution to change the Constitution.”
