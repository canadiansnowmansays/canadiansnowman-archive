Back in Part XIII, this ledger tracked SpaceX and Nvidia’s orbital-compute venture as an answer to a terrestrial problem: the data-centre backlash documented in Parts IX, XII and XVII, in which counties recall their councilmen and teenagers file public-records requests over the power bills and the noise. If the neighbours won’t have you, the logic runs, build above the neighbours. I flagged then that the depreciation questions and the unit economics were unresolved. Elon Musk’s prediction that SpaceX’s A.I. revenue would overtake everything else the company does has since come true, in the third quarter, but as Part XIII now records, none of that revenue comes from orbit. It comes from renting out data centres on the ground. Orbital compute still had no numbers at all.

Google has now supplied some real numbers. On September 24, 2026, Kate Conger and Cade Metz reported in The New York Times that the company planned, on October 1, to load a refrigerator-sized satellite onto a SpaceX Falcon 9 at Vandenberg Space Force Base and launch it into orbit. It did, on schedule. It is the first concrete step toward Project Suncatcher, Google’s plan to put A.I. data centres in space and run them on sunlight. It is also, read carefully, a lesson in the distance between a demonstration and a promise.

What is actually going up

Let us be precise about the payload, because precision is where the air goes out of the balloon.

The satellite, which Google has named MVP, contains four specialised chips called tensor processing units. Together they have the computing power of a single server in a data centre. One server. Its solar panels supply about one kilowatt of power — which, as Conger and Metz note with what I can only assume is deliberate deadpan, is roughly what you need to run a hair dryer.

The chips cannot even run continuously. They heat up when they compute, and in the vacuum of space there is no air to carry the heat away and fans are useless. Google’s engineers built a cooling system of conductive layers — a pale-green thermal putty that comes in sheets like Fruit Roll-Ups, connected to aluminium and copper and a radiator panel that throws heat into space — but it only buys about fifteen minutes of operation before the chips must shut down to cool. During those fifteen-minute windows, the satellite can answer short queries so that Google’s Gemini can respond from orbit.

So the honest one-sentence description of humanity’s first major-tech step toward space-based artificial intelligence is this: a hair-dryer’s worth of solar power runs one server’s worth of chips for fifteen minutes at a stretch, and then everybody waits for it to cool down.

The executive who keeps saying “no”

What saves this from being a puff piece — and what makes it useful for this ledger — is that Google’s own people keep undercutting the hype, and the reporters let them.

James Manyika, Google’s senior vice president for research, called the vibration-test results great and then said he still wondered what would happen once the thing reached space. Asked about the timeline, he was blunter than any sceptic could have hoped: the company does not expect, to be perfectly frank, to have anything usefully operational in the next few years. He reached for the company’s driverless-car programme as the comparison, the one that took roughly fifteen years of research before anything showed up.

Travis Beals, a senior director on the project, went further into candour than executives usually dare. If everything works perfectly five years from now, he said, it probably means Google didn’t take enough risk and didn’t learn enough. And the endpoint he described is not glory but boredom: if the whole thing ultimately succeeds, he said, it will become so unremarkable that nobody thinks anything of the fact that their Gemini query was answered in space.

File that away. An executive whose success case is that the technology becomes boring is an executive telling you not to price the moonshot into this quarter’s valuation.

The numbers that actually matter

The reason Suncatcher belongs in a ledger about money rather than a science section is the launch-cost arithmetic, and here the Times reporting connects to an earlier piece worth pulling forward. On January 1, 2026, Eli Tan and Ryan Mac reported in The New York Times on the broader space-data-centre dream and gathered the figures that decide whether any of this is a business or a fantasy.

A kilogram of material costs around 8,000 US dollars to launch into space, per the space economist Pierre Lionnet of Eurospace. SpaceX offers the cheapest rate at roughly 2,000 dollars per kilogram. An individual server rack in a data centre can weigh over 1,000 kilograms. The economics only begin to make sense, according to Phil Metzger of the University of Central Florida, if launch costs fall to around 200 dollars per kilogram — a drop he thinks would take about a decade. Google’s own Suncatcher research paper predicted costs could reach that level in the mid-2030s, and, tellingly, used the very same driverless-car comparison Manyika reached for.

Lionnet, for his part, was scathing about the whole proposition, calling the larger versions of it completely nonsensical, and offered the analogy this ledger will be quoting for a while: saying we’ll build vast space data centres once launch is cheap enough, he said, is like saying that if you could get a McDonald’s cheeseburger down to ten cents you would buy so many of them. The conditional is doing all the work, and the conditional is not met.

Set that beside the depreciation problem this ledger raised in Parts III and XIII. Tan and Mac reported that space data centres would need to be rebuilt every five years, because that is when the chips are typically replaced. A terrestrial data centre that must be replaced every five years is already an aggressive depreciation schedule, as Michael Burry’s critique in Part III argued. A data centre that must be re-launched into orbit every five years, at thousands of dollars per kilogram, is a depreciation schedule that would make an accountant reach for the smelling salts. Nobody in the reporting to date has answered how those write-downs get modelled, which is precisely the unresolved question Part XIII left open, still unresolved.

Why the giants are converging here anyway

If the arithmetic is this hostile, why are Musk, Bezos, Altman, Huang and now Google all circling the same idea? The Tan and Mac piece contains the most honest answer, and it is not about physics.

Tom Mueller, a former SpaceX executive, put it flatly: the hottest thing to invest in right now is A.I., and the second-hottest thing is space, and now the two are converging. That sentence is the reason Suncatcher belongs in a ledger about a possible bubble rather than in a science feature. When the two most overheated investment categories of the decade fuse into a single pitch — A.I. data centres, but in space — the appropriate first question is not “will it work” but “whose balance sheet benefits from the announcement regardless of whether it works.” SpaceX’s chief financial officer, Bret Johnsen, told shareholders in December 2025 that the company would explore an initial public offering partly to raise money for projects including A.I. data centres in space. The exploration did not take long. On June 11, 2026, SpaceX priced its shares at 135 dollars, raising 75 billion dollars in the largest initial public offering in history, three times Saudi Aramco’s 2019 record, and began trading on Nasdaq the next day, TechCrunch reported. The dream is already collateral.

Not everyone in the analyst trade is willing to lend against it. In February 2026, The Register reported that Bill Ray, a distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, had described the rush into orbital data centres as “peak insanity” and the money pouring in as feeding a bubble. His objections read like a checklist of the problems Google’s engineers are now testing one at a time: solar panels built for orbit costing roughly a thousand times their terrestrial equivalents, temperature swings from 100 to 400 kelvin, laser downlinks interrupted by cloud, and maintenance that would require sending engineers to space, “a capability that does not currently exist.” He allowed for niche uses, such as processing satellite imagery and managing space-based communications. Serving the terrestrial A.I. market was not among them.

Even the believers concede the arithmetic. The Register reported in April 2026 that Euwyn Poon, chief executive of the start-up Orbital, which is planning a 10,000-satellite constellation, put launch on a Falcon 9 at roughly 7,000 dollars a kilogram and said his business needed something closer to 10 dollars. “As of today, the economics of launch don’t quite work yet,” he said. “I believe we’ll get there.” That is Lionnet’s cheeseburger, ordered anyway.

There is a genuine engineering case underneath, and fairness to Google requires stating it. The pull toward orbit is real: near-continuous sunlight, no clouds, no neighbours to file noise complaints, and fewer environmental regulations than any county on Earth. The radiation problem, long treated as disqualifying, turned out to be more tractable than expected — Google blasted its chips with five years’ worth of space radiation at a Manhattan Project-era cyclotron in Davis, California, and found that restarting the chips usually reset the errors. These are not nothing. Google has done actual work and is launching an actual satellite, which is more than most of the orbital-compute talk amounts to.

But “we solved radiation in a lab” and “we can profitably run a data centre in orbit” are separated by exactly the launch-cost chasm Lionnet describes, and Google’s own research paper puts the crossing in the mid-2030s at the earliest.

The ledger’s entry

So here is how Suncatcher goes into the book.

It is the most credible orbital-compute effort yet, precisely because the people running it keep telling you not to believe the biggest version of it. Manyika expects nothing useful for years. Beals defines success as eventual boredom. The research paper points to the mid-2030s. Against that measured internal voice sits the external chorus — Musk musing about 300 gigawatts of orbital data centres, more than half of what the entire United States uses in a year — that this ledger has learned to treat as narrative rather than forecast.

The pattern is by now familiar from Parts V, VI and XIII: a genuine engineering project at the centre, wrapped in a promise several orders of magnitude larger than the project supports, floated at exactly the moment when the two hottest asset classes of the era happen to overlap. A hair dryer’s worth of power, one server’s worth of chips, fifteen minutes of compute at a time — and, orbiting it, a valuation that assumes the cheeseburger already costs ten cents.

Measure that against the plan as first announced. When Google unveiled Suncatcher in November 2025, Data Center Dynamics reported, it promised two prototype satellites with Planet by early 2027, and its research paper sketched a possible cluster of 81 satellites flying within a kilometre of one another, linked by lasers carrying roughly ten terabits a second, while cautioning that “the final scale could change.” Sundar Pichai said at the time that, like any moonshot, it would require solving a lot of complex engineering challenges. The distance between one satellite and eighty-one is the distance this ledger keeps measuring.

The satellite went up as planned on October 1, riding SpaceX’s Transporter-18 rideshare out of Vandenberg, and Google said it had made contact and the satellite was operating as expected, RuntimeWire reported. Tech Times reported on October 2, 2026 that the Falcon 9 lifted off at 2:32 p.m. Eastern time carrying 130 payloads, and that Google’s was one of them, on a spacecraft built by the Earth-imaging company Planet. The first weeks are a survival test: how the chips handle launch stress, radiation and the swing between sunlight and shadow. The satellite is expected to be useful for about a year and is set to circle the Earth for up to six before gravity drags it down to burn up in the atmosphere. Somewhere in that arc is the honest lifespan of the promise, too: real, modest, genuinely interesting, and nowhere near what it is being sold as.
