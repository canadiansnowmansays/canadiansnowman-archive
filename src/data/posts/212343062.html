Part XXIII of this series was written before either government had said much beyond a press statement. Since posting this morning, both have made their case in detail — Jamieson Greer in an interview with the New York Times, Mark Carney in a twenty-two-minute address in Ottawa — and the accounts are close enough on facts and far enough apart on meaning that they are worth setting side by side. This is not a piece about who is lying. It is a piece about two governments that spent a week in the same room and came out describing different negotiations.

I. The American Account

As Ana Swanson reported for the New York Times, Greer used a Saturday interview to lay out, for the first time publicly, what Washington says was actually on the table. The itemized version: elimination of the 10 percent softwood lumber tariff imposed last year under Section 232. Reduction of the 25 percent auto tariff, which Greer said could have fallen as low as 7 percent once Canada’s qualification for further reduction on vehicles containing any American content was applied. Steel cut to 25 percent from 50 for the majority of Canadian volume under a tariff-rate quota. Aluminum cut to 25 percent from 50 with no quota limitation at all. Downstream steel and aluminum products cut by 10 to 25 percentage points, with a floor of 15 percent. Suspension of the new 50 percent tariffs on dairy, wine, hockey sticks and the rest. Cooperation on critical minerals and transshipment.

Greer’s characterization of that package was that it would have given Canada “the most preferential treatment of any trading partner” — that the arrangement would, in his words, “continue their best access and improve it.”

Two caveats belong here rather than in a footnote. First, this is one participant’s account of a confidential negotiation, given to one reporter, with the Canadian government declining to comment on it. Nothing in it is corroborated. Second, and more substantively: every item on that list is a tariff rate. Greer’s case is that Washington offered real money. It is not, and does not claim to be, an answer to what Carney says actually ended the talks.

II. The Canadian Account

Carney’s version doesn’t dispute the rates. He disputes what came attached to them.

By his account, Canada had already agreed to a great deal: pulling its retaliatory tariffs on American steel, aluminum, and cars, and persuading the provinces to restore American liquor sales in systems that had banned them. What Canada would not do was accept the demands that arrived late — protections for French-language and Canadian content in streaming, subsidies for publishing and film production, and mandatory French labeling on packaging, all of which Washington wanted rolled back. “Threats on French language and culture and Canadian culture,” Carney said in French, “would never be acceptable for that reason. That marked the end of that stage of negotiations.”

Alongside that sat the demand Carney described as “a power play” and “a question of sovereignty”: that Canada align its tariff policy toward third countries with American tariffs. “We are the partners of choice for countries around the world,” he said, “and the Americans wanted to restrict that — unacceptable.”

He also named specific rate items Washington wouldn’t move on, which complicates Greer’s account rather than contradicting it: the U.S. refused to count Canadian as well as American parts in the auto content calculation, refused to reduce the rate on medium and heavy trucks, and offered little for the wide category of products deemed primarily steel or aluminum — everything from antennas to snowmobiles — which remain at 25 percent.

“In short, they asked too much and offered too little.”

III. Why Both Can Be True

Read the two accounts against each other and the disagreement isn’t really factual. Greer describes a set of tariff reductions. Carney describes a set of non-tariff demands. Neither man is answering the other, because the deal each is describing was, in the end, a different deal.

That’s not a rhetorical trick — it’s the actual shape of the impasse. If your objection to an agreement is that it asks you to give up domestic cultural policy and independent trade relations with third countries, then a better rate on aluminum does not address your objection. It isn’t a smaller version of what you wanted; it’s a different currency entirely. Greer can say truthfully that Canada was offered the best terms of any exporter, and Carney can say truthfully that Washington asked too much, and the two statements never touch.

This series has spent twenty-three parts arguing that the tariffs were never really about the stated grievance. Section III of Part XXII made the case that the shifting justifications were themselves the tell. What’s different this week is that the American ask finally became explicit enough to test that reading: a demand that Canada subordinate its third-country trade policy to Washington’s is not a complaint about Canadian trade practice. It is a demand about who decides. Whatever else is in dispute, that item was on the table, and Carney named it as the reason he left.

IV. What the Numbers Actually Say

The rhetoric on both sides has run hot enough that it’s worth pausing on the economics, which are considerably cooler.

As Frances Donald, chief economist at RBC, laid out in a Saturday assessment, the aggregate impact on the Canadian economy should be manageable. The Section 338 tranche covers roughly five percent of Canadian exports to the United States — which still leaves about eighty percent of Canada’s trade with the U.S. moving tariff-free, most of it shielded by CUSMA rules of origin. RBC’s own figures put Canada’s average effective U.S. tariff rate at roughly five percent before this week, rising by around a point with the new measures. For context, that leaves Canada below the world average, below the Euro Area, below Japan, and roughly a quarter of what China faces.

But Donald’s second point matters more than the first: five percent is not nothing, and the pain is concentrated rather than distributed. For the specific sectors caught in this tranche — plastics, hockey stick manufacturers, the forestry products this series noted earlier — the impact could be acute, depending heavily on what support governments provide. And it lands unevenly by province. Alberta has come through the trade war relatively unscathed because of what it exports. Ontario and Quebec have absorbed most of the damage to date. British Columbia now takes the largest incremental hit of any province from this round.

Donald was equally clear about what nobody yet knows, and the list is a useful corrective to the certainty in both governments’ statements. How long will these tariffs stay in place, given how many previous rounds were withdrawn within weeks? What will federal and provincial support actually look like? What will Canada’s retaliation target, and when? On monetary policy, she suggested this probably isn’t enough to prompt rate cuts, but it does make hikes less likely in the near term. And the hardest variable to model: Canadian confidence — how ordinary people and businesses actually feel and behave, which she noted is far more a matter of hearts and minds than anything an economist can put in a spreadsheet.

That last point deserves weight. Carney’s political position rests on it, and so does the durability of his decision. A country that feels attacked will absorb costs it wouldn’t otherwise tolerate. A country that grows tired will start asking why the deal wasn’t taken.

V. One Discrepancy Worth Noting

A small thing, but this series notices small things. Washington’s figure for the tariffed goods is about $20 billion in Canadian exports. The Prime Minister’s own written statement, issued Friday night, put it at roughly $28 billion.

Neither government has explained the gap, and it may be nothing more than different methodologies for counting affected trade lines. But it’s an odd detail in a dispute where both sides are asking their publics to accept their arithmetic on faith, and where the underlying question — how much damage is actually being done — is precisely what neither side can afford to have measured independently.

VI. What Nobody Else Has Done

There is a piece of context here worth stating plainly, because it reframes the whole week.

Britain took a deal. The European Union took a deal. Other major American trading partners took deals — smaller ones, volatile ones, arrangements that by most accounts left them worse off than where they started, with tariffs permanently baked into the relationship. Canada, as of Friday night, is the first major U.S. ally to walk away from that model rather than accept a version of it.

Whether that proves wise is genuinely unknown, and this series isn’t going to pretend otherwise. Canada is more exposed than any of those partners — the dependency figures this series has cited since Part XXII haven’t changed. Danielle Smith, Alberta’s premier, is already urging a return to the table, and Canadian industry is, by the Times’ account, aghast at the prolonged uncertainty. Doug Ford and David Eby, from opposite ends of the political spectrum, are backing Carney. That split will widen if this drags.

But the interesting question isn’t whether Carney was right. It’s what other capitals do now that someone has demonstrated the option exists. The Times’ own Canada bureau chief raised it directly: after Davos, European leaders were already treating Carney’s read of the Trump era as the authoritative one. A prime minister who then actually declines the deal, publicly, and survives the week, changes the calculation for every government that has been keeping its head down.

VII. The Ledger

Twenty-four parts in, here is what can be said with confidence and what can’t.

Both governments have now given detailed, mutually incompatible accounts of the same week, and both accounts are probably honest, because they are describing different objects. The rates were real. The sovereignty demands were also real. Canada declined the package because the second outweighed the first, and Washington is presenting the first as though the second didn’t exist.

The economics are more manageable than the rhetoric on either side suggests, and more painful than the aggregate numbers imply for the specific people caught in it. The political question — whether Canadians stay willing to pay for this position once the abstraction becomes a plant closure in Windsor or a mill in British Columbia — is the one nobody can answer yet, including Carney.

And Greer has already said there are no new talks scheduled. Whatever this was, it isn’t over.
