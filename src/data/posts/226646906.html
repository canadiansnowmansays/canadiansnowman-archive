JOINT TASK FORCE ON SUBURBAN WILDLIFE AND ORGANISED GOBBLING INTELLIGENCE BULLETIN No. GT-1126 Classification: Unrestricted. Post on every fridge. Issued: Thursday, November 26, 2026 (American Thanksgiving)

1. SUMMARY

The Task Force is tracking a loosely organised, highly territorial group active in cities and suburbs in every state except Alaska. Members are up to four feet tall, weigh as much as 25 pounds, and in the case of adult males carry spurs on the backs of their legs.

The group is known on the street as the Rafter, which is the correct collective noun, and which the Atlantic, in its recent report on the subject, failed to use. Readers noticed. The Task Force has made a note.

Residents are advised that the Rafter is not visiting. It lives here now. Roughly half of all wild turkeys in the United States now live in cities or suburbs, and their numbers in some rural areas are falling. The birds, like certain other movements, have discovered that they do better where the people are.

2. ORIGINS

Sixty years ago the Commonwealth of Massachusetts had no wild turkeys at all. The original population had been hunted and logged out of existence.

In the 1970s the state brought in thirty-seven birds. They were imported from New York. They were released in the Berkshires, where they multiplied, and from there they spread steadily east towards Boston, gaining confidence with every mile.

The Task Force notes, without further comment, that this is not the last time a loud import from New York has started small, been underestimated, and then taken over the neighbourhood.

3. IDENTIFYING COLOURS

Members of the Rafter signal aggression through the fleshy snoods and wattles on their heads. When a bird is threatened or spoiling for a fight, these turn bright red, white, or blue.

Residents should understand that these are not decorative. A bird showing all three colours is not celebrating anything. It is about to come at you.

The Task Force is aware that some members of the public find this colour scheme familiar. Readers of the original report remarked on it in the comments within minutes. The Task Force is a wildlife agency and takes no position on the matter, except to say that it is an accurate description of the birds.

4. KNOWN INCIDENTS (PARTIAL LIST)

  • Pittsburgh: a man chased nearly a quarter of a mile.

  • A Chicago suburb: police led on a thirty-minute pursuit through a home-improvement store. The suspect was not apprehended in Lumber.

  • Minneapolis: the windows of a home shattered at six in the morning, apparently to make a point.

  • Cambridge, Massachusetts, 2023: two members knocked down a postal worker, broke his hip, and pecked him while he was down.

  • Massachusetts, ongoing: a mother escorting her children to the bus stop every morning after the group scratched one of them.

  • Oakland, California (historical): a turkey known as Gerald ran a long campaign against frail-looking older women in the Rose Garden, relieving them of their lunches. He was finally brought in by an animal-control officer who disguised herself as a frail-looking older woman. Gerald fell for it. They usually do.

5. TARGET SELECTION

Postal carriers are at particular risk. The birds read colour as a challenge, so a person in a blue uniform who climbs out of a white truck and walks the same route at the same time every day looks, to the Rafter, like a rival making a regular, provocative appearance on its turf. The state biologist describes the result as frequent and violent.

The Task Force observes that the Rafter is not the first group in America to take a dislike to the postal service, and urges letter carriers to walk with an umbrella.

Members also attack parked cars. On seeing their own reflection in the paintwork, a bird concludes that another, equally handsome turkey has invaded its space, and sets about the car. It is the one rival the bird has never beaten, and it keeps trying.

6. ORGANISATIONAL STRUCTURE

The groups causing the most trouble are almost always roving bands of young males following a single ringleader. Massachusetts wildlife officials describe it as a teenage-gang mentality: one of them is genuinely bad, and the rest go along with him.

The single most important finding in this bulletin is what happens next. When the ringleader is removed, the rest of the group stops being a problem. Almost at once. They do not regroup around a successor. They simply go back to being turkeys.

The Task Force offers this finding to any jurisdiction, of any kind, that may find it useful.

One ringleader under surveillance in a Boston suburb is known to officers as the Fonz, aka Henry W. He leans against parked cars. He struts from one stopped car to the next at intersections, as if checking each for a frozen turkey in the back seat. Every morning he studies his reflection in a car door, considers grooming, and decides he is already perfect. This is just as well, since turkeys do not have combs. That is chickens. The Fonz has been told this and does not care. For him, these are happy days: no hunting allowed, no predators to speak of, and a bird feeder on every second porch. The Task Force would like the happy days to end. Officers expect him to jump the shark by spring.

7. BEHAVIOURAL PROFILE

  • Ground-dwelling. Unlike pigeons and squirrels, the Rafter spends the whole day on the ground, with us. It retires to the treetops at night, above it all.

  • Hierarchical. Members become so used to people that they count humans as part of the pecking order, and believe they must put us in our place.

  • Seasonally worse. Aggression peaks in spring, when adult males compete for mates and do not understand that the humans want no part in it.

  • Fed by the public. Much of the problem is driven by bird feeders. The Rafter grows bolder the more it is fed, and the people feeding it are often the most surprised.

  • Spineless. In the professional opinion of the state biologist, the birds are cowards at heart. They prefer to attack from behind.

8. THANKSGIVING AND CHRISTMAS ADVISORY

Today, American households will gather to carve the Rafter’s domestic cousins. Christmas follows in four weeks. The wild birds are aware of both dates, and residents should expect heightened activity in driveways and on doorsteps through the season.

The Task Force has also become aware of a second, more worrying trend. The residents are now looking back.

With grocery bills where they are, households across the suburbs have noticed that they are paying full price for a frozen turkey at the supermarket while a free one, at least twenty pounds and fully organic, stands on the lawn every morning glaring at the recycling bin. Officers report residents measuring birds by eye from their front windows. One man was seen comparing a tom on his driveway with the roasting pan.

9. RESIDENTS ON A BUDGET: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: There is a 25-pound turkey on my lawn and the supermarket wants a small fortune for one. Can I just have it? A: No. Hunting is almost always illegal in urban and suburban areas. This is, in fact, why the Rafter moved in. The turkeys read the bylaws before you did.

Q: It has eaten my vegetable garden. Doesn’t it owe me a dinner? A: The Task Force understands the logic. The law does not. The turkey may eat your garden. You may not eat the turkey. The Task Force did not write this arrangement and agrees that it seems one-sided.

Q: Are wild turkeys even good to eat? A: Readers who have tried say the meat is lean, tough and best kept for soups or stews, and that it tastes of whatever the bird has been eating. In the suburbs, that means your tulip bulbs, the neighbour’s bird feeder and the acorns on the boulevard. Residents hoping for a Butterball should lower their expectations.

Q: My grandfather says the turkey was nearly our national bird. Does that make it harder to eat? A: Benjamin Franklin is often said to have preferred it to the eagle. He was probably joking. The bald eagle is also off the menu.

Q: What about Christmas? Surely by December… A: The bylaws do not change at Christmas. Neither does the turkey’s attitude.

Q: Then how exactly are we supposed to fight back? A: See Section 10.

10. OPERATION OPEN UMBRELLA: A LAWFUL NEIGHBOURHOOD RESPONSE

Residents who are tired of being chased down their own driveways, and of paying full price for the privilege, may join the Task Force’s community programme, Operation Open Umbrella. Its tactics are entirely legal, cost almost nothing, and have been tested by the City of Cambridge:

  1. Do not run. Running is exactly what the turkeys want.

  2. Make yourself look big. Spread your arms. Stand your ground.

  3. Open an umbrella. It works, and it is the cheapest piece of security equipment on the market.

  4. Use the garden hose. Water is still, at the time of writing, cheaper than turkey.

  5. Walk in pairs. Do not let them get behind you. This is where most residents go wrong.

  6. Take down the bird feeders. Every feeder is a free buffet for the gang. Residents subsidising the Rafter with forty-pound sacks of seed while complaining about grocery prices are asked to examine their own budgets.

  7. Report the ringleader. See Section 6. Remove the one bad bird, and the rest of the gang goes back to being turkeys.

Residents are reminded that the bird charging at them, however loud, is mostly feathers. Turkeys have hollow bones. Their confidence is not.

The Task Force notes that residents who have recently arrived from Hong Kong may find the name of this operation confusing, since they remember a different Umbrella Movement. The Task Force apologises for any confusion and would add only that theirs set the standard: in 2014, ordinary people raised umbrellas against pepper spray and stood their ground against something much bigger than they were. Operation Open Umbrella hopes to be worthy of the name.

11. A NOTE ON PARDONS

The Task Force has been asked whether the annual presidential pardon of a turkey at the White House will calm the situation. It will not, and the Task Force has studied this year’s pardon system closely enough to explain why.

Every November, the President pardons one or two domestic turkeys. They have never chased a postal carrier, broken a window or knocked anyone down. They are chosen for their good temperament and their manners in front of cameras. They receive a reprieve, a short ceremony and a quiet retirement, usually at a university farm.

Elsewhere in Washington, the pardon has been working on different principles. Roughly 1,500 people charged over the storming of the Capitol were pardoned or had their sentences commuted on the first afternoon, including some who assaulted police officers. A congressman convicted of wire fraud had his sentence commuted. And on Tuesday, two days before Thanksgiving, Rudy Giuliani, pardoned last year, received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in a ceremony closed to the press.

The Task Force has compared the two systems and summarises them as follows:

  • The White House turkey is pardoned for doing nothing wrong, and gets a farm.

  • The White House ally is pardoned for what he did, and gets a medal.

  • The wild turkey on your driveway, which has broken a postal worker’s hip, gets neither, because it has not yet been asked to campaign.

Residents of the Rafter have taken note. The Task Force has received unconfirmed reports of a large tom loitering near the White House fence, snood flushed red, white and blue, waiting to be noticed. Under the current system, the Task Force cannot rule out that it will be.

12. CLOSING NOTE

The household budget and the Rafter now have a great deal in common. Both arrived quietly, both grew bolder every year, and both are eating things that used to be ours.

Only one of them can be scared off with an umbrella. The Task Force recommends starting there.
