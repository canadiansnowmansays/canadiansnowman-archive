Earlier this autumn, Chrystia Freeland was going through security at Toronto’s Island Airport, in the wing reserved for flights to the United States, when the guard at the X-ray machine recognised her. He gave her a big smile. Then he noticed which wing she was in.

“Minister, you know we’re not allowed to go there anymore,” he said.

Freeland explained that she supported Canada’s “very unofficial ban” on nonessential trips south, and that she was flying to Boston to make the case for Canada. He was unmoved. “Well, Minister, go talk to them if that’s what you need to do. But I hope you tell those sons of bitches that we’ll be better off without them, anyway.”

She tells the story in The New York Times on November 17, 2026, in an op-ed headlined “Welcome Back, America! It’s Your Turn to Be Sorry.” She says American audiences gasp at the first line and get angry at the punchline. Her point is that the guard was not being eccentric. He was stating the national consensus.

What makes the op-ed worth a full instalment of this series is that its author has spent the autumn helping to build that consensus. Part V looked at Freeland’s argument in The Atlantic in September that the only thing that works with Donald Trump is strength. Since then she has written or spoken about Canada and America three more times in The New York Times, in July, October and November. Read in order, the three make a story with a turn in it, and the turn came on November 3, 2026, when American voters went to the polls.

I. Summer: Blame Canada

Freeland’s July 28, 2026 op-ed in The New York Times was headlined “’You Aren’t Going to Like What Comes After America’.” It opened with “South Park.” Twenty-odd years ago, the show’s makers imagined a campaign to blame Canada for America’s troubles precisely because the idea was absurd. Now, she wrote, “here we are, with the actual president of the United States, well into his second term, blaming Canada for pretty much everything.”

The pretexts kept changing. First Canada was a national-security threat, which justified tariffs on steel, aluminium and cars. Then it was the boreal forest fires. Then it was dairy, booze and cars again, attached to a threat of tariffs on about $20 billion of Canadian exports, “ranging from hockey sticks to cement.” Freeland argued that the changing excuses were the point. During the 2018 trade talks, an American counterpart told her that since the United States was 10 times bigger, it should get its way 10 times more often. “True power is being able to do whatever you like for whatever reason you like, or for no reason at all.”

Trump had said as much in July: “They need us to survive. Without us, there’s no way they can survive.” Stephen Miller had told CNN’s Jake Tapper earlier in the year that the real world is “governed by strength, that is governed by force, that is governed by power.”

Freeland framed Canada’s choice in the bluntest terms she had used in public. The country could remain an ally and a partner in a relationship governed by treaties and rules, or it could become a vassal, “like the Venezuela of Delcy Rodríguez, only with snowdrifts and hockey.” Appeasement was no escape. “Giving in gracefully is a less theatrical way of arriving at the same destination.” Canada had to fight back, as it had in 2018, and do it in company, which was why she praised her former colleague Mark Carney’s call for middle powers to act together.

She ended the summer piece with a warning to Americans and a line borrowed from Leonard Cohen: “You aren’t going to like what comes after America.”

II. Autumn: Mordor and the Hobbits

By the time Freeland sat down with Ezra Klein for an episode of his podcast, published by The New York Times on October 20, 2026 under the title “Trumpism Is Backfiring,” the situation had hardened. In late August, Carney had walked away from trade talks, knowing the retaliation could be ruinous. A January deadline for massive new tariffs was approaching. Klein opened with a poll showing that 41 percent of Canadians now considered the United States an enemy country. Freeland noted that in 2023 Canadians saw America as an ally and China as an adversary. “That position has actually flipped.”

Her best moment was a lesson in the economics Washington keeps ignoring. “Aluminum is basically electricity in solid form,” she said. Quebec makes it because Quebec has cheap hydro. The aluminium Canada normally sends south contains as much electricity as more than 450 data centres, roughly the output of half a dozen Hoover Dams. A 50 percent tariff on it is a 50 percent tariff on power, imposed by a country in the middle of an artificial-intelligence boom that is short of power. Canada, she reminded Klein, is America’s largest export market, bigger than China, Japan and Britain combined, and most of what it sells south is aluminium, lumber and potash that American companies turn into other things. Tariff those, and the people who pay are American.

The politics were starting to show it. In Michigan’s Senate race, the Republican candidate, Mike Rogers, ran an ad with a can of Labatt and the Red Wings on television: “Here in Michigan, we know that Canada is not our enemy. We need to end this tariff war now.” Klein cited polling showing Carney’s counter-tariffs were more popular with Americans than Trump’s tariffs on Canada.

Freeland also told the story of where Canada’s European turn began. During Trump’s first term, she and Cecilia Malmström, then the EU’s trade commissioner, kept a private code. Washington was Mordor, the Europeans were elves, Canadians were hobbits, and when Malmström texted that “Gandalf is going to Mordor next week,” Freeland knew Angela Merkel was off to the White House. The joking-not-joking question then was whether Europe would take Canada in if NAFTA collapsed. This September, as Part VI described, Ursula von der Leyen offered Canada associate membership of the EU, and Klein noted the irony. Trump wanted a 51st state. “Instead, it might become an associate member of the European Union.”

She was careful not to romanticise the alternative. Klein called China “a predictable predatory hegemon,” and Freeland agreed it was predictable but warned against Beijing’s pitch that it upholds the rules: ask Norway about its salmon, Lithuania about Taiwan, or the two Canadians detained after the Huawei arrest. The world she feared was one in which every country that is not America or China is “engaged in a balancing act between these two predatory hegemons.”

Then she named the hinge. American business, she said, “will be on the side of power,” and had largely capitulated. Jamie Dimon’s September 28 op-ed in The Wall Street Journal, calling for a “big, beautiful” trade deal between the United States and Europe, was an early sign that the mood might shift. “I think it will depend on the midterms,” she said.

III. The Hinge Turns

The midterms happened on November 3. Freeland calls the result a “Democratic tsunami,” and she wrote the November 17 op-ed two weeks later to tell her compatriots something they did not want to hear.

They are wrong, she argues, to insist that the rupture with the United States must be irrevocable. The democracies began to give up on America “because they thought America was giving up on democracy,” watching Washington side with dictators abroad and, at home, muzzle universities and the press and unleash “masked agents who shot live ammunition at his citizens.” On November 3, Americans “categorically chose democracy.” They showed “that American democracy still actually works.”

She knows it is a hard sell, and she quotes the people who will make it hard. Hans Petter Midttun, a former Norwegian defence attaché to Ukraine, has said that after Trump’s betrayal of Kyiv “the U.S. is no longer an ally, but a strategic adversary.” She recalls Benjamin Haddad, then a French legislator, warning in early 2024 that relying on voters in Montana, Wisconsin and Michigan would leave Europe’s security “flipping a coin every four years.” And she quotes The Beaverton, Canada’s answer to The Onion, which greeted the midterms with a line built on her own “unreliable boyfriend” metaphor: Canada “wishes to gently, yet firmly, let all of America know that we will still not be taking you back.”

Her answer is not to forgive, but to renegotiate. Jean Chrétien likes to joke that Trump deserves the Order of Canada for forcing Canada to do the hard work of becoming stronger and more independent. Freeland’s point is that the hard work is precisely what makes a better relationship possible: “a 21st century partnership, in which responsibilities and authority are more equally shared.” She proposes three pillars. The first is trade, starting with a bill Democrats plan to introduce in January to lift tariffs on allies. The second is Ukraine, where Europe must “do a Draghi” and give Kyiv “whatever it takes” through what looks like a horrific winter. The third is artificial intelligence and China, where a shared transatlantic policy could form a common front. She adds a smaller gesture, borrowed from Canadian satirists: fire the American ambassador to Canada.

But she sets a clock on all of it. “Deep renewal of the democratic alliance will be possible only after the 2028 presidential election in the United States.”

IV. Weather, Climate and a Coin Toss

Part VI of this series set out two readings of the Trump years. Canada’s generals, as Philippe Lagassé reported, mostly treated him as weather: a severe storm that would pass. The allied diplomats Vivian Salama interviewed treated him as climate: a lasting change that would outlive him whoever won in 2028. Part VII found Canada’s officers drifting from the first view towards the second.

Freeland’s three essays answer the question in a way neither camp quite did. In July she asked whether Trump was “saying out loud what most Americans have really thought of their allies all along.” The midterms gave her an answer: no, not most of them. But they did not settle the second question, which is whether the country that elected him twice will do it again in some form. Her 2028 deadline concedes Haddad’s point. As long as the answer turns on a few swing states every four years, America’s allies are entitled to treat its friendship as a bet rather than a guarantee.

That is why her advice and Carney’s strategy fit together better than the headlines suggest. Carney is building what he calls a “dense web of connections,” which is the subject of Part VIII, so that no single partner can squeeze Canada again. Freeland is not asking him to stop. She is asking him to leave a place in the web for the United States, on terms that reflect what Canada has learned. The web is not a substitute for a reconciliation. It is the leverage for a better one.

What neither of them controls is the public. Freeland’s summer and autumn pieces drew on the same numbers: 41 percent of Canadians calling America an enemy, more Canadians viewing China favourably than the United States, a de facto boycott of cross-border trips. Those numbers were not produced by a single election, and they will not be undone by one. Stephen Harper, hardly a Liberal, said in early 2025 that Canada should pay whatever economic price was needed to defend its sovereignty. When a former Conservative prime minister and an airport security guard agree, a strategist would be wise to assume the feeling runs deep.

V. It’s a Start

Freeland ends the November piece on the other side of the border. When she landed at Logan, it was cold, so she put on her red maple-leaf hoodie. An older couple with New England accents approached her hesitantly and asked whether she was Canadian. Trust, but verify.

When she said yes, they smiled sadly. “We just wanted to say that we are really, really sorry.”

“That is one of the messages Americans sent to the world when they voted this month,” she writes. “It’s a start.”

It is a fine ending, and an honest one, because she does not pretend it is more. Two airports, two encounters, a few hundred miles apart. At one, a Canadian tells a former deputy prime minister to pass on the message that Canada will be better off without them. At the other, two Americans apologise to a stranger in a hoodie. Both are true, and neither cancels the other.

Even The Beaverton left the door ajar. “That’s not to say that we can’t be won back, of course! God knows we’re a sucker for second-chance romances.”

Second chances in romance usually come with conditions, and Freeland has drafted hers: lift the tariffs, stand by Ukraine, work out a common front on China, and prove it again in 2028. The summer warning still stands. Canadians may not like what comes after America. They are now working out whether they would like a different America better, and they are in no hurry to decide.
