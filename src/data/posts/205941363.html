Stephen Gordon July 10, 2026

Nine days ago, in Part XIV, I told you July 1 was a checkpoint, not a cliff — that the USMCA’s Article 34.7 joint review had come and gone without anyone driving off the edge of anything, that the treaty would simply grind into its annual-review purgatory while Washington decided, at its leisure, whether the deal it once called the fairest trade agreement ever signed was still worth signing.

I did not expect Canada to answer the question quite this fast, or quite this loudly. But on July 6, in Halifax, Prime Minister Mark Carney did something no checkpoint requires and no cliff explains: he announced the single largest defence procurement in Canadian history, handed it to a German shipbuilder over an American one — because there wasn’t an American one in contention to begin with — and then boarded a plane for the NATO summit in Ankara, where the President of the United States spent two days telling the assembled alliance that America’s patience had run out, on more than one front at once.

Call it the off-ramp. Canada built it in six weeks, mostly in public, and mostly without waiting for permission.

I. The Sky

Start with the smaller of the two announcements, because it’s the one that shows the divorce is not merely financial. On May 27, at the CANSEC defence trade show in Ottawa — the first time a sitting Canadian prime minister had ever bothered to show up — Carney announced Canada was entering negotiations with Sweden’s Saab for six GlobalEye airborne early warning aircraft, passing over Boeing’s competing Wedgetail. The stated rationale wasn’t subtle: Ottawa wants to reduce its reliance on American military technology, and a Bombardier-built jet with Swedish radar, one-third assembled on Canadian soil, does exactly that.

The technical follow-up made the point better than any politician could have. Within weeks, CBC was reporting that fully integrating the GlobalEye into NORAD would require access to America’s Multifunction Advanced Data Link — the classified stealth communications layer the U.S. has never released to a foreign manufacturer, and that experts doubt it ever will. In other words: Canada bought a surveillance jet explicitly to need Washington less, and immediately discovered that Washington’s cooperation is the one ingredient the jet can’t function fully without. That isn’t a plot hole. It’s the entire trade war in miniature — the diversification is real, and the dependency it’s diversifying away from turns out to have a lock on the door nobody thought to check before signing.

II. The Deep

The submarine announcement is the one that actually rewrites the ledger. On July 6, standing at the HMC Dockyard in Halifax, Carney named Germany’s ThyssenKrupp Marine Systems — building the Type 212CD jointly with Norway — as Canada’s preferred supplier for up to twelve new submarines, beating out South Korea’s Hanwha Ocean in a contest that had run for nearly two years. Carney called it, without qualification, the largest defence procurement in Canadian history. Estimates for the full life of the program, including decades of maintenance and infrastructure, run as high as $100 billion Canadian.

Notice what wasn’t on the shortlist. This was never a Germany-versus-America contest. The United States doesn’t build the conventional, under-ice-capable, 3,000-tonne diesel-electric submarines Canada’s navy actually needs; American doctrine has run nuclear-powered boats for decades. So this wasn’t Canada choosing Europe over Washington in some dramatic snub. It was Canada discovering — the same way it discovered with the GlobalEye — that the alternative to American dependency had been sitting there all along, fully NATO-interoperable, offered by a company that already arms more than a third of the alliance. The snub isn’t in the choosing. The snub is in how little Washington had to say about it, because there was nothing for Washington to say. It wasn’t in the room.

Carney’s own language did the satirist’s work in advance. The subs, he said, would deepen partnerships with trusted allies and open new opportunities in European supply chains — which is about as close as a sitting prime minister gets to saying, on camera, we found other people to trust.

III. The Timing, Which Nobody in Ottawa Denies Was the Point

Here is the detail I can’t improve on by inventing anything: Carney made the submarine announcement in Halifax and then flew directly to Ankara for the NATO summit. Analysts covering the deal noted outright that the choice carried geostrategic weight, pulling Canada further into Europe’s orbit precisely as the United States pulls away from the alliance it built. Nobody had to write that subtext in. Reporters covering the announcement supplied it themselves, on the record, within hours.

So picture the two-day sequence Carney flew into. On Tuesday, Trump reopened the Greenland file within hours of landing, telling reporters beside Erdoğan that the U.S. “could remove all of our soldiers out of Europe” if Denmark kept resisting, and that the island “should be controlled by the United States, not by Denmark.” By Wednesday he’d widened the complaint into something closer to a verdict on the alliance itself, calling NATO a paper tiger for declining to hand American forces open base access during the strikes on Iran — a grievance he aimed at the whole room, Canada included, not at Copenhagen alone. That NATO also spent the same two days unveiling more than $50 billion in new procurement commitments to placate him tells you which argument he was actually winning.

And sitting at that table is the leader of a country that, less than twenty-four hours earlier, quietly signed away the largest defence contract in its history to two other NATO members — not as retaliation, not with a speech, just as a Tuesday-morning fact on the ground. Trump asked for loyalty and base access. Ottawa had already filed the paperwork on a different kind of insurance policy.

V. The TACO Test, Applied to a Different Country

Longtime readers of this series know the framework by now: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — the loop Trump runs on every trading partner who hasn’t yet learned to stop answering the threats. Canada spent the first several parts of this series inside that loop, accommodating, half-capitulating, absorbing tariffs on autos and steel and softwood while Washington obfuscated about deadlines that kept sliding.

What’s notable about Parts XIV and XV, read together, is that Ottawa appears to have quietly exited the loop altogether — not by winning an argument with Washington, but by building somewhere else to shop. Carney more or less confirmed the strategy himself, days later, fielding questions in Jeddah: Canada, he said, has become over-reliant on a partner it still values, a dependency he called already demonstrated, and one his government is now deliberately working to diversify away from. That is about as close as this prime minister gets to narrating the thesis of this series back to its author.

You cannot run TACO on a country that has already placed its order with a different vendor. The threat still lands. It’s just increasingly unclear what it’s supposed to be leverage over.

VI. The Ledger’s Other Column

Here is where the story complicates itself, and I’d be doing you no favours pretending otherwise.

The same week the defence headlines were landing, Carney was in Jeddah fielding a different kind of question — about the file this series actually exists to track. Global News’ Mackenzie Gray noted that Carney had just shared a long phone call with Trump at the NATO summit, thumbs-up and all, caught on camera, and asked directly whether that warmth had produced any actual progress on trade. Carney’s answer was four words: I’ll keep you posted. That is not the language of a government that considers the American file closed, settled, or safely diversified away from. It’s the language of a government still waiting on Washington to decide something.

Then there’s the bridge. The same press conference surfaced word that the U.S. ambassador has pressed Canada on two specific asks regarding the Gordie Howe International Bridge: control of the structure, and a larger cut of the tolls. Asked point-blank whether Ottawa would renegotiate to get the bridge open, Carney didn’t say no. He said Canada is willing to clarify aspects of the current arrangement — diplomatic language, in this series’ experience, for a concession still being priced.

So the honest picture isn’t a clean divorce. It’s two tracks running in parallel. On defence, Ottawa has stopped waiting at the checkpoint entirely, and Washington had nothing to say about it because it wasn’t in the room to say it. On trade — the file with LeBlanc, the tariffs, the bridge, the USMCA review itself — Ottawa is still very much in the room, still clarifying, still keeping Washington posted. The submarines are a hedge, not an exit. Carney seems to understand the difference even if the headlines this week didn’t always make room for it.

VII. The Checkpoint, Revisited

I closed Part XIV by noting the USMCA hadn’t fallen off a cliff on July 1 — that the treaty would simply keep existing in its diminished, under-review form, technically alive, functionally uncertain. I don’t think I need to revise that. But I’d add a clause. The checkpoint didn’t collapse. On defence, Canada stopped waiting at it, put the car in gear, and took the exit marked Kiel and the one marked Linköping. On trade, the car is still idling at the checkpoint, hazard lights on, waiting to see what the toll booth wants this time.

Somewhere in Ankara this week, the President of the United States told thirty-one allies that America’s patience with freeloaders — and with anyone who wouldn’t help against Iran — had run out. One of those allies has already spent that particular patience on submarines. The same ally is still, by its own prime minister’s account, keeping Washington posted on everything else.
