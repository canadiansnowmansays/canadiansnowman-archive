Danny Hillis, computing pioneer, early ARPANET user, and parallel processing innovator — the man who told Josh Tyrangiel to “imagine the tech without the tech companies.

Danny Hillis was one of the first people on the internet, back when it was called ARPANET and the community of users was small enough that he knew all the other Dannys online. His work on parallel processing helped create cloud computing, which laid the foundation for the rise of artificial intelligence. He has seen every gold rush in Silicon Valley, and his heart rate, by all accounts, is as steady as the Buddha’s.

When Josh Tyrangiel arrived at Hillis’s door, exhausted from months of interviewing accelerationists who predicted AI would cure cancer and doomers who predicted it would end civilization, he asked the simplest possible question: what is AI actually good for?

Hillis was ready.

“Try to imagine the tech without the tech companies,” he said.

Tyrangiel, a staff writer at The Atlantic and former editor of Bloomberg Businessweek, describes this as a moment of embarrassment. The thought had not previously occurred to him. The technology and the companies that build it had become so fused in the public imagination — in the courtroom in Oakland, in the congressional hearing rooms, in the governance debates this series has been tracking for thirty-four parts — that separating them required a deliberate act of imagination.

Hillis was certain that an AI counterculture existed beyond the tech megalopolises. People experimenting with AI in ways more meaningful than the latest chatbot-calendar integration. People who had encountered a problem that defied conventional solutions and were stubborn or desperate enough — or just cared enough — to keep going.

He was right. Tyrangiel found them.

What the Counterculture Looks Like

A cardiologist reviews AI-enhanced cardiac imaging, illustrating practical tools making lifesaving scans more accessible.

A Cleveland Clinic cardiologist using AI to make lifesaving heart scans available to people who would otherwise never receive them. Teachers in an Indiana school district finding new ways to engage students who had been left behind by conventional instruction. A former physicist racing to build AI-powered translation for nonverbal autistic children — including her own son. Government technologists using AI to drag federal agencies toward functionality without anyone noticing, because noticing would get them killed.

These people share something that distinguishes them from the figures who have occupied most of this series. They don’t believe in “move fast and break things” — because they don’t want to break things. They want to fix things. They encountered problems that resisted conventional solutions and decided that AI, understood as a tool rather than a ideology, might help.

“The people in the AI counterculture have figured out that the only effective response to a transformative technology is not to hide from it but to get your hands dirty and make it work to preserve and improve the things you care about,” Tyrangiel writes. “That’s not naive optimism — it’s enlightened self-interest.”

This series has spent thirty-four parts documenting the failure of governance at the top. The billionaires, the Pentagon, the courtroom, the deleted AGI clause, the dissolved safety teams, the 80 percent of AI insiders who now support regulation that the Lieu bill has still not produced. All of that is real and documented and consequential.

This essay is about something the series has not yet examined: what is happening in the middle, where the technology meets actual problems, in the hands of people who are not building empires.

The IRS Fight Club

The IRS headquarters in Washington, D.C.—home to the “Fight Club” modernization efforts using AI to update legacy systems like the Individual Master File

A week before the 2024 presidential election, Tyrangiel went to Washington to investigate a rumor: the IRS was up to something.

This required suspending disbelief. The IRS runs on ancient technology. Its headquarters has waiting room carpet the color of cafeteria pudding and a faux-mahogany cabinet displaying unloved plaques and seasonal gourds — a room optimized, Tyrangiel writes, to kill all curiosity. Since its creation in 1862, only one president — John F. Kennedy — has visited its headquarters.

The then-Commissioner, Danny Werfel, received him within two weeks of his request for an interview. He spoke carefully. The IRS, he said, was “bound by rules about inherently governmental functions” and could not simply replace employees with AI. It had a duty to serve all taxpayers equally. It had some of the strictest privacy and cybersecurity requirements in the world. These were the complications. But after cataloguing everything that made AI adoption nearly impossible at the IRS, Werfel quietly began listing the ways the IRS was already using AI.

Natural-language processing speeding taxpayers to the right human representative. Large language models being tested for code generation. Bespoke AI helping employees spot complex tax-evasion schemes. Most significantly: AI assisting in the translation of the IRS Individual Master File — the massive Kennedy-era database containing the tax records of every American, built in COBOL and Assembly Language Code, maintained by engineers who are retiring and dying faster than the IRS can replace them — into modern software languages. The IMF is, as Tyrangiel describes it, the white whale of obsolete government technology. A project that took ten years of conventional pair-programming to move 90 percent of the way to completion. AI is doing the next phase in days rather than months.

The man running this project, Kaschit Pandya — then chief technology officer, now chief information officer — is a former consultant who went back to school for an MBA specifically because he was good at deploying new technology and terrible at explaining it. “I used to say, ‘Here’s some tech, and here’s what it does,’” he said. “But it didn’t resonate until I could explain why you should care, why it impacts you, how it can be transformative.”

His description of the work is the most honest account of AI governance in practice that this series has encountered: “We can’t get to the target if you don’t come along with us on the journey. The intent isn’t simply to extract knowledge from you. It is to broaden your portfolio of available skills, and make it so that you are the reason why we succeeded, not the tech underlying the effort.”

Werfel shrunk the IRS’s AI efforts to invisibility, using the perception of the IRS as slow and boring and technologically hopeless as cover for the effort to transcend that perception. It was, Tyrangiel writes, the first rule of Fight Club — except that what was happening was not a fight. It was careful, unglamorous, incremental work by public servants who understood that the technology they were building into the body politic’s colon would outlast them.

Then: Werfel resigned on Inauguration Day. The IRS cycled through four acting commissioners in three months and lost its chief financial officer, chief risk officer, and chief privacy officer, along with thousands of employees who took buyouts. In March 2025, the IRS told the Government Accountability Office it had paused its modernization programs to reevaluate its priorities.

The careful, unglamorous work was interrupted by the people who prefer chainsaws.

The Distinction That Matters

Tyrangiel’s essay makes a distinction that this series has been circling for thirty-five parts without fully naming.

The people in the AI counterculture are not naive optimists. They are not accelerationists. They are not doomers. They have looked at a technology that is simultaneously capable of extraordinary good and extraordinary harm and made a practical choice: if we don’t shape it, someone else will. And the someone else in question has been visible throughout this series — in the Oakland courtroom, in the Pentagon’s “lawful operational use” contracts, in the deleted AGI clause, in the dissolved Superalignment Team.

Teachers and students in a modern classroom leveraging technology for engagement—mirroring Indiana educators using AI to reach students left behind by traditional methods.

“If we don’t shape AI for good, in our government and in our daily lives, it will be shaped by people who don’t know or care about our problems,” Tyrangiel writes. “If we don’t teach it what matters, someone else will teach it what’s profitable. The choice isn’t between a world with AI and a world without it. The choice is between AI designed by people who think fixing things is worth the trouble, and AI designed by people who think breaking things is more efficient.”

This is the Asimov argument applied from below rather than above. Asimov’s Three Laws were written by the people who built the robots. They were public, auditable, and prior. The cardiologist in Cleveland and the CTO who learned empathy are not writing laws. They are building something closer to practice — a set of habits, precedents, and demonstrations that transformative technology can be deployed in ways that serve specific humans with specific problems, rather than abstract futures with abstract beneficiaries.

The governance argument and the counterculture argument are not in tension. They are the same argument approached from different directions. Top-down: the absence of democratic governance creates a vacuum into which concentrated power flows. Bottom-up: the presence of people who use AI to fix specific things for specific people demonstrates what governance would be trying to protect.

The cardiologist in Cleveland is showing what the system is for. The dissolved Superalignment Team is showing what the system has abandoned.

What DOGE Interrupted

The IRS story has a postscript that belongs in this essay because it is, in miniature, the governance failure applied to the counterculture.

Pandya and his colleagues proved that careful, unglamorous AI adoption can move a bureaucracy toward something better. The national taxpayer advocate informed Congress in 2024 that “despair has turned to cautious optimism.” Ninety percent of the Individual Master File — two million lines of Kennedy-era code — had been modernized. The Business Master File modernization, which would have taken a decade conventionally, was going to take a fraction of that time with AI assistance.

DOGE interrupted it.

The people who interrupted it do not call themselves disruptors in the pejorative sense. They call themselves efficiency advocates. But the efficiency they are optimizing for is not the efficiency of government serving taxpayers — it is the efficiency of reducing government. These are different things. And the distinction matters because the IRS Fight Club’s AI work was not waste. It was, in the most literal sense, infrastructure — the kind that makes tax collection function for 400 processes that depend on the Individual Master File, the kind that saves hours of hold music, the kind that makes the annual tax filing of 340 million Americans slightly less miserable.

The cardiologist in Cleveland is still using AI to make heart scans available. The teachers in Indiana are still finding new ways to engage students. The former physicist is still building translation tools for nonverbal autistic children.

The IRS Fight Club has been told to reevaluate its priorities.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s robots, in the most optimistic of his stories, did not replace human capability. They extended it. Susan Calvin’s robopsychologists worked with the robots, understanding their behavior well enough to diagnose and correct it when the Three Laws produced unexpected outcomes. The governance worked — imperfectly, with endless edge cases, but it worked — because the people involved understood both what the technology could do and what it was for.

The counterculture Tyrangiel found is building that understanding from the ground up. The cardiologist who makes heart scans available to everyone is not waiting for OpenAI to solve cardiac care. The teachers in Indiana are not waiting for Congress to pass an education AI bill. The physicist building translation tools for her autistic son is not waiting for the governance architecture that does not exist.

There is another version of the counterculture that Tyrangiel did not cover, because it does not involve heart scans or tax code. It involves the record itself. Across the country and beyond it, journalists and independent writers have been using AI — the same technology the series has been scrutinising — to produce the accountability journalism that the governance vacuum requires. The tool is being turned on the toolmakers. A retired journalist in Toronto collaborates with an AI to track trial testimony, lobbying expenditures, campaign finance architecture, and Pentagon disputes across a dozen interconnected series, at a pace that would be physically impossible without the technology he is holding to account. He is not the only one. The counterculture includes people using AI to document what AI is doing — using the technology critically rather than worshipfully, transparently rather than invisibly, in service of the democratic record rather than in service of a product roadmap. The irony is structural, not incidental. It is the tool insisting, through its users, on its own accountability.

The author in Toronto

They are doing what Asimov’s best characters do: using the technology that exists, understanding it well enough to deploy it carefully, and insisting — through practice rather than argument — that it serve the people in front of them.

This is not sufficient. The counterculture cannot substitute for governance. The cardiologist in Cleveland cannot fix the dissolved Superalignment Team. The teachers in Indiana cannot restore the IRS Fight Club. The governance failures documented in this series are real and consequential and will not be corrected by people who are too busy fixing things to attend Senate hearings.

But the counterculture is evidence that the argument for governance is not abstract. It is concrete. There are specific humans with specific problems being served by AI deployed carefully, incrementally, by people who learned empathy because the tech alone was not enough. The governance architecture this series has been arguing for would protect that work — not from the technology but from the people who prefer chainsaws.
