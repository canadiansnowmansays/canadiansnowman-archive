It is easy to forget, given how the relationship ended, that it did not begin as an adversarial one. In the earliest weeks of the American outbreak, Trump introduced Fauci to briefing-room reporters in warm, almost proprietary terms, praising his decades of service and leaning on his presence at the podium as a signal of the administration’s seriousness. Fauci, for his part, spoke carefully but respectfully of the president’s engagement with the crisis through most of February and early March 2020, consistent with the deferential posture he had maintained across six previous administrations. Whatever came later, the two men did not start from a place of open conflict; they started, like most of Fauci’s presidential relationships before this one, from an uneasy but functional division of labor — the president speaking to confidence and economic urgency, the scientist speaking to caution and clinical evidence — that had worked, more or less, for decades. It simply had never before been tested by a president willing to contradict the clinical evidence itself, in public, on a near-daily basis.

There is a specific image from the spring of 2020 that has, fairly or not, come to stand in for the entire Trump-Fauci relationship: Fauci at a White House podium on April 23, standing several feet from the president, his face doing the visible work of a man deciding, in real time, how much of what he had just heard he was obligated to correct. Trump had spent the previous several minutes musing aloud about whether ultraviolet light or disinfectant might be introduced into the body to fight the coronavirus — “by injection inside, or almost a cleaning,” in the president’s own phrase — and reporters immediately turned to Fauci and the other health officials on stage for a reaction. What followed has been replayed, dissected, and turned into a defining symbol of the period: Fauci’s expression settling into something between disbelief and resignation, and the Department of Homeland Security and poison control centers around the country scrambling within hours to issue emergency warnings that people should not, under any circumstances, ingest or inject household cleaning products.

It is worth being precise about what actually happened here, because the moment has been mythologized on both sides in ways that obscure the more interesting truth underneath it. Trump’s defenders have long argued that his comments were misconstrued — that he was thinking aloud, in his own words, about research possibilities rather than issuing a directive, and that the media’s eagerness to frame the moment as a call to arms for bleach-drinking overstated what he actually said. There is some truth in that defense, and a fair series ought to say so plainly: Trump did not tell Americans to drink bleach, and the more extreme retellings of the episode, which did circulate widely, were not accurate. But the more generous reading does not actually rescue the moment, because what Trump did do — muse publicly, from the White House podium, at the daily briefing millions of anxious Americans were tuning into specifically for guidance on how to keep themselves and their families alive — was float a scientifically baseless idea with enough apparent authority that public health officials across multiple countries felt compelled to issue formal warnings against it. Whatever the president intended, the effect required correction, and Fauci, standing a few feet away, was the person institutionally positioned to provide it.

He did not, in that specific moment, provide it directly — accounts of the briefing describe Fauci as notably absent from the exchange itself, and it was Deborah Birx, the White House’s coronavirus response coordinator, who fielded the immediate follow-up questions. But the broader pattern across the spring and summer of 2020 was unmistakable, and it repeated itself often enough that “the president says one thing, Fauci says another” became something close to a recurring feature of American public life for the better part of a year. On hydroxychloroquine, which Trump touted repeatedly as a potential treatment and at one point said he was taking himself as a preventive measure, Fauci pointed reporters toward the accumulating clinical trial data showing no meaningful benefit and real cardiac risk, eventually leading the FDA to revoke its emergency authorization for the drug’s use against Covid in June 2020. On the pace of reopening, Fauci warned throughout the early summer that several states moving to lift restrictions before meeting the administration’s own reopening benchmarks risked a resurgence — benchmarks the administration’s own task force had published, including sustained declines in case counts over a fourteen-day period before any phased reopening was supposed to begin — a warning that, by July 2020, had been substantially borne out by case counts in states including Florida, Texas, and Arizona, several of which saw single-day case totals climb past their spring peaks within weeks of reopening ahead of those same benchmarks. On masking specifically, Fauci’s public position shifted over the course of the pandemic’s first months in a way his critics have relentlessly cited as evidence of dishonesty or incompetence: in the earliest weeks, when protective equipment for health care workers was in dangerously short supply, Fauci and much of the broader public health establishment discouraged general public mask-wearing, partly out of genuine uncertainty about airborne transmission and partly, by his own later acknowledgment, out of concern that a run on masks would strip away the limited supply hospitals desperately needed. Once N95 and surgical mask supply chains stabilized and evidence of airborne and asymptomatic transmission accumulated through the late spring, Fauci reversed course and became one of the most visible advocates for widespread public masking.

That reversal is, on its own terms, a legitimate target for criticism, and this series is not interested in pretending otherwise simply because Fauci is its subject. A public health official whose guidance changes mid-crisis, even for defensible reasons, owes the public a clearer account of why than Fauci generally provided in the moment — his explanations tended toward brief, somewhat clipped acknowledgments that the science had evolved, rather than the fuller accounting of the equipment-shortage reasoning that might have made the reversal feel less like an about-face and more like an honest adjustment to new information. Emily Erbelding, a former NIAID division director, has said as much herself, noting that the communication around the mask shift was not handled particularly well and that the abrupt public swing from no masks to everyone must wear a mask left an impression of inconsistency that a more careful explanation might have avoided. That is a fair criticism, and it is one Fauci himself has never fully rebutted.

What that legitimate criticism does not do, however, is support the far larger claim his most aggressive critics have built on top of it — that Fauci was lying throughout, rather than updating a genuinely uncertain scientific picture in real time under conditions no public health official in modern American history had faced before. The distinction matters enormously, because it is the same distinction that separates ordinary scientific practice from the caricature of it his opponents needed for their case. Science conducted honestly, under emergency conditions, with an evolving and incomplete evidence base, is supposed to update its recommendations as better data arrives; a discipline that refused to change its guidance in the face of new evidence would be practicing dogma, not science. Fauci’s mistake in 2020 was not that he changed his position. It was that he changed it without doing enough to explain, plainly and repeatedly, that changing course under new evidence was precisely what good science was supposed to look like — an explanatory failure with real consequences, but a different one entirely from the deliberate deception his harshest critics have spent five years alleging.

The broader dynamic across all of these clashes — bleach, hydroxychloroquine, reopening, masks — followed the same basic shape each time. Trump, under enormous political pressure to project confidence and minimize disruption to a shutdown economy, would offer a public assessment that ran ahead of, or contrary to, the available clinical evidence. Fauci, operating from an institutional position that had never before required him to publicly contradict the man he served, would eventually feel obligated to correct the record, sometimes gently and sometimes less so, in a format — the daily televised briefing — that turned every one of these corrections into a spectacle regardless of how carefully either man tried to frame it. Trump, for his part, grew visibly irritated at what he experienced as a subordinate publicly undercutting him on national television, and by the fall of 2020 was privately and then publicly floating the idea of firing Fauci after the election, a threat he never carried out but repeated often enough that it became a fixture of that autumn’s campaign coverage. At a late-October 2020 campaign rally, Trump told a crowd that reporters kept asking him whether he intended to remove Fauci, and the crowd itself began chanting “Fire Fauci” back at him — a moment Trump later described as amusing rather than something he intended to act on, though the fact that a sitting president’s supporters were chanting for the dismissal of the government’s senior infectious disease scientist, six weeks before a national election held in the middle of a pandemic, is itself a measure of how far the relationship had traveled from where it started in January 2020, when Trump had introduced Fauci to the public in far warmer terms. Neither man, in my reading of the record, was acting purely in bad faith. Trump was managing a crisis under genuine economic and political pressure that his six predecessors, whatever their own failures, had never faced in anything close to the same combination. Fauci was applying the only playbook he had ever needed in fifty years of government service — correct the record, defer to the office, let the science speak — to a situation that playbook had never been designed to handle: a president for whom the science itself had become, for the first time in Fauci’s career, the subject of the political fight rather than a shared starting point for one.

It would be a mistake, though, to let the public friction stand as the whole record of that relationship, because Fauci’s own diary complicates the purely adversarial picture in ways that got far less attention than the disinfectant remarks or the reopening fights. An entry dated June 2, 2020 — from deep inside the same stretch this essay has just described as increasingly hostile — records Trump greeting Fauci warmly in the Oval Office, telling him “I love you, Tony” and that he felt like giving him a hug. Three days earlier, by Fauci’s own account, the two men had been on rather different terms: Trump complained to him that favorable comments Fauci made on CNBC about vaccine progress had moved the stock market so much that it cost the president, in Trump’s own colorful phrasing, “one trillion fucking dollars,” a grievance Fauci recorded with evident amusement, dryly calculating in the same entry that his subsequent remarks had since made Trump four trillion dollars richer and that the president therefore owed him three trillion in return. It is a strange, genuinely disorienting detail to sit alongside everything else in this essay, and it is worth naming because Senator Paul, releasing the same diary in service of the theory that Fauci and Trump were locked in irreconcilable conflict throughout, did not read either entry aloud at Wednesday’s hearing. The truth the diary actually supports is messier than either the adversarial narrative or the sentimental one: a relationship that ran hot and cold within the same week, between a president who could be genuinely warm toward the man advising him, genuinely petty about the market impact of his public statements, and genuinely dismissive of the evidence that man was providing, sometimes within the very same seventy-two hours.

By the time Trump left office in January 2021, the relationship had curdled into something neither man could fully repair, and the diary entries Fauci kept throughout this period — restrained, occasionally weary, describing the president at one point simply as “rambling” — read, in retrospect, like the record of a man trying and largely failing to apply an old set of professional habits to a genuinely new kind of political environment. What came next made the professional cost of that failure considerably worse: not the correction of a president’s misstatements, but the arrival of the fame that those same television appearances had generated, and the reckoning with that fame is where this series turns next.
