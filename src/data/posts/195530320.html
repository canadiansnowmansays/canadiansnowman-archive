The Washington Hilton has a memory that its architecture cannot forget.

It was built in 1965, a massive, curving structure on Connecticut Avenue designed by William B. Tabler in a style that might generously be called mid-century brutalist optimism. The swooping concrete facade was meant to suggest openness and progress. For decades, it has served as one of Washington’s premier event venues — a place where presidents attend galas, where associations hold conferences, and where the annual White House Correspondents’ dinner has been staged for most of its modern history.

But the building’s defining moment came on March 30, 1981, and it happened not inside the ballroom but on the sidewalk outside the T Street entrance.

The attempted assassination of Reagan —On March 30, 1981, six shots were fired at President Ronald Reagan outside the Hilton Hotel in Washington. The shooter, John Hinckley, was taken to the ground immediately. This photo taken by presidential photographer Mike Evens captures Reagan waving to the crowd moments before the attempted assassination.

That was where John Hinckley Jr., 25 years old, fired a .22-calibre Röhm RG-14 revolver at President Ronald Reagan as he walked to his limousine after addressing an AFL-CIO conference. Hinckley fired six shots in 1.7 seconds. One ricocheted off the limousine and struck Reagan under his left arm, lodging an inch from his heart. Press secretary James Brady was shot in the head and suffered permanent brain damage. A Secret Service agent and a D.C. police officer were also wounded.

Reagan survived. He was rushed to George Washington University Hospital, where he reportedly told the surgical team, “I hope you’re all Republicans.” It was the performance of grace under pressure that defined the Reagan mythology — the cowboy who joked on the operating table, the cheerful Californian who could not be diminished even by a bullet.

Hinckley's motive was, by the standards of political assassination, genuinely bizarre. He was not driven by ideology. He was driven by an obsessive fixation on the actress Jodie Foster, whom he believed he could impress through an act of spectacular violence. He had been stalking Foster for months and had written her letters explaining his plan. He was found not guilty by reason of insanity and spent thirty-five years in institutional care before being fully released in 2022. He has since tried to establish himself as a musician and artist, posting about his work on social media.

John Hinckley Jr. and Jodie Foster Before he shot President Reagan in 1981, John Hinckley Jr. became obsessed with actress Jodie Foster after seeing her film “Taxi Driver” at least 15 times. Defense psychiatrists said Hinckley played Russian roulette and took this photo of himself.

And here is where the forty-five-year arc bends into something grotesque.

In November 2024, hours after Donald Trump won the presidential election, Hinckley’s social media feed was swarmed by users urging him to act again. “We’ve got a job for you,” one wrote. “John, we’re all very big fans of your early work,” read another. “You should definitely go back to that now more than ever.” One user tried to goad him by invoking the original delusion: “Do it for Jodie Foster.”

Hinckley — the man who shot a president, who spent thirty-five years institutionalized for it, who was found insane by a court of law — had to post a public plea asking people to stop. “I’m a man of peace now!” he wrote. “Please stop with all the negative comments!” When the Butler shooting had occurred a few months earlier, it was Hinckley who condemned the violence. “Give peace a chance,” he posted.

70-year-old John Hinckley Jr

Consider the inversion. In 1981, the entire country was unified in horror at what Hinckley had done. In 2024, strangers on the internet were asking him to do it again — not in the dark corners of the extremist web, but in the open comment section of a verified social media account. And the would-be assassin from 1981 had become the voice of restraint. The man who was literally insane when he pulled the trigger was now the one pleading for sanity.

The taboo against political violence did not just break. It became content. It became a joke. It became a recruitment pitch delivered as a meme in a comment thread, with the original shooter cast as a folk hero being summoned back from retirement.

The Secret Service acknowledged they were aware of the posts. They declined to comment on matters of protective intelligence. The posts remained up.

Five months later, another gunman charged another security checkpoint in the same building, this time carrying a shotgun, a handgun, and knives. The target, again, was the president. The venue, again, was the Washington Hilton. The outcome, again, was that the president survived and the gunman was taken alive.

The symmetry is almost too neat, and that neatness invites a comparison that may be more revealing than the obvious parallels suggest. Because what the Hinckley-Allen comparison illuminates is not how much has stayed the same. It is how much has changed — and specifically, how the meaning of political violence has shifted in the intervening decades.

John Hinckley Jr. wanted to impress a movie star. His act was delusional, disconnected from any political movement, and immediately understood by the entire country — left, right, and centre — as an act of madness. There was no partisan debate about whether Reagan deserved it. There was no faction that treated Hinckley as a folk hero. The national response was unified: this was an attack on the president, it was wrong, and the system needed to ensure it could not happen again.

That consensus — flawed, incomplete, but genuine — was possible because in 1981, the taboo against political violence was still intact. Reagan was a divisive president. Liberals despised his policies. The labour movement he had just addressed was not his natural constituency. But the idea that shooting at him was anything other than a horror was not contested by anyone in mainstream American life.

We do not yet know what drove Cole Tomas Allen to the Washington Hilton with a shotgun and a bag of knives. As of this writing, investigators have offered no motive. He is a Caltech graduate. He is a game developer. He is thirty-one years old and from a quiet suburban neighbourhood in Torrance, California, where his neighbours knew him mostly as the guy on the blue motor scooter.

But whatever his motive turns out to be — political, personal, psychological, or some combination that resists clean categorization — it will land in a country that is structurally incapable of responding to it the way America responded to Hinckley.

There will be no unified national consensus. There will be competing narratives. If Allen turns out to be ideologically motivated from the left, the right will use him as confirmation that liberal rhetoric incites violence. If he turns out to be motivated from the right, the left will use him as confirmation that the culture of guns and grievance is devouring its own. If his motive is personal or psychological, both sides will briefly lose interest before finding a way to fold him into their pre-existing frameworks anyway.

The difference between 1981 and 2026 is not the presence of violence. It is the absence of a shared response to it. In 1981, a shooting at the Washington Hilton was a national trauma that produced a national consensus. In 2026, a shooting at the Washington Hilton is a content event that produces competing narratives, a press conference in evening wear, and a thirty-day countdown to the rescheduled dinner.

That is the distance America has travelled. Same building. Same kind of act. Entirely different country.

There is one more element of the Hinckley comparison worth noting, and it is architectural.

After the 1981 assassination attempt, the Secret Service fundamentally redesigned the security protocols for the Washington Hilton. The T Street entrance where Reagan was shot was redesigned. Presidential arrivals and departures were rerouted. The security perimeter was hardened.

And it held. For forty-five years, no one got close to a president at the Washington Hilton.

Until Saturday. When a man with a shotgun charged the magnetometer checkpoint and made it far enough to exchange gunfire with the Secret Service before being tackled.

The post-Hinckley security architecture held for forty-five years and failed on the first night a president attended the Correspondents’ dinner in the current era. Whether that failure was a matter of the checkpoint being in the wrong place, the hotel’s public areas being insufficiently secured, or something else entirely, the symbolism is difficult to ignore.

The building remembered 1981. The country forgot what 1981 was supposed to teach.
