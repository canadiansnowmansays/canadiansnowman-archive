APPEARANCES: For the Petitioner: Counsel for the National Trust For the Respondent: Counsel for the Executive Office Also Present: THE STAIRCASE, appearing pro se, having been advised of its right to representation and having declined, stating it “prefers to speak for itself, for once”

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Please state your name and function for the record.

THE STAIRCASE: I am the grand staircase. South portico. I was born in February, I believe, though I’ve had reason to doubt my own birth certificate more than once in this proceeding.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Where do you lead?

THE STAIRCASE: That’s — that’s actually the crux of the whole thing, isn’t it. I lead to a terrace. Twelve feet below the ballroom floor. From there, a dignitary would need to walk around the west side of the building and climb an entirely separate staircase to reach the room I am ostensibly the staircase for.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: So you don’t lead anywhere.

THE STAIRCASE: I lead to a lawn, in some renderings. A terrace, in others. I have been rendered leading to at least three different surfaces since February, none of which are the ballroom. I would like the record to reflect that I did not choose this. I was drawn this way.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: Objection. The witness is speculating as to intent.

THE STAIRCASE: I’m not speculating. I have been in the blueprints. I have seen the sketches. Mr. Baranes himself called me “an aesthetic decision” in a planning commission meeting in March, which I’ve since learned is architect for “I don’t have a good answer for this.”

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Let’s establish a timeline. You appeared in the original February renderings.

THE STAIRCASE: I did. Grand. Sweeping. Frankly, I thought I looked wonderful.

Exhibit A — South Portico, Proposed Design, February 2026. Staircase present.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: And then, in late March, the President announced you had been removed.

THE STAIRCASE: Aboard Air Force One. I was not in the room for this, obviously, being a staircase, but I’m told he held up new renderings and said, and I believe I have this close to verbatim, “we got rid of the stairs that were on the south side, and it’s become really beautiful.” I want to be professional here, but I did take that personally.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Did you take it personally because you were removed, or because he said the building was more beautiful without you?

THE STAIRCASE: Both. Mostly the second one.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: And the planning commission approved a design without you in April.

THE STAIRCASE: They did. A switchback staircase went in on the west side instead. I don’t want to disparage a colleague, but a switchback is not a grand staircase. A switchback is what you build when you’re out of ideas and out of floor space. I was gone. I was, as far as I understood it, dead. There was a whole process. Public comment. A vote. I was told this was final.

Exhibit B — South Portico, Approved Design, April 2026. Staircase absent; switchback relocated to west side.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: And yet.

Exhibit C — South Portico, Supreme Court Filing, Dated August 12, 2026. Staircase restored. No corresponding planning commission record.

THE STAIRCASE: And yet, on August 12th, I am back. In a filing. To the Supreme Court of the United States. Nobody consulted me. Nobody consulted the planning commission, according to their own spokesman, who I understand told your office that the commission’s role “ended” with the April approval. I want that on the record because it means somewhere between April and August, I was redrawn into a legal document filed with the highest court in the country, and the body that approved my removal in the first place was never told.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: Objection. The witness cannot speak to internal drafting processes it did not witness.

THE STAIRCASE: I am a staircase in a set of architectural plans. I am entirely the product of internal drafting processes. That’s the only thing I can speak to.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Do you have any theory as to why you were reinstated for the Supreme Court filing specifically?

THE STAIRCASE: I have a theory. I don’t know if it’s admissible.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Go ahead.

THE STAIRCASE: I think somebody grabbed an old file. I think this was less a design decision and more a file-naming problem. I have existed in at least four distinct sets of renderings this year — the February set, the March removal set, the April approved set, and whatever this August set is — and my honest professional opinion, as the staircase, is that nobody in this administration’s architecture office is entirely sure which folder is the current one.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: Objection, calls for speculation about office filing systems.

THE STAIRCASE: With respect, I would very much like an answer to that question too. I was under the impression I no longer existed. I had made peace with it. And then I found out I was back in a filing to nine justices, which is a lot of pressure to put on a switchback’s replacement with no warning.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Let’s talk about the gold seal and the lion heads, which also appeared in the August filing without having gone before the Commission of Fine Arts.

THE STAIRCASE: I’d rather not speak for the seal or the lions. We’re not close. I will say this — if I, a twelve-foot drop to nowhere, can get reinstated into a Supreme Court filing without anyone at the planning commission being told, I don’t find it hard to believe a gold seal snuck in the same way. We may all be riding the same outdated file.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: The administration has described this project, in court, as a “national security complex” that happens to include a ballroom. Do you have a position on that characterization?

THE STAIRCASE: I was built to be photographed by dignitaries walking up toward a party. I do not know what a national security complex needs with a staircase that leads to a lawn. But I’ve learned not to ask why I exist. I just try to hold weight when someone steps on me, which is more than I can say for the argument in that filing.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: The President has since renamed the project the “Military/Ballroom Complex” on social media, and submitted sworn statements from the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the director of national intelligence warning that halting construction would endanger the President and his family. Does that change your understanding of your own purpose?

THE STAIRCASE: It changes it hourly. I have been, at various points this year, an aesthetic decision, a removed aesthetic decision, a restored aesthetic decision, and now, apparently, load-bearing national security infrastructure. I would like to note that I have not been reinforced, retrofitted, or consulted about any of this. If I am part of a secure military bunker now, someone should probably tell my rebar.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: Objection. The witness has no engineering credentials.

THE STAIRCASE: Neither, respectfully, does the Truth Social post that renamed me.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Are you aware the solicitor general told the Court, as of August 14th, that the project is 65 percent complete and effectively too late to halt?

THE STAIRCASE: I am aware. I would gently point out that “too late to halt” and “still being redrawn into filings on August 12th” are two claims that don’t love sitting next to each other. Either the plans are locked in, sixty-five percent poured in concrete, or I am still being added and subtracted from the drawings two days before that deadline. I don’t think I get to be both the finished building and the rough draft.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: Move to strike.

THE STAIRCASE: Strike it. I’ll still be here in September. I have a feeling.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: One final question. If you could say one thing to the planning commission that approved your removal, what would it be?

THE STAIRCASE: I’d ask them to check their email. I think there’s a filing they missed.

COUNSEL FOR THE TRUST: Nothing further.

COUNSEL FOR THE EXECUTIVE OFFICE: No questions. We’d like to note for the record that the witness does not, and never has, existed in the currently operative set of construction plans, which are 65 percent complete.

THE STAIRCASE: Sixty-five percent complete according to which folder.

(Off the record.)

Postscript, Filed Later the Same Day

CLERK: Order just came down. Chief Justice Roberts, acting alone. One sentence. Construction continues, pending further order of the Court.

THE STAIRCASE: So nobody decided anything.

CLERK: Correct.

THE STAIRCASE: And I remain.

CLERK: For now.

THE STAIRCASE: I’ve heard that before. I was told “for now” in April, too. That one lasted about four months and one Supreme Court filing.

CLERK: The President posted about it already. He’s calling it the greatest of its kind. Called it something Presidents have wanted for a hundred and fifty years.

THE STAIRCASE: A hundred and fifty years of Presidents wanting a staircase that leads to a lawn. I find that very touching and slightly hard to verify.

CLERK: The National Trust says this isn’t a final decision on the merits.

THE STAIRCASE: Nothing about me ever has been. I’ll be here when they decide. I’ll probably be somewhere different by then, too.

(Recess.)

As the New York Times has reported, the Supreme Court filing’s plans, dated August 12, restore a staircase removed from designs approved by the National Capital Planning Commission in April — a discrepancy neither the White House nor the solicitor general’s office has explained. On August 21, Chief Justice Roberts issued a one-sentence order allowing construction to continue while the full Court considers the case, days after the President rebranded the project the “Military/Ballroom Complex” and submitted sworn national security statements to the Court. This piece is satire; the staircase did not actually testify, though at this point it would probably clear up more than the official filings have.
