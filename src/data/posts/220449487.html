A few days before October 12, a senior Ukrainian military officer said something to Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster of The Atlantic that I have not been able to put down. “Things are already incredibly difficult for us here,” he told them. “But apparently, there’s always another level below rock bottom. Things can always get worse.”

The days that followed proved him right.

The three foundation essays of this series ended on Sunday, October 11, 2026, with a ceasefire that existed only on Truth Social, a sanctions licence running to April 2027, and a Ukrainian government holding its drones back from Russian refineries as a gesture of good faith. This essay covers what Ukraine received in return. The short answer is: nothing it asked for, and several things it did not.

The sirens that came too late

At about 10 p.m. on Monday, October 12, 2026, Russian missiles struck Kyiv. The air-raid alerts sounded after the missiles had already been launched. For the people of the capital, that is the difference between a stairwell and a bedroom.

Ukraine does not have the satellite network of a superpower. It relies on its partners, above all the United States, to pass on the flash of a launch in time for the sirens to do their work. Col. Yurii Ihnat, the Ukrainian Air Force spokesman, described the system in an interview this summer, quoted this week by Constant Méheut and Carlotta Gall in the New York Times: “Our NATO partners inform us when there is activity involving strategic aviation. Flights from one airfield to another. Where missiles are being delivered.”

On Tuesday morning, October 13, President Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine had received no early warning from its partners. Coming four days after American negotiators at Shell Bay had reminded Ukraine how much it owed Washington for intelligence, the remark landed like a dropped plate.

What followed was a day of careful contradictions. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said intelligence sharing was continuing and the attack had gone undetected “because of a special type of weaponry which was used by the Russian side.” President Trump said: “We are helping them, and we are sharing intel.”

Then, on Tuesday night at about 10:40 p.m., it happened again. “Russian missile strike on Kyiv,” Zelensky wrote. “Once again, no warning from our partners.” The target was a heat-and-power plant supplying the capital. “Ukraine supported an energy cease-fire,” he added. “Russia is targeting yet another power plant and the lives of our people.”

Fairness requires the next detail. The Air Force later clarified that warnings did arrive for the three waves that followed the first strike on Tuesday night. On October 16, Kim Barker of the New York Times offered one possible reason for the misses: the missiles may have been S-400s, which cover short distances so fast that there is often little time for any warning. That would fit Sybiha’s “special type of weaponry.” So the honest summary is this: something failed, twice, at exactly the moment Washington had hinted it might, and the explanations do not quite agree. Secretary of State Marco Rubio later offered a third: “a software problem involving their early warning system.” On Sunday night, October 18, it happened again, as Constant Méheut reported for the New York Times.

Late on Wednesday, American officials told Julian E. Barnes of the New York Times that intelligence sharing had not been cut back, “at least so far.” Representative Jim Himes, the top Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, said any pullback on top of Trump’s “imagined” ceasefire “would be a cowardly betrayal.” In a country where a minute of warning is the margin between life and death, “at least so far” is its own kind of pressure. “We will die because we won’t receive warnings about ballistic missile attacks,” the Ukrainian military analyst Oleh Katkov told Barker. “That means we won’t be able to get to shelters in time.”

Kharkiv, and the cat

The cost of a late siren is felt most in Kharkiv, about 20 miles from the Russian border. On Monday night, drones tore into a residential building there and collapsed apartments to the ground. The mayor, Ihor Terekhov, said four people were killed.

Lyubov Smal, 69, had been cooking cutlets in her kitchen when the drone hit. Bleeding, with stitches now across her face, she made her way through the smoke and was led out by firefighters. Twelve hours later she came back, not for her papers or her savings, but to ask for help finding her cat. Two rescuers who had worked through the night climbed the burnt stairs to the third floor. “He won’t bite us?” they asked. Minutes later they came down with a big grey cat with yellow eyes, who dug his claws into a neighbour’s fleece every time a bulldozer started up.

I mention it because the arithmetic of this war can make it seem abstract, and it is not. It is a 69-year-old woman and her cat, and two tired men on a staircase.

Thirty-nine missiles

Overnight into Wednesday, October 14, 2026, Russia fired 39 ballistic missiles at Ukraine, by a Times count the largest such salvo of the entire war. The attack hit all three of Kyiv’s combined heat-and-power plants, according to Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi, and damaged 26 power-grid facilities across the country. At least 600,000 people in the capital lost power. Ukraine shot down only four of the 39.

The arithmetic behind that is grim, as Méheut laid out for the New York Times on October 15. Before the war, Kyiv’s three plants could generate about 1.3 gigawatts, roughly three-quarters of the city’s winter needs; by this autumn, years of strikes had cut that to about 500 megawatts, and Wednesday cut it again. Scheduled blackouts have begun, weeks earlier than last year. And Kyiv has shown European officials an intercepted Russian plan, obtained by the Times, estimating that some 70 ballistic missiles, 720 long-range drones and 150 guided bombs would be enough to cut Kyiv, Kharkiv and Odesa off from water, power and heat. That is roughly what gets through Ukraine’s air defences in a single month. Moscow believes one month of strikes could do what four winters have not.

Recall the “energy ceasefire” announced on Sunday. In Washington’s reading it protects Russian refineries from Ukrainian drones and nothing in Ukraine from Russian missiles. On Wednesday morning, Kyiv woke up to see what that looks like. Ukraine, meanwhile, kept its drones away from Russian refineries.

The switch nobody will admit to

Then came the blow that was not Russian.

Overnight into Wednesday, Ukraine’s military discovered that Starshield had been restricted. Starshield is the military version of Starlink, harder to hack or jam, paid for by the Pentagon and lent to Ukraine. According to Ukraine’s defence ministry, any terminal moving faster than 100 miles an hour suddenly lost its connection. There was no warning.

That speed limit is not arbitrary. As Méheut reported for the New York Times on October 14, Starshield terminals sit on almost everything the Ukrainian army moves: troop vehicles, the drones that ferry food and water to the front, the long-range drones that hit Russian supply lines, and the interceptor drones Ukraine uses against Russia’s new jet-powered Shaheds. If the restriction holds, one officer warned, Russia could reopen key supply routes within days.

“In effect,” said Serhiy Beskrestnov, who advises Zelensky on military technology, “our partners’ actions are depriving us of one of the few ways we have to defend the country against jet-powered Shaheds.”

Starlink does not operate inside Russia. So this restriction has nothing to do with refineries. It limits Ukraine on Ukrainian soil, just as its forces had begun winning ground back.

Who did it? Ukraine’s defence ministry blamed SpaceX. But Elon Musk himself said in March, as Ryan Mac reported for the New York Times, that Starshield “is operated by the US government. This is not under SpaceX control.” Either Musk was wrong then, or someone in Washington throttled an ally’s drones in the middle of a pressure campaign. Neither SpaceX nor the Pentagon would say. By Friday, a Reuters report, cited by Brynn Tannehill in The Atlantic, said the order had come from inside the White House. The President denies ordering it. Some service returned, but as late as Saturday evening Kyiv could not confirm that Starshield was working as before, and said four days of questions to the Pentagon and SpaceX had gone unanswered, as Méheut reported for the New York Times. The lesson stays: the switch exists, and someone in Washington was prepared to use it. Barnes added that any Starshield operation crossing into Russia already needed explicit American permission, and on Saturday, October 10, three days before the restriction, Musk posted that “Zelensky has gone too far.” Readers of The Musk Stack and Who Owns the Air on this Substack will recognise the problem: when one private network becomes the nervous system of a nation’s defence, whoever controls the switch controls a great deal more than a business.

Serhii Sternenko, a former adviser to Ukraine’s defence ministry, summed up the mood. Kyiv had done what Trump asked. In return, it had “seemingly only gotten more restrictions.”

What Moscow gave back

Washington gave Moscow sanctions relief on diesel, a public call for Ukraine to replace its president, and pressure on Kyiv to stop the one campaign that was costing Russia real money. Paul Sonne, Moscow bureau chief of the New York Times, summed up the reply: “Mr. Putin, in return, offered more war.”

Minutes after the diesel announcement, the Kremlin said Putin would not negotiate with Ukraine at all for now. Moscow also rebuffed Kyiv’s ceasefire offer. “I don’t think he deserves a respite,” said Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov, as David French noted in the New York Times on October 15. The next day, a Russian attack on Zaporizhzhia killed 22 people, including four children. Hanna Notte, author of a new book on Russian foreign policy called We Will Outlast Them, put Putin’s intention plainly: “to inflict a really, really nasty winter on the Ukrainians.”

The title of her book is the Kremlin’s strategy. Putin is betting that Ukraine’s friends will tire before Ukraine does. “You have to change that perception of the future, diminish that optimism, to affect their calculus,” Sam Charap of the RAND Corporation told Sonne. This week, Washington did the opposite.

“Thank you, President Putin”

The diesel itself, meanwhile, remains a mirage.

The numbers came from Aruni Soni of the New York Times, using S&P Global data. From July to September 2025, Russia exported about 693,000 barrels of diesel a day. In the same months this year, after Ukraine’s drones, it managed about 140,000. The October promise works out to roughly 75,000 barrels a day. Against that, the Middle East is shipping about 809,000 fewer barrels a day than a year ago because of the war with Iran. Analysts doubt Russia can even deliver what it promised, with 40 to 50 per cent of its refining capacity offline. At the pump, nothing has moved. On Thursday, October 15, the average American diesel price was about $6.32 a gallon, according to AAA, up from $6.29 a week earlier, as Anton Troianovski and Luke Broadwater noted for the New York Times.

None of this stopped the President from expressing his gratitude. “I want to thank President Putin, to be honest with you,” he told reporters on Friday, October 9, as Jonathan Chait noted in The Atlantic. “We have massive amounts of oil coming into our country, and it’s diesel, which is what we want. So, thank you.”

As a Canadian, I would like to place one other quotation beside that one. Speaking of my country last year, the same man said: “We don’t need their fuel, we don’t need their energy, we don’t need their oil and gas. We don’t need anything that they have.” Canada already sends New England about 154,000 barrels of diesel a day, more than twice the October Russian promise, and asks for no sanctions relief, no halt to anyone’s self-defence, and no thanks. From Canada, the President buys and complains. From Russia, he buys and gives thanks.

Who stood up

Not everyone sat still.

On Tuesday, October 13, a bipartisan group of 12 senators, six Democrats and six Republicans, wrote to the President, as Robert Jimison reported for the New York Times. “This deal is a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our allies,” they wrote. “Putin is a tyrant.” The Republicans included Mitch McConnell, John Cornyn, Thom Tillis, Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Alan Armstrong. Notably absent was Darline Graham, who carried the sanctions law named for her late brother, Lindsey Graham.

The President could waive that law’s provisions on national-security grounds. On Friday, October 16, he did, exempting Russia from the sanctions Congress had passed, as Anton Troianovski and Jim Tankersley reported for the New York Times. He had signed the law himself. The next day, Darline Graham broke with him, gently. “Personally, I would choose to put the pressure on Russia to end the war and get gas prices down,” she told reporters, as Michael Gold reported for the New York Times. “Because I don’t trust Putin, and I don’t trust Russia.” John Cornyn was blunter: “Lindsey would be appalled.” On Sunday, the President explained himself. “My decision on Russia is a very simple one,” he wrote. “I am in the process of negotiating Peace in the war between Russia and Ukraine, and don’t want to do anything that will drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table.” David E. Sanger of the New York Times, who has covered five presidents, described the steps of these ten days more plainly: they were taken “to side with the Russians.”

On Thursday, October 15, nine of the senators, four Republicans among them, went further, as Jimison reported in a follow-up for the New York Times. Led by Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire, they asked the Pentagon’s inspector general to find out whether the administration or its contractors were behind the Starshield restriction. Their letter carried a detail that brought the story home: American diplomatic, military and security personnel in Ukraine, they wrote, had been “unable to reach designated bunkers during Russian air raids.” The senators want a first briefing by November 1.

In London on Wednesday, as Michael D. Shear and Lara Jakes reported for the New York Times, Zelensky was received by King Charles III and then by Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who promised a new batch of air-defence missiles effective against Russian drones. Zelensky, choosing his words with a diplomat’s care, spoke of “a lot of different surprises” that you would not expect “when you have real partnership.” In Estonia, NATO’s secretary general, Mark Rutte, said: “Russia is failing. It is failing on the battlefield in Ukraine. And we will not be intimidated.”

Can Europe fill an intelligence gap if Washington opens one? Not entirely. Robert Murrett, a former director of the U.S. National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, told the Times the American intelligence community is “very anti-Russian.” Then he added: “That’s not to say the White House couldn’t overrule them.”

Disarming alone

By Wednesday night, the arithmetic was complete. Ukraine had kept its refinery drones on the ground for three days, honouring a bargain nobody else had signed. In those three days it lost its early warnings twice, had its drones throttled by an ally’s satellite network, and watched all three of Kyiv’s heat-and-power plants burn. Russia had fired the largest ballistic salvo of the war and declared that Ukraine’s president did not deserve a respite. Washington had thanked Putin.

There is a limit to how long any country can be asked to disarm alone. On Thursday morning, October 15, Ukraine reached it. What it did next, and what cards it still holds, is the subject of the next essay.
