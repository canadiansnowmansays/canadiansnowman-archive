In the three essays before this one, the erasing had a physical dimension you could photograph — a tour program shuttered, a placard pulled from a wall, a slavery panel carried out of a house by a crew with a screwdriver. There was, in each case, a hole you could stand in front of and point at. The blank spot on the wall was itself a kind of evidence.

This essay is about the version of the memory hole that leaves no blank spot. You go looking for something the government published — a dataset, a guidance page, a record that was there last week — and you do not find a gap where it used to be. You find a screen that tells you, with bureaucratic serenity, that the page cannot be found. No crew, no screwdriver, no photograph. Just four hundred and four, and a suggestion that you check your spelling.

Orwell had to invent the memory hole — the little chute at Winston Smith’s elbow, down which the inconvenient document vanished into the furnace. The genius and the horror of it was its totality: once the slip went down the chute, no surviving copy proved it had ever existed. This administration has built the working version, at scale, and the scale is the story.

The Numbers

Beginning in January 2025, in the days after the inauguration, federal web pages and datasets began going dark across the government. This was not a handful of embarrassing documents. By the accounting compiled since, more than eight thousand web pages and roughly three thousand datasets were removed or altered across multiple agencies — the CDC, the Census Bureau, the EPA, the Justice Department, NASA, the Department of Defense, and more. The Centers for Disease Control alone pulled hundreds of datasets from its public platform in a matter of weeks; one analysis counted over two hundred removed in the first three weeks, another tracked more than a hundred and fifty scrubbed and, in many cases, quietly reposted in altered form.

The instrument, as ever, had an innocuous name. Executive Order 14151, signed on the first day, directed the dismantling of “diversity, equity, and inclusion” programs across the federal government — and agencies interpreted it as a mandate to purge. What came down, overwhelmingly, was anything touching race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, gender, or the other sociodemographic variables that let a country see itself clearly. Pages on HIV testing and treatment. The Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System, which tracks adolescent health. Census data. EPA environmental-justice tools. The Justice Department’s information on Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women — deleted, in a country where that crisis is measured precisely because the data was, until then, collected and published.

A dataset is not a placard. It does not editorialize; it does not “frame.” It is the raw material from which a nation, and everyone who studies it, learns what is true about itself — how many, how sick, how many died, where, and who. To delete the variable for race from a health dataset is not to soften a narrative. It is to make a specific question — is this disease killing one group more than another? — permanently unanswerable from the public record. The erasure is not of an opinion. It is of the capacity to know.

The Word Swap

There is a detail in the data story that belongs in the same glass case as the Smithsonian’s “restored to 2008,” because it shows the same clerical hand at work.

Investigators tracking the CDC’s public files found that some datasets were not merely removed but taken down and reposted — with the word “gender” changed to “sex.” The data was allowed back, provided it spoke the approved vocabulary. This is Newspeak in its most literal form: not the suppression of a fact but the substitution of a permitted word for a forbidden one, so that the record survives in a shape the state finds acceptable. Winston Smith did not only destroy documents; he rewrote them, so that the past would say what the present required. A dataset reposted with its terms swapped is that same operation, automated and pushed live to a government website, where it now stands as the official record — correct-looking, searchable, and quietly edited.

The forbidden words were catalogued, too. Lists circulated — assembled by journalists from the pattern of removals — of the terms that got a page flagged: not only “gender” and “diversity” but “equity,” and a long tail of others, each a small trapdoor that could drop a page down the hole. A government does not need to read every document if it can simply search for the words it has decided may no longer be spoken, and remove whatever contains them.

The Disclaimer That Rejects Its Own Page

Now the artifact I promised — the single most Orwellian sentence this series has yet produced, and it was written not by a novelist but by a federal agency under court order.

Health advocacy groups sued. In February 2025, in a case brought by Doctors for America and Public Citizen, a federal judge ordered the Department of Health and Human Services, the CDC, and the FDA to temporarily restore deleted pages. The government complied — it put the pages back. But it put them back wearing a collar. Atop the restored material, HHS affixed a notice. It said, in part, that the page had been restored per a court order — and then went on to declare that any information on the page “promoting gender ideology is extremely inaccurate and disconnected from truth,” that the administration “rejects gender ideology,” and, in the sentence that ought to be carved into the cornerstone of this whole series: “This page does not reflect reality and therefore the Administration and this Department reject it.”

Sit with the structure of that. A court orders the government to restore a public health record. The government obeys — technically, visibly, in full compliance — and then staples to the top of the restored page an official statement that the page you are now reading is false and repudiated by the very department publishing it. The memory is returned to the shelf with a government label declaring it a lie. This is doublethink rendered as web copy: the page is up and the page is disavowed; the record exists and does not reflect reality; the department publishes it and rejects it, in the same breath, on the same screen.

Orwell’s memory hole destroyed the past. This is subtler and, in a way, more total. It leaves the past visible, and teaches you not to trust it — stations a doubt on top of the record exactly as the president’s disclaimer was stationed outside the Smithsonian’s door. The physical hole in the wall could at least be pointed at. This hole comes with a caption explaining that what you are looking at was never real.

Why the Digital Hole Is the Worst of Them

I have called several things, across this series, the center or the purest case. Let me make a different claim here: the digital erasure is the most dangerous of the forms, and it is worth being precise about why.

A pulled placard is noticed. A shuttered tour program makes the newspaper. A slavery exhibit carried out of a founder’s house draws a protest and, eventually, a judge. The physical erasures in this series all share a saving weakness: they happen in public, to objects, in front of witnesses, and so they can be seen, photographed, litigated, and sometimes reversed.

A dataset that vanishes from a government server at two in the morning has none of those defenses. There is no crowd at the overlook. There is no crew to photograph. Most people who rely on the data — a researcher, a doctor, a city planner, a journalist — discover the loss only when they go looking and meet the 404, by which time they often cannot even prove precisely what was there, or when it left, or whether it was deleted or merely moved. The absence of a physical gap is exactly what makes it effective. You cannot rally the public around a blank you cannot see.

That the losses have been partly documented at all — that we can say “eight thousand pages, three thousand datasets” — is due almost entirely to people outside the government who saw it coming and copied the record before it fell. Librarians. Archivists. Volunteer “data rescue” efforts that scraped and mirrored federal datasets in the weeks around the inauguration, precisely because they did not trust the material to survive. They are the reason a court could later order specific pages restored: someone had kept the original to compare against. In Orwell’s world, no such copy existed; the memory hole was airtight. In ours, the hole leaks, because private citizens ran to the shelves and photographed the books before the crew arrived. This series keeps finding that same figure at the edge of every erasure — the volunteer who refuses to forget, whether she is an eighty-seven-year-old tour guide or an anonymous archivist mirroring a CDC dataset at midnight.

What “Page Not Found” Actually Means

Let me end on the error message itself, because it is the tombstone of this kind of erasure, and it is worth reading as one.

404: Page Not Found. It is the most neutral sentence a computer can produce. It implies nothing was ever there. It suggests, gently, that the fault may be yours — a mistyped address, a broken link, a search term slightly wrong. It carries no author, no order, no signature, no date. It does not say this page was removed by executive order. It does not say this dataset was deleted to comply with a directive. It does not say the record you are seeking was taken down at 11:40 p.m. on a Friday and reposted with three words changed. It says only that the page cannot be found, as though the past had simply misplaced itself.

That is the perfected form of the memory hole — not a furnace, not a screwdriver, not even a disclaimer, but a blandly apologetic error screen that erases the fact and the erasing in a single stroke, and puts the burden on you to wonder whether you imagined the thing you came to find.

Charlene Aqpik Apok, whose organization tracks data on murdered Indigenous women, said it best when her subject was deleted from the Justice Department’s site. We still know what was said. That is the whole of the resistance, in five words. The government controls the server. It does not, yet, control the people who copied the page before it fell — and who remember, against the 404, that the record was real.

This is Part 4 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory. The first three essays dealt in physical erasure — a hollowed memorial, a censored museum, signs pulled from the trails. This one follows the erasing into the servers, where the memory hole becomes literal and leaves no gap to photograph, only a 404. The next essay turns from records to people: the officials being unpersoned — portraits taken down, names removed, careers written out of the account — in the oldest Orwellian tradition of all.
