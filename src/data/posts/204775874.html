I owe you a confession, and an essay built around it, because I think my own change of mind on this subject is more useful to you than any number of statistics I could simply assert.

For most of my adult life, I believed that China was the great villain of the climate story. This was not a fringe view; it was the consensus, and it was, for a long time, basically correct. Here was a country opening a new coal plant seemingly every week, choking its own cities in smog, lifting eight hundred million people out of poverty on a tide of cheap dirty energy and waving away Western lectures about emissions with the entirely fair observation that the West had burned its own way to wealth first and was now pulling the ladder up. When my fellow climate-minded Westerners despaired, the despair usually ended at the same place: but what’s the point, when China just builds more coal? China was the trump card of inaction, the all-purpose reason that nothing we did at home could matter. I played that card myself. I believed it.

I no longer believe it, and the honest thing to do is to say so plainly and show you why I changed, because a writer who never publicly revises anything is not thinking, merely performing. The facts on the ground have moved, and they have moved faster than almost anyone — certainly faster than I — expected. The country I filed under “villain” has quietly become the single most important reason to think this crisis might yet be survivable. And in one of the great ironic reversals of our age, it has swapped roles with the country I have spent a year defending against its own worst instincts. On climate, at least, the United States and China have traded costumes. The one I trusted is burning the past. The one I blamed is building the future.

Let me give you the numbers, because the scale is genuinely difficult to believe until you sit with it.

In 2024, China installed roughly 360 gigawatts of wind and solar capacity — more than half of all the renewable capacity added on the entire planet that year. Not more than any other country: more than all other countries combined, with room to spare. In a single month of 2025, May, China added 93 gigawatts of solar — which, an analyst worked out, is the equivalent of installing nearly one hundred solar panels every second, around the clock, for a month. It put up a wind turbine roughly every ten minutes. By 2025 China held more than eighty per cent of the world’s solar manufacturing capacity, which means that the global solar boom — the cheap panels going up in Texas and India and Nigeria and on my own neighbours’ roofs here in Toronto — is very largely a Chinese accomplishment, the product of a deliberate decades-long industrial strategy to dominate the technologies a carbon-constrained world would need. The Chinese company BYD has become the largest electric-vehicle manufacturer on Earth, and China is closing in on the point where more than half of all new cars sold within the country are electric — a threshold the rest of the world is nowhere near.

And here is the number that matters most, the one that may turn out to be the most important climate statistic of the decade. In 2025, China’s carbon emissions began to fall — not because its economy slowed, which is the usual and unhappy reason emissions drop, but because clean energy was finally growing faster than demand. For the first time on record, the world’s largest emitter saw its emissions decline while its electricity use rose, the growth in power met by wind and sun rather than coal. Several careful analysts now believe China’s emissions may have peaked in 2024, fully six years ahead of the 2030 target it had set itself and that the world had assumed was optimistic. If that holds — and it is not yet certain that it will — then the single largest source of greenhouse gas on the planet has turned the corner ahead of schedule, and the global emissions curve has a chance of bending that it simply did not have five years ago.

Now, I promised you honesty, and honesty cuts both ways, so let me be just as clear about what this does not mean, because the temptation to overcorrect — to swing from “China the villain” all the way to “China the savior” — is exactly the kind of lazy reversal I am trying to avoid. China is still the largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the world, by a wide margin, responsible for something close to a third of the global total. It still generates the majority of its electricity — over half — by burning coal. It is still, even now, building new coal plants: in the very plan for its showpiece “mega wind and solar bases,” a startling share of the capacity is actually coal, built to stabilize the grid against the intermittency of all that new wind and sun.

Its Nationally Determined Contribution under Paris pledges to “peak” emissions before 2030 but pointedly declines to say at what level the peak will sit — and a peak that is very high, reached and then descended only slowly, is not nearly enough to hold the planet to anything like a safe temperature. The buildout of renewables is staggering and real; the dependence on coal is also staggering and real; and both things are true at once, which is the part that neither the China-hawks nor the China-romantics can ever quite hold in their heads together.

So I am not asking you to admire China, exactly, and I am certainly not asking you to admire its government, which remains an authoritarian state with a human-rights record I have criticized elsewhere and will not pretend away here. I am asking you to notice a comparison, because the comparison is the whole point of putting this essay where I have put it — directly after the essay on American coal.

Set the two trajectories side by side. The United States, the richest country in history, possessed of the world’s finest universities and deepest capital markets and most innovative companies, has chosen this decade to withdraw from the Paris Agreement for the second time, to declare its cheapest and cleanest energy a “scam,” to pay companies billions to not build wind farms, and to force aging coal plants to keep burning in order to feed its data centers. China, a developing country still lifting its interior provinces out of poverty, has chosen the same decade to install more clean energy than the rest of the world combined, to corner the global market in every technology the transition requires, to put an EV in front of half its car buyers, and to bend its own emissions curve years ahead of schedule. One of these countries is building the energy system of the twenty-first century. The other is fighting, with the full power of its government, to preserve the energy system of the twentieth. And it is not the one I would have guessed, back when I was confidently playing the China card to excuse my own side.

Why did China do it? Not, I think, out of any tenderness for the planet — let us not be sentimental. China pursued clean energy for hard-headed reasons of national interest, and that is precisely why it should worry the United States rather than comfort it. Beijing’s planners understood three things that Washington’s current leadership refuses to. First, that energy security favors the renewable: you cannot blockade the sun, and a country that makes its own panels and turbines and batteries is not hostage to anyone’s oil tanker or pipeline. Second, that the manufacturing of clean technology was going to be one of the great industries of the century — that the nation which dominated solar and wind and batteries and EVs would hold the commanding heights of the global economy the way the nation that dominated oil and the automobile once did. And third, that getting there first meant the rest of the world would have to buy from you. China did not build all this to save the Earth. It built it to win the future, and it correctly identified clean energy as where the future was kept.

It is worth dwelling on just how complete that manufacturing victory has become, because it is the part with the sharpest geopolitical edge. China does not merely lead in deploying clean energy; it has cornered the global supply chain for nearly every component of it. More than eighty per cent of the world’s solar manufacturing capacity sits in China. It dominates the production of wind turbines, and more decisively still the batteries that the whole electrified future depends on, along with the refining of the lithium and the rare-earth elements that go into them. This is the mirror image of the twentieth-century oil order, in which a handful of petrostates held the world over a barrel — except that the new chokepoint is not a god-given geological accident but a deliberately engineered industrial achievement, built over two decades of patient five-year plans while the West dithered. The same cheap Chinese panels that are decarbonizing the roofs of the world are also a strategic dependency, and a thoughtful Western policymaker ought to find that genuinely uncomfortable, because it means the clean-energy transition, as currently structured, runs substantially through Beijing. The honest response to that discomfort, however, is not to retreat into coal, which leaves you dependent on fossil fuels and behind on the technology. It is to compete — to build your own clean-energy manufacturing, as the Americans briefly attempted before the current administration set about dismantling the effort. China’s dominance is not an argument against clean energy. It is an argument for getting into the race the United States is currently, deliberately, walking away from.

China is still the largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the world, by a wide margin, responsible for something close to a third of the global total. It still generates the majority of its electricity — over half — by burning coal. It is still, even now, building new coal plants: in the very plan for its showpiece “mega wind and solar bases,” a startling share of the capacity is actually coal, built to stabilize the grid against the intermittency of all that new wind and sun. Its Nationally Determined Contribution under Paris pledges to “peak” emissions before 2030 but pointedly declines to say at what level the peak will sit — and a peak that is very high, reached and then descended only slowly, is not nearly enough to hold the planet to anything like a safe temperature. The buildout of renewables is staggering and real; the dependence on coal is also staggering and real; and both things are true at once, which is the part that neither the China-hawks nor the China-romantics can ever quite hold in their heads together.

This is, in the end, the same lesson as the rest of the series, told from the far side of the world. China looked at the changing climate and changed its infrastructure to meet it — imperfectly, cynically, while still hedging its bets with coal, but it moved. The United States looked at the same changing climate and dug in to defend the structure it already had. The house built for winter is not only a story about old British terraces and Canadian permafrost. It is a story about which nations are willing to renovate and which are determined to insist, against all the evidence, that the old house will do just fine. The reformed villain is renovating. The reluctant hero is boarding up the windows and praising the furnace.

I was wrong about China. I would rather say so than be consistent.
