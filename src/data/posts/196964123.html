Canadian Snowman’s Guide to the Most Important Meeting Nobody Expects to Matter

On October 30, 2025, Donald Trump emerged from a sideline meeting with Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Busan, South Korea, and rated his own performance a twelve out of ten. It was the kind of self-assessment that, in any other context, would suggest a man who had lost contact with the grading scale — but in the peculiar arithmetic of Trump-Xi diplomacy, where the currency is spectacle and the exchange rate is vibes, a twelve out of ten might have been modestly understated. The two leaders had agreed to lower tariffs on Chinese imports from 57 to 47 percent. They had committed to meet again in Beijing. They had, in the language of diplomacy, “stabilized the relationship,” which is diplomat-speak for “we have agreed not to set each other’s house on fire for the next several months.”

By early February 2026, that agreement was beginning to take shape as an actual presidential visit — the first by a sitting American president to China since Trump himself had visited in November 2017. Xi had extended the invitation. Trump had accepted. Teams were talking. The word “April” was being murmured in both capitals. And for a brief, shimmering moment, it appeared that the world’s two largest economies might sit down together and discuss, in something resembling good faith, the vast tangle of disputes that had accumulated between them like unpaid bills in a shared apartment where neither tenant will admit who left the milk out.

Busan, October 30, 2025. A 12-out-of-10 performance, by the only judge who mattered.

This is the story of what happened next — which is to say, the story of how an invitation became an ordeal, how a summit became a performance, and how the most important bilateral relationship on earth arrived at a meeting that every serious analyst has already concluded will produce nothing of substance. It is a story worth telling precisely because of that paradox. When every expert agrees that the most consequential diplomatic event of the year is predetermined theatre, the theatre itself becomes the story.

I am, I should note, watching all of this from Toronto, which gives me roughly the same vantage point on great-power summitry as a man in the cheap seats at Maple Leaf Gardens had on a hockey brawl — close enough to see the blood, too far away to stop it, and constitutionally inclined to keep score. Canada has a long history of standing at the intersection of American ambition and the rest of the world’s bewilderment, going back at least to Hugh Keenleyside, the Canadian diplomat who spent the better part of the mid-twentieth century trying to explain to various empires that their imperial projects were more complicated than they imagined. Keenleyside understood something that neither Beijing nor Washington appears to have internalized: empires are easy to acquire and almost impossible to operate. The maintenance costs are what kill you.

The maintenance costs, in this case, are staggering.

To understand the invitation, you have to understand the trade truce it was built on, and to understand the trade truce, you have to understand the wreckage it was meant to contain.

The US-China trade war that began in Trump’s first term had, by his second, achieved the rare distinction of being simultaneously escalatory and exhausting. Through much of 2025, the two countries had engaged in what economists politely call “retaliatory tariff cycles” and what the rest of us would recognize as a bar fight conducted in slow motion with spreadsheets. Trump had imposed tariffs. China had retaliated. Trump had doubled them. China had boycotted American soybeans for most of the year — a move that was less about soybeans per se than about the political geography of American agriculture, where soybean farmers tend to live in states that vote Republican and where pain at the farm gate translates directly into pain at the ballot box.

The Busan meeting in October was supposed to end all that, or at least pause it convincingly. And to a point, it did. Tariffs came down from their triple-digit peaks to a merely punishing 47 percent. China resumed soybean purchases. Both sides agreed to further talks. The machinery of diplomacy began to grind again — slowly, reluctantly, with the characteristic sound of two bureaucracies that don’t trust each other trying to find a conference room they can both agree on.

The tariff roller-coaster. Now with 47% less certainty.

Then, on February 12, 2026, China confirmed it was in talks with the United States about a presidential visit, and the possibility of rolling back tariffs for up to a year as officials sought short-term economic wins. The visit was real. The calendar was real. The agenda, however, was still mostly aspirational.

Eight days later, the agenda took a direct hit.

On February 20, the United States Supreme Court ruled that President Trump could not use the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose tariffs. The decision was, in trade policy terms, the equivalent of discovering that the foundation of your house was made of plywood — technically a building material, but not the kind you want to bear the weight of your entire structure. The IEEPA tariffs had been the primary legal mechanism for the most aggressive tariffs on Chinese goods. With that mechanism invalidated, the administration was left scrambling for alternative authorities, launching new Section 301 investigations and imposing a 10 percent tariff on all countries under Section 122, a balance-of-payments provision that hadn’t been seriously invoked in decades.

The judicial plywood that just blew a hole in Trump’s tariff architecture.

Beijing’s reaction was instructive. Publicly, the Chinese foreign ministry expressed continued interest in a productive summit. Privately — and the private part is what matters — Chinese government departments were tasked with researching and assessing likely demands by the US leader and formulating possible concessions. They were scouring for bargaining chips, in the language of one report, which is what you do when you realize your negotiating partner has just had his biggest weapon taken away by his own judiciary and might therefore be simultaneously weaker and more unpredictable.

This is a combination that should concern anyone who has ever negotiated with a man who has lost his leverage but not his confidence. It is the diplomatic equivalent of playing poker with someone who has been told his credit card has been declined but has not yet looked at the bill.

The American preparation for the summit was, by most accounts, not going well.

This is a diplomatic understatement of the kind that the British Foreign Office used to specialize in — the sort of thing a career diplomat says when what they mean is “the building is on fire and nobody has called the fire department.” By late February, analysts were warning that the US team responsible for preparing the visit was, in the assessment of one source, “the worst I’ve ever seen.” Thin coordination. Rushed timelines. Weak deliverables. The kind of staff work that produces a summit communiqué approximately as substantial as a fast-food napkin.

The problem was partly structural. The Trump administration’s China policy was being run by multiple centres of gravity that did not always agree with each other or, in some cases, appear to be aware of each other’s existence. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was leading the trade negotiations, having established himself as the primary interlocutor with Chinese Vice-Premier He Lifeng through a series of meetings in Paris. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, the administration’s designated hawk, had shown little interest in the trip — partly because trade wasn’t his portfolio, partly because his entire political identity had been built on being tougher on China than anyone else in Washington, and a friendly visit to Beijing does not pair well with a career spent calling Beijing a threat to American civilization.

And then there was the President himself, who had rated his last meeting with Xi a twelve out of ten and appeared to regard the upcoming summit primarily as an opportunity to produce the kind of images — the handshake in the Great Hall, the state dinner, the motorcade through Tiananmen — that he had produced in 2017, when his first China visit had been swathed in precisely the kind of imperial pageantry that Beijing excels at deploying and that Trump has never been able to resist.

The 2017 visit is the ghost that haunts this entire enterprise. When Trump last went to Beijing, Xi rolled out a reception so lavish it had no precedent in modern Chinese diplomacy — a private dinner in the Forbidden City, a military parade, a state banquet, and a commercial package that included a headline-grabbing deal for 300 Boeing aircraft worth $37 billion. The photographs were magnificent. The atmospherics were intoxicating. And the strategic outcome was approximately zero. The Section 301 investigation that would become the legal basis for the tariff war had already been launched months before the visit. The warmth of the reception did nothing to prevent the relationship from sliding into confrontation over the following months and years. Nice photographs, as one analyst has noted, do not guarantee strategic outcomes.

Nobody in Washington appears to have absorbed this lesson. Or perhaps more precisely — everyone in Washington has absorbed it intellectually, in the way that a man who has been told that touching the stove will burn him absorbs the information right up until the moment he reaches for the burner because the stove is so attractively warm.

The American Chamber of Commerce in China — AmCham — offered its own prediction in mid-February, and it has the virtue, in retrospect, of being the most honest forecast anyone produced. The summit, AmCham’s chair said, would likely yield “very positive outcomes” but no “grand bargain.”

This is the diplomatic sweet spot that both governments were aiming for: a result impressive enough to claim as a victory, modest enough to avoid the risk of actually changing anything. China was exploring a US manufacturing fund that would allow Beijing to appear supportive of American industrial revival without actually transferring any meaningful economic leverage. The US was assembling wish lists of agricultural purchases — the same soybeans, the same beef, the same Boeing airplanes that have served as the ceremonial gift basket of US-China summitry for decades.

The proposals under discussion, as they would later crystallize, had a familiar quality. Large-scale Chinese purchases of US poultry, beef, and non-soybean crops. A commitment to buy 25 million metric tons of soybeans annually for three years. Negotiations with Boeing over a potential order involving as many as 500 737 MAX aircraft and dozens of wide-body jets. A bilateral “Board of Trade” to manage economic ties. A parallel “Board of Investment.”

The eternal summit gift: Boeing airplanes

And the other eternal gift: soybeans. The breadbasket of every US-China communiqué since 2001.

If you are a student of the relationship — and if you are reading this series, I suspect you are — none of this will surprise you. These are the same deliverables, in roughly the same denominations, that have been announced at virtually every US-China summit for the past two decades. The soybeans are always there. The Boeings are always there. The commitment to “further dialogue” is always there. They are the diplomatic equivalent of the complimentary breadbasket at a restaurant where the entrée never arrives.

The analytical framework I have been developing across these essays — what I call TACO, for Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — maps onto this moment with uncomfortable precision. Both sides were already operating in all four modes simultaneously.

The threats were real: tariffs remained at 47 percent even after the Busan truce, China had been tightening rare earth export controls, and the US was launching new Section 301 investigations. The accommodation was real too: the visit itself was a concession by both sides, an acknowledgment that the relationship was too important and too dangerous to let drift. The capitulation was already being prepared — soybeans and Boeings are what you give away when you want to produce headlines without producing outcomes. And the obfuscation was operating at full capacity: Trump calling the relationship “extremely strong” while his own administration was investigating China for structural overcapacity in manufacturing, and Xi expressing hope for “stable and predictable” ties while his government was building strategic reserves and diversifying trade away from the United States as fast as it could manage.

Meanwhile, China’s trade data told its own story. In the first two months of 2026, Chinese exports grew by 21.8 percent year-on-year — but not to the United States. Chinese exports to the US had continued to fall, dropping 11 percent year-on-year. The reorientation toward non-US markets was accelerating. Beijing was hedging against the relationship even as it prepared to celebrate it.

By the end of February, the invitation had been extended and accepted. The calendar said late March or early April. The preparation was thin. The deliverables were familiar. The expectations were calibrated to produce exactly enough success to justify the trip without risking anything that mattered. And both sides were simultaneously planning the summit and planning for the possibility that the summit would change nothing.

It was, in other words, exactly the kind of diplomatic moment at which history likes to intervene.

History intervened on February 28, 2026, when Operation Epic Fury began — the American war on Iran — and the entire architecture of the summit was blown sideways by an event that neither side had built into its careful choreography. But that is the subject of Part II.

For now, the invitation stands. Two men who have spent the better part of a decade alternately threatening and flattering each other have agreed to meet in the capital of the country that one of them has called America’s greatest strategic rival and the other has called home for his entire political life. The agenda is crowded with disputes that cannot be resolved. The preparation is thin. The historical precedent is a visit that produced gorgeous photographs and no strategic outcome whatsoever.

And every serious analyst in Washington, Beijing, and the capitals that watch both has already concluded that the most important bilateral meeting of the year will produce, at best, beans, Boeings, and a board of trade.

Welcome to the Beijing Opera. The overture is lovely. The plot is beside the point.

The stage is set. The plot, as always, is optional
