But the bill was paid by people who were not in the room, and that part I will not joke about. Dior appears briefly, as the household’s only member with no exposure to the market.

I count my essays, and this series has spent a long time with numbers that stood in for things. At 1602 the Dutch invented the share: a piece of paper that represented a fraction of a company you would never see. At 1,882 Edison sold the first product nobody could see, light by the unseeable unit, billed from a meter on the wall. Essay 1,929 is the year those two inventions met the limit of their credibility at the same moment. For ten years the people of the United States, and of Canada with them, had been counting their wealth in numbers on a paper tape. In October 1929 they discovered that the numbers had been describing something that was no longer there.

The permanently high plateau

Begin, as this series likes to, with the prophet.

On October 15, 1929, Irving Fisher — a professor of economics at Yale, one of the most respected economists in America, a man who had done genuinely important work on the theory of money — told a dinner audience in New York that stock prices had reached “what looks like a permanently high plateau.” The newspapers printed it the next morning. Nine days later, the market began to fall, and it did not stop falling for nearly three years.

I have no wish to mock Professor Fisher, who lost his own fortune in the crash and spent the rest of his life trying to understand what had happened. But he belongs in the gallery this series has been assembling since essay 1,588, where I first described certainty as the counting error committed before the count — the outcome booked in advance, the Armada named Invincible before it sailed. “Permanently high” is the Invincible Armada of financial forecasting. It is a statement about the future dressed as a measurement of the present, and in October 1929, as in August 1588, the weather did not read the newspapers.

Ten per cent down

The arithmetic that made the plateau look permanent was called buying on margin, and it is very simple. An investor in 1929 could buy a share of stock by putting up as little as ten per cent of the price and borrowing the rest from his broker. If the stock rose ten per cent, his own money doubled. If it rose twenty, he tripled it. Everyone could see this working. Shoeshine boys, as the stories have it, were giving tips. Millions of ordinary people who had never owned a share bought in, with borrowed money, because the numbers kept going up and the numbers were the only thing anyone could see.

But a margin loan cuts both ways, and it cuts faster going down. If the stock falls ten per cent, the investor’s own money is gone, and the broker demands more cash — a margin call — or sells the stock to cover the loan. And when the broker sells, the price falls further, which triggers more margin calls, which forces more selling. A number that had been rising because everyone was buying began to fall because everyone was forced to sell, and the mechanism that had multiplied the gains now multiplied the losses at the same rate. It is the most frightening machine in this series since the mobilisation timetables of 1914: a count that, once set moving in one direction, could not be paused.

The ticker runs late

Now the detail that made me want to write this essay, and it is the most purely Numbers Game moment of the entire twentieth century.

In 1929, stock prices reached investors across the country through the ticker — a telegraph-driven machine, a direct descendant of the wires of essay 1,844, that printed trades onto a narrow paper tape as they happened. It was the nervous system of the market. And on Black Thursday, October 24, and again on Black Tuesday, October 29, so many shares were traded that the ticker could not keep up. The tape fell behind. By the end of the worst days it was running hours late.

Think about what that means. All across America, in brokers’ offices crowded with frightened people, investors were watching prices on the tape — the only numbers they had — and the prices were from the morning. The real market had already moved far below them. Every decision anyone made outside the floor of the exchange was based on a number that was, by the time they read it, a historical document. A man looking at his stock on the ticker was not reading the price of his stock. He was reading what his stock used to be worth, an hour or two before, while the actual figure fell out of sight.

That, for me, is the entire crash in one image. The whole boom had been built on people trusting a number on a tape to represent a real thing. On the day it mattered most, the tape stopped representing even the present. The count could not keep up with the event. For a few terrible hours the most sophisticated financial system in the world was a room full of people reading old news and calling it the price.

Twenty-five years

The numbers, when they finally caught up, were catastrophic. The Dow Jones Industrial Average had peaked at 381 on September 3, 1929. By its bottom, in July 1932, it stood at 41 — a loss of almost ninety per cent. It would not see 381 again until November 1954, a full quarter of a century after the peak. A man who bought at the top and held on, doing nothing wrong except believing the professor, waited twenty-five years to get his money back, if he lived that long and had not been forced to sell.

But the market was not the worst of it, and this is where the essay turns serious. The crash was the trigger; what followed was the Great Depression, and the people who paid for it mostly owned no stock at all. Banks failed and took ordinary people’s savings with them. Factories closed. Unemployment in the United States rose to roughly a quarter of the workforce. In my own country the Depression fell hardest on the prairies, where wheat prices collapsed at the same moment the rain stopped and the topsoil blew away, and families who had never heard of a margin call lost farms they had broken out of the sod with their own hands. Men rode the rails from town to town looking for work. Farmers who could no longer afford gasoline hitched horses to their cars and called them Bennett buggies, after the prime minister. The people who had been counting their paper wealth lost paper. The people who had never owned any lost bread.

That is the honest ledger of 1929, and it is the same ledger as 1857 and 1897. The ones who made the numbers go up were not, for the most part, the ones who paid when the numbers came down. The cost was moved, as it always is, to a page that the people in the room never had to read.

Dior’s portfolio

The husky, I am pleased to report, came through the reckoning unscathed, because she holds no financial assets of any kind. Her entire net worth is denominated in real goods — a bed, two bowls, a rope toy of considerable sentimental value — and none of it was bought on margin. She does not read the ticker. She has never been told that her rope toy has reached a permanently high plateau. When I explained the crash to her she showed the profound indifference of an investor whose holdings consist entirely of things she can chew, and I confess I found the position hard to argue with. Nothing she owns is a number standing in for something else.

The closer

So 1929 is the year the numbers stopped meaning anything — the day the share invented at 1,602 and the unseeable unit of 1,882 met their limit together, and a whole society learned that a price is only a promise that someone else will believe the same number tomorrow. Under another dateline I write about a new boom, in machines that think, and about the money being poured into it on the strength of numbers that go up because everyone believes they will. I make no comparison. I merely file the ancestor where it can be found, with the ticker running late.

The count moves on, and it moves to a young man in Cambridge who, in the decade of the bread lines, asked the most fundamental question in the history of counting: not how much, but what can be counted at all? The next landmark is essay 1,936, and it is the year a twenty-three-year-old mathematician named Alan Turing imagined a machine made of nothing but a tape, a pencil and a rule — and proved that there are numbers no machine will ever compute. Every computer on earth descends from that imagined tape. So, I should tell you honestly, does the machine I write this series alongside.

See you at 1936, where a young man proves the count has a limit, and builds the future out of it.
