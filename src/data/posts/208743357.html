There is a particular kind of historical irony that appeals to a certain sensibility — the kind that notices how the grandest, most enduring human achievements are so often the byproduct of someone trying to solve a much smaller, much pettier problem. The Great Wall began as a border-patrol nuisance. The internet began as a Pentagon contingency plan for surviving a nuclear war. And the Silk Road — a sprawling network of routes that would eventually move silk, paper, religion, gunpowder, and the deadliest pandemic in recorded history across three continents for the better part of two millennia — began as a failed spy mission that never even achieved its actual objective.

The man responsible was named Zhang Qian, and in 138 BCE he did not set out to build a trade network. He set out to find allies against an enemy who had been humiliating his emperor for a generation.

The Han Dynasty, for all its later reputation as one of the great organizing civilizations of the ancient world, spent its early decades in a deeply undignified arrangement with its northern neighbours. The Xiongnu — a nomadic confederation of mounted warriors who controlled the steppe beyond the Han frontier — had spent generations raiding Chinese settlements, and rather than fight a war it could not reliably win, the Han court had settled into a policy called heqin: peace through kinship, which in practice meant sending Han princesses to marry Xiongnu chieftains and shipping north an annual tribute of silk, grain, and wine dressed up in the language of a marriage gift so nobody had to call it what it actually was. It worked, in the narrow sense that it kept the raiding to a tolerable level. It also meant that for decades, the most powerful dynasty in East Asia was, functionally, paying protection money to horsemen it considered barbarians, and everyone at court knew it.

Emperor Wu, who came to the throne in 141 BCE, found this arrangement intolerable, and he had heard a rumor that offered a way out. Somewhere to the west, driven from their homeland by the Xiongnu a generation earlier, lived a people called the Yuezhi, who by reputation hated the Xiongnu with the particular intensity of a defeated and displaced enemy. If the Han could find the Yuezhi and propose a military alliance, they could squeeze the Xiongnu from two directions at once and finally end the tribute system. It was, on paper, an elegant plan. The trouble was that nobody at the Han court had any real idea where the Yuezhi actually were, what lay between China and them, or whether the journey to find them was even survivable. Zhang Qian, a palace official with no particular diplomatic distinction, was given the assignment anyway, along with a modest retinue of about a hundred men and, crucially, no reliable map of anything beyond the western frontier garrisons.

The mission went wrong almost immediately. Zhang Qian and his party were captured by the Xiongnu while crossing their territory, and rather than being killed, he was kept — for roughly ten years, by most reconstructions of the timeline. He was given a Xiongnu wife, apparently treated with a degree of ordinary hospitality, and, in the telling of the Han court historian Sima Qian, never abandoned the mission he had been sent to complete. He eventually escaped, and rather than turn back toward the safety of the Han frontier, he kept going west, which tells you something about either his loyalty to the emperor or his stubbornness, and possibly both in equal measure.

He found the Yuezhi. They had, in the intervening years, migrated further west into Bactria, and having settled into rich new territory, they had lost their appetite for a war of revenge against the Xiongnu. Zhang Qian’s alliance proposal went nowhere. He was, by any reasonable diplomatic or military standard the Han court might have applied, a failure — a man who had spent over a decade of his life, endured a decade of captivity, and come home with no treaty, no allies, and no resolution to the Xiongnu problem he had been sent to solve.

What he brought back instead was something nobody at the Han court had thought to ask for, and whose value took years to become apparent even to the men who’d sent him. Zhang Qian had detailed, firsthand knowledge of the kingdoms of Central Asia — Ferghana, Bactria, Sogdiana — their markets, their crops, their political structures, and crucially, their horses. He reported that Ferghana bred an extraordinary breed of horse, one that reportedly “sweated blood” (almost certainly a parasitic skin condition rather than anything supernatural, though the name stuck), and that these horses were vastly superior to anything available in China for the cavalry campaigns the Han would eventually need to fight the Xiongnu properly. Emperor Wu became so fixated on acquiring these Ferghana horses that he later launched military campaigns specifically to secure them — a war fought, essentially, over livestock, which is its own small comedy buried inside a larger one.

But the horses were almost beside the point. What Zhang Qian’s report actually did was reveal to the Han court that the world west of their borders was not an undifferentiated wilderness of hostile nomads, but a chain of settled kingdoms, oasis cities, and markets that were already trading with each other, and that China — with its silk, in particular — had something those markets wanted badly. Within a generation, merchants were moving Chinese silk west along the routes Zhang Qian had scouted, skirting the brutal Taklamakan Desert along its northern and southern edges through a chain of oasis towns — Dunhuang, Turfan, Kashgar — each one a waystation, a marketplace, and eventually a small cosmopolitan world of its own. Goods moved east too: horses, jade, glassware, and eventually crops like grapes and alfalfa made their way into China along the same paths. Nobody in the Han court had designed this network. Nobody had voted on it or issued a decree calling it into being. It simply began happening, the way water finds a slope, because there was profit to be made and the basic geographic knowledge — however incomplete, however hard-won by one unlucky diplomat — was suddenly available to be acted upon.

By the first century BCE, that silk had reached Rome, and it prompted one of history’s more entertaining bouts of moral panic. The Romans had no idea silk came from an insect; the elder Pliny speculated, wrongly but poetically, that it was combed from the leaves of trees in a distant eastern forest. What they did know was that Roman citizens — first the wealthy, then increasingly the merely aspirational — had developed a serious appetite for the fabric, and that this appetite was draining the empire’s reserves of gold and silver at an alarming rate, since Rome had nothing the Chinese wanted in return except metal. Pliny complained bitterly that the empire was losing a fortune every year to Arabia, India, and China combined for luxuries it did not need. Seneca, writing with his usual moralizing disapproval, denounced silk garments as morally compromising, on the grounds that fabric so thin and revealing could not properly be called clothing at all and left a woman, in his memorable phrasing, unable to swear honestly that she was not naked. The Senate, at various points, debated and even passed sumptuary measures aimed at curbing silk’s spread, on both fiscal and moral grounds, with roughly the success such measures always have against a fabric everyone secretly wants to wear anyway.

None of this trade, it’s worth noting, involved direct contact between Rome and China. The silk passed through a long chain of middlemen — Central Asian traders, and above all the Parthian Empire, which controlled the overland routes through Persia and guarded its position as gatekeeper between Rome and the Far East with considerable commercial ruthlessness, marking up the price at every stage and carefully preventing Roman traders from making direct contact with Chinese sources that might have cut them out of the arrangement entirely. Rome, for all its military reach, never once managed to establish direct trade with the source of the fabric it was bankrupting itself over. This is worth sitting with for a moment: the custodians of the most formidable military and administrative machine the ancient world had produced were, by their own account, losing sleep over scarves they couldn’t even buy at the source. An empire that could conquer Gaul, subdue Egypt, and hold the Mediterranean as a private lake could not, it turned out, resist paying an extortionate markup for a nice bolt of silk, and its moralists found this more alarming than they found the silk itself pleasant. Some anxieties, it seems, are simply older than we give them credit for.

This is the story this series intends to tell — not as a single continuous narrative but as a chain of empires, cities, ideas, and disasters, each one picking up the route where the last one left off, none of them fully in control of what they had inherited, and most of them convinced, in their turn, that they were the ones who truly understood what the route was for. The Kushans would control the routes through what is now Afghanistan and Pakistan and grow rich enough to mint their own gold coinage in imitation of Rome’s. The Sassanid Persians and the Byzantines would fight, at times literally, over the silk trade, each trying to break the other’s monopoly on the fabric neither of them could produce themselves, until Byzantine monks reportedly smuggled silkworm eggs out of China hidden in hollow bamboo canes in the sixth century, ending China’s centuries-long monopoly on the one commodity that had started the entire network. The Tang Dynasty would preside over the road’s most genuinely cosmopolitan century, when Persian, Sogdian, and Central Asian merchants lived openly in Chinese cities and foreign religions were tolerated with a openness China would not see again for over a thousand years. The Islamic caliphates would inherit and extend the routes after the seventh century, turning Baghdad and later Samarkand into some of the wealthiest cities on earth. And the Mongols, under Genghis Khan and his successors, would eventually unify the entire route under a single political authority for the first time in its history, making travel along it, briefly, safer and faster than it had ever been or would be again.

That unification came at a terrible cost that nobody could have anticipated. The same infrastructure that let merchants and missionaries move safely from Beijing to the Black Sea also let disease move with them, and in 1347, according to the near-contemporary account of the Italian notary Gabriele de’ Mussi, a besieging Mongol army at the Genoese trading post of Caffa on the Crimean coast, itself ravaged by plague, catapulted infected corpses over the city’s walls in an early and gruesome experiment in biological warfare. The Genoese merchants who fled the siege by ship carried the disease with them to Sicily, and from there it tore through a Europe that had no immunity, no understanding of contagion, and no defense beyond flight and prayer, eventually killing somewhere between a third and half of the continent’s population within a few catastrophic years. The road that had built the medieval world’s luxury trade also built its worst pandemic. Both were unintended consequences of a network nobody had originally designed to do either.

Which brings us, finally, to the man most people associate with all of this, and whose presence here, so late in the story, requires something close to an apology to the reader. When Marco Polo set out for China with his father and uncle in 1271, the Silk Road was already roughly fourteen hundred years old. He did not discover it, open it, or pioneer it in any meaningful sense; he traveled a network of routes, caravanserais, and merchant communities that had been fully established and thoroughly used by generations of Sogdian, Persian, Arab, and Central Asian traders long before any Venetian had thought to make the trip. What Polo did was survive the journey, spend roughly seventeen years in the service of the Mongol emperor Kublai Khan, and then, on his return, end up imprisoned in Genoa after a naval skirmish, where he happened to share a cell with a writer of romances named Rustichello da Pisa, who transcribed Polo’s account into the book that would make both men famous and that generations of European readers would treat, with considerable justification for skepticism, as a wildly embellished travelogue. Polo was, by the time his book began circulating, essentially a well-traveled and highly entertaining tourist arriving at a well-established destination and presenting his account of the crowd as evidence that he had found something new — a phenomenon not entirely unfamiliar to anyone who has watched a modern traveler “discover” a restaurant that has been in business, and beloved by locals, for thirty years. His account made him famous in Europe and made the road famous by association in the Western imagination for centuries afterward. But the road had already done its most consequential work — moving Buddhism into China, moving the plague into Europe, moving paper, the compass, and eventually gunpowder into a world entirely unprepared for any of them — long before Polo ever left Venice, and it would go on doing consequential work long after he died, largely forgotten, in 1324.

This series will follow that longer, stranger chronology rather than the one built around Polo’s fame: the empires that fought over the route and occasionally weaponized it; the cities — Samarkand, Dunhuang, Kashgar, Venice — that grew rich, scholarly, or both because of their position along it; and the ideas, diseases, and objects that traveled its length whether or not anyone who set them in motion intended them to arrive where they did. It begins, appropriately enough, with a failed spy who never secured his alliance, spent a decade as a captive for his trouble, and returned home to discover — though he likely never fully grasped it himself — that he had inadvertently built something considerably larger and more durable than the war he had been sent to start.
