The field, June 2026: Alex Bores, Micah Lasher, Jack Schlossberg, and George Conway. Thirty-two percent of voters still undecided with 2 weeks to go.

“The AI oligarchs who are rigging our economy are scared of me. They named me their No. 1 enemy and said they would spend at least $10 million to defeat me.” — Alex Bores, PIX11 debate, June 4, 2026

“My name is Jack, or John Bouvier Kennedy Schlossberg if you want to know the whole thing.” — Jack Schlossberg, campaign fundraising email, March 5, 2026

There are two weeks left in the race to represent New York’s 12th Congressional District, and the strangest thing about the most expensive, most chaotic, most narratively overstuffed Democratic primary of the 2026 cycle is that nobody has actually won it yet.

Thirty-two percent of likely Democratic primary voters remain undecided. The top three candidates — Assemblyman Alex Bores, Assemblyman Micah Lasher, and Jack Schlossberg, grandson of the 35th President of the United States — are running in a statistical dead heat. George Conway, the former Republican and Lincoln Project co-founder who registered as a Democrat last year and based his entire campaign on impeaching Donald Trump, is polling at ten percent and drawing his support almost entirely from voters sixty-five and older.

The field is, in other words, exactly as messy on day 224 as it was on day one.

And yet something has clarified.

What We Know Now That We Didn’t Know in November

Jack Schlossberg. The name still carries weight — but in 2026, is it enough?

When Jack Schlossberg announced his candidacy on November 11, 2025 — in an email to supporters, which is apparently how dynasties are launched now — the assumption was that the name would be enough. Not enough to coast. But enough to carry him through the first few months, to let the Kennedy inheritance do the heavy lifting while the mechanics of an actual campaign caught up.

It worked, for a while. Early polling by GQR, commissioned by Conway’s campaign with no candidate information provided, had Schlossberg at 25 percent. A second poll, paid for by the pro-AI super PAC Leading the Future — yes, the same one that would later spend $6.2 million attacking his opponent — had him leading the field. Pure name recognition. The surname as a poll result.

By May, Emerson College had him at eleven percent.

The collapse is not mysterious. The New York Times, in a profile that appears to have caused considerable discomfort in the Schlossberg camp, reported what had been circulating in political circles for months: an esoteric social media presence, a sporadic work history, questions about campaign discipline. The candidate himself, apparently confirming the critique in real time, tweeted: “Needed a quick nap!! Even though I just woke up. Rest of the day / month wide open lmk if you wanna hang? Wait nvm changed my mind really busy can’t make it.”

This is not a tweet from someone who is going to win a Democratic primary in one of the most politically engaged districts in the United States.

The Schlossberg story is, in miniature, the same story the Kennedy dynasty has been telling for sixty years: that the name contains more than it can deliver. The dynasty produced genuine giants — Jack and Bobby, in whose shadow every subsequent generation has operated — and it has been living off that capital ever since. Jack Schlossberg is thirty-three years old. He is the son of Caroline Kennedy and the grandson of a president. He went to Yale and then Harvard Law. He has a social media following and Nancy Pelosi’s endorsement. He is not, on the evidence available, ready.

Which is not quite the same as saying he can’t win. Stranger things have happened in Manhattan Democratic primaries. The name still means something to the third of the electorate that has not made up its mind. And early voting begins June 13 — ten days from now — which means the race that exists at this moment, in this configuration, with this level of undecidedness, is the race that will determine the outcome.

But if Schlossberg wins, he will have won despite his campaign, not because of it. And if he loses, the dynasty’s long recessional from the halls of power will have taken another step.

The Real Race

The fight that has actually mattered, since roughly March, is the one between Bores and Lasher.

Micah Lasher — the institutional favourite, West Side establishment candidate, and current slight leader in the race.

They are almost perfectly counterposed. Lasher is the West Side candidate — endorsed by Nadler, endorsed by Bloomberg, backed by Governor Hochul, with the institutional machinery of Manhattan Democratic politics firmly in his corner. He is a former child magician turned state assemblyman, a biographical fact that this series has already addressed at length and which requires no further elaboration except to note that it remains, objectively, remarkable. He is disciplined, experienced, and supported by the kind of establishment infrastructure that usually wins Democratic primaries.

Alex Bores — East Side reformer, AI regulation advocate, and the target of $6.2 million in outside spending.

Bores is the East Side candidate — the tech-industry reformer, the AI regulation pioneer, the man at the center of a $12 million proxy war funded by the competing wings of Silicon Valley. He has passed twenty-seven bills in the Assembly, which is approximately six times the legislative output of the rest of the field combined, a statistic he mentioned in a debate forum in a tone suggesting he finds it relevant. He left Palantir in 2019 because of the company’s work with ICE, forfeiting what he describes as $2 million in shares and “generational wealth,” which is either an admirable moral stand or a very good story, or both simultaneously.

The polling crosstabs tell the tale of two separate coalitions. Bores leads among men at 27 percent; Lasher leads among women, who are 57 percent of this electorate, at 24 percent to Bores’s 15 percent. Bores’s strongest age cohort is 35-to-49-year-olds, where he reaches 34 percent. Neither candidate has broken through to a commanding lead because each is essentially running a different race inside the same primary.

The debate on June 4 sharpened the contrast. The informal PIX11 viewer poll — not remotely scientific, but atmospherically useful — had Bores winning with 40 percent, Lasher at 38, Schlossberg at 21, and Conway at 1. The sharpest exchanges were between the two assemblymen. Bores repeatedly noted his legislative record. Lasher, endorsed by the congressman whose seat is at stake, made the case for continuity and institutional effectiveness. Neither landed a knockout.

Prediction markets, as of this writing, give Lasher a narrow edge, driven by the endorsement infrastructure and the demographic math of who actually shows up in Manhattan Democratic primaries. Women. Older voters. The reliably organized. These are Lasher’s people.

But 32 percent undecided, with seventeen days left and early voting starting in ten, means the math is still being written.

The $12 Million Sideshow That Isn’t a Sideshow

It would be tempting to treat the AI money war as a sideshow — the kind of outside-spending story that generates media coverage without actually determining outcomes. That temptation should be resisted.

The numbers are clarifying. Close to $75 million has flowed to the pro-AI super PAC Leading the Future from Silicon Valley heavyweights including Joe Lonsdale, a Palantir co-founder, and Greg Brockman, a co-founder of OpenAI. The PAC has spent $6.2 million against Bores in the NY-12 race alone. Attack ads — “Alex Bores. Wrong on A.I. Wrong for Congress.” “Hypocrite.” “Liar.” — have saturated the district since January.

On the other side, Anthropic employees have donated at least $186,000 directly to Bores’s campaign, and four super PACs with ties to Anthropic or its ideological allies have spent about $6.5 million defending Bores or attacking his opponents. Billionaire crypto investor Chris Larsen dropped $3.5 million into the pro-Bores column after learning, according to his spokesman, that OpenAI was deliberately targeting Bores’s campaign.

What makes this more than a campaign finance story is what it represents. The RAISE Act — Bores’s New York State legislation regulating advanced AI models — became the pivot point around which the entire industry’s political allegiances reorganized. Anthropic, which has argued publicly for more government oversight and a cautious approach to AI development, found a congressional candidate whose policy instincts aligned with its philosophy. OpenAI and its investors found a threat.

The result is a Democratic primary in Manhattan that has become a proxy war for the future of AI governance in America. Michael Paluszek, a forty-year resident of Stuyvesant Town, told the Times he had never heard of Bores until he saw the attack ads. He then switched his support to Bores and claimed the corner of East 14th Street and Avenue A as his campaign territory. The attack ads, in other words, created a constituency they were designed to destroy.

This is one of the more elegant failures in recent campaign history.

Bores said at the PIX11 debate what he has been saying for months: “The AI oligarchs who are rigging our economy are scared of me.” It is a line that plays well in a dark-blue Manhattan district where “AI oligarchs” functions as a nearly complete political argument. Whether it is enough to close the gap with Lasher’s institutional advantages is the question the next seventeen days will answer.

Conway’s Recessional

A word, before the curtain, about George Conway.

He came to this race with a story that seemed tailor-made for the moment: the conservative lawyer who spent four years watching his wife serve in the administration of a president he publicly despised, who co-founded the Lincoln Project, who ultimately registered as a Democrat and put his candidacy where his commentary had been for years. It was, as political narratives go, genuinely compelling.

The district didn’t buy it.

Ten percent. Support concentrated almost entirely among voters sixty-five and older. At the debate, informal viewer polling put him at 1 percent of those who thought he’d won. He has been running, essentially, a one-issue campaign — the impeachment and accountability of Donald Trump — in a district where that issue, while popular, is not sufficient. The voters who agree with Conway about Trump are choosing between candidates who also agree with Conway about Trump, and who have the legislative records, the local relationships, and the policy depth to back it up.

Conway’s defeat, if it comes, will not be a defeat for the anti-Trump cause. It will be a defeat for the proposition that passionate opposition to a president is a complete qualification for Congress. It is not. It is a starting point.

There is a photograph from a recent Times profile — Conway walking his corgi Clyde through Central Park, looking every bit the candidate who ran the most photogenic campaign in the race. It is a good photograph. It may be the best memorial to a candidacy that deserved more than it could deliver.

New York’s 12th Congressional District — where the future of AI regulation in America may be decided this June.

What It Means

This race has always been about more than who fills Jerry Nadler’s seat. Nadler served this district for thirty-four years. He was the Judiciary Committee chairman. He was a figure of genuine institutional weight, earned across decades. Whoever wins on June 23 will arrive in a House Democratic caucus fighting for its political life, in a cycle where the anti-Trump wave is either building or cresting depending on whom you ask, representing one of the wealthiest and most educated congressional districts in the country.

They will also arrive having won a primary financed in part by the competing interests of the AI industry — which means whoever wins carries that history into every tech policy vote, every AI governance debate, every hearing on the future of the technology that is reorganizing the global economy.

If Bores wins, he arrives as the candidate the AI oligarchs couldn’t stop, which is either a mandate or a target, and probably both. If Lasher wins, he arrives as the institutional choice of a party trying to fight a two-front war — against Trump and against the structural deterioration of democratic norms — with the endorsement of the man who held the seat for three decades. If Schlossberg wins, the Kennedy dynasty gets one more chapter, and the narrative industry that has sustained itself on that dynasty’s mythology for sixty years will have another decade of material.

Any of these outcomes is possible. The 32 percent undecided will decide.

From a considerable distance north of the 49th parallel, the race looks like what New York always looks like from here: too big, too loud, too expensive, and yet somehow also the place where the argument America is having with itself actually gets resolved. The Upper East Side meets the Upper West Side meets Midtown Manhattan, and somewhere in the overlap they pick someone to go tell Washington what they think.

Fourteen days.
