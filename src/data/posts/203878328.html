Reverend Faye asked: what kind of world do we need to build so that people are not afraid to bring what they have into the open? She sat down. The church was quiet. Gerald had taken notes.

Reverend Faye Drummond had been working on the sermon for eleven weeks.

This was longer than she usually took. A good short sermon — the kind she had been delivering at St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon United Church for eleven years — typically required three drafts and a walk along the river. The river is useful for sermons, she had found, because it does not offer suggestions and does not ask whether you have considered the counterargument.

She had six drafts of this one. She had walked the river four times. She had arrived at the fifth draft convinced it was right and then read something that changed it, and had arrived at the sixth draft convinced it was right and then read something that changed it again. She had filed the four previous drafts, which is not a thing she usually does with draft sermons, but which she had done this time because she had a feeling the earlier drafts contained things she had thought and then abandoned that she might need to know she had thought.

The sermon was about artificial intelligence.

She had not planned, eleven weeks earlier, to preach about artificial intelligence. She had planned to preach about what we owe each other across borders, which was a subject that the year had made urgent. But as she read and walked and drafted, the two subjects had converged in a way she could not separate: the question of what we owe each other across borders was, she had come to believe, inseparable from the question of what we owe each other when the machines begin doing the work that people used to do and the people who used to do it are left to work out what comes next.

She preached it on a Sunday in August, to a congregation that included, in the third row, two software developers from the Festival’s administrative team who had heard, through the particular telegraph of a small city, that this was a sermon worth attending.

She began with a question. She said: what is work for?

She said she did not mean this economically. She knew what work was for economically. Work was for income, which was for shelter and food and the education of children and the maintenance of vehicles and the Perth County Pantry’s butter tart, which was worth the eight dollars. She understood all of that. She was not asking what work produced. She was asking what work was.

She said she had been thinking about a man she knew — she did not name him — who walked dogs in Elsinore every morning at six-fifteen, eight dogs, six days a week, in all weather, because that was what he did. She said she had asked him once, not recently, why he walked dogs, and he had said: because they need walking and I am here. She said she had thought about that answer for three years.

She said: because they need walking and I am here.

She said she had come to believe that this was not a small answer. She said it was possibly the largest answer available to the question of what work was, which was that work was the way a person said: I am here, in this place, doing this thing, because it needs doing and I am the one to do it.

She said she had been reading about machines that could do, or would soon be able to do, a very large number of things that people currently did in order to say: I am here. She said she was not against the machines. She was a person who used a dishwasher and a furnace and a car, and she was aware that the dishwasher and the furnace and the car had all, in their times, displaced someone who had previously done the thing by hand. She was not a Luddite. She was a minister, which was a different kind of person with a different kind of concern.

Her concern, she said, was not whether the machines could do the work. Her concern was what would be left for people to say: I am here.

She said she had found, in the process of writing this sermon, that she kept returning to the parable of the talents, which she had always found somewhat difficult, because the parable of the talents seemed to reward those who already had the most and to take from those who had the least, and this had always struck her as a curious thing for a parable to do. She said she had come to think, recently, that she had been reading it wrong. She said the talent buried in the ground was not buried out of laziness. It was buried out of fear — specifically, the fear of a servant who did not believe his labour would be valued, who had received something and hidden it because the world he was in had taught him that what he had was not enough to risk. She said she thought this was the more interesting problem.

She said the question the parable was actually asking was not: what did you do with what you were given? The question was: what kind of world do we need to build so that people are not afraid to bring what they have into the open?

She said she thought this was also the question that the machines were asking, whether they knew it or not, and that the answer was not a technological one.

She paused. The church was quiet in the way that churches are quiet when a congregation is attending to something rather than waiting for it to end.

She said: what we owe each other is not the protection of any particular job. What we owe each other is a world in which the thing a person can do is worth doing, and the person who does it is worth more than the output.

She sat down.

Gerald was in the fourth row. He attended St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon on the second and fourth Sundays of each month, not out of particular doctrinal conviction but out of the view, which he had held since approximately 1994, that certain things were worth showing up for on a regular basis whether or not they were strictly required.

He had taken notes. He did not usually take notes during sermons. He had brought his small notebook because he had a feeling, when he arrived, that this was a sermon for which notes would be appropriate.

He wrote three things.

He did not share them with anyone. He filed the notebook under St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon — Sermons — Notable — Drummond, F. — 2026.

He walked home at his own pace. He read the news on his phone when he got in, because someone had to, and because Hansard was on the filing system chair in the particular way that suggested the chair was no longer Gerald’s and the arrangement was permanent.

Jim walks eight dogs at six-fifteen every morning because they need walking and he is here. He has not been replaced by a machine. The dogs require a specific person on a specific street at a specific time, and that person is Jim, and the dogs know it.

Jim had not been at the sermon. He had been walking his six-fifteen dogs at the time, and then the eight-thirty dogs, and then the mid-morning dogs, which was a schedule that left little room for institutional attendance of any kind.

He heard about it from Maggie at Sunday dinner, who had heard about it from Debi, who had been in the congregation in her capacity as a person who attended church on the second and fourth Sundays and who had texted Maggie a summary immediately afterward because the summary was too good to wait.

Jim listened to Maggie’s account of Debi’s account of Reverend Faye’s sermon.

He passed the bread.

He said: because they need walking and I am here.

He said it in the particular tone of a man who has recognised something.

Maggie looked at him.

He said he thought the reverend had got it right.

They ate their dinner. The boys ate their dinner.

The two software developers from the Festival’s administrative team attended the morning service and stayed for coffee hour, which is a thing they had not previously done. They spoke with Reverend Faye for eleven minutes by the coffee urn. One of them worked in the Festival’s digital programming team. The other worked on the ticketing system and had spent the previous six months managing the implementation of an automated pricing algorithm that adjusted ticket prices in real time based on demand.

He did not mention the algorithm during coffee hour. He thought about the talent buried in the ground. He drove home. He thought about it for three more days.

He did not change the algorithm. He had not been asked to change it. He had simply been given something to think about, and had thought about it, which was either the purpose of a sermon or a side effect, and which Reverend Faye, if asked, would have said was the same thing.

Carly covered the sermon for the Beacon. Norman had not assigned it — she had asked. He had looked at her over his reading glasses in the way he looks at people who are developing judgment.

He said: eight hundred words.

She used seven hundred and ninety.

The piece did not summarise the theology. It described the church, the congregation, the quality of the August morning light through the east window, the reverend’s voice and the particular silence that followed the question about what work was for. It quoted three things directly. It ended with the butter tart.

Norman read it and did not edit a word.

He filed it under things he had been waiting for her to write.

He did not tell her this. He texted: Good.

A Note for the Record

The question of what the machines will do with the work is not new. It has been asked at every significant technological transition — the loom, the steam engine, the combine harvester, the personal computer, the search engine. The answer, historically, has been: the machines do the work, the work changes, new work appears, the people who were doing the old work have varying degrees of success finding the new work, and the people who owned the machines do well.

This time the question is being asked more urgently, because the machines being built are not replacing the movement of physical things but the movement of thought — the drafting, the analysis, the categorisation, the production of language, the tasks that previously required a person to sit down and attend to something and produce a result. The economists call the effect ambiguous. The people whose work is being replaced call it something else.

To the south, the federal government has reduced its workforce by methods that have included the offer of a buyout that many have described as a threat in the language of an offer, the dismissal of probationary employees without cause, and the dismantling of agencies whose functions had been established by Congress. The argument is efficiency. The counter-argument is that the people who did the work were doing things that needed doing, and that the machines, in these cases, are not machines at all but decisions — about who is worth paying and who is not, about what the government is for and what it is not for, and about who gets to make those decisions.

Reverend Faye asked: what kind of world do we need to build so that people are not afraid to bring what they have into the open?

In Elsinore, Jim walks eight dogs at six-fifteen every morning because they need walking and he is here. He has not been replaced by a machine. The dogs require a specific person on a specific street at a specific time, and that person is Jim, and the dogs know it.

This is either the last kind of work that will remain or an example of what all work actually is, and Reverend Faye, who has been thinking about this for eleven weeks, has a view on which, and has expressed it in seven hundred and ninety words in the Elsinore Beacon, which Norman Stalker ran without editing.

Gerald has the notes from the sermon. He filed them on Sunday afternoon, after the roast and before Hansard reclaimed the chair.
