Part XXII closed on an honest admission: this series would rather be surprised by a genuine Canadian no, held past a deadline, than proven right again by another quiet accommodation dressed up as firmness. I said I wouldn’t bet heavily on the surprise. Late Friday night, Mark Carney proved that bet wrong.

I. What Actually Happened

As Matina Stevis-Gridneff, Ian Austen, and Ana Swanson reported for the New York Times, talks between Canadian and American negotiators collapsed late Friday, minutes before the deadline that would trigger new 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian exports. Carney said he had suspended the talks himself, because the American side had introduced last-minute terms that were “unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal.” He credited the weeks of negotiation with real progress, but said it had “not been enough to meet our objectives for Canadians.”

Greer told reporters that Canada had walked away from an offer to receive “the best treatment of any major exporter” to the American market, and blamed “new demands and walk backs of other commitments by Canada” for upending what he described as a carefully balanced agreement. U.S. Customs and Border Protection issued tariff guidance to importers before Greer had even finished his briefing. At 12:01 a.m. Saturday, the tariffs took effect. Carney said Canada would retaliate “dollar for dollar,” to “protect our workers and businesses.”

Whatever else this is, it isn’t scenario one from Part XXII. It also isn’t quite scenario two as originally framed, since a delay and a near-deal preceded the collapse rather than a clean refusal from the outset. But the ending is the one this series didn’t expect: tariffs landing, retaliation announced, and no deal signed.

II. Where the Steel and Aluminum Tariffs Actually Came From

It’s worth recapping this here, because the final numbers being fought over Friday only make sense against the history behind them. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 lets a president restrict imports if Commerce finds they threaten national security. Trump used it in 2018, in his first term, to impose a 25 percent tariff on steel and 10 percent on aluminum globally, on the theory that Chinese overcapacity and subsidized production had weakened the American metals industry to the point of threatening its ability to supply defence and critical infrastructure. Canada and Mexico fought that designation and, in 2019, won a permanent exemption as part of finalizing USMCA. For six years, Canadian steel and aluminum crossed the border tariff-free.

That exemption ended in February 2025, early in Trump’s second term, when he reimposed the full 25 percent on steel and raised aluminum to match, revoking every country-specific exemption at once — including Canada’s. The executive order’s stated justification pointed to Canadian import volumes running roughly 18 percent above the 2015–2019 historical average, framed as the kind of surge the original 2018 finding had warned about. From there this series has already tracked the rest: a threatened doubling to 50 percent over Ontario’s electricity surcharge in March, walked back after Ford and Lutnick talked, and then an actual doubling to 50 percent in June 2025 that has held, with only narrow carve-outs on the table, through this week’s collapse.

Whether any of that was fair depends on which part of the claim you’re evaluating. Global steel and aluminum overcapacity, mostly Chinese, is real, and no serious economist disputes that it has hollowed out North American and European producers for years — that much of the 2018 finding wasn’t fiction. What’s harder to defend is treating Canada as part of that problem in 2025. Canada isn’t a competitor flooding the U.S. market with underpriced subsidized metal; it’s an integrated supplier the U.S. itself relied on enough to exempt permanently in 2019, specifically because Washington had already concluded Canadian metal wasn’t the security threat the 2018 tariffs were built to address. Freeland made exactly that argument at the time — Canadian aluminum, in her words, does not undermine U.S. national security — and Canada had already tried to get the WTO to rule Section 232 itself illegal as a disguised protectionist tool rather than a genuine security measure, back in 2018. Reimposing the tariff in 2025 didn’t answer that objection. It just outran it, using the same national-security language for a country the U.S. had already decided, once, wasn’t the problem.

That gap — a national-security label doing the work of ordinary trade leverage — is the same gap this series named across the shifting justifications Freeland’s essay catalogued in Part XXI: smoke, discrimination, security, whatever fit the moment. The steel and aluminum story isn’t a new example of that pattern. It’s the oldest one, predating almost everything else this series has covered, and Friday’s collapse happened with that same tariff, on that same pretext, still sitting at the center of the fight.

III. What Was Actually Being Asked, One More Time

It’s worth being precise about what Carney turned down, because the terms on the table in the final days were not a good outcome dressed up as a bad one — they were a genuinely limited concession from Washington. Per reporting in the days leading up to the collapse, the final American offer would have cut steel and aluminum tariffs to 25 percent from 50 percent, but only for a limited volume, with the remainder still facing the full rate. Autos would have dropped to 15 percent from 25 percent, adjusted for American parts content that typically makes up about half the value of a Canadian-built vehicle. Industry executives and analysts had already concluded that arrangement would leave Canadian auto production unprofitable regardless — Flavio Volpe’s math, cited in Part XXII’s research, put the effective tariff after the parts discount at 7.5 percent against an industry profit margin of roughly 6 percent. Carney didn’t walk away from a deal that solved Canada’s central complaint. He walked away from a deal that would have kept the industry’s slow bleed running at a slightly reduced rate, while asking Canada to formally sign off on ending the liquor boycotts, dropping the streaming and online-news protections, and calling it settled.

That distinction matters for how this gets read. This wasn’t a government holding the line against an acceptable offer for the sake of appearances. It was a government concluding that the offer on the table wasn’t actually the relief it needed, and choosing tariffs it didn’t want over a deal that wouldn’t have fixed the underlying problem.

IV. The Vance Problem

Three days before the collapse, Vice President JD Vance was caught, in leaked remarks to donors at a Southampton, New York fundraiser and published by The Canadian Press, mocking Carney’s negotiating posture — describing him as someone who “puffs his chest out” and claims he’ll “out-tough” Trump while, in Vance’s telling, actually “climbing down on a lot of issues.” That comment was reported as the latest confirmation of the dominance framing this series has tracked since Freeland’s essay in Part XXI: an administration official, on the record in substance if not in venue, describing Canadian firmness as theater covering routine capitulation.

Friday night makes that comment look premature in a way its author surely didn’t intend. Whatever Carney’s negotiating style looked like from inside a Southampton ballroom three days earlier, the actual outcome was a prime minister letting tariffs land rather than signing a deal his own advisers judged insufficient. Vance’s mockery wasn’t wrong about the pattern up to that point — this series has spent twenty-two parts documenting exactly the climb-downs he was describing. It was wrong about this one holding the same way.

V. Reading the Domestic Numbers

Carney isn’t walking a purely principled line without cover, and it’s worth being honest about why. A Léger poll found 56 percent of Canadians opposed to any further trade concessions, which gave him room to walk away that he might not have had a year ago, when this series first started tracking the gap between what Canadians wanted from Carney and what he was actually delivering. Doug Ford, the premier of Ontario — the province with the most to lose from a failed deal on autos — endorsed the decision publicly and pointedly: “everything needs to be on the table,” he said, adding that Ontario was “ready to do its part.” That’s a notably different Ford than the one this series quoted in Part XX saying, of the Gordie Howe Bridge snub, that he still believed “Canadians love Americans.” Something has shifted in how far Ontario’s own premier is willing to go along with accommodation.

Coletto’s framing from the previous update in this series holds up well here: Canadians were always going to judge this by what Canada gave up and whether the crisis reduced the country’s dependency, not simply by whether tariffs came off. By that standard, walking away from a deal that reduced tariffs without eliminating them, in exchange for concrete concessions Canada would have had to make immediately, may read to Canadians as the government finally treating the underlying vulnerability as the actual problem — not just the latest round of pressure.

VI. What Doesn’t Change

None of this resolves the asymmetry this series has spent its whole run describing. Canada still sent 71.7 percent of its merchandise exports to the United States last year, per the same figure cited in Part XXII’s discussion of India’s comparatively cheaper refusal. Candace Laing, of the Canadian Chamber of Commerce, called the outcome “a body blow to North American competitiveness in this self-defeating trade saga” — and she isn’t wrong that both economies absorb real damage from tariffs actually landing rather than being threatened and withdrawn. The legal provision Trump used for the new 50 percent rate, Section 338, is a previously untested 1930s trade law that sits outside the Supreme Court’s February ruling striking down most of his other tariffs — which means these tariffs could face their own legal challenge in the coming weeks, a genuinely open question this series will need to track separately from the political one.

Nor does one held line settle whether this is a turning point or a single data point inside a longer pattern that resumes next month under different packaging. Trump has reversed course on Canada before, and the two governments have a full year of on-again, off-again threats behind them. Retaliation “dollar for dollar” is also a choice with its own costs, and how Ottawa actually structures it — which goods, which sectors, how it lands on the provinces that just got asked to end their own boycotts — will say as much about the seriousness of this moment as the walk-away itself did.

VII. The Ledger

Twenty-three parts in, the series gets to record something it didn’t expect to be recording: the surprise it said it would rather have. Whether this is the moment Canada actually started treating its American dependency as the problem to be solved, rather than the constraint to be managed, is still an open question — one deal collapsing under pressure isn’t the same as a country restructuring its trade relationships the way India spent eight months doing. But for one Friday night, at least, the pattern this series has tracked since October broke instead of confirming itself again. That’s worth stating plainly, without hedging it back into the old frame just because the old frame has been right so often before.

Freeland asked, in Part XXI, whether the world would eventually conclude that Washington’s treatment of its allies was never really a departure from what Americans thought of them all along. Friday night doesn’t answer that question either. But it’s the first time in this series that Canada’s answer to Washington wasn’t a concession dressed up as a stand. It was tariffs, taken on the chin, over a deal it didn’t think was good enough. Whether that holds past the next round of talks is the thing to watch for in whatever comes next.

Update, August 22, 2026

Carney didn’t let Friday night’s walk-away stand as a quiet fact. On Saturday morning he gave what The New York Times called a “muscular, 22-minute” address, and the language in it was sharper than anything this series has quoted from him before: “You’re at war when you’re attacked, and we got attacked.” He called the American proposal “a bad deal” outright, and added a line worth sitting with on its own — “we recognize that sometimes, its signature is written in pencil” — which is about as direct as a sitting prime minister gets toward accusing a treaty partner of negotiating in bad faith.

The actual reason for the collapse turns out to be more specific than Friday night’s “unfair, uneconomic” language let on. Carney said American negotiators pushed, at the last minute, to roll back Canada’s protections for French-language and Canadian content in streaming services, subsidies for publishing and film, and mandatory French-language labeling — and that this, not the metals or auto numbers, was what actually ended the talks. “Threats on French language and culture and Canadian culture,” he said, “would never be acceptable.” He also said Washington wanted Canada to align its own tariff policy toward third countries with American tariffs — which he called “a power play” and “a question of sovereignty” rather than a trade demand at all. Read against this series’ running thesis, that’s a notably different kind of ask than dairy quotas or liquor shelves: it isn’t a grievance about Canadian trade practice, it’s a demand that Canada stop having an independent foreign economic policy. That fits the dominance frame this series has tracked since Part XXI more precisely than almost anything before it.

The retaliation now has real shape. Canada’s countertariffs take effect September 8, aimed at steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics, alongside a promised 25 billion Canadian dollars in assistance for affected domestic companies. Trevor Tombe, a trade economist at the University of Calgary, estimates the new American tariffs alone could cost Canada upward of 90,000 jobs — a number that gives the “dollar for dollar” pledge some real weight, and some real risk, since Carney himself acknowledged Canada is retaliating “reluctantly,” aware it will raise costs at home too.

Two things worth flagging rather than resolving. First, Doug Ford has been pushing Carney to weaponize Canadian oil, gas, and electricity exports — Carney has resisted, citing Canada’s reputation for reliability, but on Saturday he came closer than before to floating it as a live option: “I don’t think they want us to stop sending it.” That’s not a threat. It’s an acknowledgment that the option exists and that Washington knows it. Second, Greer told Fox News Saturday morning that the U.S. was “moving forward with measures that respond to Canadian retaliation” — meaning Washington is already signaling a further round before Canada’s own retaliation has even taken effect.

This series isn’t arguing Canada should hold its retaliation back over that signal. But it’s worth remembering how the same signal played out the last time this administration faced a trading partner willing to match it rather than fold. When China answered April 2025’s tariffs with tariffs of its own, Washington didn’t treat the response as a bargaining move to be met in kind — it escalated, and kept escalating, until American tariffs on Chinese goods hit 145 percent and Chinese tariffs on American goods hit 125 percent, with an estimated $600 billion in bilateral trade grinding to a halt and roughly $5 trillion wiped off U.S. equity markets in the process. That spiral only broke because both sides eventually needed it to — the Geneva talks that May brought tariffs down to 30 and 10 percent, not because either government had a change of heart, but because the standstill had become too expensive for both of them to sustain. The lesson isn’t that retaliation doesn’t work. It’s that this particular opponent doesn’t de-escalate on its own; it escalates until the cost becomes unbearable for both sides, and only then negotiates back down from a worse starting point than either side began at. Canada retaliating “dollar for dollar” is the right instinct. Whether Ottawa is prepared for what “further measures” might actually mean before cooler heads have any reason to prevail is the open question Greer’s comment leaves sitting on the table.

Whatever this week settled, it didn’t settle the cycle. The new American tariffs cover only a fraction of the $382 billion in goods Canada sold the U.S. last year, and pointedly exclude oil, gas, electricity, and potash — the exports Washington still can’t afford to lose, tariffs or no tariffs. That detail alone says as much about the real balance of dependency here as anything either government has said aloud.
