The renaming was reversed in February. The only thing required to restore the library’s name was the removal of a tarp. By May, the tarp was still there. The board said the matter was under review.

The Elsinore Public Library has been the Elsinore Public Library since 1923.

The building was funded in part by a Carnegie grant, like a great many small Ontario libraries, and bears above its entrance, carved into the stone lintel, the words ELSINORE PUBLIC LIBRARY, which have been there for a hundred and three years and have required, in that time, no alteration, because the library has been the public library that whole time and the carving has remained accurate.

In February, the library board voted to rename it.

The vote followed a donation.

A local developer — not Bradley Fenn, though Bradley supported the proposal; a man named Garrett Voss who had made a considerable amount of money building subdivisions on the edge of town and who had recently turned his attention to what he described as “giving back” — had offered the library a substantial gift toward its renovation fund. The gift was generous. The renovation was genuinely needed. The roof, in particular, had reached the stage of needing rather than wanting.

The gift came with a condition. The condition was that the library be renamed the Garrett Voss Family Library.

The board, which had a roof to fix and a donor offering to fix it, voted in favour. The vote was five to two. The two were the chief librarian, who noted that the building had been the public library for a hundred and three years and that the public had a stake in its name that predated any donor, and a board member named Frances Okafor, a retired teacher, who said that she did not believe a library funded by the public and named for the public should be renamed for a private individual in exchange for money, however generous, and that if it could be, then the name of every public thing in Elsinore was for sale and only the price remained to be discovered.

The board overruled them. The new lettering was ordered. It read: THE GARRETT VOSS FAMILY LIBRARY.

The chief librarian did not let it go.

She consulted the library’s founding documents, which she retrieved with Gerald’s assistance — Gerald had a folder, of course, labelled Elsinore Public Library — Founding — Carnegie Grant — 1923 — and which established that the library had been incorporated under a provincial statute that named it the Elsinore Public Library and that specified that the name could be altered only by a process involving public consultation and a vote of council, not by a vote of the library board alone.

The board, in other words, had renamed something it did not have the authority to rename.

The chief librarian brought this to council. Frances Okafor supported her. Constance Hay, who was not on the board or the council but who had used the library since 1952 and who regarded the question of its name as a question she had standing to address, attended the council meeting and said: “The library belongs to the public. The founding statute says so. A board may accept a gift. A board may not sell a name the public was given and the board was only asked to keep.”

Council reviewed the founding statute. The statute was clear. Council determined that the board had exceeded its authority, that the renaming was invalid, and that the library was, as it had always been, the Elsinore Public Library.

The donor’s gift, it should be noted, was not contingent on the renaming in any legally enforceable way — the condition had been a request the board had chosen to grant, not a binding term — and the developer, when the renaming was reversed, expressed disappointment but did not withdraw the gift, because the gift had always been, in part, about being seen to give, and being seen to give does not require the name to stick. It requires the giving to be known. The giving was known. The roof would be fixed.

This should have been the end of it.

It was not the end of it.

The new lettering had not yet been installed when the reversal came, but the old lettering — ELSINORE PUBLIC LIBRARY, carved into the stone since 1923 — had been covered, in anticipation of the change, by a contractor who had erected scaffolding and hung a tarp over the lintel in preparation for installing the donor’s name.

When the renaming was reversed, the carved original name was still there, under the tarp, where it had been for a hundred and three years. All that was required was to remove the tarp.

The tarp was not removed.

It stayed up. It stayed up through March. It stayed up through April. The scaffolding stayed with it. The carved words ELSINORE PUBLIC LIBRARY remained hidden behind a grey tarpaulin pulled tight against the stone, visible to no one, while the library beneath operated normally, lending books, hosting the Thursday children’s reading hour, being in every functional respect the public library it had always been, with its actual name concealed behind a tarp that no longer had any installation to prepare for.

When asked about the tarp, the board said the matter was “under review.”

When asked what was under review, the board said the situation was “being assessed.”

When asked who was assessing it, the board said the appropriate parties were involved.

The tarp stayed up.

Frances Okafor raised the tarp at the May council meeting.

She noted that the renaming had been reversed in February. She noted that the only thing required to restore the library’s name to public view was the removal of a tarp. She noted that it was now May. She asked, directly, why the tarp remained.

The board’s representative said the removal involved scaffolding logistics and contractor scheduling and would be addressed in due course.

Frances Okafor said the scaffolding had been erected in a single afternoon and could presumably be removed in one.

The representative said these things were more complicated than they appeared.

Frances Okafor said they were not. She said the tarp was hiding a name the public owned, that the name was correct and carved and a hundred and three years old, that the only reason to keep it hidden was that someone preferred not to see the reversal made visible, and that keeping a tarp over the public’s library because a private renaming had failed was, in her assessment, “petulance with a permit.”

She used that phrase. Petulance with a permit. Norman printed it. It is now, in Elsinore, the phrase people use for a particular kind of behaviour, and it has outlived the tarp, which is the fate of a good phrase.

The tarp came down nine days after Constance Hay wrote three words to the Beacon: Take it down. Behind it, the name carved in 1923. A tarp is not an argument. It is a delay pretending to be a process.

The tarp came down in June.

It came down after Constance Hay wrote a letter to the Beacon — not a long letter; Constance does not write long letters — which Norman published, and which said, in its entirety:

The library has been the Elsinore Public Library since 1923. Its name is carved in stone above the door. It is still carved there. It has been carved there throughout. The only thing standing between the public and its library’s name is a tarp, and a tarp is not an argument. It is a delay pretending to be a process. Take it down. — Constance Hay

The tarp came down nine days after the letter ran.

Behind it: ELSINORE PUBLIC LIBRARY, carved in 1923, exactly as it had always been, weathered and correct and entirely unbothered by the tarp that had hidden it, the way the truth is generally unbothered by the things that hide it, requiring only that the things be removed.

The scaffolding came down the same day.

Garrett Voss’s gift fixed the roof. A plaque inside the entrance — a modest plaque, of the kind that has acknowledged donors in libraries for as long as there have been donors and libraries — notes his contribution to the renovation. The plaque is accurate. The plaque is appropriate. The plaque does not require anyone to pretend the library is named after him, because it is not, because the library is named after the public, because the public was given it and the public kept it.

Frances Okafor was re-elected to the library board in the autumn, at the top of the poll.

A Note for the Record

A name carved in stone is a kind of promise. It says: this thing is what it says it is, and will remain so, and the carving is the community’s way of making the permanence visible. You can change a carved name — names do change, legitimately, through the processes that legitimately change them — but you cannot change it by simply deciding to, and you certainly cannot un-change it back by hanging a tarp over the original and refusing to take the tarp down.

To the south, the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts — named by Congress in 1964, two months after Kennedy’s assassination — was renamed by a board that had been reconstituted to include the president as its chairman. A federal judge ruled that the board lacked the authority to rename it, that Congress had given the Center its name and only Congress could change it, and ordered the name restored. The president’s name was removed from the facade.

And then a tarp went up. A tarp and scaffolding, covering the restored original name, hiding from the public the very restoration the court had ordered. The tarp stayed up for weeks. A federal judge had to issue an order demanding to know the purpose and status of the tarp. The plaintiff accused the defendants of “still hanging a tarp over the Kennedy Center’s restored façade in petulant defiance.”

Petulance with a permit. The phrase travels.

The tarp hid a thing that was already settled — the name restored, the carving there, the court having ruled — from a public that had the right to see the settlement made visible. Its only function was to spare the feelings of the person who lost, by denying everyone else the sight of the loss.

In Elsinore, the tarp came down nine days after Constance Hay wrote her letter. Behind it, the library’s name, carved in 1923, exactly as it had always been.

The Kennedy Center’s tarp is, as of this writing, a matter still before a federal judge.

The carving beneath it has not changed. It says what Congress carved. It is waiting, as carved names wait, with the patience of stone, for the tarp to come down.

It will come down. Tarps do. The question is only how long the petulance lasts, and who has to write the letter, and whether the person who writes it has, as Constance Hay has, the standing that comes from having used the library since 1952 and the brevity that comes from knowing exactly what needs to be said.

Take it down.

Three words. They worked in Elsinore.
