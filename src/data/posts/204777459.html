This summer the United States turned two hundred and fifty years old, and the birthday arrived in a furnace.

I have been writing, in a companion series, about this anniversary — about what the American experiment has meant, about the gap between the promise of 1776 and the performance of 2026, about a republic taking stock of itself at an age few republics reach. I did not, when I began that series, expect it to collide with this one. But the collision is unavoidable, because the semiquincentennial is happening in the same weeks as the heat waves that opened these essays, and there is something almost unbearably apt about a nation conceived in the temperate eighteenth century blowing out two hundred and fifty candles in the hottest summers its citizens have ever lived through. The candles, one might say, are hardly necessary. The air will do.

Let me indulge the historical imagination for a moment, because I think it clarifies the stakes. When the men in Philadelphia signed their names in the summer of 1776 — and it was a hot summer, famously, the delegates sweltering in a closed room with the windows shut against the flies — they were writing for a world of profound climatic stability. The seasons they knew were the seasons their grandparents had known and the seasons they assumed their great-grandchildren would inherit. The whole edifice of the republic they designed — the agriculture, the geography, the slow rhythms of a continental nation — rested on an unspoken assumption so deep they never thought to write it down: that the natural world would hold still. The climate was the one thing not up for debate at the Constitutional Convention, because it had never occurred to anyone that climate was the sort of thing that could change. It was the floor beneath the entire structure, and floors do not need defending. Until they do.

Two hundred and fifty years later, the floor is moving, and the republic built upon it is the one I described in the essay on beautiful clean coal: the most powerful nation on Earth, possessed of every resource needed to meet this moment, choosing instead to insist that the moment is not happening. There is a terrible symmetry to it. The country that was the great political innovation of the eighteenth century — the bold bet that people could govern themselves, that the future could be better than the past, that reason could build something new — has become, on this one civilizational question, the great refuser, the nation digging in to defend the old energy, the old certainties, the old house. The America of 1776 was the future arriving. The America of 2026, on climate, is the past entrenching.

And yet I do not want to write the United States off, partly because writing off the United States has historically been a fool’s wager and partly because the same anniversary that exposes the failure also contains the resource for its repair. Here is the thing about that founding generation that the current refusal forgets: they were not conservatives in the matter of physical reality. They were the opposite. They were enthusiasts of science, of invention, of the new. Franklin flew his kite into the storm and pulled the lightning out of the sky to prove what it was, then declined to patent the more efficient stove he invented, on the principle that useful knowledge should be given freely to all. He proposed instruments for measuring humidity and corresponded with the learned societies of two continents. The man on the hundred-dollar bill, the patron saint of American commerce, was first and most enduringly a scientist, and he would have regarded the deliberate refusal to look at a measurement as a kind of intellectual cowardice unworthy of a free people.

But it is Jefferson who furnishes the detail that ought to shame the current denial most precisely, because Jefferson was, quite literally, one of the first systematic climate observers in American history. For fifty years — from July 1776, the very month of the Declaration, until June 1826, a matter of days before his death — he kept a near-continuous record of the weather, accumulating something on the order of eighteen thousand individual observations at ninety-nine locations: temperature at sunrise and again in mid-afternoon, barometric pressure, wind direction and force, rainfall, the first appearance of the spring birds, the first peas and strawberries of summer. He called these “the indexes of climate,” and he gathered them because he believed meteorology was the science that had made the least progress in his lifetime and that a self-governing people had a duty to understand the natural world it inhabited. He planned to place a thermometer with a trusted observer in every county of Virginia. He instructed Lewis and Clark, in their official commission, to record the climate of the continent they crossed “as characterized by the thermometer.” He tracked the temperature of his own wine cellar and icehouse. This was not a hobby; it was a conviction — that the patient accumulation of honest data was the foundation of knowledge, and knowledge the foundation of liberty.

These were people who, confronted with a changing material reality, would have wanted to measure it, understand it, and engineer their way through it. The notion that loyalty to the founders requires denying the science is not merely wrong; it is precisely backward. The founders were the data-keepers, the inventors, the men who would have read the temperature record and believed it because they took the trouble to keep one. There is no version of Thomas Jefferson — the man who logged eighteen thousand weather readings by hand — who looks at the modern temperature record, the most exhaustively documented dataset in the history of science, and calls it a hoax. He would have been transfixed by it. He would have wanted the raw numbers. He would have believed them, because believing the numbers you have honestly gathered is the entire creed by which he lived.

So the 250th summer poses a question that is really the question of the whole series, addressed to the country where it matters most. The United States is, right now, a house built for winter — built, more precisely, for the temperate and stable climate of its founding, and now sweltering in a summer its founders could not have imagined. It can respond as it has been responding, by boarding up the windows, praising the furnace, and insisting that the heat is a Chinese hoax or a scam or a passing thing. Or it can respond the way the founders actually would have — by keeping the records, believing the measurements, and bringing the full force of American ingenuity to bear on the problem of building something fit for the world as it now is. Both responses are, in their way, deeply American. The denial and the can-do are twins, born of the same restless confidence. The tragedy of this moment is that the country has, for now, chosen the version of confidence that consists of refusing to look.

There is a personal dimension to my writing this, and I will own it, as I have tried to own everything in this series. I am a Canadian, and Canadians have spent two hundred and fifty years defining ourselves in the long shadow of the American experiment — admiring it, resenting it, fearing it, learning from it, sheltering under it and chafing at the shelter. I have been, in these pages, a relentless critic of what the United States has lately become, and I will not pretend the criticism away on the occasion of the birthday. But criticism is not the same as contempt, and I find that what I feel watching the 250th summer is not triumph but something closer to grief — the grief of watching a magnificent and consequential thing, a thing that genuinely changed the human story for the better in ways I will spend my America at 250 series detailing, refuse the one challenge that could prove its founding faith all over again. The climate crisis is the kind of problem the United States was, in principle, built to solve: continental in scale, demanding of innovation, requiring the mobilization of a free people toward a common future. That it is instead the problem the United States has chosen to deny is the deepest disappointment I have, and I have many.

And it is a disappointment sharpened by how recently the opposite was true, because the American refusal is not some ancient condition but a very fresh choice, made almost within living memory of the leadership that made the other one. It was American science, funded by the American government, that discovered the problem in the first place: it was a scientist at NASA, testifying to the American Congress in the sweltering summer of 1988, who told the world in plain language that the greenhouse warming had begun and was caused by human activity. It was American laboratories that built the climate models, American satellites that took the measurements, American universities that trained much of the world’s climate expertise. For a stretch of decades the United States was not the laggard but the instrument of understanding — the nation that saw the thing coming and taught everyone else to see it. The infrastructure of global climate knowledge is, to a remarkable degree, an American creation, the direct descendant of Jefferson’s thermometers and Franklin’s instruments, the same restless empirical genius carried forward into the age of the supercomputer. The tragedy is not that America never understood. The tragedy is that America understood first, and best, and then, having handed the world the knowledge, turned around and declared the knowledge a hoax. You cannot un-ring the bell your own scientists rang. The refusal is not ignorance; it is denial in the strict and clinical sense — a turning away from something known, by the very nation that did the most to know it.

The birthday came and went. There were fireworks, and they went up into air thick with the heat of a changed world, and perhaps, here and there, into the haze of Canadian smoke. The republic turned two hundred and fifty, and the question its founders bequeathed it — can a people govern themselves wisely enough to secure the future? — hung over the celebrations in a form those founders never anticipated and would, I think, have found entirely worthy of their attention. They kept the records. The least their heirs could do is read them.

There is a final symmetry I cannot resist, because it binds the whole essay together. Thomas Jefferson made the last entry in his fifty-year weather diary in June of 1826. He died, as every American schoolchild once learned, on the Fourth of July, 1826 — the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration he had written, expiring within hours of John Adams on the very same day, as though the two old revolutionaries had timed their exits to the calendar of the republic. Jefferson kept his climate record almost literally to his dying breath. Now the republic he helped found arrives at its two hundred and fiftieth Fourth of July, and the heirs of the man who would not stop measuring the weather have, in their highest offices, decided to stop believing it. Jefferson logged the temperature until the week he died. His country, on its semiquincentennial, has resolved not to look at the thermometer at all. I cannot think of a sadder distance to have traveled in two hundred and fifty years, nor a clearer measure of how far the house has drifted from the people who built it.

Degrees

1776 — the year of American independence, declared in a sweltering closed room in Philadelphia, in a climate its authors assumed would hold still forever. Two hundred and fifty years later the assumption is gone, and the republic faces, in the heat, a test of exactly the kind of self-government its founders gambled a nation on. The candles were lit. So, increasingly, is everything else.
