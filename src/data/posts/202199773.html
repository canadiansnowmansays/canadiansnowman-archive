The original 1812 Gerry-mander: Elbridge Gerry’s Massachusetts senatorial district, redrawn into a salamander shape. A Boston newspaper added the head, wings, and claws — and gave a name to a 214-year-old beast that has never become extinct.

Before Elbridge Gerry became a footnote, he was a governor. Before he was a governor, he was a Founding Father — a signer of the Declaration of Independence, a delegate to the Constitutional Convention, a man who had helped build the republic whose institutions he would later bend. In 1812, as governor of Massachusetts, he signed a bill redrawing the state’s senatorial districts into shapes so contorted that one of them resembled a salamander. A Boston newspaper added wings, claws, and a head, and called it a Gerrymander.

The United States Gazette issued a prophecy shortly after. Gerrymandering, the paper wrote, “like the word mammoth, will probably be familiarly understood long after the filthy beast to which it is applied, shall become extinct.”

It was an excellent prediction, except for the last part. The beast is not extinct. In 2026, as Jake Lundberg reported in The Atlantic, it is breeding.

Elbridge Gerry, signer of the Declaration of Independence and governor of Massachusetts. In 1812 he signed the bill that birthed the gerrymander — an act that outlived him by more than two centuries.

The Oldest Trick

The gerrymander predates the party system it has always served. James Madison helped write the Constitution and then watched his fellow Virginian Patrick Henry try to district him out of a congressional seat before the new government had held its first session. The ink on the ratification was barely dry. The manipulation was already underway.

This series has spent thirty-four instalments documenting the redistricting death spiral that followed the Supreme Court’s Louisiana v. Callais ruling in April — Tennessee eliminating its lone majority-Black district within a week, Louisiana signing Cleo Fields’s seat out of existence, Florida shifting four Democratic districts toward Republicans, Alabama caught in a legal loop that may have been resolved by an election calendar before the Supreme Court could act. Six states in five weeks. The machinery moved with extraordinary speed.

What Lundberg’s historical survey makes clear is that the machinery has always moved this fast when the people operating it wanted it to. The speed is not new. The scale is not new. What is new is the convergence — the simultaneous operation of the machinery in so many states at once, enabled by a Supreme Court ruling that removed the last significant legal friction.

Contemporary congressional districts can be as contorted as the original Gerry-mander. Post-Louisiana v. Callais 2026, states moved with historic speed to redraw maps and eliminate competitive or majority-minority seats

The Reform That Always Becomes the Next Weapon

In 1842, the Whig Party passed the Apportionment Act, mandating geographically contiguous, single-member congressional districts. The measure was designed to eliminate the general-ticket system, which allowed a majority party to sweep a state’s entire congressional delegation. It was also, the Whigs calculated, a way to preserve their own power, because the states using the general ticket leaned Democratic.

The reform worked, in the narrow sense. The general ticket was eliminated. But the single-member district it mandated became the instrument through which every subsequent gerrymander has operated. The Whigs solved their problem and handed their successors a better tool for a different problem.

This is the pattern Lundberg traces across two centuries, and it is the pattern this series has documented in real time. Louisiana abandoned its jungle primary — the open, all-candidates-on-one-ballot system — in 2024, reverting to a closed partisan primary that filtered out the independent voters who might have softened Bill Cassidy’s defeat. The jungle primary had itself been a reform, designed to produce more moderate outcomes. It was discarded the moment it stopped serving the party that had adopted it.

Ranked-choice voting had bipartisan support until Maine adopted it and Democrats started winning. Open primaries face resistance from both parties because the model challenges their dominance. In California, top Democrats have never loved the state’s voter-approved nonpartisan primary — and are now moving to eliminate it after facing the risk of being shut out of the governor’s runoff.

The trap, as Part XXXI of this series documented, is structural: any reform that would reduce the power of the two parties requires the two parties to enact it. Any reform one party embraces becomes, by that act, a partisan proposal the other party must oppose. Yesterday’s victims of gerrymandering become, reliably, today’s gerrymanderers.

The filthy beast, alive and well. From 1812 to 2026, every reform meant to slay gerrymandering has been absorbed and turned into a more effective weapon by the next party in power.

Stoughton Cooley’s Lonely Shout

In 1892, a little-known writer named Stoughton Cooley published two essays in The Atlantic that read, in 2026, less like history than like dispatches from a parallel present.

Cooley had watched Ohio be gerrymandered and re-gerrymandered six times in fourteen years. He had observed the long-standing single-party rule in states like Kansas. In “Legal Disfranchisement,” he traced the mechanism by which districting does not merely produce unfair representation but corrodes the entire political culture. Voters in minority parties abandon the process altogether. Political leaders become aligned with the narrow interests of their parties rather than the people they nominally represent. “A premium is put upon mediocrity,” Cooley wrote. “A reward is offered for dishonesty.”

The diagnosis is 134 years old. It describes Louisiana in 2026 with clinical precision. It describes the voters this series has quoted from diners and parking lots and church pews — the truck driver in Port Allen who said they must fear the vote or they would not try so hard to suppress it, and the fast-food manager in West Baton Rouge who said he had stopped paying attention because nothing ever followed through.

The Gerry-mander, 214 years later. The beast that Elbridge Gerry unleashed in 1812 has never become extinct — but democracy is still fighting back

Cooley’s prescription, in “The Slaying of the Gerrymander,” was proportional representation — parties receiving seats according to their share of the statewide vote, districts eliminated altogether. The result, he argued, would be representative government defined by a genuine contest of ideas and energized by broader participation.

Part XXXI of this series introduced Lee Drutman, who made the same argument in 2026 and called Callais a potential hinge point. Kamala Harris mentioned multimember districts in an interview last week as part of what she called a “no-bad-ideas brainstorm.” Cooley made the case in 1892. Drutman is making it now. The argument has not changed. The political conditions required to enact it have not yet arrived in either century.

The Détente That Was Not

Lundberg identifies what appears to be a long period of gerrymandering restraint in the early twentieth century and explains why it was not restraint at all. In the Jim Crow South, where most Black Americans could not vote and Democratic rule was a given, there was little pressure to redraw maps. The system was unfair enough without them. Many states outside the South froze their maps entirely, allowing rural and small-town representatives to maintain their advantages as populations shifted toward cities and suburbs. By the early 1960s, a House district in Atlanta contained more than 820,000 people. A rural district in the same state sent a representative to Congress on behalf of fewer than 275,000.

The Voting Rights Act and the Supreme Court’s 1964 Wesberry decision — mandating equal population districts and establishing “one person, one vote” — broke that frozen system open. States began redistricting after every census. Majority-minority districts were required in the South. The playing field was, for a generation, more level than it had been or would be again.

Callais ended that generation. The requirement for majority-minority districts is gone. The states most affected are the ones where the VRA was written to operate — the Deep South, where, as the New York Times reported, roughly 60 percent of Black Americans now live, a share that has grown in recent years as the Great Migration reverses. The redistricting is hitting the part of the country where Black Americans are increasingly choosing to build their lives. The protection was removed precisely where it was most needed.

Where Democracy Fights Back

The argument for proportional representation — first made by Stoughton Cooley in 1892 and echoed by reformers today — remains the most direct way to slay the beast. Yet the two parties who benefit from the current system have never been willing to enact it.

Every instalment of this series asks the same question.

Cooley asked it in 1892. The United States Gazette asked it in 1812, when it hoped the filthy beast might eventually go extinct. Garret Graves asked it from the inside of the institution last month, when he said he had zero hope that Congress would act.

Lundberg’s survey offers a clarifying answer, though not a comforting one: the beast has never gone extinct because it has never needed to. It adapts. It absorbs reforms and converts them into new instruments. It survives every attempt at slaying because the people who would do the slaying are also the people who benefit most from letting it live.

With as few as 18 genuine toss-up House seats in the coming midterms — in a chamber of 435 — the question of who draws the lines has rarely mattered more. The 45 percent of Americans who identify with neither party are shut out of the primaries that decide those lines. The courts that might intervene are stocked with appointees of the party doing the drawing. The reform movements are fragmented across three tiers that do not speak to each other.

Elbridge Gerry signed that map in 1812 and gave his name to a practice that has outlasted him by more than two centuries. The United States Gazette was right that the word would be familiarly understood long after the beast should have become extinct.

It was wrong about the extinction.

The beast is in its golden age.
