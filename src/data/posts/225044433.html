In September 2025, survivors of Jeffrey Epstein gathered in public for the first time, on the grass outside the United States Capitol, to demand the release of the files this series has followed ever since. One of them, a woman of 37 named Marina Lacerda, spent the morning studying the faces of the other women. Some of them looked familiar. She was trying to find the girl inside each adult face, and the question she kept wanting to ask was not one any rally had been built to hold: did I bring you to him?

Part XLI ended by asking whether the victims, rather than the campaigns, would finally be the people the answers were for. A week later, the New York Times Magazine published a long account by Jen Percy that shows how hard that question is going to be. Percy spent many hours this summer with Lacerda and with women she brought to Epstein’s house, corroborated their accounts with other survivors and checked them against thousands of pages from the files. The piece is about the part of this story that almost nobody in Washington wants to touch. Epstein did not just abuse girls. He made girls bring him other girls, and then the system he left behind decided which of them were victims and which were criminals.

How the ring grew

Lacerda was 13 in 2001, living in Astoria, Queens, the daughter of a Brazilian cleaning woman, working several jobs to help keep her mother and five-year-old sister fed. A slightly older girl from the neighbourhood told her a rich man would pay $300 for a massage. That girl had been brought the same way, by another girl from the neighbourhood. Nobody in this chain was kidnapped off a street. Every one of them was walked in by somebody she knew.

Epstein’s method, as Percy reconstructs it, was patient. He asked about families, schools and siblings. He backed off when a girl refused and came back to the same request weeks later. He found out what each girl lacked, whether money, a parent or a future, and offered to supply it. Then he put a price on new girls. Once a girl was in his orbit, Lacerda says, he told her he got bored easily, and that if she wanted to stay she would have to bring friends. He paid $300 a head.

The arithmetic did the rest. A friend Lacerda met while walking her dog, a 14-year-old who had lost her mother in a car crash and was sleeping on couches, became one of the most prolific recruiters in the network. She found that a new girl drew Epstein’s attention away from her, so she went looking for girls everywhere: nail salons, shopping centres, clubs. She looked for girls with no parents, no job and no money, because she recognised them. She thought she was rescuing them. By her own estimate she did it for about seven years.

This is not a quirk of one network. Asking victims to recruit is how traffickers grow and how they keep control. A girl who has brought her friends has a secret she cannot tell without accusing herself. Epstein sharpened that by setting the girls against one another, praising one and ignoring another, and by telling them what would happen if they talked. One girl, a runaway ballet student, was told that if she went to the police no one would believe her and she would be the one charged with prostitution. Lacerda, who was waiting on a green card, came to feel she needed him to stay in the country. More than a dozen girls came out of a single high school in Palm Beach because friends brought friends. That was the design.

Who the law protected

Now set what happened to the girls beside what happened to the adults.

In May 2006 the Palm Beach police asked for an arrest warrant for Haley Robson, who had been brought to Epstein at 16 by another girl and who then brought him around 20 more, some as young as 14. Percy reports that Robson resisted Epstein, was threatened and harassed, and came to believe she had a choice between recruiting and being abused herself. The police sought charges against her even after she agreed to co-operate. Her name went to the press as a co-conspirator, and the death threats, Percy reports, have never stopped. In 2008 one of the girls she brought sued her.

The year after that warrant request, federal prosecutors in Miami signed the non-prosecution agreement that let Epstein plead to state charges and serve 13 months in a Palm Beach County jail, much of it on work release. The clause this series has returned to again and again reads, in full: “The United States also agrees that it will not institute any criminal charges against any potential co-conspirators of Epstein, including but not limited to Sarah Kellen, Adriana Ross, Lesley Groff, or Nadia Marcinkova.” As Mother Jones reported, Florida lawyers who had worked on the case said they had never seen a clause like it in a non-prosecution agreement. The prosecutor who agreed to the wording later told the Justice Department’s ethics office she had thought it harmless, because she believed Epstein had acted alone, as the Florida Bulldog reported in January. Ghislaine Maxwell later tried to use the same clause to overturn her own conviction.

One of those four names turns up in Percy’s story. When the ballet student, terrified that her mother’s brain tumour would go untreated if she crossed Epstein, wanted to come back to him, it was Epstein’s assistant Lesley Groff she phoned. Groff’s lawyer told the Times that she never knew of any wrongdoing during her employment, and she has never been charged with a crime. She did not need to be. The federal government had already promised, by name, that it would not try.

So the adults on Epstein’s payroll got immunity by name. Anyone else who might have helped him got it without being named at all. The 16-year-old from a Palm Beach high school got a warrant request and her name in the newspaper.

The lawyer who said go home

The pattern held in New York. When FBI agents came to Lacerda’s door in 2008, she was 19, frightened about her immigration status, and her first call was to Epstein. He told her not to call him and said a lawyer would be in touch. The lawyer was paid for by Epstein’s side. When she asked him why she was the only one in trouble, when other girls had brought her and she had brought others, she recalls him telling her that she was simply the one they had found. Months later, she says, he told her she had nothing to worry about and should go home and forget about it. He told Percy he acted in her best interest, never dealt with Epstein directly and does not recall what was said.

What Lacerda did not know was that a 53-page federal indictment had been drafted and shelved. A federal judge later ruled that the deal had broken the Crime Victims’ Rights Act, because the girls had not been told about it. The adults had negotiated with each other. The children were told to forget.

The word

That leaves the word “conspirator”, which has outlived Epstein.

In February, outside the House Oversight Committee’s deposition of Bill Clinton, Representative Anna Paulina Luna of Florida told reporters it was clear Epstein had groomed some victims to bring him others, but that once a victim turned 18 she should no longer be treated as a victim at all. In May, Representative Nancy Mace of South Carolina went further, calling any adult woman who had recruited underage girls a prostitute, a predator and a trafficker. Mace had stood beside survivors at a news conference in February and said in a press release that “these girls were young. They were groomed.” Both things are in the record. Her February words, taken at face value, describe most of the women her May words would prosecute.

The line at 18 sounds tidy. It is not how any of this worked. The women Percy interviewed were brought in at 13, 14 and 15 and kept for years. A girl did not wake up on her 18th birthday freed from the debts, threats, shame and dependence that had been built into her since childhood. Lacerda’s therapist, Randee Kogan, who has worked with Epstein’s victims since the Palm Beach investigation, told Percy that drawing the line there makes no psychological sense. She also told her that one of Epstein’s victims took her own life this year, unable to live with having brought other girls to him.

None of this means the harm done by recruited girls was not real. The women they brought say plainly that it was. One of them told Lacerda that she had been assaulted on her only visit and had never been warned. Another told the woman who brought her that she had lost her childhood because of her. The ballet student, now a choreographer named Danielle Bensky, has described herself as trafficked and has said in the same breath that the girls who brought her were victims too. That is the honest position, and it is the hardest one to say into a microphone: a 15-year-old can do real harm to another child and still have been a child doing what an adult had built her to do. The adults who built her are the ones the word was invented for.

The files cut both ways

This series has spent more than a year arguing for the release of the files. Percy’s piece shows what that release has meant for the people inside them.

The January batch is how Lacerda learned that she had brought Bensky to Epstein. It was in an FBI report. Bensky had known for years and had not been able to tell her. When they met by chance at the Capitol rally in 2025 and Lacerda asked the question, Bensky’s first instinct was to protect her, and she said no.

The same releases exposed two of Lacerda’s friends. Their names appeared unredacted, and strangers have harassed them since. This summer someone threw a rock through one woman’s window. The other saw men photographing her house and moved. Part XLI quoted The Atlantic’s description of the department’s release process as hasty and error-filled. Here is what an error looks like from the other side of the page.

The survivors asked for the files so they could understand what had been done to them, and some of them have learned things they did not know, including about themselves. What they did not ask for was a government careless enough to put their names in front of the internet while it still guards the names of the men around Epstein.

What the new majority should ask

In January, Jamie Raskin takes the gavel at Judiciary and Robert Garcia at Oversight. As Part XLI reported, Raskin’s first priority is the roughly 2.5 million documents the department says it no longer owes Congress. That fight matters. It should not be the only question.

Before any more pages come out, the committees can ask the Justice Department to say on the record how it will treat victims who were minors when they were made to recruit. They can ask what redaction protocol failed, who signed off on it, and what has changed. They can ask whether the “potential co-conspirators” clause is still being treated as binding by anyone in the department, and on whose behalf. And when members of either party reach for the word conspirator, they can be asked which conspirators they mean: the girls on the couches in Queens, or the adults whose names were typed into a federal agreement to keep them out of court.

The sky

When Lacerda first walked into the massage room on East 71st Street, she noticed the ceiling was painted like the sky. As a little girl in Brazil, carsick in her grandparents’ back seat, she was told to lie down and look up, and it always helped. In the years that followed, she told Percy, when she could not make sense of what was happening to her she would look up at that ceiling.

She is 38 now, a year into therapy, with a 13-year-old daughter of her own. Only this September, Percy writes, did she cry about Epstein for the first time. She says it will take everyone a long time to accept that “we all brought each other.”

She is right about the girls. It is time the record was right about the adults. The girls brought each other because a man paid them to. The men around him were protected by name. The children were left to settle the account among themselves.
