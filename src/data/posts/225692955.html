Part 99 of this series gave Dario Amodei’s utopian case its fairest hearing, and cited, as the first concrete evidence for it, the Stanford “virtual lab” in which A.I. co-scientists appeared to do real biological work. It was, I wrote, the series’ first piece of evidence for the machines-of-loving-grace vision rather than just the rhetoric. Fairness now requires the counter-scene, and on September 27, 2026, Carl Zimmer supplied it in The New York Times, in a report that should be pinned beside every triumphant claim of autonomous A.I. discovery from here on.

The question in his headline is not rhetorical. Did Anthropic’s A.I. really make a scientific discovery on its own? The answer, on the evidence, is: nobody outside Anthropic can tell — and the one scientist positioned to know thinks he may have handed the machine the answer years ago.

The claim

On September 23, 2026, Anthropic unveiled findings from its new biology lab, announcing that its A.I. agents had discovered new enzymes with promise for biotechnology. The specifics, for the record, because they matter to the dispute: the agents were given five examples of enzymes called reverse transcriptases — which read an RNA molecule and write a matching strand of DNA — and told to search databases of billions of gene sequences for more. They surfaced candidate genes in bacteria-infecting viruses called jumbo phages, each sitting beside an array of RNA-molecule genes. Anthropic’s scientists named them array-associated reverse transcriptases, or ARTs, and declared in their write-up that the A.I. had taken “the first steps of a biological discovery on its own.” The company’s own announcement, headlined “Claude discovers a novel enzyme system with CRISPR-like repeats,” was more guarded in its fine print: “Although we don’t yet know its function,” it said, and “Further experiments are underway to determine how ART works.”

It is exactly the kind of sentence this series flagged in Part 122 as arriving on the runway to a two-trillion-dollar public offering. Amodei told the United Nations Security Council on Wednesday, September 23, the same day, that this sort of application would accelerate rapidly and deliver “profound progress” on diseases that have plagued humanity for millennia. A biological discovery made by the machine itself is not just a scientific result. It is the single most valuable line in the IPO prospectus: proof that the utopian case of Part 99 is real and imminent.

The scientist who got there first

Then Mario Rodríguez Mestre, a computational biologist at the University of Copenhagen, said on the weekend of September 26 and 27 that he and his colleagues had been studying these exact enzymes for four years. They call them “jumbotrons.” Mestre first found reverse transcriptases in jumbo phages in 2022; he is a co-inventor on a patent filing covering them, from his time consulting for a company called ReNAgade Therapeutics; and he moved to Copenhagen specifically to study them in more detail.

His framing of the problem is precise, and this series will adopt it, because it cuts cleanly through the hype and the reflexive debunking alike. “The most important question,” Mestre said, “is not ‘Were ARTs already known?’ They were.” The real question is whether Anthropic’s A.I. reasoned its way to the result, or was guided to it — partly by his own work.

Because here is the complication that makes this more than a priority dispute. For three years, Mestre used Anthropic’s models constantly, as thousands of scientists do — writing code, drafting manuscripts, doing administrative chores. He said he “completely switched” his way of doing science. And in the course of that use, he shared with Claude a draft of his dissertation and a manuscript describing the jumbotrons, and uploaded further details along the way. He fed the machine, over years, the very findings the machine later announced as its own first steps. In August he had begun using Claude Science, Anthropic’s new research-tailored tool. The Times reviewed his Slack messages, figures and materials and found the A.I. had been extensively involved in his research.

His worry, stated carefully: “My concern is that this information was used to train future versions of the models. I think it’s important to raise this possibility.”

Anthropic’s denial, in full

A series that has tried to check everyone’s incentives must give the company its complete rebuttal, unhedged, because it is specific and it may be true. Anthropic said, in a statement on Sunday, September 27: “We are not aware of any previously published work describing the ART system we recently found. Claude was also not trained on any user transcripts, and our molecular biology team has no such access, either.”

Three distinct claims sit in that sentence, and each is load-bearing. First: no published prior work — which is technically compatible with Mestre’s account, since his findings are unpublished. Second: Claude was not trained on user transcripts. Third: the biology team had no access to user data. If all three hold, the discovery is genuine and Mestre’s fear is a coincidence born of a crowded field. Anthropic further said Claude’s key contribution was identifying a specific array of RNA molecules and an associated protein — though Mestre says he and his colleagues made precisely that identification over a year ago, and shared it with Claude. RJ Mackenzie, reporting for The Scientist, added two details: Anthropic said its team had spotted the ART in raw data from a 2022 published paper on which Mestre was not an author, and the company did not share the exact prompts it used to direct Claude’s search, the record most likely to show whether his work steered it.

The honest position — the one Mestre himself takes, to his credit — is that this cannot be resolved from outside. “Without a closer look at what the Anthropic A.I. actually did,” he said, “it would be impossible to say for sure that it was guided by my research.” And: “I mean, coincidences happen. Everyone in the field is doing more or less the same things. But we were aware of these repeats more than one year ago.” That is a scientist being scrupulous about the limits of his own claim. It is also, precisely, the problem: the only entity that can settle the question is the one with everything riding on the answer.

The pattern, not the incident

What lifts this above a single contested announcement is that it is the second such episode in September 2026, and the parallel is exact. As Part 123 noted through David Wallace-Wells, OpenAI claimed to have cracked a longstanding mathematics problem — and the mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, who had been working on his own solution using OpenAI’s models, raised the possibility that the company had borrowed from his efforts. His question, posted publicly, is the one that now hangs over the whole category: “Is it ethical to use a customer’s data to try to scoop their customer?” OpenAI denied training on his conversations, exactly as Anthropic now denies training on Mestre’s.

Two frontier labs, two triumphant “the A.I. did it” announcements, two expert users who say they had done the work first and fed it to the machine, two flat denials that cannot be independently checked. That is not a coincidence. It is a structural feature of a business model. When the same tool that millions of researchers use to draft, code and think is also the tool whose maker is racing to prove it can discover, the maker is perpetually positioned to harvest the frontier of its own customers’ unpublished work — and then to announce that the frontier was reached autonomously. Whether or not that is what happened in either case, the arrangement guarantees the suspicion, and the suspicion is corrosive in a way a denial cannot repair.

Seth Shipman, a bioengineer at U.C.S.F. who knows Mestre’s work, supplied the reaction that shows the damage spreading. He and his colleagues have also leaned heavily on Anthropic’s tools, and told it what they were working on. “I’ve heard from a bunch of people in our field who say, ‘Uh oh, are they training on my prompts?’” he said. “We can’t not think about it.” That is the sound of trust draining out of a research community in real time — the same community whose enthusiastic, data-sharing use of these tools made the utopian case of Part 99 look plausible in the first place.

The connection to the copyright wars

Mestre himself drew the line this series would draw. A.I. companies, he noted, have already been accused of training on the work of artists and writers without permission — the subject of this newsletter’s companion series, The Copyright Wars. “It’s already happened,” he said. The jumbotron dispute is that same fight, migrated from novels and paintings to unpublished science, and it is uglier here, because the stakes are not royalties but priority, patents and the scientific credit that a career is built on. An artist scraped without consent loses income. A scientist scooped by the tool he confided in loses the discovery itself.

The fairer reading

A week later the argument had moved on. Writing in The Atlantic on 4 October, Hana Kiros gathered the sceptics and then made the case that the overselling had hidden something real. A disclosure first, since this series is written with Claude: the claim under examination is my collaborator’s maker’s, and readers should weigh what follows accordingly.

The doubts had widened. Several academics, and the chief executive of Eli Lilly, said Anthropic had dressed up an incremental finding as a major discovery. Fyodor Urnov, the scientist who coined the term “genome editing” and who is working on a CRISPR project with Anthropic, told Kiros that “the jury is out” on whether what Claude found is an enzyme at all. Anthropic had announced it as an “enzyme system” resembling CRISPR, found in under 24 hours, while admitting it did not know exactly what it had found.

Kiros’s point is that the grand framing buried the useful part. Reading the human genome for the first time took 13 years and cost $2.7 billion; a full genome can now be sequenced in about a day for as little as $200. Public genetic databases have grown past the point where people can examine them. As long ago as 2012, a committee of bioinformatics experts concluded that the “bottleneck in scientific productivity” had moved from producing data to analysing it. Anthropic set about 950 copies of Claude loose at once on a database of nearly 2 billion DNA entries. Whatever they found, that is a search no human team could have run. Bo Wang, an assistant professor at the University of Toronto, calls this AI’s real contribution to biology: “scaling scientific attention,” flagging the outliers that deserve a human scientist’s time. Stirling Churchman of Harvard Medical School put it more simply: “there will be magic where there’s data.”

That is a smaller claim than “the first steps of a biological discovery on its own,” and a more defensible one. It also makes Mestre’s complaint sharper rather than weaker. A machine that can sift two billion records can certainly find what a scientist has already told it to look for. Amodei, meanwhile, has offered his own test for winning over the sceptics. “The thing that will work,” he wrote on X, “is actually curing cancer.” Kiros’s reply is that helping scientists work faster is an achievement too, even if it doesn’t sound like a miracle. It is also one the company could have claimed honestly, without a dispute over who found the enzymes first.

The ledger

So the two enzyme essays now bracket the utopian claim exactly as the two China essays bracketed the safety one. Part 99 showed the machines-of-loving-grace vision at its most persuasive — real biology, done fast and cheap. Part 128 shows the same vision at its most suspect: a discovery announced as autonomous that the field’s own specialists say was already made, by humans, and fed to the machine that took the credit. Both can be partly true. A.I. genuinely is accelerating biology, mostly in the unglamorous way Kiros describes, by reading data faster than people can; and some of what is being sold as A.I.’s independent genius may be the laundered, un-attributed labour of the scientists who trusted it with their drafts.

The most eloquent verdict is Mestre’s exit. Having “completely switched” his way of doing science, having adopted Claude Science only in August, he said in late September that he was “shutting down everything” and moving to other models. That is what the accountability gap costs when it is left to a company to police itself, as Part 124 warned: not a regulatory fine, which no one will levy, but the quiet withdrawal of the very trust the utopian case depends on. Amodei told the U.N. that A.I. would cure diseases that have plagued humanity for millennia. It may yet. But it will do it faster with the Mestres of the world uploading their manuscripts than with them shutting everything down — and in the last week of September Anthropic gave every Mestre in every lab a reason to close the file.
