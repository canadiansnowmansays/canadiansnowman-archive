The raw material of intelligence — legally purchased or pirated? The Copyright Wars begin here.

In June 2025, a federal judge in San Francisco split the baby.

Judge William Alsup of the Northern District of California had before him a question that the artificial intelligence industry had been dreading since the first large language models went public: does training an AI system on copyrighted books, without permission and without payment, constitute copyright infringement? His answer was simultaneously a victory for the AI industry and a warning it could not ignore. Training on legally purchased books, he ruled, is fair use. Downloading seven million books from pirate websites, storing them in a searchable database, and training on those — that is not.

The case was Bartz v. Anthropic. The plaintiffs were three authors — Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson — who had filed a class action in 2023 alleging that Anthropic had built its Claude models, in part, on a digital library assembled through mass piracy. They were right. Anthropic had downloaded millions of books from shadow libraries including Library Genesis and Pirate Library Mirror. It had also purchased books, torn off their bindings, scanned each page, and stored them in what internal documents described as a permanent central library of “all the books in the world.”

Judge Alsup drew the line at the piracy. In September 2025, rather than face a December trial on damages that could have reached the hundreds of billions under statutory copyright law, Anthropic settled. The settlement was $1.5 billion — the largest copyright settlement in American history.

Three thousand dollars per work.

The authors who had not opted out accepted it. Those who had done the arithmetic did not.

Judge William Alsup, Northern District of California. His June 2025 ruling drew the critical line between fair use and piracy.

What The Copyright Wars Is

This is a litigation diary covering the cluster of copyright cases that will determine the legal foundation of the artificial intelligence industry. The anchor case is Bartz v. Anthropic — settled, but not yet finally approved, and generating a wave of follow-on litigation from authors who rejected the settlement as inadequate. The broader docket includes In Re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation in the Southern District of New York, the New York Times v. OpenAI case, music publisher claims against multiple AI companies totalling several billion dollars, and the Disney v. Midjourney image generation suit.

The Copyright Wars is not a legal summary service. What it offers is context — the analytical framework of The Asimov Gap, applied to the specific rulings, settlements, and opt-out decisions emerging from federal courthouses in San Francisco and New York.

Each dispatch will be organized around what the latest proceedings revealed about the underlying question: who owns the intellectual foundation on which a trillion-dollar industry was built, and what remedy, if any, is adequate?

Why These Cases Cannot Be Watched Alone

The Asimov Gap series has documented, now across forty-six parts, the governance vacuum at the heart of the AI era — the gap between what the technology can do and what any democratic institution is empowered to address. The copyright cases are that vacuum rendered in property law.

The AI industry was built on text. Enormous quantities of it — books, articles, websites, academic papers, forum posts, song lyrics, legal filings, and everything else that human beings have written and published and posted since the invention of the internet. The companies that built the large language models did not, for the most part, ask permission. They did not pay. They assumed — correctly, as it turned out in the Alsup ruling for legally purchased material — that training on publicly available text was transformative enough to qualify as fair use under American copyright law.

What Judge Alsup’s ruling established, and what the settlement confirmed, is that the assumption held for legal acquisition but not for piracy. That distinction sounds clean. In practice it is anything but. The same companies that downloaded from LibGen also scraped millions of websites without explicit permission. The legal status of that scraping — different from purchasing, different from piracy, somewhere in a grey zone the courts have not yet fully mapped — is the question that the OpenAI and New York Times litigation will resolve.

The stakes are not symmetrical. For authors, the question is whether the industry that has most disrupted their economic model will be required to compensate them for the raw material that made it possible. For AI companies, the question is whether the legal foundation of their training datasets — assembled over years, at enormous expense, before the copyright questions were litigated — can survive judicial scrutiny. For everyone else, the question is whether copyright law, designed for a world of discrete human-authored works, is the right instrument for governing the training of systems that no single human authored and no single copyright covers.

The Asimov Frame applies here with particular force. Copyright law is prior law — it existed before large language models, and it is being applied to them retrospectively. Whether it is adequate to the task, or whether it will require legislative revision that democratic institutions have so far declined to provide, is the governance question running beneath every courtroom dispatch.

The State of Play

$1.5 billion. $3,000 per work. 91% of authors accepted. The rest went to war.

The Bartz v. Anthropic settlement produced three distinct categories of response, each of which is now generating its own litigation.

The majority of class members — 91.3 percent of eligible works, a claim rate ten times the typical class action average — filed claims and accepted the settlement. The Authors Guild described this as a testament to the breadth of the alleged infringement and the growing awareness among writers that their books had been taken. The actual payout, at roughly $3,000 per work, reflects both the settlement fund’s fixed size and the unexpectedly high claim rate, which compressed individual payments below early projections.

A second group opted out and filed individual suits. Six authors — including two-time Pulitzer Prize winner John Carreyrou — filed individual copyright infringement actions against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, xAI, and Perplexity simultaneously, seeking $150,000 per title per defendant under the Copyright Act’s statutory damages ceiling. Their argument is direct: $3,000 is two percent of the statutory ceiling of $150,000. The settlement protected Anthropic from its maximum exposure. It did not protect authors from their minimum.

A third group filed objections. Seven objectors were given two minutes each at the May 14, 2026 fairness hearing before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín — who took the matter under submission without approving the settlement, and ordered Anthropic to file a supplemental brief addressing whether late opt-outs should be honoured.

Final approval has not yet been granted. Until it is, the settlement is not binding. And the opt-out suits — Cruz v. Anthropic, Kwon v. Anthropic, and the Carreyrou group — are active in parallel.

Meanwhile, the cases against OpenAI are at an earlier stage but potentially larger in scope. The New York Times suit involves not books but journalism — a different legal argument, a different damages theory, and a defendant with a different relationship to the piracy question. The music publisher suits involve a different category of creative work entirely, with different licensing infrastructure and a different history of digital rights enforcement.

The Copyright Wars has many fronts. This diary will cover them all.

The Analytical Framework

The Alsup Distinction. Judge Alsup’s ruling — fair use for legal acquisition, not fair use for piracy — is the load-bearing legal structure on which the entire post-settlement landscape rests. Every follow-on case will be argued in its shadow. Understanding what it established, what it left open, and what it explicitly declined to decide is the analytical foundation of this series.

The Governance Vacuum. Congress has not legislated on AI and copyright. The Copyright Office has issued guidance that carries no binding legal authority. The courts are filling a vacuum that democratic institutions created by inaction. This is not new — copyright law has always evolved through litigation more than legislation — but the pace of AI development has compressed the timeline in ways that leave authors, companies, and courts all improvising simultaneously.

The Valuation Problem. What is a book worth to a large language model? The $3,000 per work figure in the Bartz settlement reflects one answer — derived from negotiation under litigation pressure, not from any principled assessment of the contribution each work made to Claude’s capabilities. The statutory damages ceiling of $150,000 reflects a different answer — derived from a regime designed to deter individual infringers, not to price the wholesale appropriation of an industry’s output. Neither figure is right. The courts are working with instruments designed for a different problem.

The Section 230 Question. The Florida litigation raises a related issue — whether AI-generated content qualifies for the same liability shield as user-posted social media content. One Florida judge has already signalled scepticism. If Section 230 protection does not extend to AI output, the copyright liability question becomes dramatically more complex.

The Canadian Outsider Perspective. Canadian copyright law is not American copyright law. The fair dealing doctrine differs from fair use in ways that matter. Canadian authors whose works were taken face different legal options and different practical prospects. This series will note the Canadian dimension where it is relevant.

The Cast

Authors push back: “The books were taken. The question is what happens next.

Judge William Alsup — U.S. District Judge, Northern District of California. His June 2025 summary judgment ruling is the most consequential judicial decision yet on AI and copyright. The distinction he drew between legal acquisition and piracy is the legal fault line along which all subsequent litigation is being organised. He is not presiding over the Bartz settlement — that moved to Judge Martínez-Olguín — but his ruling governs its terms.

Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín — U.S. District Judge, Northern District of California. Presiding over the Bartz settlement approval. Her decision at the May 14 fairness hearing to take the matter under submission — rather than approving immediately — and her order requiring Anthropic to address late opt-outs signals careful attention to the settlement’s procedural fairness, not merely its dollar amount.

Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, Kirk Wallace Johnson — The three named plaintiffs in Bartz v. Anthropic. Bartz’s explanation for why she stepped forward — she couldn’t come up with a good enough reason not to — is the author community’s relationship to this litigation in miniature. The books were taken. The question was whether to say so in court.

John Carreyrou — Two-time Pulitzer Prize winner, author of Bad Blood, and one of six opt-out authors who filed individual suits against six AI companies simultaneously in December 2025. His presence in the opt-out group signals that the litigation is not over when the settlement is approved — it is simply entering its next phase.

Dave Eggers, Vendela Vida, Angie Cruz — Among the twenty-eight authors in the Cruz v. Anthropic opt-out suit, filed in the Northern District of California in January 2026. Their demand: statutory damages, a jury trial, and a permanent injunction against continued Claude training and deployment on their works. The injunction demand is the most significant element — it asks not for money but for the models to stop.

The Music Publishers — A separate but parallel front. Music publishers have filed multi-billion dollar claims against AI companies for training on lyrics and compositions. The music industry has a longer and more litigated history of digital rights enforcement than book publishing. Its involvement signals that the copyright wars are not a books problem. They are an industry-wide problem.

A Note on Sources

The Copyright Wars will draw on reporting from Publishers Weekly, the Authors Guild, the Authors Alliance, the New York Times, and legal analysis from Norton Rose Fulbright, Kluwer Copyright Blog, and Fieldfisher. Court filings will be cited where available. The Authors Guild’s coverage of the Bartz proceedings has been particularly detailed and will be a primary source throughout.

This diary is written from Toronto. Canadian authors whose works were taken have their own interests in these proceedings, and the Canadian perspective on intellectual property in the AI era is not identical to the American one.

A Final Note on Why This Matters

The Bartz settlement resolved one case. It did not resolve the question.

Judge Alsup’s ruling said that training on legally purchased books is fair use. It did not say that training on scraped websites is fair use. It did not say that training on licensed news content is fair use. It did not say that training on music is fair use. It drew one line, in one case, involving one company’s relationship to one category of pirated material.

Everything else is still being decided.

The opt-out authors who are asking for $150,000 per work per defendant are not being unreasonable. They are using the instruments the law provides. The AI companies that settled for $3,000 per work are not being generous. They are buying the right to continue operating without the uncertainty of a jury verdict.

Neither position is wrong. Both positions are inadequate.

The Copyright Wars will not end in a settlement. They will end in legislation, or they will not end at all — because the underlying question, of who owns the intellectual foundation on which the AI industry was built, is not a question copyright law was designed to answer.

The courts are doing their best with the instruments they have.

This diary will watch them try.
