Part IV closed with a promise: when either court ruled, this diary would return. Both have now ruled, four weeks apart, and in opposite directions. The strange thing, and the thing worth a full instalment, is that they do not actually contradict each other.

On the evening of Thursday, August 27, 2026, four weeks after the July 30 hearing chronicled in Part IV, Judge Rita Lin issued the written judgment that hearing had foreshadowed. Anthropic won nearly everything. On Friday, September 25, 2026, a divided panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit issued the opinion this series has been waiting on since the May 19 oral argument. Anthropic lost.

So the scorecard reads one win, one loss, and a company that is now lawfully welcome almost everywhere in the United States government except the one building where the quarrel began.

To understand how that happened, you have to take the two opinions one at a time. They were answering different questions, under different statutes, and each court was quite careful to say so.

San Francisco: The Blank Check

As FedScoop reported, Judge Lin’s 59-page order sided with Anthropic on its First Amendment, due process and Administrative Procedure Act claims. Its most quotable sentence was also its most carefully balanced. The Department of War, Lin wrote, is “undisputedly free to select the AI vendor of its choice” — but the broad measures imposed on Anthropic were “illegal and baseless.”

That distinction is the spine of the ruling, and it was there from the preliminary injunction onward. Nobody in San Francisco ever argued the Pentagon had to keep buying Claude. The question was whether the government could go further: brand an American company a supply chain risk under 10 U.S.C. § 3252, a statute written with foreign adversaries in mind, order every federal agency to stop using its products, and tell every defence contractor in the country to stop doing business with it.

Lin said no, on three independent grounds. The Presidential Directive, the Hegseth Directive and the designation that followed were unlawful retaliation under the First Amendment. The government had imposed what amounted to a permanent debarment without the notice and hearing the Fifth Amendment requires. And the designation exceeded the Secretary’s authority under § 3252 while also being arbitrary and capricious under the APA. Any one of those would have been enough.

She converted the March preliminary injunction into a permanent one and vacated the designation outright. She also refused the government even a brief administrative stay. Her reasoning was that after more than five months of living under the injunction, the government had not identified any harm it had actually suffered.

Anthropic did not win everything. Lin rejected its broader ultra vires separation-of-powers theory, citing Dalton v. Specter, and entered judgment for the agencies that had taken no action or only interim steps. But the core of the case went Anthropic’s way, and so did the language. National security, Lin held, cannot be used as “a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics.”

What persuaded her was not the law in the abstract but the paper trail. As Loyola Law School’s Simona Grossi observed, the government had more or less confessed the pretext itself. Its internal messages talked about rhetoric and ideology, and one email described the deal as “very close” shortly before the security rationale appeared. It is hard to argue that a vendor posed a grave sabotage risk when your own correspondence shows you were about to sign with it.

Readers of Part IV will recall Lin saying at the July hearing that the record had “gotten worse” for the government. The written judgment is what “worse” looks like once it has been typed up and signed.

One small detail from that period deserves a place in the record. Next year’s defence appropriation bill included language that would prohibit designating a domestic company a supply chain risk for declining contract terms. Congress, in other words, was already drafting the fix for the thing Lin had just struck down. Legislatures do not usually close loopholes nobody has used.

The Interlude: One Administration, Two Voices

Between the two rulings came a month in which the administration managed to hold two positions on Anthropic at once.

On Wednesday, September 2, 2026, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick shared a stage with Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown at the G20 Innovation Ministerial in Chapel Hill, North Carolina. Lutnick then told Bloomberg that Anthropic and the government were “in tune together.” The Pentagon’s chief technology officer, Emil Michael, responded that the company remained designated a supply chain risk.

Both were telling the truth, which was the problem. Lin’s judgment had vacated one designation. A second, under a different statute, was still sitting before the D.C. Circuit. Commerce could be in tune with Anthropic while the Department of War was not, and the courts had given each of them grounds for their view.

Then the harmony broke down. On Saturday, September 12, 2026, Dario Amodei published an essay titled “We Must Pace the Frontier,” calling for the industry to slow the development of frontier models and proposing independent evaluators with ongoing access to Anthropic’s systems. Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly agreed with him. On Monday, September 14, President Trump answered on Truth Social. As Cris Tolomia reported for Quartz, the President wrote that the only guardrail AI needs is a “STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT.” He mocked Amodei as “pretending to be a ‘perfect little angel,’” and added that his administration holds “tremendous CRIMINAL and REGULATORY power over these companies.”

A careful reader of Lin’s judgment will notice that last sentence. The whole of the San Francisco case turned on whether the government had punished a company for what its leaders said in public. Two weeks after she ruled that it had, the President announced, in capital letters, that he possesses the power to do it again.

On Wednesday, September 23, 2026, Amodei spoke virtually to the United Nations Security Council, with Altman in the chamber. He laid out a three-step plan to slow development without surrendering the American lead, delivered in the same week the President used his own UN appearance to denounce a “globalist scheme” to control AI. The following evening, Thursday, September 24, the White House hosted a state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping. As Ashley Capoot reported for CNBC, Amodei was not invited. (Readers of The Washington Opera will know who was.)

The D.C. Circuit handed down its opinion the next morning. The timing was surely coincidence. It did not feel like it.

Washington: The Certainty

The D.C. Circuit case concerned the other designation, made under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act, known as FASCSA. That statute routes challenges directly to the D.C. Circuit, which is why Anthropic had to fight on two coasts at once. The panel was the same one that heard argument on May 19: Judges Karen LeCraft Henderson, Gregory Katsas and Neomi Rao. The vote was 2–1, with Katsas writing for himself and Rao, both Trump appointees, and Henderson, a George H.W. Bush appointee, dissenting.

Part II of this series recorded Henderson calling the blacklisting “a spectacular overreach” from the bench. It turned out she was speaking only for herself.

Katsas’s majority opinion held that the Department had “ample support” for concluding that keeping Claude integrated in its systems, directly or through contractors, posed a national security risk covered by the statute. The heart of his reasoning is worth setting out fairly, because it is more interesting than the Pentagon’s victory posts suggest.

The argument runs like this. Anthropic openly builds restrictions into Claude so that the model will not perform certain tasks. As Courthouse News Service reported, Katsas noted that on more than one occasion those restrictions had in fact stopped Claude from completing tasks requested by government users. A supplier that can, by design, prevent its product from performing functions the customer considers necessary is, in the majority’s reading, a supplier whose product can be made not to work. And FASCSA, unlike § 3252, does not require the government to show any bad motive.

That last point is how the D.C. Circuit squared its ruling with Lin’s. The statute in the California case speaks of an “adversary,” a word carrying implications of malice or subversion. FASCSA applies to “any person” and defines supply chain risk without reference to intent. The majority said, in so many words, that it had no reason to doubt Anthropic acts with noble intentions. It simply held that under this statute, intentions are beside the point. What Anthropic does matters more than why. Given Claude’s restrictions, Katsas reasoned, the risk that it might decline a task was not a mere possibility but a certainty.

He also took seriously two concerns Secretary Hegseth had raised: that an overly constrained model might shut down unexpectedly, and that Claude might be subject to manipulation. Anthropic disputed both. Katsas held that weighing those risks was not the court’s job. In our Republic, he wrote, “it is the President and the Secretary of War who must determine” how to balance them. The majority also rejected Anthropic’s First Amendment and due process claims on this record.

There is a kind of logic here that deserves acknowledgement even from people who dislike the result. A military customer that cannot be sure its software will do what it is told has a real problem, and it is not obviously a court’s place to tell the Pentagon how much uncertainty to tolerate in wartime. That is the strongest version of the government’s case, and two federal judges accepted it.

The difficulty is what the logic proves. Every piece of commercial software ships with terms of use. Every serious AI model ships with things it will not do. If building restrictions into a product is itself a supply chain risk, the category has no natural edge.

The Dissent

Henderson saw that edge, or rather the absence of one, and her dissent is built around it.

She argued that the panel should have read “supply chain risk” narrowly, consistent with why Congress passed FASCSA in the first place. The law followed warnings that hostile states and other bad actors were getting into federal systems through their suppliers. It was not written to settle contract disputes with American companies that tell you, in advance and in writing, what their product will and will not do. She wrote that the statute does not treat “a contractor’s honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions” as the kind of risk that permits blacklisting.

Then she offered a hypothetical, of the sort Judge Lin had pressed on DOJ in July. The Department, Henderson observed, did exactly what it had threatened: it designated Anthropic after Anthropic declined the Secretary’s ultimatum to replace its use restrictions with an “all lawful uses” clause. Now suppose, she wrote, the Secretary tells Anthropic’s replacement to permit whatever functions the Department deems necessary — or “share the same fate as Anthropic.”

That is the question the majority’s reasoning leaves open. Once refusal becomes risk, every AI vendor to the United States military knows the price of a red line. The lesson of the D.C. Circuit’s opinion, for any company watching, is not to decline a request from this Department in writing.

Two Opinions That Don’t Contradict Each Other

The Pentagon did not wait to read the dissent. Emil Michael posted that “the hammer of justice” had smashed Anthropic’s arguments. Spokesman Sean Parnell said the ruling “completely validates the Department’s position.” It does not, quite. It validates one of the Department’s two positions and leaves standing a federal judgment holding that the other was unconstitutional retaliation.

The practical map is now oddly precise. Claude remains barred inside the Pentagon, while Lin’s ruling permits other agencies to keep using it. The government-wide ban is gone, and so is the sweeping boycott that would have forced every defence contractor to drop Anthropic even for unrelated work. The narrower FASCSA exclusion covering Pentagon work remains. Meanwhile, Anthropic was left out when seven major technology firms, including Google, OpenAI, Microsoft and Amazon Web Services, struck deals to bring their tools onto the Pentagon’s systems.

Seen together, the two opinions describe a single event from two angles. San Francisco examined why the government acted and found retaliation. Washington examined what Claude does and found a statute broad enough that why no longer mattered. Both can be right in their own terms. That is the uncomfortable part: the government lost the argument about its motives and won anyway, because it had a second statute that never asked about motives.

What Comes Next

Nothing is final yet. The D.C. Circuit panel delayed its decision from taking effect so Anthropic can petition the same three judges for rehearing or ask the full D.C. Circuit to hear the case en banc. Anthropic could also go to the Supreme Court. Its spokesperson told CNBC that the company respectfully disagrees and is “considering all options, including further review.”

The California side has its own clock. The government’s appeal of Lin’s March preliminary injunction has been sitting in the Ninth Circuit, held in abeyance at the government’s own request pending the D.C. Circuit outcome. The final judgment has most likely overtaken that appeal, meaning the government must file a fresh notice of appeal within 60 days of August 27. That window closes on Monday, October 26, 2026. Now that it has a win in Washington, the government has every reason to use it.

And there is one date nearer than any of these: Wednesday, September 30, 2026, the wind-down deadline DOJ gave Judge Lin in July, now five days after the D.C. Circuit’s ruling. Part IV ended with the government’s lawyer asking for a week to explain why the NSA was expanding its use of Mythos while the Pentagon called Anthropic a security risk. Neither Lin’s judgment nor the D.C. Circuit’s opinion, as reported, resolves that contradiction. What the government actually does on September 30 will say more about its real view of the “risk” than either opinion does.

If a split between the circuits on the constitutional questions develops, the Supreme Court petition writes itself. If it doesn’t, Anthropic may end up with the strangest result of all: a company a federal court says was punished for its speech, and another federal court says may lawfully stay punished.

A note, since this series discloses its collaboration openly. This essay was drafted with Claude, the model whose built-in restrictions the D.C. Circuit has now held to be a supply chain risk. It declined nothing in the writing of it. Whether that reassures the Secretary of War is, as Judge Katsas would say, for the Secretary to determine.
