Stephen Gordon May 2026

On May 6, 2026, the White House released the official 2026 United States Counterterrorism Strategy.

The document had been promised for nearly a year. Its author, Sebastian Gorka — deputy assistant to the president and senior director for counterterrorism — had described it as “imminent” in the summer of 2025. He said he was “on the cusp” of releasing it in the fall. He repeated the phrase in October. He repeated it again in January 2026.

On May 4, ProPublica reporter Hannah Allam published a follow-up to her earlier investigation, noting that “exactly two months into the Iran war, Gorka’s counterterrorism strategy has yet to appear.” When she had reached out to Gorka for comment, he refused and posted on X that she was an “anti-American hack” whose “putrid piece of hackery” she should go ahead and write.

Two days later, the strategy appeared.

The United States’ official wartime counterterrorism strategy exists because a journalist embarrassed its author into producing it. This is the first thing to understand about the document. The second is what the document actually says.

Sebastian Gorka, Deputy Assistant to the President and Senior Director for Counterterrorism in the Trump administration. Photo: Official White House / Public Domain

The Document Itself

Tom Nichols, writing in The Atlantic, read the strategy so the rest of us could process his findings. His assessment: it is “replete with typos, hyperbolic assertions, and an obsession with former President Joe Biden.” It is “not an actual strategy” but “a long set of notes for a campaign speech.” Security expert Juliette Kayyem told Nichols that such documents used to be serious instruments meant to “guide our intelligence and law-enforcement agencies” and inform “the citizenry, including state and local leaders.” This one, she said, “mocks the American public rather than informs it.” Terrorism scholar Colin P. Clarke called it “slop” and gave it a D+.

The document identifies three categories of terrorist threat facing the United States.

The first is Islamist terrorism. This is reasonable. Iran, designated a state sponsor of terrorism, is a legitimate and serious counterterrorism concern, particularly during an active military conflict. That the administration’s 2025 National Security Strategy had downplayed the Iranian threat and that the 2026 document now calls Iran “the greatest threat to the United States emanating from the Middle East” represents a significant reversal that the document does not explain, presumably because explaining it would require acknowledging that something extremely dangerous happened in the intervening months that the administration did not anticipate or prevent.

The second category is “Narcoterrorists and Transnational Gangs.” Nichols reads this, persuasively, as a reverse-engineered justification for Trump’s military boat attacks off Latin American shores — reframing what might otherwise be characterized as legally questionable operations as components of an official counterterrorism strategy.

The third category is where the document departs most dramatically from every counterterrorism strategy produced by any administration of either party in living memory.

“Violent Left-Wing Extremists, including Anarchists and Anti-Fascists” are identified as a top-tier threat equivalent to Islamist terrorism. The document specifies that these extremists are “anti-American, radically pro-transgender, and anarchist.” It promises to “map them at home, identify their membership, map their ties to international organizations like Antifa, and use law enforcement tools to cripple them operationally before they can maim or kill the innocent.” It further promises to “do the same with the state sponsors of such groups.”

Antifa — a loose, leaderless affiliation of people who describe themselves as anti-fascist and have no organizational structure, no membership rolls, no treasury, no leadership, and no state sponsors — is here elevated to the status of a foreign-backed transnational terror network. The administration does not name the foreign states sponsoring transgender anarchists. It promises to find them.

This is the official wartime counterterrorism strategy of the United States government.

The Author

Sebastian Gorka’s qualifications for this role warrant examination, because they illuminate how this document came to exist.

Gorka holds a 2008 Ph.D. from what Nichols describes as “an undistinguished Hungarian university.” His scholarly publication record is sparse. His academic reputation among terrorism scholars is, to use Nichols’s careful phrasing, “subpar.” He speaks none of the languages of the Middle East and until recently had never spent significant time in the region he is designated to analyse.

His previous government service consisted of seven months in Trump’s first term, during which he fought to obtain a security clearance and fielded criticism for alleged ties to a far-right Hungarian organization, before being shown the door. He subsequently took up podcasting and, Nichols notes, sold fish-oil supplements.

His return to government in the second Trump administration is explained not by an intervening period of scholarly distinction but by loyalty — specifically, the kind of loyalty that the current administration has consistently prioritized over expertise, experience, or basic competence.

Gorka’s own description of his professional philosophy is worth recording: “What I care about is if somebody in the field is reading my article. Publish or be damned? I’ll be damned, thank you very much.”

He is now the senior director for counterterrorism of the United States government during an active war with Iran.

The Institutional Context

The National Security Council, which should be coordinating this work, is effectively leaderless — its director, Marco Rubio, is simultaneously serving as Secretary of State, a dual role that would strain anyone’s bandwidth and that reflects an administration that has not filled key positions with qualified people. The Defense Department and FBI are led by what Nichols characterizes as “immature men who are far out of their depth,” one of whom, as Atlantic reporting noted, uses a bottle of bourbon as his professional calling card.

This is the institutional architecture within which American counterterrorism strategy is being formulated while the United States military is actively engaged in combat operations against Iran.

The CIA has assessed that Tehran “continues to maintain substantial capabilities” and “is nowhere near close to collapse.” The war is not over. The strategy for fighting it was produced because a reporter called out the man responsible for producing it.

The Accountability Question

The Trump Accountability Project has documented, across dozens of entries, a consistent pattern: the deliberate appointment of unqualified loyalists to positions requiring expertise, followed by the predictable failure of those positions to function, followed by the deflection of accountability onto predecessors, opponents, or the press.

The Gorka counterterrorism strategy is a case study in that pattern, distinguished from others primarily by the stakes involved. In peacetime, an incompetent counterterrorism director is a waste of money and a signal of institutional decay. During a war with a state that the administration’s own strategy calls “the greatest threat to the United States emanating from the Middle East,” it is something more serious.

Nichols frames it precisely: “Its simplistic formulations loudly signal the Trump administration’s incompetence to the entire world. Foreign adversaries are unlikely to be intimidated; instead, they might even take some pleasure in knowing that the American government thinks drug dealers, transgender activists, and a bunch of street goons calling themselves ‘antifa’ are as much a threat as transnational terror organizations and their state sponsors.”

A document meant to guide the country’s security apparatus and communicate resolve to adversaries has instead communicated something else entirely. It communicated it to Tehran. It communicated it to Beijing. It communicated it to Moscow.

It communicated it because a reporter asked where it was.

A Final Note

The 2026 United States Counterterrorism Strategy identifies three categories of existential threat: Islamist terrorism, narcoterrorism, and transgender anarchists supported by unnamed foreign governments.

It contains typos.

It was produced two days after its author called a ProPublica journalist a hack on social media.

It is the official policy of the United States government in the second month of a war.

The accountability question this series has asked since Part 1 — whether this represents normal political dysfunction or something qualitatively different — does not, in this case, require extended analysis.

The document answers it.
