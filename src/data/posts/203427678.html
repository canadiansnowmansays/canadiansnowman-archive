The capability Anthropic refused to enable. The dispute that followed.

On May 18, 2026, a jury in Oakland deliberated for ninety minutes and rejected Elon Musk’s claims against OpenAI on the statute of limitations. The trial that had been expected to produce the AI era’s first major public accountability verdict ended before the merits were argued.

The next morning, May 19, a three-judge panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit convened to hear oral arguments in Anthropic PBC v. United States Department of War. The panel — Judges Karen Henderson, Gregory Katsas, and Neomi Rao — had denied Anthropic’s stay request six weeks earlier, an outcome that court watchers had read as a signal of likely defeat on the merits.

The hearing lasted nearly two hours.

What emerged from the bench was not the deference to executive authority that the April stay denial had suggested. It was something considerably more significant — a panel that appeared to have read the briefs, engaged with the statutory text, and arrived at the hearing prepared to say, clearly, what it thought.

What Judge Henderson thought, she said directly: the Pentagon’s action was “a spectacular overreach by the department.”

The Safety Trial had found its voice.

What the Panel Was Asked to Decide

The D.C. Circuit had directed the parties, in its April 8 stay denial order, to brief three specific questions before oral argument. These questions — set by the panel itself — reveal what the judges considered the genuine legal uncertainties in the case, as distinct from the merits question of whether the Pentagon acted lawfully.

The first question concerned jurisdiction: whether the D.C. Circuit has statutory authority to review Pentagon supply chain risk designation decisions at all. If the answer is no, the case ends regardless of the merits. The statute under which Hegseth acted — 10 U.S.C. § 3252 — does not contain an explicit judicial review provision, and the government argued that Congress intended these national security designations to be committed entirely to executive discretion.

The second question concerned the conceptual difficulty of issuing a static judicial ruling for a technology whose capabilities and risks are rapidly changing. If the court rules today that Claude does not pose a supply chain risk, does that ruling bind the government if Claude’s capabilities change significantly next year? The panel wrestled with this openly during argument — how do courts write durable opinions about technology that changes faster than the appellate process?

The third question concerned the factual record: whether, even if the statute applies and the court has jurisdiction, the government’s designation was supported by sufficient evidence under the Administrative Procedure Act’s arbitrary-and-capricious standard.

These three questions — jurisdiction, durability, evidence — framed the nearly two-hour argument.

The Argument

March 3, 2026. The designation that set two circuits in motion.

The government was represented by DOJ attorney Axel Swingle. Anthropic was represented by Kelly Dunbar.

Dunbar’s core argument was direct: once you strip the government of its “headline-grabbing theory” — that Anthropic could somehow reach into active military operations in Iran and pull the plug while American jets were carrying out a strike — the entire factual basis for the designation collapses. That theory, Dunbar argued, was factually incorrect. Claude does not have a remote kill switch. Anthropic cannot reach into deployed military systems and disable them. The government knew this, had been told this, and had chosen to invoke it anyway as the rhetorical foundation of a designation built on a statute designed for Huawei.

Swingle’s response was the government’s most revealing moment of the hearing. Even if Anthropic does not have a back door available to it today, he argued, “it doesn’t take away a risk that they could put one in in the future.”

The government’s case rested not on demonstrated harm, not on evidence of current capability, but on speculative future risk: Anthropic might, at some undetermined future date, insert a capability it currently lacks, in order to do something it has explicitly committed not to do, to a military system it no longer has access to. Courts have historically been skeptical of national security designations built on this kind of inferential chain, particularly when applied to American companies exercising First Amendment-protected speech.

Judge Henderson was not skeptical. She was direct.

“I don’t see that the department has in any way supported its determination that there is a supply chain risk with Anthropic, much less a significant supply chain risk,” she said from the bench. The statute cited by the government, she noted, was specifically crafted to address risks from hostile nations and other bad actors. None of the criteria appeared to apply to an American company that had declined a contract term.

“A spectacular overreach by the department.”

Judge Katsas and Judge Rao — a George H.W. Bush appointee and a Trump appointee respectively — rounded out the panel. Their bench questions explored the jurisdictional and durability questions more than the merits. Neither appeared to embrace the government’s position with the confidence the April stay denial had implied.

Dunbar, for Anthropic, framed the case’s core as a contract dispute weaponized through national security law. “The question here isn’t that we’re trying to force contracts onto the department that the department doesn’t want,” she said. “We’re simply attempting to make sure that the department is not misusing, in our view, a narrow supply chain risk designation to gain leverage in a contract dispute to retaliate against Anthropic for its perceived disagreement with the department.”

That framing — retaliation for disagreement — is the First Amendment theory at its clearest. The government used a national security statute as a weapon against a company for the content of its publicly stated beliefs. If that is what happened, it is unconstitutional regardless of whether the national security concerns were sincere.

After the Hearing: The Supplemental Brief Order

The panel did not rule from the bench. It took the matter under advisement.

Two days later, on May 21, the panel issued a per curiam order on its own motion — without being asked by either party — directing both sides to file supplemental briefs of no more than 2,500 words, due by May 28. The parties jointly moved to extend that deadline to June 4. The motion was unopposed and granted.

The supplemental briefs were filed on June 4. A written opinion is pending.

Per curiam orders issued on a panel’s own motion after oral argument are not routine. They signal that the judges have identified something in the argument — a question, a gap in the briefing, a factual uncertainty — that they want addressed before writing. The specific subject of the supplemental brief order has not been publicly disclosed in detail, but the panel’s focus during argument on the jurisdictional question and the durability problem suggests those are the areas requiring additional briefing.

The written opinion, when it arrives, will be the most significant federal appellate ruling on AI governance since the cases began. It will address, for the first time at the circuit level, whether the government can use national security procurement law as a weapon against an American company for the content of its AI safety policies. It will address whether courts have jurisdiction to review these designations at all. And it will be written against the backdrop of a preliminary injunction in San Francisco that has already found the government’s theory likely unconstitutional — a split that, if maintained, points directly to the Supreme Court.

The Iran Contradiction, Revisited

Fort Meade, Maryland. Six Anthropic engineers are inside.

The D.C. Circuit’s April stay denial cited an active military conflict — Iran — as a reason not to grant emergency relief. The implication was that forcing the Pentagon to disentangle itself from Anthropic’s technology in the middle of combat operations would cause operational harm.

The oral argument revealed the contradiction embedded in that reasoning.

If the Pentagon was using Claude in active operations in Iran at the moment it was designating Claude a supply chain risk too dangerous to be near military systems — then either the risk designation was not credible, or the Pentagon was knowingly operating with a system it believed posed a risk of sabotage and subversion. Neither conclusion supports the government’s position.

Dunbar pressed this point without quite stating it directly. The government had no answer. Swingle’s future-risk argument — Anthropic might put a back door in later — is difficult to reconcile with the government’s simultaneous reliance on Claude in a live combat theater.

The contradiction did not appear to escape the panel’s notice.

The Supreme Court Question

The Jones Walker legal analysis, written after the April stay denial and before the May 19 hearing, identified the most likely path forward with precision: two circuits examining overlapping questions about supply chain risk designations, on substantially the same administrative record, under two related statutes. If the Ninth and D.C. Circuits diverge on the technical question about Anthropic’s ability to affect Claude after delivery, or on the First Amendment analysis, a cert petition writes itself. If they converge, the dispute may resolve in the courts of appeals.

The May 19 hearing, and Judge Henderson’s “spectacular overreach” bench comment, makes divergence less likely than it appeared in April. A D.C. Circuit ruling for Anthropic on the merits, combined with the Ninth Circuit’s ongoing consideration of the California injunction, would represent circuit convergence rather than a split — two courts finding the same government action unlawful through different legal routes.

Convergence does not eliminate Supreme Court interest. A case establishing the outer limits of government authority to designate American AI companies as national security threats, decided at the circuit level, is the kind of case the Supreme Court takes regardless of whether the circuits agree.

The written opinion from the D.C. Circuit is the next development. When it arrives, The Safety Trial will be there.
