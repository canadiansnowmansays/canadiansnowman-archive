On April 17, 1982, Queen Elizabeth II signed a proclamation on Parliament Hill in Ottawa, and Canada finally — one hundred and fifteen years after Confederation — became the master of its own constitution.

Until that moment, Canada had been a country that governed itself in every practical respect but could not amend its own foundational law. The British North America Act of 1867 was a British statute, and it could only be amended by the Parliament at Westminster. This was not, by the 1980s, a practical impediment — Westminster would have approved any amendment the Canadian government requested. But it was a symbolic one, and symbols matter to countries the way they matter to people: the knowledge that your birth certificate sits in someone else’s filing cabinet affects how you think about yourself, even if no one ever actually opens the drawer.

Pierre Elliott Trudeau — intellectual, provocateur, Jesuit-educated bilingual Montrealer, charismatic authoritarian, and the most polarizing prime minister in Canadian history — made patriation his mission. It consumed years of negotiation, produced bitter federal-provincial conflict, threatened to fracture the country, and culminated in the “Kitchen Accord” of November 1981, where nine of ten provinces agreed to a constitutional deal negotiated, literally, in a kitchen at the Ottawa Conference Centre.

The province that did not agree was Quebec.

That absence — the absence of French Canada from the constitutional consensus that was supposed to finally bring Canada home — is the wound at the heart of the patriation project, and it has never fully healed. René Lévesque, the Quebec premier, described it as a betrayal. The phrase “the night of the long knives” — borrowed, with deliberate provocation, from a much darker episode in another country’s history — entered the Quebec political vocabulary. And the subsequent attempts to bring Quebec into the constitutional fold — the Meech Lake Accord of 1987, the Charlottetown Accord of 1992 — both failed, leaving the original wound open and the original question unanswered: can Canada have a constitution that its founding French-speaking nation has not endorsed?

The answer, apparently, is yes — but uncomfortably. Which is, once again, the Canadian way.

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms — the twenty-three words of Section 27 among them — embedded a constitutional commitment to multiculturalism that would reshape Canada over the following four decades.

But what Trudeau brought home was more than a repatriated statute. Embedded in the Constitution Act of 1982 was the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms — the document that, more than any other, defines modern Canadian identity and distinguishes the Canadian constitutional tradition from the American one.

The Charter guarantees fundamental freedoms: expression, religion, assembly, the press. It guarantees democratic rights, mobility rights, legal rights, and equality rights. It guarantees minority language education rights — the right of English-speaking and French-speaking minorities to educate their children in their own language wherever numbers warrant. It prohibits discrimination on the basis of race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, or mental or physical disability.

And in Section 27, it states that the Charter shall be interpreted “in a manner consistent with the preservation and enhancement of the multicultural heritage of Canadians.”

That single section — twenty-three words — is the hinge between old Canada and new Canada. Before the Charter, Canadian identity was defined primarily by the English-French duality, by the British parliamentary tradition, and by the negative definition that had emerged from the War of 1812: we are not American. After the Charter, Canadian identity acquired a positive content: we are a multicultural society, and that multiculturalism is not an accident or a concession but a constitutional commitment.

The implications were enormous and they unfolded over decades. Immigration policy, which had historically favoured European and specifically British immigrants, was reformed. The points system — which assessed immigrants on skills, education, and language ability rather than national origin — had been introduced in 1967, but the Charter gave the multicultural framework constitutional weight. Canada began to receive, and to define itself by receiving, immigrants from every part of the world.

Multiculturalism not as abstract policy, but as daily life: a Bhutanese-Canadian household in Toronto, one of hundreds of thousands shaped by the Charter’s promise

My husband Ugyen came from Bhutan. His journey to Canada — and to our kitchen table in Toronto, where he is probably making tea as you read this — is part of the story that the Charter tells. A country that constitutionally committed to multiculturalism in 1982 became, over the following decades, one of the most ethnically diverse nations on earth. The Canada that I grew up in — London, Ontario, in the 1950s and 1960s, a city so British in character that it had a Thames and a Piccadilly Street and a general conviction that the world made sense as long as the Queen was on the money — is not the Canada that exists today. Today’s Canada is Brampton and Surrey and Markham and the Danforth. It is Sikh temples and Chinese shopping centres and Caribbean carnivals and Filipino grocery stores and, in our household, Bhutanese butter tea and a husky named Dior who has no opinion on any of this but strong opinions about when walks should happen.

The Charter did not create this diversity — immigration policy and global migration patterns did. But the Charter constitutionalized it. It made multiculturalism not just a fact on the ground but a principle in the constitution. And that distinction matters, because principles in constitutions are harder to reverse than policies in legislation. A future government can change immigration policy. It cannot easily repeal a constitutional commitment to multiculturalism without reopening the entire constitutional framework — and Canadians, having survived two failed attempts to amend the constitution in the 1980s and 1990s, have approximately zero appetite for doing so again.

The Charter also fundamentally altered Canada’s relationship with the United States in ways that are often overlooked in the discussion of trade and security.

Before 1982, the Canadian and American constitutional traditions were parallel but separate — both rooted in British common law, both committed to individual rights, but diverging in emphasis and structure. After 1982, the two countries’ constitutional approaches began to diverge more sharply, producing two societies that share a border, a language (mostly), and a political tradition but that have chosen fundamentally different answers to the question of what a free society looks like.

The American Bill of Rights is primarily a set of negative liberties — restraints on what the government may do to the individual. The government may not abridge speech. The government may not establish a religion. The government may not take your guns. The framework is one of protection — the individual shielded from the state.

The Canadian Charter includes these protections but adds something the American framework does not: positive commitments. The commitment to multiculturalism. The commitment to minority language education. The commitment to equality — not just formal equality before the law, but substantive equality that takes into account the real conditions of people’s lives. And, crucially, Section 1 — the “reasonable limits” clause — which provides that the rights and freedoms in the Charter are subject to “such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society.”

Section 1 is the most un-American sentence in the Canadian constitution. It says, in essence, that rights are not absolute — that a free society may, in defined circumstances, limit individual rights for the collective good. The American constitutional tradition recoils from this proposition. The Canadian constitutional tradition embraces it, because Canada has always been a country organized around the negotiation between communities rather than the assertion of individual sovereignty.

The consequences of this divergence are visible in policy after policy. Canada has universal healthcare; the United States does not. Canada has gun control; the United States has the Second Amendment. Canada regulates hate speech; the American First Amendment protects it. These policy differences are not arbitrary. They flow from constitutional commitments that reflect fundamentally different visions of the relationship between the individual and the community.

Neither vision is wrong. Both have produced remarkable achievements and significant failures. But they are different, and the difference matters — particularly now, when the American model is being tested to destruction by a president who treats constitutional norms as obstacles, and when the Canadian model is being tested by the proximity of a neighbour whose constitutional culture is increasingly hostile to the principles that Canada has chosen to enshrine.
