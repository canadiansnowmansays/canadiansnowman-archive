Postmark: Your living room. A disc-shaped machine bumping methodically into the furniture. The future, delivered at floor level.

The first episode of The Jetsons aired on September 23, 1962. It was titled “Rosey the Robot.” In it, an overworked Jane Jetson hires a domestic robot to help manage the household. Rosey — model XB-500, an outdated model bought secondhand — becomes part of the family. She has a Brooklyn accent, strong opinions, and an unwavering loyalty to the Jetsons that the show uses to argue, gently but persistently, that the best robots would not merely be useful but beloved.

Sixty-three years later, the domestic robot has arrived. It is a disc approximately thirty-three centimetres across. It bumps into furniture. When it finishes cleaning, it returns to its charging station and makes a satisfied chime. Its name is Roomba.

Rosey the Robot’s opening question — where is my domestic helper? — has received a partial, somewhat deflating answer. We got a vacuum. A very good vacuum. A vacuum sold by MIT roboticists that has moved more than fifty million units and that the Smithsonian Institution has collected for its archives as a landmark of American technology. A vacuum whose manufacturer, iRobot, spent three decades trying to build the future and ultimately filed for bankruptcy in December 2025, acquired by its Chinese contract manufacturer after a failed Amazon acquisition and an erosion of market share to cheaper competitors.

The Roomba is a genuinely remarkable machine. It is also not Rosey. The gap between the two is the most instructive distance in domestic robotics — and it is finally, definitively, beginning to close.

What the Vacuum Solved

The Roomba launched in September 2002 and sold a million units within two years. Within a decade it was the best-selling vacuum in the United States. Its success was not accidental. Three MIT roboticists — Colin Angle, Helen Greiner, and Rodney Brooks — had spent the previous decade building military robots and space rovers before turning to the domestic market. The insight that produced the Roomba was both simple and profound: before you could build a general-purpose domestic robot, you needed to solve specific domestic problems in isolation, at a price consumers would pay.

The Roomba solved vacuuming. Specifically, it solved the problem of autonomous navigation in a domestic environment — how to move through a room full of unpredictable obstacles, chair legs and pet bowls and children’s toys, without getting stuck or missing significant patches of floor. The first Roombas were not particularly smart. They navigated by collision and randomness, bumping into walls and furniture and redirecting until the floor was statistically likely to be clean. Later models added mapping, cameras, and machine learning. The current generation can identify and avoid a sock on the floor, distinguish between a reclining chair and a pet, and build a detailed map of a home that it uses to plan efficient cleaning routes.

That twenty-year arc of improvement — from random bumping to spatial intelligence — is the arc that every domestic robotics category follows. The question is not whether the robots will improve. It is how fast, and what comes next.

The Hierarchy of Difficulty

Here is something that domestic robotics has taught researchers over thirty years of work: the tasks that seem simplest to humans are often the hardest for robots, and the tasks that seem most difficult are sometimes surprisingly tractable.

Vacuuming: solved. The floor is a two-dimensional surface. The obstacles are mostly static. The success criterion — the floor is clean — is measurable. The Roomba is not a perfect vacuum cleaner. It is a good-enough vacuum cleaner that operates without human supervision, which turns out to be far more valuable than a perfect one that requires attention.

Lawn mowing: largely solved. Robotic mowers have been available for decades. They operate on similar principles to the Roomba, extended to outdoor environments with boundary wires or GPS mapping. The market is growing rapidly as the technology improves.

Window washing, pool cleaning, gutter clearing: partially solved, specialist machines available, significant market penetration in the professional sector if not yet the consumer one.

Cooking: partially addressed, but complex. Robotic cooking systems exist for specific applications — coffee machines have been autonomous for decades, pizza assembly robots operate in commercial kitchens, soup and salad assembly is increasingly automated. General-purpose cooking — the ability to open the refrigerator, assess what is available, choose a recipe, prepare it, and clean up — remains well beyond current capability. The problem is not mechanical. A robot arm can chop a vegetable with more precision than a human hand. The problem is contextual judgment: knowing that the onion is too old to use, that the chicken needs to be defrosted first, that the guest coming for dinner does not eat gluten. These are not programming problems. They are reasoning problems.

Dishes: partially solved in the narrow sense that dishwashers exist, but the loading problem — taking a plate from a table, scraping residue, placing it correctly in a machine — is robotically complex. Loading a dishwasher requires the same deformable-object reasoning as laundry, applied to fragile objects of irregular shape at close quarters.

Laundry: the hardest domestic problem in robotics. It is not merely hard. It is the benchmark problem against which the entire field measures itself.

The Laundry Problem

Pick up a T-shirt that has been laundered and left in a pile. Notice what you do not notice. You find the sleeve without looking for it. You orient the shirt by feel. You fold it into a rectangle through a sequence of motions that you have performed so many times they require no conscious attention. If the shirt is inside out, you correct this automatically. If it is damp, you adjust your grip. If it slides off the table, you catch it without interrupting the process.

Every one of those automatic adjustments is a problem that robots cannot currently solve reliably.

Fabric is what engineers call a deformable object — its shape changes continuously as it is handled, and every different starting configuration (crumpled, spread, folded, balled) requires a different approach. Unlike a coffee cup, which is the same object in essentially the same configuration every time you pick it up, a T-shirt in a laundry basket is never the same object twice. There is no fixed geometry to recognise, no single correct grasp point, no memorisable sequence of moves. Success requires real-time adaptation to continuous change.

A researcher at KTH Royal Institute of Technology, whose 2025 paper on robotic fabric manipulation is the definitive treatment of the problem, put it plainly: “Manipulating fabrics requires both advanced hand manipulation capabilities and high-level reasoning.” The robot needs hands sensitive enough to find the edge of a sleeve and smart enough to understand what a sleeve is and why it matters.

Figure AI’s Helix system — a humanoid robot — achieved the first fully autonomous laundry folding by a humanoid using an end-to-end neural network in 2025. It can fold towels. The folding takes considerably longer than a human would take. The success rate with complex garments remains imperfect. Weave Robotics, founded by former Apple engineers, has deployed its Isaac folding robot in San Francisco laundromats, where it handles common items at roughly sixty percent of human throughput and requires human supervision for edge cases. At $8,000, Isaac is not a consumer product. It is a signal.

The best commercial systems today — Dyna Robotics claims 99.4 percent success rates in controlled commercial trials for common garments — handle what you would expect: T-shirts, towels, napkins, linens in standard configurations. They struggle with irregular shapes. They cannot identify a stain that requires pre-treatment. They cannot make the judgment call that a wool sweater should not go in the dryer.

Rosie could do all of those things. Rosie could also presumably refold the blanket if it came out crooked, notice that the kitchen needed attention while she was folding, and deliver a running commentary on the household’s domestic standards.

We have the towel folder. Rosie remains aspirational.

What Has Arrived at the Door

In October 2025, a Norwegian-American company called 1X Technologies opened consumer pre-orders for a humanoid robot called NEO. Price: $20,000 outright or $499 per month on subscription. Delivery: beginning in 2026 in the United States. Height: 1.68 metres. Weight: 30 kilograms. Capabilities on delivery day: basic autonomy for simple tasks, growing over time through software updates, with a human expert available remotely for complex tasks NEO has not yet learned to perform alone.

This is genuinely new. Not in principle — humanoid domestic robots have been announced many times over many decades — but in practice. There is a price. There is a button to press. There are units shipping to homes. The 1X NEO is the first consumer-ready humanoid robot in the sense that matters: not the most technically impressive, not the most capable, but the one with an actual purchase mechanism and a company that has committed to the consumer market rather than treating home users as an afterthought.

What NEO cannot do on day one is, frankly, most of what Rosey could do. It cannot cook. It cannot do laundry reliably. Its operating manual cautions against use around children and pets without supervision. For complex tasks, a 1X expert accesses the robot remotely during scheduled windows to guide it through operations it has not yet mastered independently.

What NEO represents is not the arrival of Rosey. It is the beginning of the curve that ends with Rosey — the first commercial humanoid in the home, gathering real-world data from real domestic environments, learning from every household it operates in, iterating at the pace that only deployment at scale makes possible. This is the flywheel that the robotics industry has been waiting for: every home a NEO operates in teaches the system something the lab could not. The crumpled sock behind the couch in a specific family’s living room is data that no simulation generates. A thousand homes produce a thousand variations of domestic reality that make the AI genuinely smarter rather than merely better-tested.

The Roomba was not a particularly smart machine in 2002. Twenty-three years of deployment in fifty million homes made it a very smart machine. The lesson is not lost on 1X, on Amazon, on every company that is now racing to put a robot in the home — not because the home is profitable on day one, but because the home is the training environment that makes everything else possible.

Amazon’s Astro, a wheeled home robot priced around $1,600, has been in limited release since 2021. It has no arms. It cannot pick anything up. But it can follow you from room to room, conduct video calls, monitor security, and navigate domestic spaces with the confidence of a machine that has processed an enormous amount of real home data. Its manufacturer spent those years understanding what home robots actually need rather than what engineers imagine they need, which is not the same thing.

iRobot, the company that started all of this with the Roomba in 2002, filed for bankruptcy in December 2025. The proximate causes were competition from cheaper Chinese manufacturers and a failed Amazon acquisition blocked by European regulators. The deeper cause is that solving vacuuming turns out not to be a sufficient business model for a company that wanted to build general-purpose domestic robots. The Roomba was a brilliant product and a partial answer to the domestic robot problem. It was not the whole answer, and the market for partial answers has ceiling effects that whole answers do not.

Why the Kitchen Table Matters

The wonder of domestic robotics is quieter and more intimate than anything else in this series. It does not have the drama of a machine holding a position for forty-five days against Russian attacks, or the clinical precision of the da Vinci system removing a prostate tumour, or the spectacle of a robotic arm assembling the International Space Station 400 kilometres above the Earth.

What it has is this: millions of people spend hours every week on tasks — vacuuming, laundry, dishes, lawn care, cleaning — that they find neither meaningful nor enjoyable, and that a significant body of research links to reduced life satisfaction and time poverty. The promise of domestic robotics is not glamorous. It is the recovery of time that currently goes to things that do not need to be done by the specific person doing them.

That recovery is not equally distributed, and this matters for the kitchen table postcard in ways that the other territories do not require. Access to domestic robots today is a function of income. The Roomba starts at several hundred dollars — accessible to most middle-class households in wealthy countries but not to most of the world. The 1X NEO at $20,000 is firmly in early-adopter territory. The path from early-adopter pricing to mainstream accessibility follows the same curve as every other consumer technology — it always falls faster than the pessimists predict and slower than the optimists promise.

Rosie was never expensive in The Jetsons. She was secondhand — an outdated model, bought because the Jetson family could not afford a newer one. That detail was played for comedy. It contains the most realistic prediction in the entire show.

The future that is arriving at the kitchen table is real. It is partial, expensive, and getting better faster than most people have noticed. The domestic robot that can fold laundry reliably, cook a simple meal, and load the dishwasher without supervision is not decades away. It is years away. The companies building it are now shipping units, gathering data, and iterating on the most complex version of the biomimicry problem: how to build something that can navigate a house the way a human navigates a house — because a house was built for humans, and nothing about it was designed with a robot in mind.

Rosey would understand.
