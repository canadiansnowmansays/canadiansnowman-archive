Every entry in the Reversal Ledger has a column headed “What can’t be put back.” When this series began, the plan was to fill it only where the answer was obvious. By the time the ledger reached 144 entries, the column was filled for about three-quarters of them, and the entries had begun to fall into kinds.

The previous seven parts of this series have asked how each reversal could be undone, and how long it would take. This part asks a different question. Suppose everything that can be restored is restored: every executive order revoked, every rule rewritten, every law repassed, every lock eventually opened. What would still be missing?

The answer comes in five kinds, and each is a different sort of permanence.

The physical

The simplest losses are the ones that obey physics.

Carbon dioxide emitted into the atmosphere stays there for centuries. Every tonne released by a power plant freed from its carbon standard, by a coal unit kept running past its retirement date by emergency order, or by a car built to a weakened fuel economy standard adds to the warming of the planet, and no future regulation will remove it. The repeal of the endangerment finding on 12 February 2026 and of the power-plant standards on 17 September 2026 will be reversible on paper. The emissions in the interval will not be.

Land behaves the same way. A road cut into a national forest that was protected under the Roadless Rule is not uncut when the rule returns. A tree logged is not unlogged. Wetland drained or forest cleared in the years when the rules under the Endangered Species Act no longer treated habitat loss as harm does not come back, and a species pushed below the numbers it needs to recover may not recover at all. The two coal plants that federal money is helping to build in Alaska and West Virginia, the first new ones in the United States since 2013, are designed to run for decades. An inefficient furnace or refrigerator sold under a postponed efficiency standard runs for fifteen years. The cars of model year 2031, being designed now to a standard of 34.9 miles per gallon rather than 50.4, will be on American roads into the 2040s.

None of this can be voted back. It is a matter of time running in one direction.

The legal

The second kind of loss is legal, and Part IV described most of it.

A pardon cannot be revoked. About 1,500 people charged in connection with the attack on the Capitol were pardoned on 20 January 2025, and their legal consequences are gone for good. An oil lease issued in the Arctic is a contract; cancelling it invites a claim for damages. A mining claim staked on land removed from a national monument can survive the monument’s restoration, because a valid claim is property. A rule overturned under the Congressional Review Act cannot be reissued in substantially the same form without a new law. And a treaty the other side has since left cannot simply be rejoined: the INF Treaty died when the United States and Russia both walked away in 2019, and Open Skies, which the United States left in 2020, lost its purpose when Russia followed in 2021.

Most of these can be worked around. Leases can be bought back. New laws can be passed. New treaties can be negotiated. But each workaround costs money, time and political capital that the original did not, and a pardon cannot be worked around at all.

The record

The third kind of loss is the one the public notices least and historians will notice most: gaps in the record.

When the federal government stopped asking about gender identity on its surveys, it did not just change a form. It broke a series. Statisticians cannot go back and ask people in 2026 how they would have answered. When about 400 authors of the National Climate Assessment were dismissed in April 2025, the past reports were taken offline that June and work on a sixth assessment stopped, breaking a report series required by law since 1990. The authors published their own collection on 22 September 2026, but it cannot carry the government’s authority, and planners who need an official baseline will not have one for these years. The record of the presidency itself may be thinner still. On 1 April 2026 the Justice Department declared the Presidential Records Act unconstitutional and told the President he need not comply, and although a federal judge has since ordered White House offices to keep preserving records, the order does not reach Trump or Vance personally. Records that were never kept cannot be recreated.

Research is similar. When a National Institutes of Health grant is cancelled in the middle of a clinical trial, the patients enrolled in it disperse. When a long-running study of a group of people, a cohort, loses its funding for two years, the people in it grow two years older, and some die or move away. The study cannot simply resume where it stopped. The twenty-two mRNA vaccine projects cancelled in August 2025, and the research teams disbanded with them, represent work that will either be done again from the beginning or not done at all.

Some losses of record are moral as much as scientific. A student whose harassment complaint fell outside the narrower Title IX definition has graduated, or left, without an answer. A pattern of discrimination never examined because the disparate-impact standard was removed will not be examined retrospectively. Foreign bribery cases dropped during the six-month enforcement pause in 2025 have, for the most part, stayed dropped.

The people

The fourth kind of loss is people, and it is the largest.

About a quarter of a million federal employees left the government in 2025, net of hiring. Some were near retirement and would have left soon anyway. Many were not. They were the people who knew how to inspect a mine, process a veteran’s claim, audit a grant, run a weather model or answer a citizen’s question correctly. A new appropriation can pay for new people. It cannot give them the twenty years their predecessors spent learning the work.

The same is true across the ledger. Transgender service members discharged from the military are civilians now, with careers that will not be resumed at the rank they would have reached. Refugee resettlement agencies, many run by churches and charities, closed offices when admissions fell to a few thousand; the caseworkers found other jobs. The trained counsellors who answered the 988 crisis line’s LGBTQ youth option were dispersed. Voice of America’s journalists, laid off and kept out despite a court ruling in their favour, have left audiences in many countries to state broadcasters with very different aims. Researchers who left American universities when their funding was frozen are now working elsewhere, many of them abroad. The authors of the climate assessment have moved on.

And some people cannot come back in any sense. Families deported after their Temporary Protected Status was terminated, people sent back to Haiti, Syria or Venezuela, cannot simply return when a future president redesignates their countries. Clinics funded by USAID and PEPFAR closed, and the supply chains for HIV medicines and other treatments were interrupted. The ledger records, in its plainest entry, “lives lost to interruption.” No restoration reaches them.

The trust

The fifth kind of loss is the hardest to measure and may prove the most expensive.

For more than eighty years, markets, journalists and voters assumed that the monthly jobs figure from the Bureau of Labor Statistics was produced by people who would not be fired for producing a bad one. That assumption ended on 1 August 2025. A future commissioner can be given every protection Congress can devise, but everyone will now know that the convention protecting the office was broken once, and can be broken again.

The same has happened elsewhere. American allies who watched the United States join the Paris Agreement, leave it, rejoin it and leave it again now assume that any American climate commitment lasts one term. Investors who watched offshore wind projects with every permit halted by order now price the risk that it will happen again. Parents who watched the federal vaccine advisory committee fired wholesale and the childhood schedule cut have less reason to trust federal vaccine guidance, whichever administration issues it next. And since the Supreme Court’s ruling in Trump v. Slaughter on 29 June 2026, which ended ninety years of protection for independent regulators, no promise by a future president to respect the independence of regulators, or of the inspectors general fired en masse in January 2025, can bind the president who comes after.

What that assumption costs was on display in Antalya on 22 November 2026. The United States sent no delegation to COP31, the annual United Nations climate conference, having left the Paris Agreement in 2025 and the convention beneath it in 2026, and the European Union had lowered its own ambitions partly because of that absence, as Chatham House observed. The talks ran almost two days into overtime and then collapsed, when the EU, Britain and Pacific island states refused a final text that no longer mentioned fossil fuels. CNN called it the longest climate summit on record and the first major breakdown since Copenhagen in 2009. Washington did not do the blocking; Saudi Arabia and Russia did. But the producers who once had to be pulled along by American weight now face no one with the weight to pull, and few of the delegates left in the room expect the United States to come back and stay.

Trust, once broken this way, is not restored by reversing the action that broke it. It is restored, if at all, by years of behaviour that proves the break will not recur, and that proof cannot be given by any single government, because the next one may break it again.

The scars

In Poe’s story, the prisoner escapes the pendulum by smearing the strap that binds him with food so that the rats gnaw through it. He rolls free, but Poe makes a point of telling us that by then the blade had cut through his robe and the linen beneath, and that he had felt it. He survives. He is not unhurt.

That is the most honest description this series can offer of what restoration would mean. A country that elects governments to reverse every reversal in the ledger will survive, and much of what it lost can be rebuilt in some form. But it will carry the losses in this part permanently: the carbon in the air, the roads in the forest, the breaks in the record, the people who left or were sent away, and the knowledge, now shared by every ally, investor and citizen, that the foundations can be removed.

The final part of this series asks what a serious restoration would actually look like, given all of that: what could be done, in what order, how long it would take, and what it would have to do differently to last.
