Every part of this series so far has leaned toward fragility: circular financing arrangements, off-balance-sheet lease liabilities, a depreciation-accounting gap Michael Burry sized at roughly $176 billion, rising Treasury yields, and a Gen Z backlash organising itself street by street. It is worth pausing to give the other side of the ledger its full argument, not as a rhetorical courtesy but because the person making it has an unusually good track record of being right.

As The New York Times’ Jeff Sommer reported on August 28, 2026, Edward Yardeni — an independent strategist and economist with a Yale doctorate — called the bottom of the 2020 pandemic crash in real time, then declared a few months later, correctly, that a “Roaring 2020s” was underway. In late August he was doubling down: an 80 per cent probability, in his estimation, that the rally continues through at least the early 2030s.

A track record that earns the argument a hearing

It is easy, inside a series this committed to documenting bubble mechanics, to treat bullishness as naivety. Mr. Sommer — hardly an uncritical booster himself, and candid in his own piece about keeping cash and bonds on hand as a parachute — resists that temptation, and this series should too. Mr. Yardeni’s 2020 call was made in March, with the pandemic death toll still climbing and vaccines still months away; his August 2020 declaration of a roaring decade came before ChatGPT existed, let alone before anyone had modelled what generative AI would do to corporate earnings. The S&P 500 is up 138 percent since the end of 2019. Whatever else is true about the AI capital stack this series has spent thirteen parts pulling apart, the person who called this rally before there was a rally to call is not obviously the one who should be dismissed as a fool a year later.

His long-run argument is essentially historical, not speculative: reaching his target of an S&P 500 at 10,000 by the end of the decade would require U.S. equities to grow at only the pace they have averaged since the end of the Second World War — a bit over 7 per cent annually in price terms, north of 10 per cent including reinvested dividends. That is not a forecast of continued euphoria. It is, in his own words, merely a return to “normal” after several fantastically abnormal years. Joe Abbott, his firm’s chief quantitative strategist, ran the numbers against every great stock-market decade since the 1870s: the 2020s, at 138 per cent and counting in late August, already sat close to the 1919-29 decade’s 139.9 per cent, with room to climb toward the 1979-89 (227.4 per cent), 1949-59 (257.3 per cent), or 1989-99 (315.7 per cent) decades if the pace holds. None of that requires AI to be a bubble or not a bubble. It requires only that American corporate earnings keep compounding roughly the way they have for eighty years.

The 1929 ghost he keeps in the room on purpose

To his credit, Mr. Yardeni does not pretend the historical parallel that should worry him most doesn’t exist. The original Roaring Twenties ended in a crash and a depression, and he is explicit about the cautionary tale of Irving Fisher, the eminent Yale economist who declared, two weeks before the 1929 crash began, that the market had reached a “permanently high plateau.” Professor Fisher’s reputation never recovered. Mr. Yardeni’s own hedge against becoming this decade’s Irving Fisher is his insistence that the current rally is being driven primarily by genuine corporate earnings growth rather than pure sentiment — a distinction this series has tested rigorously against Nvidia’s circular financing (Part III), Oracle’s roughly 500 per cent debt-to-equity ratio (Part VI), and the hyperscaler bond issuance now exceeding $200 billion (Part X). Those parts found real grounds for concern about the financing structure underneath the earnings. They did not, on their own evidence, establish that the earnings themselves are fictional.

Where his own risk list overlaps with this series’ evidence file

Mr. Yardeni is not a pure optimist reciting talking points; his list of what could derail the rally reads like a table of contents for this series. Geopolitics — the wars in Iran and Ukraine, tariff disputes, the U.S.-China rivalry this series has tracked since Part 86 of the Asimov Gap — tops his list, alongside the possibility of runaway inflation. Bond yields get their own extended treatment, and here his framing connects directly to Part X’s account of hyperscaler debt issuance pushing yields upward. He coined the phrase “Bond Vigilantes” back in 1983, warning that if fiscal and monetary authorities wouldn’t discipline the economy, bond investors would do it for them. His verdict in August: “the bond vigilantes are back,” driven by rising deficits, uncertain Federal Reserve leadership, and — his phrase, not this series’ — huge quantities of bond issuance specifically tied to AI infrastructure build-out. That is Part X’s thesis, delivered independently by a bull rather than a bear, which should carry real weight: when the optimist’s own risk list matches the sceptic’s evidence file almost line for line, the disagreement is narrower than the headline framing suggests. It is a dispute over probability weighting, not over which facts are real.

His other stated worry deserves a place in this series’ running account of deregulatory risk: he explicitly flags that the Trump administration’s deregulatory agenda, while stimulating the current rally, could allow risk-taking to run far enough to produce the kind of corporate fraud that culminated in Enron and WorldCom in the early 2000s. That is not a hedge dressed up as caution. It is a specific, named historical failure mode, offered by a bull as the way his own thesis could go wrong.

The number that should worry a bull more than Mr. Yardeni lets on

One figure belongs in Mr. Yardeni’s own risk column, whether or not he has weighed it. As The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong and Charlie Warzel reported on September 1, 2026, AI-related expenditure now accounts for roughly one-third of all U.S. GDP growth in 2026, and AI-infrastructure spending has propped up the S&P 500 to the point that the piece describes the country, only half-jokingly, as an Nvidia-state. That is a considerably more concentrated dependency than Mr. Yardeni’s own historical framing assumes — his case rests on the idea that this decade’s gains reflect broad-based corporate earnings growth of the kind America has compounded for eighty years, not the performance of one sector’s capital-expenditure cycle standing in for the whole economy’s health.

The revenue projections underneath that spending are also worth setting against his 209.5 per cent decade-end target. In pre-IPO filings, SpaceX projected a future revenue opportunity of $28.5 trillion, driven largely by AI — nearly matching the entire U.S. gross domestic product. Anthropic, the maker of the Claude model this series is written with, has told prospective investors in its own planned public offering that its addressable market exceeds $30 trillion. As Cris Tolomia reported for Quartz on August 25, 2026, the figure rests on “the full scope of work that could be completed using AI models,” and the New York University finance professor Aswath Damodaran said such figures were “reaching the end of what’s plausible and pushing beyond.” Anthropic made its finances public and filed for that offering on October 26, The New York Times reported. Figures at that scale are not earnings forecasts in any conventional sense; they are addressable-market claims of a kind no company in history has ever actually realised, and they sit closer to the “science-fiction-type investing” language this series’ Asimov Gap sibling examined in Part 91 than to the disciplined, historically grounded compounding case Mr. Yardeni is making. If a meaningful share of the market’s confidence in continued growth rests on numbers this speculative, that is itself a risk factor — one Mr. Yardeni’s own 80 per cent probability call does not appear to have explicitly priced in.

The bull trims his sails

Within three weeks of Mr. Sommer’s column, the bond vigilantes Mr. Yardeni had warned about made him act on his own warning. As Namrata Sen reported for Benzinga on September 17, 2026, he cut his year-end S&P 500 target from 8,400 to 7,900, after the 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.041 per cent, its highest since 2007, and Brent and West Texas Intermediate crude rose more than 20 per cent in a month to above $100 a barrel. He said “the likelihood of a downturn has grown over the next three to six months,” and advised investors to “proceed with caution.” He did not abandon the long view: the 10,000 end-of-decade target stood, and 24/7 Wall St. reported the same day that he had moved the 8,400 figure to mid-2027 rather than dropping it. His explanation turned on valuation: “If it kind of hangs around 5%, I think that’s going to erode the valuation multiples somewhat,” he said, while insisting that “Earnings, I think, are going to be fantastic.”

That is the narrow point on which he and this series still differ. Both trace the higher yields partly to AI-related bond issuance; he reads them as a valuation problem that leaves the earnings engine intact, while this series has treated them, since Part X, as a cost of the AI build-out flowing outward into the wider economy. The market has not chosen between them either, though it has started to lean. Aaron Rennie’s Yahoo Finance market wrap for October 2, 2026 recorded the S&P 500 closing at 7,722.72 that day, after a September jobs report showing only 29,000 positions added against 84,000 expected, and Advisor Perspectives noted the same day that the index stood about 1 per cent below its record close of August 13 and up 12.81 per cent for the year. Since then the lean has been downward. Technology stocks fell broadly through October, led by the chipmakers; by the last week of the month the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index was in a bear market, 26 per cent below its June peak, and the Nasdaq had just had its largest drop since June, The New York Times reported on October 26. That is a fall in the most AI-exposed corner of the market rather than the whole of it, and it is not yet the kind of break that would refute an eighty-year average. It is, though, exactly the part of the market Mr. Yardeni’s critics say his average leans on most.

What this series takes from the other side of the argument

Nothing in Mr. Yardeni’s case requires retracting anything in Parts I through XIII. The circular financing, the depreciation-accounting questions, the tenant evictions and the tax-break recall campaigns this series has documented are all still true, still worth tracking, and — on his own admission — still on his list of things that could go wrong. What his case adds is a needed corrective to any implicit assumption, inside a series built around forensic scrutiny of a boom, that scrutiny itself implies the boom must be false. It might not be. Eighty years of compounding says it might just be normal, arriving, as it usually does, dressed up as something extraordinary.
