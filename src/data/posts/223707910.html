Sighle Williams took her first order at Mac’s Drive-Thru in Gainesville, Florida, in July 1987. She took her last one on October 11, 2024. In between, for 37 years, she passed burgers, fries and change through a sliding window, and somewhere along the way she came to know very nearly everyone in town.

That is not a figure of speech. As Erin Clements reported for People on November 5, 2026, a documentary coming to Netflix, One Last Order, follows Williams through her final shift. The trailer describes a woman who knew her customers’ families and children, and who often had the order ready before the customer finished asking for it. The restaurant’s owner, Ron DeFilippo, decided that every dollar Mac’s earned on her last day would go to her.

Word got around Gainesville. Cars wrapped around the block. People waited three hours in a Florida drive-thru lane for a meal that would ordinarily take them five minutes, and the filmmakers found that every one of them had brought a story about her: something she had said, something she had noticed, some ordinary day she had made a little less ordinary. Director Lauren DeFilippo, Ron’s daughter, has said that she and her filmmaking partner Sam Soko were floored by what they found that day: the long lines, people laughing and crying, and nobody arriving without a memory to share.

The short film has since done rather well for itself. As Kristin Chase reported for WCJB on May 1, 2026, it had screened at festivals in Miami, Milwaukee and Aspen, and its audience and jury awards had qualified it for Oscar consideration. Williams, who was born and raised in Gainesville, graduated from Eastside High School and raised five daughters, attended a free hometown screening at Bo Diddley Plaza on May 2, with food supplied, naturally, by Mac’s.

Asked by the Gainesville Sun for the philosophy behind all of it, she offered something that would fit on a napkin: “If you treat people the way you like to be treated, it’ll work.” She added that you never know what someone is carrying that day, so a simple greeting or a kind word can turn it around.

That is the whole sermon. It took her 37 years to deliver it, and she delivered it one car at a time.

The Oldest Rule in the Book

What Williams said is very old. It is older than Florida, older than hamburgers, and considerably older than the United States.

The commandment to love your neighbour as yourself first appears in Leviticus, in the Hebrew Bible, set among rules about honest weights and fair wages. A century before Christ, the rabbi Hillel was challenged to sum up the whole of the Torah while standing on one foot, and he answered that you should not do to your neighbour what is hateful to you, and that the rest is commentary. Confucius gave his students the same rule in the negative. When Jesus was asked which commandment mattered most, he named two: love God, and love your neighbour as yourself.

Then a lawyer, looking for a loophole, asked the obvious follow-up question: and who is my neighbour? The answer was the parable of the Good Samaritan, in which the neighbour turns out to be the stranger on the road, the one from the wrong tribe, the one nobody expected to stop.

In other words, the neighbour is whoever pulls up to your window.

That was Sighle Williams’ theology, whether or not she would call it that. Thirty-seven years of strangers, regulars, students, grandparents, tired parents with cars full of children, people having the best day of their lives and people having the worst, and she met each of them with the same question: how are you doing? It is the shortest version of the Golden Rule there is. It costs nothing. Almost no one in power seems able to afford it.

The Man Who Sold the Book

Which brings us, as most roads do these days, to Donald Trump.

The president has a long and complicated relationship with the book in which that commandment is written. In June 2020, after federal officers cleared protesters from Lafayette Square, he walked across to St. John’s Episcopal Church and held a Bible aloft for the cameras. He did not open it. The church’s bishop said afterwards that no one had asked permission. In March 2024, he began endorsing the “God Bless the USA” Bible at $59.99 a copy, calling it his favourite book. As Christianity Today reported in June 2025, his financial disclosure showed he had earned $1.3 million from the endorsement in 2024. The same magazine noted that when he was asked, as a candidate in 2015, to name a favourite verse, he declined on the grounds that the question was too personal.

It is fair to say he has made more money from the Bible than any drive-thru worker in Gainesville has made from burgers. It is also fair to say that the Bible has made considerably less impression on him than it made on her.

“I Hate My Opponent”

The clearest statement of the difference came not at a rally but at a memorial service.

On September 21, 2025, at State Farm Stadium in Glendale, Arizona, tens of thousands gathered to mourn Charlie Kirk. His widow, Erika Kirk, stood up and forgave the man accused of killing her husband, because, she said, that is what Christ did, and the answer to hate is not hate. It was one of the most extraordinary acts of public grace in recent American memory.

The president spoke next. He praised Kirk for not hating his opponents and for wanting the best for them. Then, as PBS NewsHour reported, he departed from the text to say that this was where he and Charlie disagreed: “I hate my opponent and I don’t want the best for them.” He apologised to the widow, and the crowd laughed. The White House later explained that he was simply being his authentic self.

It is difficult to imagine a plainer refusal of the second great commandment. He did not misunderstand the rule, or stumble over it, or fall short of it on a bad day, as all of us do. He heard it stated at its most costly, by a grieving woman who had every reason to hate, and he answered that it was not for him.

A Name on Nothing

There are other contrasts, and they are almost too neat.

Sighle Williams spent 37 years at a window with her name on nothing. There was no sign, no logo, no brass lettering. Her name was not on the building or the menu or the paper bags. It existed only in the memories of the people she served, which is apparently a more durable medium than brass, because when it mattered, the whole town turned up to say it out loud.

Donald Trump has put his name on towers, hotels, golf courses, steaks, vodka, neckties, a university, a cryptocurrency and a Bible. Above the main entrance of Trump Tower on Fifth Avenue, the name stands in brass capitals 34 inches high. One gets the impression that he fears it would otherwise be forgotten, and he may be right. Names stamped on buildings have a way of coming down when the building changes hands. Names carried in people’s memories tend to stay put.

The Giver and the Taker

Then there is the matter of the money.

Ron DeFilippo, a man who owns a burger joint, looked at a day’s takings and decided they belonged to the woman who had earned his customers’ affection for 37 years. Nobody made him do it. There was no tax advantage and no photo opportunity, at least not one he could have foreseen; his daughter happened to have a camera, and the rest is now Netflix.

Donald Trump’s record runs the other way. In 2016, he paid $25 million to settle fraud claims from students of Trump University. In November 2019, a New York judge ordered him to pay $2 million for misusing his own charitable foundation, which was dissolved under court supervision. The pattern across his business life has been to take the day’s takings and keep them, and the drive-thru lane in that story has generally been full of people waiting for something that never came.

I spent 18 years in a complaints office at a large Canadian telecommunications company, handling the calls nobody else wanted. I can tell you what I learned there, and it is exactly what Sighle Williams learned at her window: most people arrive braced for a fight, and nearly all of them soften the moment someone treats them as a person rather than a problem. Kindness is not weakness in that work. It is the only tool that reliably works. Sighle knew it. Every good customer service representative knows it. It is a peculiar thing to watch the most powerful man in the world build an entire political career on the opposite theory.

A Note Passed Through the Window

So here is the life lesson, Mr. President, offered gently, since gentleness is rather the point.

A woman with no title, no fortune and no name on any building spent 37 years in a small box on a Gainesville street, being kind to whoever pulled up. She did not check their party registration. She did not ask whether they had voted for her, because nobody ever voted for her. She did not keep a list of enemies. She asked how they were doing and meant it.

When she left, the town lined up around the block for three hours to thank her. Nobody was bused in. Nobody was handed a hat. Every one of them came because they wanted to, carrying a story about a day she had made better.

You have spent a lifetime demanding exactly that kind of loyalty, and you have never once received it freely, because it cannot be demanded. It cannot be bought for $59.99, licensed through a holding company or stamped in brass. It is only ever paid back, slowly, one car at a time, to people who gave it first.

Love your neighbour as yourself. Sighle Williams put it more simply: treat people the way you would like to be treated, and it will work. You may know it by its other name, Mr. President, and it ought to be right up your street, because they call it the Golden Rule. You have gilded the Oval Office, promised Americans a Golden Age, launched a Gold Card and ordered up a Golden Dome. Here, at last, is something golden you cannot buy, cannot license and cannot screw to a wall. It can only be lived. Of all the gold in your collection, it is the one kind that never tarnishes.

It is not too late to try it. The window is still open. But there is a line forming, and the people in it have long memories.
