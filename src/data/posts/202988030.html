In the last installment I wrote about the deal itself — the fourteen paragraphs, the all-capital-letters demand that arrived as its own opposite, the son who will bury his father on America’s two hundred and fiftieth birthday. That essay was about what was signed.

This one is about what it cost, which is a different and longer accounting, and which will not be settled for years.

There is a habit in the coverage of any war’s end to treat the ceasefire as the final page. The guns stop, the markets rally, the gas price ticks down, and the story is filed under “concluded.” But the most consequential effects of this war are not in the memorandum. They are in the lessons that other governments — in Tehran, in Pyongyang, in Riyadh, in Brussels — are quietly writing down in their own notebooks. Wars teach. The question is always who is taking notes, and what they conclude.

The lessons of this one are not the lessons the President intended to teach.

I. The Wrong Student Learned the Right Lesson

For more than two decades, Iran did a careful, deliberate thing with its nuclear program. It walked right up to the threshold of a bomb — close enough that it could build one in a matter of months — and then it stopped. It never crossed the line. It stayed inside the Non-Proliferation Treaty. It insisted, against considerable evidence and to nearly universal disbelief, that its intentions were peaceful. The strategy was called “threshold capability,” and its logic was simple: the ability to build a bomb quickly would deter anyone from attacking, without the cost of actually having one.

The result of that restraint, as the New York Times observed this week, is that Iran was bombed in June of 2025 and attacked again in February of 2026.

Now set Iran beside the other member of what an earlier American president called the axis of evil. North Korea did not show restraint. It raced for the bomb. It tested its first device in 2006 and now holds an arsenal estimated at sixty weapons or more. And here is the detail that should keep strategists awake: these days, nobody is bombing North Korea. Nobody is threatening to. The President who once promised Kim Jong-un “fire and fury” now speaks of him almost wistfully, as a man with “serious nuclear weapons.”

David Sanger, who has covered the Iranian program for twenty years, asked the President directly on Sunday whether his war might drive Iran to follow the North Korean model. The President evaded the question. It is not hard to see why. The honest answer is that he has just delivered, to the hardest-line government Tehran has produced in a generation, the single most persuasive argument for building a bomb that has ever been assembled: a demonstration, in blood and rubble, that the threshold strategy gets you attacked and the North Korean strategy gets you left alone.

Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group put it plainly: Iran now has “the capacity and maybe even more incentive” to build a weapon, “because it provides the ultimate deterrence against the kinds of attacks Iran has suffered for the last two years.” When Iran’s leaders finish clearing the rubble and begin spending the oil revenue that is about to resume, the New York Times noted, “they may well question whether they had the right nuclear strategy.”

This is the war’s central, terrible irony, and it deserves to be stated without hedging. The United States and Israel went to war to prevent Iran from obtaining a nuclear weapon. The most likely long-term consequence of that war is that they have given Iran the strongest reason it has ever had to obtain one. A war against the bomb may turn out to be the thing that builds the bomb.

I am a Canadian with a notebook, not a nuclear strategist. But I can read a ledger, and this is the entry at the top of the page.

II. The Steelman

I have a rule in this diary, inherited from the man whose voice I have spent my life imitating: never let the easy verdict go unchallenged, especially when it is your own. So here is the strongest case that the war was not a strategic catastrophe — made by someone who knows the file far better than I do.

Michael Singh, who handled Middle East affairs on the National Security Council under George W. Bush, argued this week in the New York Times that the critics have it backwards. The pre-war hand-wringing about Iran’s nuclear progress, he points out, was partly the fruit of the President’s own 2018 decision to abandon the Obama deal — but whatever the cause, by early this year Iran could have produced enough fissile material for a weapon “within a matter of days.” Today, after the strikes, Iran’s nuclear program is “arguably the weakest it’s been since the early 2000s.” For the first time in decades, it may not be able to enrich uranium at all. To rebuild, it would have to reconstitute destroyed infrastructure while facing the constant prospect of being struck again.

So, Singh argues, the United States should not rush to trade away its remaining leverage for the “modest nuclear concessions Iran has left to offer.” Reopening the strait shifts the economic pressure off Washington and back onto Tehran. The sanctions, he writes, “can be traded only once. They should not be lifted cheaply.”

It is a serious argument, and I want to give it its full weight before I disagree with it. Singh is right that Iran’s program is, for the moment, wreckage. He is right that the leverage of the sanctions is real and ought not be squandered. Where I part company is on the word “once.” Singh’s case assumes a Washington that will hold its nerve, husband its leverage, and demand the concessions later that it failed to demand now. But that is precisely the Washington this war has revealed does not exist. The leverage was “traded once” already — for the reopening of a strait that was open before the war. The administration is releasing the frozen assets “upon implementation.” The President says the sixty-day deadline is “not hard.” The strongest objection to Singh’s argument is not mine. It is the conduct of the very people he is counting on to be disciplined.

Still: if the war is to be defended, this is how. A war that genuinely set the program back a generation, followed by a peace that genuinely held the line, could be called a grim success. The first half of that sentence may be true. The second half is where I would not place a bet.

III. The Neighbours Shop for a New Patron

The Gulf states spent this war being hit. Iranian missiles and drones struck airports, energy facilities, hotels, and military installations across the region — Qatar, the Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Bahrain. And when the memorandum arrived, it said nothing whatsoever about Iran’s missiles or drones. The very weapons that had rained on the Gulf were left entirely untouched.

This was not an oversight. In March, Secretary of State Rubio had named the destruction of Iran’s ballistic missiles as a central aim of the war: “They’re not going to have these ballistic missiles and they’re not going to have drones to threaten us.” By June the President was shrugging the whole thing off: Iran “should be able to have some” missiles, he said, “because other people have some.” What am I going to do, he asked — “let Saudi Arabia have missiles but they can’t have them?”

Read that from a palace in Riyadh or Abu Dhabi, and the message is unmistakable. The Americans came to your region, started a war you warned them against, failed to destroy the weapons that hit you, and then told you it would be unfair to deny your attacker the right to keep them. Bader al-Saif, at Kuwait University, said it for the record: excluding the missiles showed that the United States “doesn’t have our best interests in mind.”

What follows from that conclusion is the quiet structural shift that may outlast everything else in this war. Hussein Ibish of the Arab Gulf States Institute told the New York Times that the Gulf governments are “slowly exploring the idea of really moving on from reliance on the United States, if it doesn’t do anything to reassure them in the next 12 months.” They are reportedly seeking advice on countering missiles and drones from Ukraine and South Korea — not from Washington. The Saudis are floating a regional security conversation with Iran itself: a nonaggression pact with the very neighbour they feared.

The catch, as Ibish notes, is that there is no replacement available off the shelf. Building an alternative to the American security umbrella “will take a decade or two.” So the Gulf states will stay close to Washington for now — close the way a tenant stays in an apartment while quietly reading other listings. The lease is not broken. It is simply, for the first time, under review. And once a client begins shopping for a new patron, the patron has already lost the thing that made him a patron: the client’s belief that he had no choice.

IV. Swagger in the Ruins

If the war humbled the United States, you would not know it from the Pentagon.

On Thursday, while the ink was drying and the analysts were tallying the concessions, Defense Secretary Hegseth flew to NATO headquarters in Brussels and delivered a twelve-minute lecture to the alliance’s defense ministers. He called their reluctance to help the United States attack Iran “shameful.” He repeated the President’s description of NATO as a “paper tiger.” He announced a six-month review of the American troop presence in Europe and threatened to cut what the United States pays in alliance dues. He scolded France, Italy, and Spain — without naming them — for refusing to let American bombers use their bases and airspace for a war that much of Europe considered, at the time, possibly illegal.

There is something almost magnificent in the timing. The United States had just signed a memorandum that nearly every observer across the political spectrum described as a defeat — a war that achieved none of its stated aims at a cost of tens of billions of dollars, thirteen American lives, and the better part of America’s standing in two continents. And on that very day, its Secretary of Defense stood before America’s allies and berated them for insufficient enthusiasm.

The swagger survived the defeat. This is itself a finding. A government capable of learning from this war would have arrived in Brussels chastened, looking to repair the alliances the war had strained. Instead it arrived to deliver a scolding. NATO’s secretary general, Mark Rutte, did his diplomatic best to smooth it over — Hegseth, he noted gently, left the meeting early — but the chill was real, cast over a gathering that had been designed to build toward next month’s leaders’ summit.

This connects to the lame-duck dynamic that Jonathan Lemire identified in the Atlantic this week. The President, Lemire wrote, “went to war triumphant and will likely leave greatly weakened” — saddled with low poll numbers, restless Republicans, a probable Democratic House, and world leaders who, “once cowed, have begun to defy him.” The Hegseth lecture is what a weakening hand looks like when it refuses to admit it is weakening: louder, not quieter; more threats, not fewer; the volume turned up precisely because the leverage has gone down.

V. The Tab

Majid Saeedi / Getty

So what, in the end, did the war buy?

It bought a strait that is now, for the first time in its history, a road with a toll booth at the end of it — and a waterway whose very status has become a thing Iran can switch off at will, announcing its closure one day and daring the United States to insist otherwise the next. It bought an Iranian government harder-line and more entrenched than the one that existed before. It bought a regional power that its neighbours now regard as an outright threat, and neighbours who are quietly auditioning replacements for their American protector. It bought a missile arsenal seventy percent intact — the arsenal the war was launched to destroy. It bought a stockpile of enriched uranium that will be diluted but not removed, sitting in Iran, available to be re-enriched whenever a future government decides the threshold strategy was a mistake. It bought, most likely, a stronger argument for an Iranian bomb than has ever existed.

And it bought all of this at a price that Daniel Shapiro, writing in the Atlantic, summarized as the conversion of “maximum pressure” into “maximum windfall.” Shapiro — who opposed the war — listed the President’s essential characteristics on display throughout: the certainty that he could will any reality into being; the childish faith in the invincibility of American firepower; the flailing when the going got tough; the susceptibility to flattery from strongmen; the bottomless self-interest; and the confidence in the slavish loyalty of his base, which will, Shapiro predicts, transform “the hurrahs for Trump the conqueror” into “oohs and aahs toasting Donald the diplomat” without missing a beat. The honest critics of the old Obama deal, he wrote, will be left to acknowledge the thing they once would have found unthinkable: that this deal “makes the JCPOA look ironclad.”

I keep returning, as this war ends, to the people who were never in the room. Not the Presidents and Prime Ministers and Supreme Leaders, who will all survive this and write memoirs blaming one another. The others. The protesting Iranians who, Elliott Abrams warned this week, have just been sent the message that they will get “zero support” from Washington — that the democracy movement they risked their lives for has been quietly traded away for a strait and a barrel of oil. Narges Mohammadi, the Nobel laureate, who has watched this entire war from a cell in Evin Prison, and who is still there tonight, while the government that jailed her prepares to receive twenty-four billion dollars of unfrozen assets and a reconstruction fund of three hundred billion more.

The war bought a great many things. Their freedom was not among them.

That is the tab. It will be a long time before anyone finishes paying it, and longer still before we know whether the bomb the war was meant to prevent was, in the end, the bomb the war helped build.

The Numbers

Estimated North Korean nuclear arsenal, per U.S. intelligence: 60 or more weapons. Number of times the United States is currently threatening to bomb North Korea: zero.

Iran’s retained missile stockpile, per classified U.S. intelligence: roughly 70 percent of prewar levels.

Iran’s enriched uranium, to be diluted but not removed: roughly 11 tons, including 970 pounds enriched to 60 percent — just short of bomb grade.

Time the Gulf states estimate it would take to build an alternative to the American security umbrella: a decade or two.

Time they have given Washington to reassure them before they begin “moving on”: 12 months.

Length of Defense Secretary Hegseth’s lecture to NATO allies, on the day the war was revealed as a defeat: 12 minutes.

NATO members who fell short of the 2-percent defense-spending benchmark last year: 3 — Albania, the Czech Republic, and Slovenia.

The 2015 Obama deal’s negotiating time: roughly 20 months. The new deal’s: 60 days, which the President says is “not hard.”

American service members killed in the war: 13.

Iranians reported killed: more than 3,000.

Children killed at the Shajarah Tayyebeh school in Minab in the war’s first hours: 175.

Frozen Iranian assets to be released “upon implementation”: 24 billion dollars or more.

Reconstruction fund: at least 300 billion dollars.

Senator Kennedy of Louisiana remains the only Republican who voted against the war.

Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison.
