July 2026

Part XII of this series ended with a sentence that felt, at the time of writing, like a conclusion.

The rockets land beautifully. The engineer is brilliant. The ambition is Promethean. The power is unprecedented.

It was not a conclusion. It was a setup.

The series mapped twelve layers of a private empire — rockets, satellites, cars, robots, chips, social media, AI, an encyclopedia, a government experiment, and the political turn that connected all of them to one man’s grief and grievance. The stack was already the most concentrated portfolio of strategic power any private citizen had ever assembled. And then, eight days before SpaceX was scheduled to begin trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, the sentence that changes everything appeared in a CNBC report almost as an afterthought:

Musk has discussed with colleagues the possibility of folding SpaceX and Tesla together.

In the vocabulary of this series, that sentence has a precise meaning. The stack is not finished. The stack is becoming one.

What a Stack Actually Is

The word “stack” entered technology vocabulary as an engineering term — the layers of software and hardware that sit on top of each other to make a system function. The operating system sits on the silicon. The database sits on the operating system. The application sits on the database. Each layer depends on the one below it. Change the bottom layer and everything above it shifts. The stack is both a description of structure and a theory of power: whoever controls the foundational layer controls everything that runs on top of it.

This series borrowed that metaphor because it fit. SpaceX provides orbital access — the foundational layer on which Starlink sits. Starlink provides connectivity — the layer on which X and Grok are distributed. X and Grok shape discourse — the layer on which every other enterprise in the stack is narrated and defended. Tesla provides the hardware platform — the layer that generates the data that trains the models that power the robots that are manufactured in the facilities that the chip fabs supply. Each layer reinforces every other. The stack is self-reinforcing not just commercially but strategically.

But through twelve essays, the stack had one structural inefficiency: it was legally two companies. Tesla traded on Nasdaq under TSLA. SpaceX was private, then filing, then pricing at $135 a share and $1.77 trillion. Two boards. Two sets of minority shareholders. Two sets of fiduciary duties. Two sets of lawyers reviewing every intercompany transaction for conflicts. Two regulatory relationships with every agency that touches either business.

A merger eliminates the inefficiency. It does not create the stack. The stack already exists in the engineering and the plumbing. It formalizes it in law. And in doing so, it solves a problem that has frustrated Musk since Tesla first went public — a problem that has nothing to do with rockets, robots, or orbital compute, and everything to do with control.

The Ownership Problem — And Its Solution

Musk owns approximately 20% of Tesla’s equity. This has been a source of friction for years. He has pursued milestone-based compensation packages — the controversial 2018 plan, voided by a Delaware court and resubmitted to Tesla shareholders in 2024 — that would increase his stake to approximately 25% upon achieving operational and financial targets. The targets are ambitious. The timeline is uncertain. And the Delaware Court of Chancery has already demonstrated it will scrutinize the structure.

The compensation battles are well documented: a judge voided his $56 billion pay package in early 2024, citing the unfair process by which Tesla’s board approved it. Musk moved Tesla’s incorporation to Texas in response. The resubmitted package passed a shareholder vote, but the litigation risk did not disappear. Tesla shareholders have shown, repeatedly, that they are willing to use their governance rights to constrain Musk’s compensation and related-party transactions. The SolarCity acquisition lawsuit. The compensation voiding. The Tesla-SpaceX intercompany scrutiny. The pattern is clear: Tesla’s minority shareholders are the one institutional check on Musk’s behaviour that has actually functioned.

Here is the insight that the merger chatter makes visible: a SpaceX-Tesla combination, structured with SpaceX as the acquiring entity, solves the Tesla ownership problem entirely — and does so without Musk needing to hit a single operational milestone.

Under a stock-swap merger, Tesla shareholders would receive SpaceX Class A shares. Musk’s Tesla stake — approximately 20% — would convert into a proportional interest in the combined entity. But Musk already controls 82% of SpaceX’s voting power through Class B shares. The combined entity’s governance structure would be SpaceX’s, not Tesla’s. The Delaware court’s jurisdiction over Musk’s Tesla compensation would become irrelevant. The Tesla shareholders who voted against his pay package would become Class A SpaceX shareholders with one-tenth the voting weight of Musk’s Class B shares.

In a single transaction, Musk would convert a 20% economic interest in a company whose minority shareholders have successfully litigated against him into a proportional economic interest in a combined entity where no such constraint is possible. The milestone-based compensation path — 25% ownership contingent on hitting targets, subject to court review and shareholder votes — becomes unnecessary. The merger is the milestone. The combination is the compensation.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is arithmetic. The governance mechanics of the proposed combination, taken at face value, produce exactly this outcome. Whether Musk designed it this way, stumbled into it, or simply recognises the opportunity now that SpaceX is public is a question nobody outside his circle can answer. What can be said with confidence is that the combination solves his Tesla governance problem more completely and more permanently than any compensation package a court could void or a shareholder vote could reject.

The Plumbing That Already Connects Them

The merger chatter would be easy to dismiss as analyst speculation if the two companies were not already so deeply entangled that separating them would require surgery rather than paperwork.

Tesla’s Q1 2026 filing disclosed a $2 billion equity investment in SpaceX — made while SpaceX was still private, without a meaningful Tesla shareholder vote. Tesla shareholders now own a proportional share of a SpaceX stake they did not choose and cannot easily exit.

TeraFab — the $20-to-$25 billion semiconductor fabrication facility being built on the Gigafactory Texas campus — is a joint venture between Tesla, SpaceX, xAI, and Intel. The same physical building will produce chips for Cybercabs, Optimus robots, AI data centres, and eventually the orbital compute infrastructure that Starlink’s next-generation satellites will carry. The silicon does not know which company ordered it. The supply chain does not distinguish between the two balance sheets.

Musk acknowledged the complexity on Tesla’s Q1 earnings call: “SpaceX is going to take care of the initial phase of the scaled up TeraFab. Any kind of intercompany thing has to be approved by both the SpaceX and Tesla board of directors.” That sentence is an inadvertent description of the problem a merger solves. Every shared engineer, every shared facility, every shared chip order currently requires approval from two boards whose fiduciary duties run to two different sets of shareholders. The friction is real. The merger removes it.

Suppliers already experience the two companies as one customer. When Nvidia agreed to divert a $500 million GPU order from Tesla to xAI at Musk’s request in 2024, the diversion happened because the man who controls Tesla also controls xAI. The supplier saw one decision-maker. The legal structure saw two companies with separate interests. The gap between those two realities is where shareholder litigation is born — and where a merger ends it.

The Governance Arithmetic

Here is where the stack analysis becomes uncomfortable for anyone holding Tesla stock.

Musk controls approximately 20% of Tesla’s equity and roughly 20% of its votes — enough to be the largest individual shareholder but not enough to be unchallengeable. Tesla shareholders have exercised their rights. They voted on his compensation package. They filed lawsuits over the SolarCity acquisition. The Delaware Court of Chancery voided his 2018 compensation plan. The adversarial relationship between Musk and Tesla’s minority shareholders is documented, litigated, and ongoing.

Musk controls approximately 42% of SpaceX’s equity and more than 82% of its votes after the IPO. His Class B shares carry ten votes each. Class B holders, voting separately, elect 51% of the board. He serves simultaneously as CEO, CTO, and chairman. He cannot be fired without voting to fire himself.

A merger of these two governance regimes produces a predictable outcome: Tesla shareholders, currently holding shares in a company where they have meaningful if limited governance rights, would convert into Class A shares of a SpaceX-controlled entity where those rights are structurally diminished. The dilution is not financial. It is democratic. They would own a piece of a larger, more powerful enterprise with less ability to influence how it is run.

Wedbush’s Dan Ives has put the odds of this merger at 80 to 90 percent by early 2027. Prediction markets are more cautious — Kalshi traders placed roughly 33% odds on a pre-May 2027 combination. The gap between Wall Street’s enthusiasm and the market’s scepticism may reflect a simple fact: Wall Street prices the strategic logic, and the strategic logic is compelling. The market prices the governance risk, and the governance risk is real. What neither is adequately pricing is the ownership logic — the degree to which this merger is, from Musk’s perspective, the cleanest solution to a Tesla governance problem that courts and shareholders have spent years creating.

What the Combined Stack Looks Like

Set the governance question aside for one moment and consider the engineering argument, because it is the argument Musk will make publicly and it deserves honest engagement.

Tesla’s core assets: the data flywheel (millions of vehicles generating real-world driving data), the autonomy stack (FSD and its end-to-end neural architecture), the manufacturing platform (Gigafactories, the Optimus production line), and the hardware layer (Cybercab, Optimus, the AI4 and AI5 chips).

SpaceX’s core assets: the launch capability (Falcon 9, Starship), the connectivity layer (Starlink’s 10.3 million subscribers across 164 countries), the compute infrastructure (Colossus and Colossus II, currently leasing capacity to Anthropic at $1.25 billion per month), and the orbital ambition (data centres in space, direct-to-smartphone service, the one-million-satellite FCC application).

Combined, these assets describe a single coherent system. Tesla’s chips power the robots and the vehicles. SpaceX’s launch capability puts Tesla’s next-generation chips into orbit. Starlink connects the orbital compute to the ground network. The ground network connects to the Cybercab fleet and the Optimus robots. The robots generate labour replacement economics at scale. The vehicles and robots generate data. The data trains the models. The models run on the chips. The chips are fabricated at TeraFab, which sits on a campus that is already jointly operated.

Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX's president and COO, has begun making the orbital compute case in her own voice. "I actually think we can put a constellation of AI satellites in orbit before we could actually build the power capability that we would need to power the data centers here on Earth," she told Time magazine recently. This matters not just as a data point about orbital strategy but as a signal about operational alignment: the person who runs the day-to-day company has internalized the thesis that justifies the combined valuation. When Shotwell speaks, engineers listen. She has been translating Musk's visions into achievable company goals for 24 years. Her adoption of the orbital compute argument means it has crossed the threshold from founder fantasy to operational roadmap.

The combined company would be the most vertically integrated enterprise in human history — more integrated than Ford’s River Rouge complex, more integrated than Standard Oil, more integrated than any technology conglomerate that has come before it. From lithium in the ground to chips in orbit, from factory robots to satellite internet in the Southern Ocean, every link in the chain would be owned, operated, and controlled by one entity under one man’s voting authority.

This is not a tech company. This is not an auto company. This is not a space company. This is infrastructure — the word this series has been building toward since Part I. The combined stack would be infrastructure in the way that the electrical grid is infrastructure, the way the internet is infrastructure, the way the telephone network was infrastructure. Except that the electrical grid, the internet, and the telephone network are all subject to public utility regulation, common carrier obligations, and democratic oversight. The combined Musk stack would be subject to none of these things, because no regulatory framework has been designed for a single private entity that spans all of it simultaneously.

The Pro-Forma That Nobody Wants to Read

The financial case for merger is more complicated than the strategic case.

Put Tesla and SpaceX together on a pro-forma basis and the combined GAAP earnings are approximately negative $1 billion per year. Tesla earns. SpaceX loses — $4.9 billion last year, $4.3 billion in Q1 2026 alone, driven by the AI segment’s $6.35 billion operating loss and $12.7 billion in capital expenditure. SpaceX’s free cash flow deficit hit $14 billion last year. The S-1 acknowledges this will continue for “an extended period.”

Tesla’s cash generation, which has been the company’s financial backbone through multiple crises, would become the combined entity’s primary source of internal funding for SpaceX’s orbital ambitions. Starlink’s $7.2 billion adjusted EBITDA already subsidizes xAI’s losses. Tesla’s automotive margins would join that subsidy regime.

The market is being asked, once again, to price trajectory rather than earnings. The trajectory argument is that orbital AI compute, autonomous ride-hailing, and billion-unit robot deployment will generate returns that dwarf the current losses. The sceptic’s argument is that this is the same trajectory argument that justified each previous consolidation — xAI into SpaceX, X into xAI — and that each consolidation transferred losses to a profitable entity while the promises remained on the horizon.

There is a third reading that sits between optimism and scepticism. The merger makes strategic sense if you believe the trajectory. It makes financial sense if you accept losses as investment. And it makes personal sense — for one specific person — because it resolves a governance problem that no amount of milestone-based compensation could clean up as completely or as permanently.

The compensation path required hitting targets. Courts could review it. Shareholders could reject it. The merger path requires only that two boards approve a transaction. One of those boards, post-IPO, is controlled by the man proposing it.

The Survivor and the Seams

There is one more person whose position the merger changes profoundly, and she has been largely absent from the merger conversation despite being central to everything that makes the combination conceivable.

Gwynne Shotwell is SpaceX’s fifth-largest Class A shareholder. She has run the operational company for 24 years. She was last year’s highest-paid SpaceX executive at over $85 million in total compensation. Former colleagues describe her with a single word: survivor. She has outlasted every other senior executive at a company whose CEO is not known for loyalty to anyone who fails to match his pace. She is, by every operational measure, the adult in the room — the person who turns Musk’s visions into schedules and his impulses into company policy.

In a combined SpaceX-Tesla entity under SpaceX’s Class B governance structure, Shotwell’s formal authority does not increase. Her equity converts into Class A shares in a structure she already navigates. Her operational role — translating Musk’s ambitions into executable goals — continues as it has since 2002. Nothing changes for her in form.

What changes is the scale of what she is managing, and the degree to which the combined entity’s operational complexity would fall, as it always has, primarily on her. The merged stack would span rocket manufacturing, satellite internet, electric vehicles, autonomous driving, humanoid robotics, semiconductor fabrication, AI infrastructure, social media, and orbital compute. Musk will provide the vision. Shotwell, or whoever holds her role, will be responsible for making it not explode.

She has done this before. Three times, in fact — in 2008, in 2018, and in the quieter crisis years between. The stack grows. The job grows with it. The credit, as always, flows upward.

Part XII of this series noted that the stack has no institutional check, no democratic accountability, no single regulator with jurisdiction over the whole. A merger seals those seams permanently. What it does not change is the human architecture of the operation: one man with the vision and the votes, one woman with the engine and the execution, and no mechanism for anyone outside that arrangement to alter its terms.

This is what the combination looks like from the inside. It is not a merger of equals. It is the formalization of a power structure that has been operational for 24 years, extended now to cover everything both companies touch, governed by a share structure that ensures it will never need to answer to anyone who did not choose to be there.

The Stack Complete — Revised

Part XII of this series ended with a list.

SpaceX launches the satellites. Starlink connects them to the world. X shapes what the world discusses. Grok answers the world’s questions. Grokipedia writes the world’s reference material. Tesla builds the vehicles that collect the data. Optimus builds the robots that replace the labour. TeraFab fabricates the chips that power everything.

The list was accurate. It was also incomplete, because it described a stack of companies rather than a stack. The distinction matters. A stack of companies has seams — legal seams, governance seams, fiduciary seams — where the layers meet. A stack has none. It is one thing, integrated from bottom to top, with no point of entry for a minority shareholder’s lawyer, a Delaware judge, or a board member who disagrees.

The combination, if it happens, closes the seams. What emerges is not a conglomerate in the traditional sense — a holding company with diverse subsidiaries whose only common thread is a balance sheet. What emerges is a vertically integrated system with one owner, one voting bloc, one set of priorities, and no institutional mechanism for any of the layers to be held accountable to anyone outside the structure.

And one more thing: one man with a significantly larger effective ownership stake in everything, achieved not through milestones approved by courts and shareholders, but through a transaction approved by boards he controls.

One man. One stack. No seams. No milestones required.

The rockets land beautifully. The engineer remains brilliant. The ambition is still Promethean. The power, combined, would be without precedent in the history of private enterprise.

And the question this series asked in its conclusion remains the only question that matters:

Who governs this?

The answer, after the combination, would be even cleaner than before.

No one.
