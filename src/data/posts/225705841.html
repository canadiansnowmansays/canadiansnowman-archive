On September 24, 2026, President Trump hosted Xi Jinping at a state dinner, and the photographs from the East Room showed who was in favour: Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Jensen Huang, Mark Zuckerberg. The Diplomat’s Ceiling entry noted the absence at the time. Dario Amodei, the chief executive who had just asked the industry to slow down, was not there.

On September 27, Ashley Capoot reported for CNBC that he would get a dinner of his own. Amodei was to join the president privately at the White House that Sunday evening, September 27 — the first one-on-one meeting between the two men, according to a source familiar with the arrangements. The explanation offered was a scheduling conflict: Amodei could not make the state dinner, so Trump invited him to a separate one. Perhaps. But the week surrounding that supper tells a more complicated story about how this administration treats a company that says inconvenient things.

The angel remark

The context is Amodei’s September slowdown essay (Parts 97 and 99), with its three-step proposal and its call for more government oversight. It won support from Altman and Musk. It enraged the president. Trump’s response on Truth Social deserves to be quoted in full, because it tells you how the White House reads safety talk:

“The only control or ‘guardrails’ that AI needs is a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT, and the U.S.A. has that, in spades! The Trump Administration has stopped AI ‘people’ from doing bad, or potentially bad, ‘things,’ like Dario (Anthropic!), who is now pretending to be a ‘perfect little angel’ — and we will continue to do so!”

Set aside the capital letters. The substance is an accusation this series has examined from the other side in Part 129: that a company’s safety posture is a performance. Trump thinks Amodei is pretending. Some readers suspect OpenAI of pretending when it withholds a model. The president and the sceptics arrive at the same suspicion from opposite directions — with the difference that Trump’s alternative to company self-restraint is not independent regulation but, in his words, himself.

Amodei was already in a difficult position. He supported Kamala Harris in 2024 and was a vocal Trump critic. Yet before the slowdown essay, CNBC reports, the relationship had been on the mend.

Two governments, one company

This is where the reporting is most revealing, because it shows that “the administration” is not one relationship but several, running in parallel and in contradiction.

At Commerce, things are warm. Howard Lutnick, who temporarily imposed export controls on two of Anthropic’s most advanced models in June, told Axios the company was “back on the right side,” and said on the sidelines of a G20 ministerial in North Carolina: “We trust Anthropic.” He hosted a fireside chat there with Tom Brown, the Anthropic co-founder and chief compute officer who, CNBC reports, replaced Amodei as the company’s negotiator with Commerce over the export directive. Part 102 noted the access asymmetry in September: Amodei frozen out, Brown with regular access. Now it has been confirmed on the record. On the same day, Michael Kratsios, director of the White House science office, told CNBC the administration was “in a good cadence” with Anthropic, which was “open” and actively sharing models with the government under Trump’s voluntary executive order.

At the Pentagon, things could hardly be colder. Talks collapsed in February, when the Defense Department demanded unfettered access to Anthropic’s models “across all lawful purposes” and Anthropic sought assurance that its technology would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or domestic mass surveillance. The department then labelled Anthropic a “supply chain risk” — an unprecedented designation implying the company threatens national security — barring the military and its contractors from using its models. Anthropic sued. On Friday, September 25, a federal appeals panel in Washington upheld the Pentagon. The D.C. Circuit split two to one. Judges Gregory Katsas and Neomi Rao found that “the Department had ample support for its conclusion” that keeping Claude in its information systems “presented a statutorily covered national-security risk,” and added: “But in our Republic, it is the President and the Secretary of War who must determine how best to balance the competing risks.” Judge Karen LeCraft Henderson dissented, arguing that the statute reaches deliberate, deceptive acts, and not the “honest and upfront enforcement of restrictions” on how a product is used, as Ana-Maria Stanciuc reported for The Next Web. The ruling left two courts at odds, since a federal court in San Francisco had struck down the other designation in August. “We remain confident in our position and are considering all options, including further review,” an Anthropic spokesperson told CNBC. It did not stay undecided for long: on October 13, CNBC reported that Anthropic’s lawyers had formally petitioned the D.C. court for a panel or en banc rehearing. By mid-November, the full court had not ruled.

The victory laps were not dignified. Emil Michael, the department’s technology chief, posted on X: “The hammer of justice has smashed @AnthropicAI arguments. They are a Supply Chain Risk to the defence industrial base serving the @DeptofWar. Warfighters will sleep better knowing that no private company will insert their opinions in the chain of command.” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who had accused Anthropic of “duplicity” and of trying “to strong-arm the United States military into submission,” added: “Confirmed: @AnthropicAI = Supply Chain Risk.”

What the red lines were

It is worth stating plainly, because the posts are designed to obscure it: Anthropic has been designated a national-security risk because it refused to let its models be used for fully autonomous weapons or for the mass surveillance of Americans. Those are the “opinions” Michael says will no longer be inserted “in the chain of command.” One can argue about whether a private company should set conditions on military use of its products; reasonable people disagree, and a defence department that cannot rely on its suppliers has a real problem. But the conditions themselves — no killing without a human decision, no surveillance of the domestic population — are not exotic. They are close to what most democracies would describe as ordinary law.

How the ban began

The feud has a paper trail. As David E. Sanger, Dustin Volz, Ana Swanson and Julian E. Barnes reported for The New York Times on August 16, 2026, the talks collapsed in a February meeting at which Amodei told Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth that Anthropic would not sell to the military without two assurances: no use for domestic surveillance of Americans, and no fully autonomous weapons operating without a human in the loop. Hegseth treated the request as a category error. Raytheon, he argued, does not get to dictate how its missiles are used. It is a coherent position with one flaw: a large language model is not a missile. A missile does what it is pointed at; a model can be asked to do things nobody ever verified it against, which is the whole reason Anthropic exists as a separate company.

What followed was a household arguing about the thermostat. In mid-July the Air Force ordered contractors to strip all Anthropic products from weapons and control systems by September 1, on pain of losing their Pentagon business. A month later it quietly told them to disregard the order pending litigation, while the department insisted publicly that the purge remained “absolute.” Emil Michael, asked what Anthropic could do to get back into the department’s good graces, answered: not at the Department of War. And yet, when Anthropic released Mythos, the most capable vulnerability-hunting model in existence, the National Security Agency was allowed to keep using it on an “experimental” basis, both to harden Pentagon networks and to probe those of China, Russia, North Korea and Iran. The ban was absolute everywhere except where the government most needed the product. That is not governance. It is a thermostat war with classified stakes.

What is new is the pattern the week reveals. Commerce trusts Anthropic enough to share models with it; the Pentagon calls it a threat to the defence industrial base; the president mocks its chief executive as a pretend angel on Monday and dines with him privately on Sunday. And on Tuesday, September 29, Amodei was back at the White House among the industry leaders Trump had summoned — the same meeting that, as Capex Ledger XXV reports, sent Republicans in close races scrambling to distance themselves from the president’s data-centre push.

After the dinner

The dinner did happen, and the president came out of it talking differently. In an interview with Time published on Thursday, October 1, Trump said: “I had a dinner with Dario for two and a half hours.” He called Amodei “a very smart guy” who was “maybe different than I thought,” and said: “I spoke to him about his views, and they’re much different, I think, than what is portrayed in the media,” as Matt Novak reported for Gizmodo. Gizmodo also placed Amodei among the A.I. executives at the White House event outside the West Wing on September 29, two days after the dinner.

Not everyone in the president’s orbit wanted the thaw. Before the dinner, opponents of Amodei circulated a hostile brief about him to the White House, Axios reported on September 27, 2026. One line, quoted by The American Bazaar, read: “Dario claimed that AI would eliminate half of all entry-level white-collar jobs as soon as this year. WRONG!” The memo did not stop the dinner. It does show that access in this White House is contested by rival courtiers before it is granted by the king.

The I.P.O. in the room

None of this happens in a vacuum. Anthropic was preparing for what was widely expected to be one of the largest public offerings in history (Capex Ledger XX and XXIV). When it priced on November 18, it was the largest ever: $81.3 billion of stock, valuing the company at more than $1.5 trillion, The New York Times reported. A company going to market cannot carry an open feud with the president and a Pentagon blacklist into its prospectus without a line on risk factors that investors will read closely. It did not try. The filing, reported in early October, warned of possible “material revenue losses or business disruptions” from the February presidential order halting federal agencies’ use of its models, from the Pentagon’s supply-chain designation, and from the June export restrictions, according to Proactive Investors on October 2. As of early October 2026, the company was aiming to list as soon as mid-November. A private dinner, whatever else it is, is a hedge.

That is not a criticism of Amodei for accepting. A chief executive invited to dinner by the president goes to dinner. It is a description of how power works in this administration: not through regulation, which it has refused, nor through the independent evaluation Part 124 found wanting, but through access, favour and public humiliation, dispensed personally. White House officials put it in the house style: “America will lead the world in Super Intelligence, while protecting American consumers.”

The ledger

A company that is a national-security risk to one department and a trusted partner to another, mocked on social media and fed at the president’s table in the same week, is not being governed. It is being courted and punished by a court — in the older sense of the word. Anthropic’s safety positions, whatever their mix of sincerity and strategy (Part 130 examines that mix), have been answered not with law but with the royal favour of Part 102’s “Blind Leading the Sighted,” and with the language of the “hammer of justice” from a Pentagon that bans a supplier for refusing to build autonomous weapons.

Hinton said in Part 118 that the danger was not only the technology but a political system in which lawmakers fear the president more than the risk. The last week of September showed the other half of that: a system in which the companies fear the president too. Beyond the president’s account to Time, nobody knows what was said over dinner. That is rather the point.
