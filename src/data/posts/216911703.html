Article 27 of the UN Charter is, on its face, a modest piece of drafting: decisions of the Security Council on procedural matters require the affirmative vote of any nine of the fifteen members, while decisions on all other matters require nine votes including the concurring votes of all five permanent members. It does not use the word “veto” anywhere in its text. But that clause, agreed at Yalta and defended without apology at San Francisco, has done more to shape what the United Nations has and has not been able to do over the past eighty years than any other sentence in the Charter, and its record since 1946 is now long enough, and specific enough, to answer the question the last essay in this series left open: did concentrating power in five hands actually produce an organisation more capable of acting than the League’s unanimity rule had been, or did it simply relocate the same paralysis to a smaller, more predictable address?

The first veto, and the pattern it set

The Security Council’s first veto was cast on 16 February 1946 — barely four months after the Charter entered into force — by the Soviet Union, blocking a resolution concerning the withdrawal of foreign troops from Lebanon and Syria. It was an early signal of what the veto’s actual use would look like in practice, and the pattern it established held for the better part of a quarter-century. Between 1946 and 1970, the Soviet Union cast the overwhelming majority of all vetoes recorded — more than one hundred of them, against a combined total in the single digits from every other permanent member — while the United States did not cast its first veto until 17 March 1970, on a resolution concerning white-minority rule in Southern Rhodesia. A significant share of the USSR’s early vetoes, roughly half by some counts, were used for a purpose that has mostly faded from public memory: blocking the admission of new UN member states Moscow judged too closely aligned with the West, a Cold War numbers game played out one membership application at a time in a chamber built, in theory, to keep the peace between great powers rather than to referee who got a seat at all.

There is a mechanical nuance worth pausing on here, because it becomes important almost immediately once this series turns to the Korean War in the next essay but one. A permanent member’s veto blocks a resolution only if that member actively casts a negative vote; an abstention, by contrast, does not block anything, even though the Charter’s text technically calls for the “concurring votes” of all five permanent members on substantive matters. This reading — that an abstention counts as acquiescence rather than as a missing concurrence — was never explicitly spelled out in San Francisco and has been treated, by long-standing Council practice rather than by any amendment to the Charter’s actual wording, as settled law since the late 1940s. It is the kind of quiet interpretive shortcut that keeps an institution functioning day to day, and it would prove decisive, almost by accident, the first time a permanent member’s absence from the table — rather than its presence and its veto — let the Council act with real consequence.

Around this same basic mechanism sat the “double veto” wrinkle the previous essay in this series flagged at San Francisco itself: because any permanent member could veto even the preliminary question of whether a given matter was procedural or substantive, the supposedly narrow, well-policed boundary of the veto’s reach turned out, in practice, to have almost no fixed edge at all. Diplomats spent the Council’s early years testing exactly how far that elasticity extended, and the answer, consistently, was: about as far as whichever permanent member wanted to stretch it on a given day.

The arithmetic shifted after 1970, and shifted specifically, rather than generally. Since casting that first veto over Rhodesia, the United States has used the veto more than eighty times — more than any other permanent member across the same fifty-five-year span — and the overwhelming majority of those vetoes have concerned one recurring subject: resolutions relating to Israel and the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, more than forty of them by most counts, covering settlement construction, military operations in Gaza and Lebanon, and proposed ceasefire and humanitarian resolutions across five decades of recurring warfare. The United Kingdom has cast around thirty vetoes in total, the earliest during the 1956 Suez crisis; France has cast fewer, eighteen, its first in August 1947 over the Indonesian question, and its most consequential alongside Britain in 1956, the two of them belligerents rather than neutral arbiters. Britain and France were on the same side at Suez. China, which inherited its Council seat from the Republic of China in 1971, with has used the veto sparingly — twenty-one times, counting one Republic of China veto in 1955 — , France, at 18, has used it less than China.

The question this series keeps returning to

Look across the full run of vetoes cast since 1946 — several hundred of them, well over half attributable to just two of the five permanent members, the Soviet Union/Russia and the United States — and a pattern emerges that has nothing to do with which side of the Cold War, or its aftermath, any particular veto-caster happened to sit on. The vetoes cluster, overwhelmingly, around conflicts and disputes in which a permanent member has direct interests at stake: its own military actions, its client states, its territorial claims, or its closest strategic allies. Resolutions condemning apartheid South Africa were repeatedly vetoed, jointly, by the US, UK, and France through the 1970s and 80s. Resolutions on Vietnam, Afghanistan, and Central America drew vetoes from whichever superpower’s own conduct was under discussion. Resolutions on Syria since 2011 have drawn a long run of Russian and Chinese vetoes, protecting an ally the Council might otherwise have moved against. And the Israeli-Palestinian resolutions that account for the bulk of American vetoes since 1970 involve, each time, a state Washington has treated as a core strategic partner for the entire period in question.

This is, in other words, not really a pattern of paralysis in the way the League’s failures were paralysis. The League could not act against Japan, Italy, or Germany because every member state, individually weak, needed to agree collectively to act against a member state that simply refused. The UN Security Council, by contrast, can act with real speed and real consequence against states that no permanent member is protecting — as the next Parr VI in this series shows, using the Korean War as its case study, and as the Council has repeatedly demonstrated since, imposing binding sanctions regimes on North Korea, on Iran’s nuclear programme before 2015, on various African states during civil conflicts, none of them permanent members or close enough to one to trigger its protection. What the Council cannot do — structurally, permanently, by the design San Francisco’s framers chose with their eyes open — is act against a permanent member itself, or against any state a permanent member has decided to shield. That is not a bug the UN’s architects failed to anticipate. It is, quite precisely, the trade Roosevelt’s generation made at Yalta: an organisation the great powers would actually join and fund, purchased at the specific, foreseeable price of an organisation that could never bind those same great powers against their will.

East-West deadlock, then and now

This is the structural fact this series flagged at its very start that the Council goes silent, reliably, on precisely the contentious East-West questions where the world most needs it to speak, and that this is not the Council malfunctioning but the Council functioning exactly as designed. Hungary in 1956, when Soviet tanks crushed a popular uprising against Moscow’s client government within days of the Council even convening to discuss it, and the Council could do nothing beyond debate, because the aggressor held a veto and used it without hesitation. Afghanistan in 1979, when the same dynamic applied to the Soviet invasion itself, the Council able to hold emergency sessions and draft resolutions but never to pass one with binding force. Syria for most of the past decade and a half, protected by a patient, repeated Russian and Chinese veto — more than a dozen combined vetoes since 2011 alone — whenever the Council has moved toward binding action on the civil war, chemical weapons use, or humanitarian access. Ukraine since February 2022, where the Council has been unable to pass a single binding resolution condemning or constraining the invasion, for the simplest possible reason: the invading power sits permanently at the table with the power to block any resolution concerning its own conduct, a structural fact no amount of Assembly condemnation — and the Assembly has condemned the invasion repeatedly, by lopsided majorities exceeding 140 states, in resolutions that bind no one — can work around. In each of these four cases, separated by nearly seventy years and covering opposite ends of the Cold War and its long afterlife, the mechanism of failure is identical: not a lack of votes, not a lack of will among the other fourteen members, but one seat at the table occupied by the party whose own conduct is the question on the floor.

The Suez crisis of 1956 supplies a case worth dwelling on separately, because it shows the same mechanism operating from the opposite political direction and, crucially, previews the workaround this series covers next. When Britain and France, in coordination with Israel, invaded Egypt that October following Gamal Abdel Nasser’s nationalisation of the Suez Canal, both Britain and France sat as permanent Council members with the power to veto any resolution condemning their own military action — and both did exactly that, casting joint vetoes on 30 October 1956 to block a US-sponsored ceasefire resolution. It was Britain’s first veto ever (France had cast its first nine years earlier, over Indonesia) deployed not to protect an ally’s conduct but their own, and it demonstrated with unusual clarity that the veto’s paralysing effect applied just as forcefully to Western permanent members acting in their own perceived interest as it had, for the preceding decade, applied almost exclusively to Soviet obstruction. Faced with a Council frozen by its own founding members, the Assembly reached for a procedural tool devised six years earlier for an entirely different crisis — the “Uniting for Peace” mechanism this series takes up properly in part VI — and used it to authorise the UN’s first large-scale peacekeeping force, deployed to Suez within days, a genuine institutional success that emerged directly from a Council failure rather than in spite of one.

The Congo, and an attack on the Secretariat itself

The veto’s reach was not always confined to Council votes; the Congo crisis of the early 1960s shows the same great-power friction spilling over into an attempt to break the office meant to sit above it. When Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld authorised UN peacekeeping intervention in the newly independent Congo in 1960, deploying troops to contain a secessionist crisis the Council itself had only loosely mandated, Moscow accused him of favouring Western interests over those of Patrice Lumumba, the Congolese prime minister dismissed in September 1960 and murdered in January 1961 under circumstances that implicated Belgian and possibly American intelligence involvement. Soviet Premier Khrushchev responded not merely by criticising Hammarskjöld’s judgment but by proposing, before the General Assembly in September 1960, that the office of Secretary-General be abolished altogether and replaced with a three-person “troika” — one representative each from the Western bloc, the Soviet bloc, and the non-aligned world — each holding an effective veto over the Secretariat’s own actions, on the theory that a single Secretary-General could never be trusted to act neutrally between Cold War camps. The proposal went nowhere, and Hammarskjöld himself died a year later, in September 1961, in a still-unexplained plane crash in Northern Rhodesia while flying to negotiate a Congo ceasefire, with Moscow still pressing the troika idea. But the episode is a useful reminder that the veto’s underlying logic — that no institutional actor should be trusted to bind a great power without that power’s own consent — was never confined to the Council chamber alone; it was an instinct the same governments were prepared to extend to the Secretariat, the peacekeeping mission, and, had Khrushchev’s proposal succeeded, to the very idea of a single official capable of speaking for the organisation as a whole.

The vetoes that were never cast

Raw veto counts, however striking, understate the mechanism’s actual reach, because the number that matters most is not how many resolutions were vetoed but how many were never drafted, or were quietly softened before a vote, precisely because everyone involved already knew how a particular permanent member would respond. Diplomats at the UN call this the “pocket veto” or sometimes, less formally, the “hidden veto” — the resolutions that die in a Secretariat drafting room or a Council consultation, stripped of any binding language a permanent member has signalled it would block, long before they ever reach a formal vote that would register in the historical count. There is no reliable tally of how often this happens, by its nature, but Council diplomats and UN-watchers alike generally treat it as the more common outcome by a wide margin: the formal veto, cast on the record, is the exception that occurs when a drafting state either miscalculated or deliberately wanted the veto on the record for political effect, rather than the typical mechanism by which the threat of a veto shapes what the Council does and does not attempt.

None of this makes the Council’s design a simple failure, any more than the League’s design was simply a failure of good intentions. It makes it a very specific kind of institution: one built to manage disputes among states the great powers are not personally invested in, and structurally incapable of managing disputes involving the great powers themselves. Whether that is the best available trade, or merely a more sophisticated version of the same unanimity problem that sank the League, dressed up as a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight, is the question this series keeps circling back to, and it is worth sitting with rather than resolving prematurely — because the next several essays, moving from Korea’s accidental loophole through the Cold War’s theatre and on to Rwanda, Iraq, and Syria, are really extended case studies in exactly how that trade has played out, conflict by conflict, for eighty years.
