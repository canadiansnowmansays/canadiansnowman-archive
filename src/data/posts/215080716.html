A Restaurant, a Beggar, and the Same Street

On a rooftop in Kabul, at a restaurant called Ziyafat, men sit under illuminated planters and eat a version of the modern Gulf-state dinner scene the Taliban would like the world to see. A short walk away, on the same evening, a girl named Marwa — dirt on her face, wearing an orange dress — begs near the same illuminated storefronts. As Elian Peltier reported for The New York Times on September 10, 2026, twenty-five years to the week after the attacks that first put Afghanistan on Washington’s target list, this is the country the Taliban now govern: a rooftop dinner and a child in the street, thirty feet apart, both true, neither cancelling the other out.

I finished the Coda to this series on August 15, 2026, arguing that The Atlantic’s “silver lining” framing — al-Qaeda hasn’t struck the West since 2021, therefore something in Afghanistan is working — measured the country by a single Washington-facing metric and called the result a sunrise. The anniversary piece published this week doesn’t overturn that argument. It sharpens it, with a level of specificity the August piece didn’t have room for, and it arrives alongside five more months of exactly the pattern the Epilogue documented in April: Pakistan’s war grinding on, the Taliban’s authority fraying at its edges, and every actor in the region still applying the same self-interested test to the same government — is it useful to me this week — that this series has been tracking since Part 1.

What “Moderately Cooperative” Looks Like Up Close

The Coda relayed the Biden-to-Trump-era counterterrorism assessment: the Taliban rate as “moderately cooperative” against the Islamic State’s regional franchise, ISKP, and al-Qaeda has not rebuilt itself into a force capable of striking Western capitals. The anniversary piece supplies the texture behind that verdict, and the texture complicates the compliment.

A few dozen al-Qaeda members live in Afghanistan today — far below the tens of thousands present before 2001, but not zero, and not hidden. Some of them live openly in Kabul. The Afghan government has issued national identity cards to leadership figures in al-Qaeda’s South Asian branch, according to the United Nations’ monitoring group on terrorist networks. Colin Smith, who coordinates that group’s reporting from more than forty intelligence agencies, put it about as plainly as an official monitoring al-Qaeda’s role in a country can put it: nobody can say with confidence what that role actually is anymore.

That is not the profile of a government that has expelled its old guests. It’s the profile of a government that has learned to keep them close enough to manage and far enough from the cameras to maintain deniability — the same balancing act Part 9 described the Taliban performing in the run-up to 2001, before deniability collapsed on a Tuesday morning in September. Bin Laden’s successor, Ayman al-Zawahiri, was killed by an American drone strike on a Kabul balcony in 2022; his would-be successor is reportedly sheltering across the border in Iran, and the Taliban — for reasons of self-preservation, not principle — appear unwilling to let him back in. That is the entirety of the “moderately cooperative” case. It is real. It is also thinner than a magazine headline about silver linings suggests.

Roads Are Safe, Markets Are Busy

There is a version of this anniversary story that isn’t cynical, and it deserves a paragraph before the ledger gets grim again, because the anniversary piece is honest enough to include it. Afghans interviewed for the September 10 report describe their country now mostly by what it is no longer: at war, in the conventional sense of front lines and artillery inside Kabul itself. Roads outside the capital that were death traps in 2019 are passable. Markets are busy. Mining deals with Uzbekistan and India move forward. New restaurants — Ziyafat among them — mimic the polish of Dubai for a small class of Afghans and foreign visitors who can afford them. Weapons are still everywhere and former fighters still guard checkpoints, but the daily terror of drone strikes and ambush crossfire that Part 9 documented through the occupation years has genuinely receded for people who don’t live near the Pakistani border.

This is the honest half of the Taliban’s pitch, and it is the half that makes the rest of the story so hard to dismiss as pure propaganda. A country can be simultaneously calmer than it has been in a generation and still failing the people who most need it to succeed — the amputee children at Ghazni’s rehabilitation centres, the girls locked out of secondary school since 2021, Marwa on her corner near the rooftop restaurant. Calm is not the same as just. The Taliban have delivered a measure of the first. Twenty-five years on, they have delivered almost none of the second, and the anniversary piece’s own photographs — a girl begging feet from an upscale dinner, a woman in a niqab standing at the edge of a men’s conversation she is barred from joining — make that gap visible without needing a caption to explain it.

The Larger Ecosystem Washington Doesn’t Count

Al-Qaeda was never the whole militant picture in Afghanistan, and the anniversary reporting is useful for laying out just how crowded the field still is. United Nations experts estimate 15,000 to 20,000 militants scattered across roughly twenty armed groups inside the country; Russian officials put the number as high as 23,000. Thousands of Islamic State fighters move through the borderlands with Pakistan. And the largest contingent by far is Tehrik-e-Taliban Pakistan — the Pakistani Taliban, the TTP — using Afghan territory as a staging ground for attacks across the border, with what security officials and analysts describe as a permissive environment the Afghan Taliban either cannot or will not close off.

This is the detail that ties the anniversary piece directly back to the war this series has been documenting since the Epilogue. The Afghan Taliban deny arming the TTP. Privately, by the anniversary reporting’s own account, Afghan officials admit they could not rein the group in even if they wanted to — the same admission of limited central authority that Part 8 traced back through Ahmad Shah Durrani’s collapsed eighteenth-century empire, the same mountain-and-valley loyalty structure that let Nuristani tribal elders negotiate their own local ceasefire with Chitral in April without so much as inviting Kabul to the table. Twenty-five years, and the government sitting in the capital still cannot fully answer for what happens in its own mountains. That was true of every government this series has covered. It remains true of this one.

The War Didn’t Stop. It Just Stopped Making Headlines.

Readers of the Epilogue will remember where the Pakistan-Afghanistan war stood in late April: a Ramadan ceasefire broken within hours, a drug rehabilitation centre named Omid — Hope — reduced to rubble with roughly 400 dead, a Chinese-brokered talks process in Urumqi that produced warm language and no enforceable terms, and a university in Kunar shelled on the day I finished writing.

The anniversary piece confirms the war is still the backdrop, not a closed chapter. Anti-Taliban opposition groups — three of them, led by former officers of the American-trained Afghan military that collapsed in August 2021 — have claimed more than fifty attacks on Taliban checkpoints, officials, and buildings since July 2026 alone.

Pakistani airstrikes, in retaliation for continued TTP activity, have killed more than 500 Afghan civilians this year by the United Nations mission’s count. Two-thirds of Afghan territory remains contaminated with unexploded ordnance from four decades of continuous war.

And on August 15, 2026 — the same day I published the Coda — Taliban soldiers paraded through Kabul with an effigy of President Trump, then stabbed it, in commemoration of the fifth anniversary of their return to power.

The photograph is almost too on the nose to need commentary: a government marking its survival by ritually murdering a doll of the American president whose administration cut the humanitarian aid this series’ Part 10 documented as killing Afghan children by the thousand, while that same government privately courts Washington’s counterterrorism apparatus as a “moderately cooperative” partner against a rival jihadist faction.

Threaten the old enemy in effigy. Accommodate the new one in private briefings. The TACO pattern this series has applied to Washington fits Kabul just as well; it simply runs in the opposite direction, toward whichever patron currently has leverage.

It is worth remembering, too, what Farhad Sangari — the Taliban commander of a special-forces unit running traffic patrol in Kabul, quoted in the anniversary reporting — actually said about the war he fought to win: that he misses the front line, misses the sense of purpose that came with jihad. Twenty-five years after the attacks that brought American troops into these same mountains, the men who eventually outlasted them are now the ones providing security, running checkpoints, and privately admitting they’d rather be fighting than governing. Michael Semple, the Queen’s University Belfast analyst the anniversary piece cites, asks the harder question underneath that admission: how many hundreds of thousands of young men have now been educated in madrasas built since 2021, taught an ideology that glorifies exactly the kind of fight Sangari misses? A government staffed by men who long for combat, training a rising generation to want the same thing, is not a government that has solved its militancy problem. It has simply relocated where that problem currently points — outward, at Pakistan, for now.

The Ledger, Updated Again

The Coda proposed what an honest ledger for Afghanistan would need to include if it wasn’t just counting attacks that never reached Western capitals. Five months and one anniversary piece later, here is that ledger, updated:

No successful al-Qaeda attack on the US or Europe launched from Afghan soil since 2021 — true, and the entire basis of the “silver lining” argument.

Twenty-one point nine million Afghans needing humanitarian assistance before Pakistan fired a shot in February, a number this series has not seen improve in any subsequent reporting.

A war with a “major non-NATO ally” that has now run for more than half a year, killed hundreds of civilians in strikes on a rehabilitation centre and a university, and drawn nothing sharper from Washington than an acknowledgment of Pakistan’s right to keep striking.

Fifty-plus attacks by anti-Taliban resistance factions since July — the first sustained armed opposition to Taliban rule since 2021, a detail that got no mention in the silver-lining framing because it doesn’t touch the metric that framing was built to measure.

A few dozen al-Qaeda members holding government-issued identification in the capital, present in numbers too small to threaten New York or Paris and too significant for the UN’s own monitors to describe with confidence.

None of these facts contradicts the narrow claim that al-Qaeda hasn’t restruck the West. All of them explain why “silver lining” was the wrong metaphor from the start. A silver lining implies a cloud that is, on the whole, passing. Afghanistan’s cloud has not passed. It has simply stopped raining on the one capital keeping score.

The Count, Again

2,500 years since the first Persian satraps learned that Afghanistan answers to no one for long. Roughly 250 to 300 of those years spent at genuine peace. The rest — invasion, occupation, resistance, collapse, and now this: a government that survived the empire that tried hardest to remake it, sheltering the remnant of the movement that provoked that empire’s arrival in the first place, fighting a war with its neighbour that a magazine three thousand miles away chose not to mention while calling the country’s condition a silver lining.

Marwa is still on that street near Ziyafat. The restaurant is still serving. The effigy has already been replaced with a new one, somewhere, for whatever comes next. Twenty-five years after the attacks that gave this country its second American war, the mountains have not moved, the tribal loyalties have not thinned, and the pattern — invite, use, discard, deny — is still running exactly as it ran for the Persians, the British, the Soviets, and the Americans before this year’s Pakistani bombs and this week’s anniversary reporting.

The graveyard keeps its accounts in centuries. Washington keeps its in news cycles. Only one of those ledgers is actually being paid down.
