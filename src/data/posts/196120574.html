The Empress Dowager Cixi, who governed China in everything but title for forty-seven years — choosing the dynasty's survival over its transformation. The dynasty did not survive.

The Empress Dowager Cixi ran China for nearly half a century without ever formally holding power.

She arrived at the imperial court as a low-ranking concubine of the Xianfeng Emperor in 1851, at the age of sixteen. She was intelligent, politically skilled, and patient in a way that the court’s formal power structure — which excluded women entirely — could not accommodate directly. When the Xianfeng Emperor died in 1861, she manoeuvred herself into the position of co-regent for her five-year-old son, neutralised the eight senior officials the emperor had appointed as regents, and governed China in everything but title for the next forty-seven years, through the reigns of two emperors she effectively controlled.

She is one of the most consequential figures in Chinese history, and also one of the most blamed. The Century of Humiliation accelerated under her management: the defeats in the Sino-French War, the catastrophe of the Sino-Japanese War, the decision to support the Boxer Uprising, the flight from Beijing in a cart disguised as a peasant. The case against her is substantial. She crushed the Hundred Days’ Reform that might have modernised the Qing state in time to resist further dismemberment. She spent naval appropriations on rebuilding the Summer Palace rather than buying modern warships — a decision that became famous when the Chinese fleet encountered the Japanese at the Battle of the Yellow Sea in 1894 and discovered that its flagship’s cannon were loaded with practice shells rather than live ammunition, because the powder budget had been reallocated to garden construction.

What the case against Cixi often underweights is the scale of what she inherited. The Taiping Rebellion — which raged from 1850 to 1864, precisely during the formative decades of her regency — was one of the deadliest conflicts in human history. A failed examination candidate named Hong Xiuquan, convinced after a nervous breakdown that he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ, raised an army in southern China that captured the city of Nanjing and held it for eleven years as the capital of the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace. The Qing armies required more than a decade, the assistance of foreign-commanded mercenary forces, and an estimated twenty to thirty million deaths — some estimates run considerably higher — to suppress the rebellion.

The Qing dynasty that Cixi ran for forty-seven years had been half-destroyed by an internal civil war before the foreign powers finished the work. The question of whether better leadership could have saved it is genuine, but the dynasty Cixi inherited was not a healthy state facing external pressure. It was a severely weakened state facing external pressure that it could no longer resist by any available means.

The case for her is more complicated, and Chinese historians have in recent decades attempted to make it. She was operating within institutional constraints that no amount of personal intelligence could dissolve. The Qing dynasty was a Manchu dynasty governing a Han Chinese majority through an administrative system designed for stability rather than adaptation. Reform at the pace the situation required would have destabilised the dynasty itself. She chose the dynasty’s survival over its transformation — and the dynasty did not survive.

When Cixi died on November 15, 1908, one day after the Guangxu Emperor she had kept confined for a decade, China had less than four years left as an imperial state.

The revolution that ended the dynasty in 1912 was not the revolution its architects had planned.

Sun Yat-sen, the physician turned revolutionary who had spent decades organising anti-Qing resistance from exile in Japan, the United States, and Europe, was in Denver, Colorado, reading a newspaper, when the Wuchang Uprising broke out on October 10, 1911. The uprising was triggered by a group of New Army soldiers who discovered that their names were on a list of revolutionaries about to be arrested — and decided that launching the revolution immediately was preferable to waiting to be executed. It was, by any measure, an accidental revolution.

It succeeded anyway. Provincial assemblies across China declared independence from the Qing in rapid succession. The court, desperate, recalled Yuan Shikai — the general who commanded the most effective military force in China, whom the Empress Dowager had dismissed from service after her death — and gave him the power to negotiate with the revolutionaries. Yuan Shikai negotiated successfully. He negotiated for himself.

On February 12, 1912, the Xuantong Emperor — the child Puyi, aged five — abdicated in favour of a republic. The last emperor of China signed the document his regent placed before him without understanding what he was signing. He would later star, involuntarily, in some of the 20th century’s more extraordinary historical sequences: warlord period, Japanese puppet emperor of Manchukuo, Soviet prisoner of war, Maoist reeducation subject, and finally, in his last years, a gardener at the Beijing Botanical Garden who signed autographs for tourists.

Sun Yat-sen became the provisional president of the new Republic of China. He held the position for forty-five days before handing it to Yuan Shikai in exchange for Yuan’s agreement to support the republic and ensure the abdication remained peaceful.

It was Sun Yat-sen’s greatest mistake, and he knew it almost immediately.

Yuan Shikai negotiated the abdication successfully — for himself. Within three years he dissolved parliament; in 1915 he tried to declare himself emperor. The provinces refused.

Yuan Shikai had no interest in a republic. He had interest in power, and a republic was a means of acquiring it from a position that the old imperial system no longer offered. Within a year, he was ruling by decree and suppressing the parliamentary opposition. Within two years, he had dissolved parliament. In 1915, he attempted to declare himself emperor of a new dynasty, with himself as founder.

The provinces that had rallied to the republic refused. Military commanders who had supported the revolution against the Qing were not prepared to support a new imperial system under Yuan. The Eight-Power Rebellion against the new empire collapsed Yuan’s ambitions faster than the revolution had collapsed the Qing. He died of kidney failure in June 1916, three months after abandoning the imperial project, still president of a republic that no longer functioned.

What followed his death is known in Chinese history as the Warlord Era, and it is one of the more remarkable episodes in the political history of any country: a vast, ancient civilisation with a sophisticated bureaucratic tradition simply dissolved into competing military fiefdoms for more than a decade.

The logic was straightforward, if brutal. Yuan Shikai had concentrated military power in the hands of regional commanders to fight the republic’s opponents. Those commanders, absent central authority, became autonomous powers. They controlled territory, collected taxes, maintained armies, and fought each other for advantage. Beijing changed hands repeatedly. The nominal government of the republic collected foreign customs revenue — the foreign powers maintained their own mechanisms for this — but had little authority beyond the capital, and sometimes not even there.

For the Chinese population, the Warlord Era meant conscription into armies that provided no pay and extracted food from the countryside, taxation by whoever was currently in control of the district, and the general insecurity of living in a state that had ceased to function as a state. Estimates of deaths from warlord conflict, famine, and displacement between 1916 and 1928 vary widely, but millions died in a period of violence that has been overshadowed in Western historical memory by the later catastrophes of the Japanese occupation and the Civil War.

Out of this chaos, two political movements emerged that would define the next thirty years of Chinese history.

The Chinese Nationalist Party — the Kuomintang, or KMT — was Sun Yat-sen’s organisation, reorganised with Soviet assistance after Sun’s disillusionment with Western support for Chinese democracy. Sun died of liver cancer in 1925, before he could test his reorganised party against the warlords. His successor as KMT leader was a military officer who had trained in the Soviet Union and returned to command the Whampoa Military Academy in Canton: Chiang Kai-shek.

The Chinese Communist Party was founded in Shanghai in July 1921, in a French Concession building — a detail worth savouring, given subsequent history. The meeting was attended by thirteen delegates representing perhaps 57 party members nationwide. One of the delegates was a twenty-seven-year-old former library assistant from Hunan named Mao Zedong.

For most of the 1920s, the two parties cooperated in the First United Front, nominally allied against the warlords. Chiang Kai-shek’s Northern Expedition, launched in 1926, drove north from Canton and unified much of China under KMT control within two years. In April 1927, in Shanghai, Chiang turned on his Communist allies, ordering the massacre of Communist Party members and trade union organisers throughout KMT-controlled territory. The death toll ran to thousands. The First United Front was over.

The Chinese Communist Party went underground, then to the countryside. Mao Zedong, whose contribution to this period was the insight that a peasant revolution rather than a workers’ revolution was the correct strategy for China’s overwhelmingly agricultural society, began building a rural base in Jiangxi province. What followed — the decade of Communist survival against KMT encirclement campaigns, the Long March, the Second United Front against the Japanese, and the Civil War — is the subject of the next parts of this series.

The May Fourth Movement deserves a separate note here, because its significance extends well beyond its immediate occasion.

On May 4, 1919, thousands of students gathered in Tiananmen Square — the first major political demonstration in that space that would become the recurring stage of Chinese political history — to protest the terms of the Treaty of Versailles. The treaty had transferred Germany’s colonial concessions in China’s Shandong province not to China, which had contributed 140,000 labourers to the Allied war effort, but to Japan, which had done less but negotiated better.

The Shandong Question, as it was known, was the immediate trigger. But the May Fourth Movement became something larger: a cultural and intellectual revolution that questioned not just the foreign powers exploiting China but the Confucian tradition that, in the students’ analysis, had made China vulnerable to exploitation. The movement produced the generation of intellectuals who would lead both the KMT and the Communist Party, who would debate the relative merits of democracy, socialism, and anarchism in journals and coffee shops across China’s major cities, and who would eventually channel those debates into the guns of a civil war.

“Mr. Science and Mr. Democracy” — the two concepts the May Fourth Movement elevated as China’s salvation — became the shorthand for an era. The irony that neither fully arrived, and that what arrived instead was four more decades of war, occupation, and revolutionary violence, is one the participants could not have foreseen.

What the May Fourth Movement produced, beyond its immediate political impact, was the first generation of Chinese intellectuals who read Marx, Mill, Kropotkin, and Dewey in translation and tried to apply their ideas to the specific conditions of a country in civilisational crisis. The journals multiplied. New Youth, founded by Chen Duxiu in 1915, published calls for the abandonment of Confucian hierarchies, the liberation of women, the adoption of the vernacular in writing — the classical written language, which differed substantially from spoken Chinese, was an instrument of elite control that the movement attacked as a barrier between the people and the ideas that might save them. The decision to write in the vernacular was as radical in its context as Luther’s decision to publish in German rather than Latin. It democratised access to political ideas at the precise moment when political ideas were becoming matters of survival.

The debate that mattered most — between those who believed China’s salvation lay in liberalism and gradual reform, those who believed it lay in Marxist revolution, and those who believed it lay in nationalist mobilisation regardless of economic system — was not resolved by argument. It was resolved by the Japanese invasion, which made the question of political theory subordinate to the question of who could actually fight. The answer to that question would determine everything else.

Chen Duxiu, the intellectual leader of the May Fourth generation and co-founder of the Chinese Communist Party, died under house arrest in 1942 after being expelled from the party he had helped create for the crime of Trotskyism. Li Dazhao, the other co-founder, was strangled by warlord soldiers in 1927. Sun Yat-sen died of cancer. Chiang Kai-shek retreated to Taiwan. Mao Zedong became emperor in everything but name and presided over the deaths of tens of millions of his own citizens.

The generation that wanted Mr. Science and Mr. Democracy got the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution.

History has a particular cruelty toward idealists who arrive at the right moment with the right ideas and the wrong century.

Puyi, the last emperor of China, abdicated at the age of five — signing the document his regent placed before him without understanding it. He would later be warlord captive, Japanese puppet, Soviet prisoner, and finally a botanical-garden gardener.

The dynasty fell in 1912. What replaced it was not a republic in any functional sense. It was a competition — among warlords, nationalists, communists, and Japanese invaders — for the right to determine what China would become. That competition would not be resolved until October 1, 1949, when a man who had survived the Long March stood on the Gate of Heavenly Peace and told the world that the Chinese people had stood up.

But the ground on which Mao stood had been prepared by everything that came before. The Empress Dowager’s compromises. Yuan Shikai’s betrayals. The warlords’ chaos. The May Fourth generation’s dreams. The KMT’s Northern Expedition and its massacre of the left. All of it fed into the conditions that made a Communist revolution possible — and in the Chinese analysis, necessary.
