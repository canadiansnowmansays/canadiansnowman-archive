August 5, 2021: President Joe Biden signs an executive order on electric vehicles at the White House, surrounded by UAW leadership and executives from GM, Ford, and Stellantis. Tesla, despite dominating the U.S. EV market, was not invited.

There is a version of this series in which Part VIII does not exist. In that version, Elon Musk remains what he was through approximately 2020: a broadly apolitical technologist with libertarian instincts who voted for Obama, donated to both parties, supported a carbon tax, and treated politics as a distraction from engineering. In that version, the stack is a collection of technology companies run by a man whose primary interest is making them work. In that version, there is no X. There is no Grok positioned as an “anti-woke” chatbot. There is no Grokipedia designed to replace Wikipedia. There is no DOGE.

That version does not exist. Something happened between 2020 and 2022 that transformed Musk from an engineer who occasionally complained about regulations into an active combatant in the American culture war — a man who purchased a social media platform for $44 billion, gutted its content moderation apparatus, restored hundreds of banned far-right accounts, and aligned himself with a political movement whose relationship to empirical evidence is, to be charitable, inconsistent.

This essay examines what happened. The answer is not singular. It is a sequence of events — one institutional, one deeply personal — that converged with the radicalising dynamics of social media ownership to produce the political Musk who now shapes the stack’s every decision.

But honesty requires acknowledging that the political turn did not begin from zero. The kindling was already stacked before the match was struck. Musk had publicly fought California’s COVID lockdowns in 2020, defying Alameda County health orders to reopen the Fremont factory and threatening to move Tesla’s headquarters to Texas — which he subsequently did. He had sparred with the SEC over his tweeting habits since the 2018 “funding secured” episode. He had expressed scepticism about university culture, DEI programmes, and what he perceived as credentialism replacing competence in American institutions. His libertarian instincts — anti-regulatory, anti-union, suspicious of institutional authority — had been visible since the PayPal years documented in Part III of this series. The political Musk of 2022 did not emerge from nowhere. He emerged from a man already drifting rightward who encountered two events that gave his drift an emotional engine and a $44 billion budget.

The Snub

On August 5, 2021, President Joe Biden hosted an electric vehicle summit at the White House. The event was designed to announce an executive order setting a target of 50% zero-emission new vehicle sales by 2030. The CEOs of General Motors, Ford, and Stellantis attended. The president of the United Auto Workers attended. Executives from the three companies that employed the most UAW members stood with the president as he signed the order.

Tesla was not invited.

The company that had created the American electric vehicle market — that had proven EVs could be desirable, that had built more electric vehicles than every other American manufacturer combined, that held over 50% of the U.S. EV market share — was excluded from the event celebrating the electric vehicle future it had made possible.

When asked why, White House Press Secretary Jen Psaki offered a response that was admirably transparent in its political logic: “Well, these are the three largest employers of the United Auto Workers, so I’ll let you draw your own conclusion.”

The conclusion Musk drew was the one Psaki invited. Tesla’s workforce was not unionised. Biden’s political coalition depended on union support. Therefore, Tesla — the actual market leader — was invisible to the administration. Three months later, Biden compounded the insult. At a November 2021 event, he turned to GM CEO Mary Barra and said: “You electrified the entire automobile industry.” At the time, Tesla had delivered 115,000 EVs that quarter. GM had delivered 26.

President Biden with General Motors CEO Mary Barra. In November 2021, Biden told Barra that GM had ‘electrified the entire automobile industry’ — at a time when Tesla was delivering over four times as many electric vehicles per quarter

Musk’s response was immediate and public. “Seems odd that Tesla wasn’t invited,” he wrote on Twitter. Later, at the Code Conference, he said Biden was “controlled by unions” and that the administration was “not the friendliest.” He was correct on both points. The Biden White House had made a political calculation: union patronage was worth more than acknowledging the company that had done more for electrification than any entity on Earth. It was not a policy decision. It was not an oversight. It was the Democratic Party rewarding its donor base by pretending the EV revolution had been led by companies whose combined electric vehicle output could not fill a single Tesla delivery day. It was, as Kamala Harris herself later acknowledged in her memoir, a “big mistake.”

Harris wrote that she believed Biden was “sending a message about Musk’s anti-union stance” but that excluding him as the nation’s top EV innovator was counterproductive. She told Fortune in October 2025: “I have to assume that that was something that hit him hard and had an impact on his perspective.”

It hit him hard. And here is where the first principles framework from this series becomes essential. A first principles thinker, confronted with a political slight, would ask: what is the structural cause of this, and what is the most efficient response? The structural cause was clear — the UAW’s political leverage within the Democratic coalition. The efficient response would have been to either engage the political system on its own terms (lobbying, campaign contributions, strategic alliances) or to ignore the slight and let Tesla’s market performance speak for itself.

Musk did neither. He took it personally. And a first principles thinker who takes things personally is no longer thinking from first principles. He is thinking from grievance. The distinction matters because grievance, unlike physics, does not produce reliable engineering specifications.

The Loss

Elon Musk with his children from his first marriage in the early years.

The second event is more difficult to write about, and more important.

Elon Musk and Justine Wilson — married at Queen’s University, the Canadian connection that runs through this series — had six children together. Their first, Nevada, died of sudden infant death syndrome at ten weeks. Through IVF, they subsequently had twins in 2004, followed by triplets in 2006. The marriage ended in 2008.

One of the twins — raised as Xavier — came out as transgender at sixteen, took the name Vivian Jenna Wilson (adopting her mother’s maiden name), and in April 2022, at eighteen, petitioned a California court to legally change her name and gender marker. In the petition, she stated that she no longer wished to be related to her biological father “in any way, shape, or form.” The court approved the petition.

Musk has spoken publicly about this. In Walter Isaacson’s biography, Musk blamed the Crossroads School for Arts and Sciences in Santa Monica — a prestigious progressive school with $50,000 annual tuition — for what he described as infecting his child with “the woke mind virus.” He told Isaacson that Vivian “went beyond socialism to being a full communist and thinking that anyone rich is evil.” He said he had made “many overtures” that were rejected. Isaacson reported that the estrangement “pained him more than anything in his life since the infant death of his firstborn child Nevada.”

In a July 2024 interview with Jordan Peterson, Musk said he had been “tricked” into consenting to gender-affirming treatment and described his child as having been “killed by the woke mind virus.”

Vivian Wilson has offered her own account. She has described her father as “a pathetic man-child” who was “often absent, cold, quick to anger, uncaring, and narcissistic.” She has said he harassed her for displaying “femininity and queerness.” She has stated she has been financially independent from him since coming out in 2020. She rejects his characterisation of events. She is, by any measure, a young woman who has exercised her legal right to define her own identity and her own relationships.

I am going to be direct about what this essay can and cannot do.

It cannot adjudicate the family dispute. It cannot determine whether Crossroads School radicalised a teenager or whether a teenager with an absent father found a community and identity that her family did not provide. It cannot assess whether Musk’s grief is proportionate or performative, genuine or instrumentalised. These are questions for the people involved, not for a Canadian essayist in Toronto.

What this essay can do — what this series requires it to do — is trace the line from a father’s pain to a global information architecture.

Because the line is direct. Musk has drawn it himself.

“Unless the woke mind virus, which is fundamentally anti-science, anti-merit, and anti-human in general, is stopped, civilisation will never become multiplanetary.”

That sentence connects a personal family crisis to a civilisational mandate. The “woke mind virus” is not, in Musk’s framing, a disagreement about educational philosophy or gender-affirming care for minors. It is an existential threat to the multiplanetary future — the Douglas Adams–derived vision that animates the entire stack. The same imperative that drives SpaceX to Mars, Tesla to autonomy, and TeraFab to a terawatt now drives the purchase of Twitter, the design of Grok, and the creation of Grokipedia. The culture war is not separate from the engineering programme. In Musk’s mind, it is the same project.

This is the moment first principles thinking died in the Musk stack.

A first principles analysis of gender identity in adolescents would start from the medical literature, the psychological research, the lived experiences of transgender people, and the clinical evidence for and against various approaches to gender-affirming care. It would be rigorous, evidence-based, and open to conclusions that might be uncomfortable. It would look like the engineering analysis that produced reusable rockets: start from the physics, ignore the conventions, follow the evidence.

Musk did not do this. He started from his pain, interpreted that pain through an ideological framework (”the woke mind virus”), and built an information architecture designed to propagate that framework globally. This is not first principles. This is a father’s grief processed as a political crusade and funded at a scale that allows it to reshape the information environment for billions of people.

The Convergence

The Biden snub and the Vivian estrangement did not operate independently. They converged, between 2021 and 2022, with a third factor: the radicalising effect of owning a social media platform.

Musk began tweeting more frequently and more politically through 2021 and 2022. His posts shifted from SpaceX updates and engineering discussions to culture-war provocations, anti-regulation screeds, and attacks on the Democratic Party. The algorithmic feedback loop of Twitter — in which engagement metrics reward provocation, and the most extreme statements generate the most interaction — pulled Musk toward increasingly combative positions. He was not merely using Twitter. Twitter was using him, reinforcing the grievances he brought to the platform with the dopamine rewards of mass engagement.

By April 2022 — the same month Vivian Wilson petitioned to change her name — Musk made his offer to purchase Twitter. By October 2022, he owned it. The timing is not incidental. The man who felt his child had been taken by an ideology, and who felt his company had been disrespected by an administration beholden to that ideology’s political allies, now owned the platform where that ideology operated. The purchase was not a business decision. It was a weapon acquisition.

Elon Musk inside Twitter headquarters shortly after the 2022 acquisition. What he described as creating a digital town square became a central pillar of the political turn

Musk has never framed it in those terms publicly. He has spoken about free speech, about “the digital town square,” about the importance of open discourse. These framings are not entirely dishonest — Musk genuinely believes content moderation as practised by pre-acquisition Twitter was excessive and politically biased. Reasonable people can disagree about where the line between moderation and censorship falls.

But the pattern of decisions after acquisition tells a different story than the free speech framing. Musk did not create a neutral platform with transparent, consistently applied rules. He restored accounts banned for hate speech and harassment. He amplified accounts aligned with his political views through algorithmic changes. He attacked journalists and researchers who documented the platform’s changes. He positioned Grok — the AI chatbot integrated into X — as explicitly “anti-woke,” a direct product of the ideological framework born from personal loss. He launched Grokipedia to compete with Wikipedia, whose editorial conclusions about Musk and his enterprises he disputes.

The through-line from the Biden snub to the Vivian estrangement to the Twitter acquisition to Grok to Grokipedia to the Trump endorsement to DOGE is continuous and legible. Each step follows from the one before. Each is animated by the same conviction: that institutional power — unions, universities, media companies, content moderators, encyclopaedias — has been captured by an ideology hostile to Musk’s values, his family, and his vision for civilisation. Each response escalates the previous one. And each response adds another layer to the stack that serves not engineering but grievance.

What Was Lost

The cost of the political turn is measurable.

Tesla’s brand damage in Europe — where Musk’s association with far-right politics and the Trump administration has alienated customers — contributed to a sales decline that saw Tesla lose nearly half its European market share in 2025. The stock dropped 21% in the first months of 2026. Institutional investors who had held Tesla as a technology growth story began questioning whether the CEO’s political activities constituted a material risk to shareholder value. The UBS upgrade documented in my Tesla series explicitly cited “sentiment and narrative” as drivers of Tesla’s volatility — code for the Musk problem.

The attention cost is harder to quantify but possibly more significant. The months Musk spent on DOGE — documented in Part X of this series — were months not spent on the Cybercab launch, Optimus production, TeraFab buildout, and the dozen other initiatives that define Tesla’s transformation. Every hour tweeting about the “woke mind virus” was an hour not spent on the engineering problems that made Musk worth listening to in the first place.

Anti-Musk protest in Europe. Tesla’s brand faced measurable backlash in several markets following Musk’s political activity and Trump alignment

And the reputational cost extends beyond financial markets. Musk was, through approximately 2020, one of the most admired figures in technology — a real-life Tony Stark, a man building the future. By 2025, he was one of the most polarising. The admiration has not disappeared, but it now comes primarily from one end of the political spectrum rather than from the broad cross-section that once made “Elon Musk” a synonym for ambitious engineering rather than a political statement.

First principles thinking built SpaceX, Tesla, and Starlink. Personal grievance, institutional resentment, and a father’s pain built X, Grok, Grokipedia, and DOGE. The stack contains both. The question for this series — and for the investors, regulators, and citizens who must navigate the stack’s consequences — is whether the grievance-built layers will damage the engineering-built layers, or whether the engineering is robust enough to survive the politics of the man who created it.

That question cannot be answered yet. But the political turn that created it can be traced to two events — a White House snub in August 2021 and a court filing in April 2022 — that together transformed the most productive engineer of his generation into a culture warrior with the resources to wage that war at planetary scale.
