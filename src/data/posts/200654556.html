On Wednesday, the United States House of Representatives voted 215 to 208 to rebuke Donald John Trump over the war in Iran, invoking the War Powers Resolution to demand he withdraw American forces or seek congressional approval for the conflict.

He called it a meaningless vote.

He called the Democrats “Dumocrats” — his spelling, not mine, though I confess a certain admiration for a man who has operated at the highest levels of American power for a decade and still cannot resist a schoolyard spelling joke on Truth Social. He called the four Republicans who crossed the aisle “grandstanders” who should be “ashamed of themselves.” He called the whole exercise unpatriotic.

What he did not do — what he has never done, in any context, at any point in his public life — was hear the word being said to him.

No.

The four Republican grandstanders, for the record, were Thomas Massie, Brian Fitzpatrick, Tom Barrett, and Warren Davidson. They joined a united front of Democrats, plus Independent representative Don Davis, to push the resolution over the line by seven votes. Democrat Jared Golden of Maine, who had previously voted against similar measures, gave his support this time.

Tom Barrett, a Republican from Michigan, was asked whether he was worried about retribution from Trump for his vote.

“I vote my conscience for what I think is right,” he said, “and I’m willing to accept that.”

That is a man who rode to the meadow knowing the king would remember his face.

I want to be careful not to over-romanticize four Republican congressmen — they are politicians, not saints, and three of the four will be watching their primaries nervously come next cycle. But Barrett’s quote deserves to be read slowly. He did not hedge. He did not say I support the president’s overall vision but. He said: conscience. Willing to accept the consequences.

In the long argument about who is and is not subject to the law, that sentence will age better than the Truth Social post it answered.

The vote was the fourth attempt by the House to rein in Trump’s war powers. The Senate advanced a similar resolution in May but has yet to hold a full floor vote. The White House has dismissed the measure as an unconstitutional attempt to restrict presidential power — which is, with some historical irony, exactly what every king has said about every charter, every parliament, and every court that has ever told a sovereign the word he finds so difficult to process.

As reported by BBC News, Michael Glennon, a professor of constitutional and international law at Tufts University, put it plainly: Congress is saying, he noted, that this is unlawful and the forces need to come home. The political impact, he observed, is real — a majority of one House has gone on record, in an actual vote, that American armed forces need to be withdrawn from hostilities in the Persian Gulf.

Whether it has legal force is a different question. As a concurrent resolution, if passed identically by the Republican-controlled Senate, it would not require the president’s signature. If the Senate tweaks the language, it becomes a joint resolution — which would require his signature, which he would veto, which Congress cannot override. The road to enforcement is thorny, procedural, and almost certainly blocked.

But that, too, is historically familiar. The Magna Carta was annulled by the Pope within months of being signed. King John died having largely escaped its immediate consequences.

The document survived anyway.

A New York Times/Siena poll taken in May found that 64 percent of registered voters believed Trump was wrong to go to war with Iran, compared to 34 percent who believed he made the right call. Among independents — the bellwether group that decides American elections — 73 percent opposed the war. Even within his own party, 30 percent of Republicans broke with the president’s position.

These are not the numbers of a popular war.

Trump, who tracks numbers the way some people track breathing, has not mentioned these particular ones.

There is a word for the inability to perceive one’s own condition accurately. It appears in the medical literature as anosognosia — the neurological phenomenon whereby a patient is genuinely unaware of their own deficit. I have written about it elsewhere in these pages, in a different context, as The Baseline Problem. The principle is the same: you cannot correct what you cannot perceive.

I am not diagnosing the President of the United States. I am not qualified to do so, and it would not be appropriate if I were.

I am simply noting that a man who has been told no — by voters in 2020, by courts on dozens of constitutional questions, by four members of his own party on Wednesday, by 64 percent of the American public on the specific question of this war — continues to operate as though the word has not been spoken.

King John heard the word at Runnymede. He didn’t like it. He spent his remaining months trying to un-hear it. He failed.

The word, it turns out, does not require the listener’s consent to mean what it means.

I will have more to say about the two Johns — the one who signed under duress in a Surrey meadow in 1215, and the one who posts on Truth Social at dawn — when this writing reaches its 1,215th essay. That number is still some weeks away. I am not cramming to get there.

But I will say this now: the vote on Wednesday was 215 to 208.

The year was 1215.

I did not plan that either.

The numbers, as I have established, find you.

The four Republicans who voted with Democrats on Wednesday — Massie, Fitzpatrick, Barrett, Davidson — should be noted by name. The record is the resistance.
