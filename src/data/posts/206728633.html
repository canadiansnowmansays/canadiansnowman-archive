Part XVIII closed with a monarchy that survived by giving away its power gradually, in advance of confrontation. Hawaii’s own royal history offers this series almost the exact opposite trajectory: a kingdom built through genuine unification and diplomatic skill, whose last monarch, rather than following Britain’s path of gradual concession, tried to win back the powers her brother had been forced at gunpoint to surrender, and who was overthrown not by her own people demanding reform, but by a small group of foreign resident businessmen who wanted her kingdom’s land and sugar economy for themselves, backed by an armed landing party from the United States Navy.

Before European contact, the Hawaiian archipelago was divided among several independent chiefdoms, each ruled by an aliʻi nui, a paramount chief whose authority rested on genealogical descent traced back through generations of oral tradition to the god Kāne and other ancestral deities, a legitimating structure this series recognizes immediately from Part I as broadly comparable to the divine-descent claims of ancient Egypt and, in its emphasis on unbroken sacred genealogy specifically, to Japan’s own imperial line examined in Part XVI. Kamehameha I, born around 1758 on the island of Hawaii itself, rose through exactly this traditional chiefly system, and his path to unifying the entire island chain under a single crown for the first time in recorded Hawaiian history combined the kind of demonstrated military capability this series traced through Shaka’s Zulu kingdom in Part XII with a shrewd and early adoption of European weapons and naval technology acquired through trade with visiting American and European ships.

Kamehameha’s own path to that unification began years earlier, following the death of the great Hawaii island chief Kalaniʻōpuʻu in 1782, when the deceased chief’s authority was divided between his son, who inherited the position of paramount chief, and Kamehameha himself, who inherited guardianship of the war god Kūkāʻilimoku, an inheritance that gave him a religiously sanctioned claim to military leadership entirely separate from, and ultimately more consequential than, his cousin’s inherited civil authority, a split legitimacy arrangement that produced years of internal conflict before Kamehameha emerged as sole ruler of his home island by 1791. His decisive campaign to extend that authority beyond his home island came at the Battle of Nuʻuanu in 1795, fought on the island of Oʻahu, where his forces, having already conquered his home island of Hawaii and the neighboring island of Maui, drove the defending Oʻahu warriors of King Kalanikūpule back to the edge of the Nuʻuanu Pali, a sheer cliff face on the windward side of the island, where hundreds were reportedly forced over the precipice to their deaths rather than surrender. This victory left Kamehameha in control of every major Hawaiian island except Kauai and Niʻihau, whose ruler, Kaumualiʻi, ultimately chose peaceful submission to Kamehameha’s authority in 1810 rather than risk a similarly devastating military confrontation, completing the unification of the Hawaiian archipelago under a single crown for the first time in the islands’ recorded history. Kamehameha’s subsequent reign, until his death in 1819, established the Kamehameha dynasty as Hawaii’s ruling house and introduced a body of law, the Kānāwai Māmalahoe or Law of the Splintered Paddle, protecting travelers and noncombatants from harm during conflict, a legal principle still cited today in Hawaii’s modern state constitution as an enduring symbol of the kingdom’s own governing values.

The Kamehameha dynasty that followed navigated the nineteenth century’s escalating pressure from American and European commercial interests with a genuinely sophisticated diplomatic strategy, one that shows real parallels to the accommodation strategy this series traced through Britain in Part XVIII. Kamehameha III, who reigned for three decades until his death in 1854, presided over the Great Māhele of 1848, a sweeping land reform that formally divided Hawaiian land into private ownership for the first time, a decision made under considerable pressure from foreign residents and missionaries eager for secure land titles, and one whose long-term consequences proved genuinely damaging to ordinary Native Hawaiian landholders, many of whom lacked the resources, legal literacy, or documentation to successfully claim title to lands their families had worked for generations, while foreign residents and a small class of well-connected native elites accumulated substantial holdings instead. The kingdom also secured formal diplomatic recognition of its full sovereignty from the United States, Britain, and France during this same period, a genuine achievement that placed Hawaii, for several decades, in a considerably stronger international legal position than most of the smaller monarchies examined elsewhere in this series ever achieved.

The kingdom’s final and most consequential monarchs, King David Kalākaua and his sister and successor Queen Liliʻuokalani, did not belong to the Kamehameha line at all, which had ended in 1874 with the death of King Lunalilo, who left no heir; the legislature elected Kalākaua to the vacant throne that same year, founding a new royal house, and he and his sister confronted a resident foreign business class, predominantly American in origin and increasingly concentrated in the immensely profitable sugar plantation industry, whose economic interests grew steadily more difficult to reconcile with continued Hawaiian sovereignty. Kalākaua’s own reign, before that final crisis, is worth pausing on briefly, because it shows a monarch actively and deliberately reviving indigenous Hawaiian culture at precisely the moment foreign commercial pressure on his kingdom was intensifying, a strategy of cultural rather than purely constitutional self-assertion this series has not quite seen in this specific combination elsewhere. Kalākaua, nicknamed the Merrie Monarch for his personal warmth and evident enjoyment of music and dance, actively promoted the public performance of hula, which Christian missionary influence over previous decades had suppressed as immodest, and commissioned the construction of ʻIolani Palace, completed in 1882 and outfitted with electric lighting and indoor plumbing before either feature had been installed in the White House itself, a deliberate architectural statement asserting Hawaii’s full standing as a modern, sovereign kingdom rather than a fading indigenous chiefdom. He also undertook a genuinely remarkable round-the-world diplomatic tour in 1881, becoming the first reigning monarch of any nation to circumnavigate the globe, meeting with heads of state from Japan to Egypt to Britain in a deliberate effort to secure Hawaii’s international standing and explore potential alliances that might offset the kingdom’s growing dependence on American commercial interests specifically. It was this same king, having spent much of his reign asserting Hawaiian sovereignty and cultural pride so visibly, who was forced in 1887 to sign what became known derisively as the Bayonet Constitution, presented to him by an armed militia of resident businessmen and sympathetic local elites who threatened him with violence if he refused, a document that stripped the monarchy of most of its remaining independent executive authority and imposed property and income requirements on voting that disenfranchised the substantial majority of Native Hawaiians and Asian immigrant labourers while preserving the franchise for the wealthier, disproportionately American and European resident population. This document is worth pausing on directly, because it shows this series’ Part VI theme, a monarch forced under armed duress to sign away power he never intended to grant, replaying almost four centuries after King John’s own experience at Runnymede, though with considerably less ambiguity about who benefited from the coercion and considerably less time before the underlying conflict escalated further still.

Liliʻuokalani, succeeding her brother in 1891 as Hawaii’s first reigning queen, attempted to reverse the Bayonet Constitution’s most damaging provisions and restore genuine executive authority to the crown, drafting a new constitution intended to expand voting rights back toward the broader Native Hawaiian population and reduce the resident business class’s outsized political leverage. This attempt at reasserting royal authority, rather than the accommodating gradualism this series traced through Britain’s own long constitutional evolution, proved to be the immediate trigger for the kingdom’s final overthrow. A group calling itself the Committee of Safety, composed substantially of American and European resident businessmen with direct financial interests in the sugar industry, and supported actively by John L. Stevens, the sitting United States Minister to Hawaii, moved in January of 1893 to depose the queen entirely, declaring a Provisional Government and requesting American military support to secure their position against any popular resistance from Native Hawaiians loyal to the crown.

Stevens obliged this request without any authorization from Washington to do so, ordering the landing of some one hundred and sixty-two armed sailors and Marines from the USS Boston, stationed conveniently in Honolulu harbor at the time, who marched through the streets of Honolulu and took up positions near ʻIolani Palace, the government building known as Aliʻiolani Hale, and other key administrative sites, in a deliberate show of force whose stated public justification was the protection of American lives and property, but whose actual practical effect was to deter the queen’s own loyal forces and the broader Native Hawaiian population, who vastly outnumbered the resident conspirators, from mounting any organized resistance to the coup already underway. Liliʻuokalani, facing an occupying foreign military force she had every reason to believe would use lethal force against her own people if she resisted, made the decision to yield her authority not to the Committee of Safety directly, but formally to the government of the United States itself, under explicit protest, in a carefully worded statement asserting that she surrendered only to avoid bloodshed and trusting that the United States government, once informed of the facts, would undo an action taken by its own diplomatic representative without proper authorization.

This appeal very nearly succeeded, and the episode that followed offers this series a genuinely rare example of an American president actively investigating and condemning a coup his own country’s officials had helped engineer. President Grover Cleveland, taking office shortly after the overthrow and troubled by early reports of Stevens’s unauthorized conduct, commissioned a formal investigation led by former congressman James Blount, whose resulting report concluded unambiguously that the overthrow had been accomplished through illegal American military intervention against the wishes of the clear majority of the Hawaiian population, and Cleveland himself subsequently declared to Congress that the United States had committed an act of war against a peaceful and friendly nation without justification, formally requesting that Liliʻuokalani be restored to her throne. The Committee of Safety’s provisional government, and the Republic of Hawaii it soon reorganized itself into under President Sanford Dole, simply refused to comply with Cleveland’s request, and Congress, dominated by expansionist sentiment and unwilling to force the issue against an American president who would leave office within a few years regardless, took no action to compel restoration, leaving Liliʻuokalani deposed in practice despite her own government’s formal diplomatic vindication.

The final chapter came swiftly once American political sentiment shifted decisively in favor of annexation amid the broader wave of American territorial expansion accompanying the Spanish-American War of 1898, a conflict that heightened American strategic interest in Pacific naval bases and made Hawaii’s annexation, previously blocked by Cleveland’s own objections, considerably easier to secure under his successor William McKinley. The Republic of Hawaii’s government formally ceded the islands to the United States that same year, and Hawaii became an American territory, remaining so for over six decades until achieving American statehood in 1959, a final legal transformation that occurred entirely without any renewed consultation of the Native Hawaiian population whose kingdom had originally been seized. Liliʻuokalani herself lived until 1917, composing music, including the enduring song Aloha ʻOe, and writing a memoir titled Hawaii’s Story by Hawaii’s Queen asserting her kingdom’s stolen sovereignty until her death, having witnessed her own nation’s complete legal absorption into the country whose own naval officer had made that absorption possible. It would take almost exactly a century for the United States government to formally acknowledge the injustice Cleveland’s own investigation had already documented at the time: in 1993, on the one-hundredth anniversary of the overthrow, the United States Congress passed a joint resolution, signed into law by President Bill Clinton, formally apologizing to Native Hawaiians for the United States’ role in the illegal overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii and the deprivation of the rights of Native Hawaiians to self-determination, language that echoed Cleveland’s own century-old conclusions almost precisely. The resolution, however, carried no binding legal or restorative mechanism attached to it, and a modern Native Hawaiian sovereignty movement, ranging from advocates for a form of federally recognized self-governance modeled loosely on Native American tribal status to a smaller but persistent contingent arguing for full restoration of an independent Hawaiian kingdom, continues to debate exactly what meaningful redress for the events this part has described might actually look like, well over a century after Liliʻuokalani’s own forced surrender under explicit protest.

What Hawaii’s case demonstrates, set directly against Part XVIII’s account of Britain’s successful strategy of gradual accommodation, is that a monarchy’s willingness to negotiate and concede power offers no protection whatsoever when the actual threat to its sovereignty originates not from its own domestic population’s demand for reform, but from an external commercial and military interest determined to seize the kingdom’s land and economy regardless of how its monarch chooses to govern. Liliʻuokalani’s own attempted reforms, aimed at restoring genuine popular sovereignty to a broader share of her own Native Hawaiian subjects, made her, if anything, a more direct threat to the resident business class’s interests than a more passive monarch would have been, a genuinely different calculus than any other collapse examined so far in this series, where the crown’s own domestic population, rather than a foreign commercial class, was almost always the primary source of pressure for change.

It is worth closing this part with one further thread this series will pick up again directly in Part XX, because the contrast is instructive rather than merely coincidental: Tonga’s own ruling dynasty, unifying its islands under King George Tupou I only a few decades before Kalākaua’s own reign in Hawaii, was watching the same escalating pattern of Western commercial and colonial pressure across the wider Pacific that would eventually consume Hawaii’s independence, and drew from it a starkly different set of lessons about how a small Pacific kingdom might actually survive contact with far larger foreign powers. Where Hawaii’s monarchs pursued formal diplomatic recognition and cultural revival while allowing a resident foreign business class to accumulate steadily greater practical economic and political leverage within the kingdom’s own borders, Tonga’s rulers moved earlier and more aggressively to formalize land protections and constitutional constraints specifically designed to prevent foreign residents from ever accumulating comparable leverage in the first place. Whether this divergence reflects genuinely superior foresight on the Tongan crown’s part, or simply Tonga’s more modest strategic and agricultural appeal to nineteenth-century American commercial interests compared to Hawaii’s uniquely valuable mid-Pacific shipping position and highly profitable sugar economy, remains a genuinely open question this series is not in a position to resolve definitively. What is not in question is the outcome each strategy produced.

Part XX stays in the Pacific, for the one island kingdom in the entire region that managed to preserve its independent throne into the present day, having watched Hawaii’s fate unfold from a considerable distance and drawn its own, evidently successful, lessons from it.
