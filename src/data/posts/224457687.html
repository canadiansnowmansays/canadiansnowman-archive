In the early hours of November 4, with the count still running, Chuck Schumer stood before reporters in Washington and offered the country five words. “Relief is on the way,” he said, as the Associated Press reported. Democrats had taken the House by a wide margin and the Senate with 54 seats, by NBC News’ count, flipping seats in places such as Texas, Ohio and South Carolina that nobody had bothered to mark on the map in the spring.

Within hours the relief had acquired a target. “We’re going to see RFK Jr’s head on a platter,” Erik Polyak of the Democratic PAC 314 Action told Politico. Senator Angela Alsobrooks of Maryland posted that Kennedy “must resign or be fired. End of discussion,” renewing a demand she first made more than a year earlier, as The Hill noted. On November 10, Robert Garcia, the incoming chair of the House Oversight Committee, told reporters that what Kennedy had done to the health department and to vaccination in America was “horrific as far as impeachment goes,” according to NBC News’ live blog.

The platter line travelled fast, and not everyone who backed it stood behind it. Shaughnessy Naughton, the founder and president of 314 Action, disowned her own executive director's words. "That is not rhetoric that belongs in this conversation," she told Sheryl Gay Stolberg of The New York Times on November 12, though she still read the election as a repudiation of Kennedy. Bernie Sanders, likely to chair the Senate health committee in January, told CNN he was "absolutely open" to impeachment. "R.F.K. Jr. is, by far, the most dangerous health and human services secretary in our nation's history," he said in a statement, promising that his committee "will hold Secretary Kennedy accountable."

The department's reply was brief. Kennedy, said HHS spokesperson Emily Hilliard, would "continue delivering President Trump's Make America Healthy Again agenda." His ally Robert Malone said Kennedy was "not a quitter." Kennedy put it his own way on X three days after the vote: "It's time to unleash the Kraken," a line from the 1981 film Clash of the Titans that became a rallying cry in 2020 among far-right figures who claimed Democrats had stolen that election. For once, nobody had reason to doubt any of them.

This essay is about what comes after the platter. Because the honest answer to the question every Democrat in Washington is now being asked, can you get rid of him?, is no. The useful question is different: what would it take to repair what he has done, and who might help?

The arithmetic of removal

Start with the numbers, because they are not in dispute. Impeachment needs a simple majority in the House, which Democrats will have in January. Conviction needs two-thirds of the Senate, 67 votes, and Democrats hold 54. Unless thirteen Republicans decide that Kennedy’s vaccine record is worth breaking with a president who campaigned with him at a rally in Fort Myers on October 23, the Secretary stays.

The same arithmetic governs legislation. Trump has said he will veto Democratic bills. Overriding him takes 290 votes in the House and 67 in the Senate. A Democratic Congress can pass almost anything it likes and enact very little of it alone.

From a Canadian vantage point there is something almost charming in this. In Ottawa, a minister who had presided over the return of measles, lost the confidence of his own advisory bodies and been rebuked by a federal court would be gone before the next Question Period, pushed by his own caucus if not by the House. In Washington, a cabinet secretary answers to one man. Kennedy has one constituent, and that constituent has just lost both chambers of Congress, which may make him more loyal to the people who stood beside him, not less. Axios’ Future of Health Care newsletter reported on October 26 that the popular assumption of a post-election departure had faded, and that the better question had become why Kennedy would leave at all.

Kennedy himself thinks the defeat has freed him. At a MAHA Action party in Washington on November 9, he complained that Republican senators had threatened his nominees whenever he tried "to do something robust." "The Senate no longer has any leverage over me," he said. "And you know, that's a big deal." His hand inside the White House may be stronger too. Trump's new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, replaces Susie Wiles, who had been accused of blocking MAHA policies, and Miller's wife, Katie, was Kennedy's spokeswoman during the transition. "Next to me, Stephen Miller is the strongest MAHA supporter in the administration," Kennedy posted. Even Democrats eager for a fight are rationing it. "If there are any impeachments, it should start with Secretary Hegseth," said Representative Chellie Pingree of Maine.

So the platter will stay empty. What fills the next two years is a different kind of work.

What the voters said about MAHA

The election delivered a second verdict that matters as much as the first. Kennedy and the groups aligned with him put their names behind 97 candidates. At least 64 lost, according to The Independent, and the count was still climbing. In Iowa alone, MAHA’s picks for governor and Senate lost, as did two House incumbents it backed, Mariannette Miller-Meeks and Zach Nunn. Florida’s surgeon general, Joseph Ladapo, told a MAHA call the day after the vote that the “honeymoon period” was over, and that a split from MAGA might even be healthy, because MAHA should never have been partisan.

The break had been building since February 18, when Trump signed an executive order using the Defense Production Act to boost glyphosate production and shield its makers from liability. Kennedy, who once called the herbicide “one of the likely culprits in America’s chronic disease epidemic,” backed the order on national-security grounds. The MAHA activists who had carried him into the administration called it a broken promise, as Reuters reported at the time. Republican Thomas Massie of Kentucky answered with the No Immunity for Glyphosate Act, according to Straight Arrow News.

Democrats noticed. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez had been meeting MAHA grassroots leaders for months and published a health agenda with a section titled “Reclaiming MAHA and Protecting American Families.” Abdul El-Sayed, a physician and former Detroit health director, won Michigan’s Senate seat on a “Building a Healthier America” platform that promised to ban certain pesticides and get lead and microplastics out of drinking water while holding firm on vaccines. Polling by the Center for American Progress found that 37 per cent of Trump voters were open to a Democrat running on core MAHA issues, as Axios reported on October 28.

The coalition is fragile, and the first test came within a day. When Alsobrooks posted her demand for Kennedy’s removal, Vani Hari, the activist known as the Food Babe, replied that this was “not how you win a coalition or work together,” as STAT recorded on November 5. Meanwhile Annie Andrews, the pediatrician who won a Senate seat in South Carolina, calls Kennedy her “professional archnemesis” and lists his removal among her top goals.

Others answered Alsobrooks from inside her own party. "This makes no sense," wrote Tim Ryan, the former Ohio congressman. "Why wouldn't we work together to see what wins we can get?" Zen Honeycutt, the founder of Moms Across America and a former Democrat, made the MAHA side's pitch on a MAHA Action webinar the day after the election: "I just urge every Democrat that is considering working against him to sit down and talk with him. I think you're going to love him." Even Tony Lyons, who runs MAHA Action and once promised $100 million to elect "MAHA-aligned, Trump-endorsed" Republicans, has changed his tune. "We have the building blocks of an incredibly powerful, independent movement that is beholden to no one," he said.

Both instincts are right, and Democrats will have to hold them at once. Kennedy’s vaccine record deserves every hearing it gets. The people who came to MAHA because they were worried about what is in their children’s cereal and their town’s water deserve to be treated as allies, not as accomplices.

The ledger

To make that concrete, this series has built a companion: The Recovery Ledger, an interactive list of 25 initiatives for the new Congress. Each entry records what Kennedy did, what should replace it, which lever can realistically move it, and whether MAHA voters are likely to be allies, split or opposed. Readers can filter by any of those, and every entry carries a status that can be updated through 2027 as bills are drafted, attached to spending packages, passed or vetoed.

The entries fall into four kinds. Some reverse a Kennedy decision outright. Some repair or rebuild what was cut. Some build something new. And a handful, deliberately, say keep this and make it stronger, because Kennedy has not been wrong about everything, and a ledger that pretends otherwise would persuade nobody it needs to persuade.

What follows is the shape of it, organized by the levers that will actually work.

First lever: the money

The president must sign a Labor-HHS appropriations bill or shut the government down. That makes funding levels and binding riders the strongest tool Democrats have, and it is where the ledger puts its first-hundred-days priorities.

Measles comes first. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention counts 5,182 confirmed cases in 2026, more than double the 2,289 confirmed in all of 2025, as Sky Sandoval reported for Nation of Change on November 10. States have confirmed eight deaths this year, six of them in Pennsylvania, according to Annika Kim Constantino of CNBC, and a new Johns Hopkins tool puts the economic cost of this year’s outbreaks at nearly $83 million, most of it spent by health departments tracing contacts rather than treating the sick. The Pan American Health Organization’s commission is meeting behind closed doors from November 10 to November 13 to decide whether the United States has lost the elimination status it has held since 2000, as ABC News reported. Through all of it, the federal response has been close to silent. “We have seen virtually no national messaging. We’ve seen no ad campaigns,” Dr. Jonathan Temte, a former chair of the CDC’s vaccine advisory committee, told The New York Times in July. The ledger proposes an emergency supplemental for state immunization grants, outbreak teams and catch-up clinics, with a national campaign fronted by local pediatricians rather than anyone in Washington. Vaccinating children is cheaper than chasing their contacts.

Second is the vaccine advisory system. Kennedy fired all 17 members of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices in June 2025 and on January 5, 2026, cut the childhood schedule from 17 diseases to 11 without consulting it. On March 16, Judge Brian Murphy stayed both the new schedule and the appointments of 13 Kennedy picks, along with every vote they had cast, as CIDRAP reported. HHS is appealing. A rider barring funds for any schedule change made outside a lawfully constituted ACIP would protect families whatever the First Circuit decides, and so would tying insurance coverage to the schedule that stood on January 1, 2025.

Third is research. Congress refused the administration’s proposed 40 per cent cut to the National Institutes of Health for 2026 and instead raised its budget by $415 million, as the Washington Examiner noted in April. Grants were cancelled anyway. Anti-impoundment language with deadlines, and reinstatement of terminated grants on merit, belong in the bill.

Fourth is a line most readers will recognize from this series. In late October, Kennedy issued an enforcement-discretion policy letting pharmacies compound six unapproved peptides, BPC-157 among them, as Annabel Kartal Allen reported for Pharmaceutical Technology on October 29. FDA staff scientists had opposed the move, Matthew Perrone of the Associated Press reported the day before. The ledger proposes a rider revoking the policy, paired with funded clinical trials of the most popular peptides. Many MAHA followers use these products. Answering their question with data, rather than a flat ban, is the respectful way to protect them.

Second lever: the record

Oversight needs no presidential signature. Hearings and subpoenas can establish who drafted the January 5 schedule memo, who ordered the CDC in November 2025 to say that “vaccines do not cause autism” is not an evidence-based claim, and why Medicaid funds to California and Minnesota were frozen in the summer.

The same scrutiny should go to the vaccine-injury initiative Kennedy launched on October 8: an NIH clinic for people who believe they were harmed, plus a proposed billing code that would pay doctors for each report filed to the federal adverse-event system, as CNN reported. This series examined that design in “Paid by the Report.” The ledger’s answer is not to dismiss the families who show up at that clinic. It is to give them a system that deserves their trust: better-funded safety monitoring, more special masters for the compensation programme, faster awards and an injury table set by independent scientists. Per-report payments reward volume. Families need accuracy.

Impeachment hearings, if Democrats choose them, belong in this lane too. They will not remove Kennedy. They can produce a public record that outlasts him.

Some of the questions may come from inside Kennedy's own movement. Calley Means, a former administration adviser and one of MAHA’s best-known voices, recently went on Joe Rogan’s podcast to allege "demonic" efforts by lobbyists with close ties to senior officials to shape White House policy, as Michael Scherer reported in The Atlantic on November 9. A movement that came to Washington promising to drive industry influence out of health policy now has one of its own saying the influence got in anyway. Oversight committees will be able to ask him whom he meant.

Third lever: the coalition

This is where the MAHA split becomes useful. On a handful of issues, Democrats and MAHA Republicans already want the same thing, which makes a veto-proof majority at least imaginable.

The best test is glyphosate. A Republican-authored bill to repeal the liability shield, a Republican president who signed the shield, and a Republican base furious about it: if any coalition can reach 290 and 67, this one can.

Next come the food entries, and here the ledger gives Kennedy his due. He pushed the food industry hard to drop petroleum-based dyes, and most major companies pledged to do so. “By the end of this year, all nine petroleum-based synthetic food dyes will be gone,” he told a MAHA rally in Austin in March, as FoodNavigator-USA reported. Those are voluntary pledges. The ledger proposes writing the phase-out into law, so it survives the next administration and the one after. The same goes for closing the loophole that lets companies declare their own new additives safe without telling the Food and Drug Administration, and for an ultra-processed food definition grounded in evidence, which Kennedy promised for April and which still has no workable test.

Clean water belongs here too: enforceable limits on “forever chemicals,” money for lead pipe replacement, and a national programme to measure microplastics. None of it is controversial in a suburb, whichever party holds the seat.

Fourth lever: the bill at the kitchen table

The finding that should shape everything else came from the campaign itself. MAHA voters were more likely to name lowering health care costs as their top concern than improving food safety, as NOTUS found. Clean food is the movement’s banner. The doctor’s bill is its daily worry.

That puts Medicaid at the centre. Kennedy has defended nearly $1 trillion in reductions over the next decade, mostly through new work requirements, while insisting there are no cuts, according to an Associated Press account of his April hearings carried by WTOP. Five Democratic governors have asked HHS to tie compliance dates to state readiness instead of the calendar, as the Advisory Board reported. Full repeal will have to wait for a different signature. Delay, restored funding and protection for rural hospitals can travel on a spending bill now.

The ledger also asks Democrats to take MAHA’s language about chronic disease more seriously than Kennedy has. He talks about it constantly; very little has changed in what Medicare and Medicaid pay for. Covering medically tailored meals and nutrition counselling, funding kitchens so schools can cook on site, and pairing state limits on soda and candy purchases under SNAP with incentives for fruit and vegetables would turn the rhetoric into something a family can feel at the checkout.

The TACO in the mirror

Readers of this series know the pattern this column has used to describe Kennedy’s department: threaten, accommodate, capitulate, obfuscate. Threaten the vaccine schedule; accommodate the courts with a new charter; capitulate on glyphosate the moment the president signs; obfuscate with a rally speech about food dyes.

The temptation for a new majority is to run the same play in reverse. Threaten impeachment, accommodate the cameras, capitulate when the Senate math bites, and obfuscate the failure with a press release. The ledger is an argument against that. It asks for fewer threats and more line items: a rider here, a subpoena there, a bipartisan bill where the votes already exist, and an honest acknowledgement of the things Kennedy got right.

Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions. Washington has spent two years watching a health department do exactly that. Relief, if it comes, will not arrive on a platter. It will arrive in January, in the dullest possible form, one appropriations line at a time.
