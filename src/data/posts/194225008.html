Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Israeli fighter jets during the opening strikes of the Six Day War, June 5, 1967

It took six days.

From June 5 to June 10, 1967, the Israeli Defence Forces destroyed the Egyptian, Jordanian, and Syrian air forces, largely on the ground, in the first hours of the war. They then proceeded to conquer the Sinai Peninsula, the Gaza Strip, the West Bank including East Jerusalem, and the Golan Heights. When the ceasefire came, Israel had tripled its territory.

In Western popular memory, the Six Day War is the ur-text of Israeli military genius — the plucky democracy outnumbered and surrounded, striking first with devastating precision, emerging victorious against impossible odds. The image has the clean moral geometry of a Western: the small righteous force, the encircling enemies, the miraculous deliverance.

Like most clean moral geometries applied to this conflict, it requires careful examination.

What is not in dispute: Egypt had closed the Strait of Tiran to Israeli shipping, had expelled the UN peacekeeping force from the Sinai, and had massed 100,000 troops on Israel’s border. Syria had been shelling Israeli settlements in the Galilee. The Arab states’ rhetoric — including Nasser’s public declarations — spoke explicitly of destroying Israel. The threat was real and the Israeli security establishment’s fear was genuine.

What requires examination: whether a military strike that resulted in Israel controlling the territories of 1.5 million Palestinians who had no part in the threatening behaviour of Arab governments was a defensive act, a preemptive act, or something with a longer political agenda that the security crisis of 1967 made possible.

The answer, supported by the declassified record, is all three simultaneously. And the consequences have been accruing for fifty-eight years.

The Morning of June 5

Egyptian aircraft destroyed on the ground, Sinai, June 1967

At 7:45 a.m. on June 5, 1967, Israeli aircraft took off in carefully coordinated waves and struck Egyptian air bases across the Sinai. Within three hours, the Egyptian Air Force — the largest in the Arab world — had been effectively destroyed, most of its planes still on the ground. The operation, planned in meticulous detail over years, was one of the most precisely executed preemptive strikes in military history.

Jordan, believing Egyptian reports that the war was going well for the Arab side, entered the conflict by shelling West Jerusalem. Israel responded by attacking Jordanian positions and then advancing into the West Bank. Syria, after two days of watching from the Golan Heights, began shelling Israeli positions and was then attacked.

By June 7, Israeli paratroopers had fought through the Old City of Jerusalem and reached the Western Wall. General Moshe Dayan, the Defence Minister, arrived at the Wall and declared — in a phrase that has echoed ever since — “We have returned to our holiest places, never to leave them again.” The soldiers wept. Photographs of the paratroopers at the Wall — young men, rifles slung, faces open with what looked like awe — became among the most reproduced images of the 20th century.

Israeli paratroopers at the Western Wall, June 7, 1967

The emotional reality of that moment for the Jewish people was genuine and should not be dismissed. Two thousand years of exile, eighteen years of separation from the Western Wall, and then — in six days — the city was whole again. Whatever one thinks of what came after, the human weight of that moment was real.

What came after is what this instalment is about.

The Territories and the Decision Not Made

Before and after 1967: Israel’s territorial expansion

Israel now controlled the West Bank, Gaza, the Sinai Peninsula, and the Golan Heights. Within these territories lived approximately 1.5 million Palestinians — in the West Bank and Gaza — and several hundred thousand Syrians in the Golan.

The Israeli government faced a decision of historic consequence: what to do with the territories.

Three options were available in the immediate aftermath of the war. The first was annexation — incorporating the territories into Israel proper, with their Palestinian populations becoming Israeli citizens or residents. This would resolve the territorial question but create a demographic one: Israel with the West Bank and Gaza would have an Arab population approaching 40 percent, threatening the Jewish majority on which the Jewish state’s democratic character depended. The government rejected explicit annexation — with the exception of East Jerusalem, which was annexed immediately and unilaterally in a move the United Nations promptly declared null and void.

The second option was negotiated return — offering to trade the conquered territories for peace treaties and Arab recognition of Israel. This was the framework of UN Security Council Resolution 242, passed in November 1967, which called for Israeli withdrawal from “occupied territories” in exchange for Arab recognition of Israel’s right to live in peace within secure and recognized boundaries. The “land for peace” formula. Israel accepted Resolution 242 in principle. The Arab states, meeting in Khartoum in August 1967, responded with the three no’s: no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with Israel. The diplomatic path closed — at least temporarily.

The third option was the one Israel chose: indefinite military occupation, administered as temporary pending a final political settlement, with no defined end date and no commitment to either annexation or withdrawal.

The word “temporary” requires examination. Fifty-eight years have now passed since the Israeli military occupied the West Bank and Gaza. The occupation that was described as temporary in 1967 is now the second longest military occupation in modern history. There are Palestinians in the West Bank who have lived their entire lives — birth, childhood, marriage, parenthood, grandparenthood — under Israeli military administration. Their grandchildren will likely do the same.

At some point, “temporary” becomes a word that has lost its meaning. We passed that point decades ago.

Resolution 242 and the Word That Changed Everything

UN Security Council in session during debates over Resolution 242

UN Security Council Resolution 242 is one of the most carefully parsed documents in the history of international diplomacy, and the parsing is not academic. The specific wording of a single phrase has been the subject of legal and political argument for more than half a century.

The resolution calls, in its operative clause, for the “withdrawal of Israeli armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict.”

Not “the territories.” Not “all territories.” Just “territories.”

The omission of the definite article — “the” — was deliberate. The British diplomat who drafted the resolution, Lord Caradon, later acknowledged that the ambiguity was intentional: the resolution was designed to be acceptable to Israel, which argued that it was not required to withdraw from all the occupied territories, only from some of them, and that the final borders would be determined by negotiation. The Arab states argued, with equal legal plausibility, that the absence of “all” did not mean “some” — that the resolution required full withdrawal.

This ambiguity has served as the legal foundation for Israel’s position ever since: that its presence in the West Bank and Gaza is not illegal under international law, that the territories are “disputed” rather than “occupied,” and that their final status is a matter for negotiation rather than unilateral determination.

The International Court of Justice, the United Nations General Assembly, the International Criminal Court, and the overwhelming consensus of international legal scholarship disagree. The Fourth Geneva Convention, which prohibits an occupying power from transferring its civilian population into occupied territory, applies. The occupation is illegal under international law. The settlements built in the occupied territories are illegal under international law.

Israel has never accepted this legal consensus and the United States, as we will examine in Part XII, has used its Security Council veto to prevent any enforcement mechanism from being applied.

Jerusalem: The Annexation Nobody Recognized

Of all the decisions made in the immediate aftermath of the Six Day War, the annexation of East Jerusalem was the most consequential and the most deliberately irreversible.

Within days of the war’s end, Israel demolished the Moroccan Quarter of the Old City — a residential neighbourhood of 135 Palestinian homes adjacent to the Western Wall — to create the Western Wall Plaza. The residents were given hours to leave. The demolition was carried out at night. The neighbourhood, which had stood for centuries, was gone within days.

Israel then extended the municipal boundaries of Jerusalem to absorb East Jerusalem and a large surrounding area, declared the enlarged Jerusalem the “eternal and undivided capital” of Israel, and has treated it as sovereign Israeli territory ever since.

No country in the world recognized this annexation — including the United States, which maintained its embassy in Tel Aviv for fifty years rather than recognize Jerusalem as Israel’s capital. When Donald Trump moved the US Embassy to Jerusalem in 2018, he broke with that fifty-year consensus and was widely condemned by the international community for doing so. The embassy move was celebrated in Israel and contributed to the 2020 Abraham Accords — normalization agreements between Israel and several Arab states that carefully set aside the Palestinian question rather than resolving it.

The Palestinians of East Jerusalem exist in a legal limbo that is one of the more precise illustrations of what occupation actually means in human terms. They are permanent residents of Israel — not citizens, permanent residents — which means they have the right to live in Jerusalem but not to vote in national elections. They carry Jordanian passports in many cases. They can be stripped of their residency rights if they are deemed to have established their “centre of life” elsewhere — a provision that has been used to cancel the residency of thousands of Palestinians who studied or worked abroad. They live in a city that their government claims as its undivided capital, in neighbourhoods where Israeli settler organizations are systematically acquiring properties to establish Jewish presence, under a planning regime that makes it extraordinarily difficult to obtain permits to build or expand their own homes.

This is not a theoretical legal problem. It is the daily reality of hundreds of thousands of people in a city that three billion humans regard as sacred and that no political arrangement has successfully governed for three thousand years.

The Occupation: How It Actually Works

Daily reality of the occupation: checkpoints that control Palestinian movement across their own territory.

Western coverage of the occupation tends to focus on its most dramatic manifestations — settler violence, military incursions, checkpoint confrontations — and to underreport the grinding, bureaucratic, mundane machinery of control that is the occupation’s daily texture for the 2.9 million Palestinians living in the West Bank.

The West Bank is divided, under the Oslo Accords framework that we will examine in Part VIII, into three administrative zones. Area A — covering the major Palestinian population centres — is under Palestinian Authority civil and security control. Area B is under Palestinian civil control and Israeli military control. Area C — covering approximately 60 percent of the West Bank’s territory, including all the Israeli settlements and the Jordan Valley — is under full Israeli military and civil control.

The implications of this division for ordinary Palestinian life are comprehensive. Palestinians in the West Bank require Israeli-issued permits to travel between zones in many cases, to access agricultural land that falls in Area C, to build structures on their own property if it falls in Area C, to connect to water and electricity infrastructure, to move goods to markets. The permit system is administered by the Israeli military’s Civil Administration — a bureaucratic apparatus that has been described by Israeli human rights organizations, including B’Tselem and Yesh Din, as a system designed not to facilitate Palestinian life but to control and constrain it.

Water is perhaps the most precise illustration of how the occupation functions as a system of structural inequality. Under agreements negotiated in the 1990s, Israel controls the shared aquifers of the West Bank and allocates water from them. Israeli settlers in the West Bank receive, on average, approximately four times the water allocation per person of Palestinian residents in the same territory. Israeli settlements have swimming pools. Adjacent Palestinian villages have water trucked in on a schedule. This is not a market outcome. It is an administered allocation made by a military authority.

B’Tselem, the Israeli human rights organization whose documentation of the occupation spans decades, has characterized the system in the West Bank — taken as a whole, including the Gaza blockade — as a system of apartheid: a single authority governing two populations on the same territory, granting them radically different rights based on their ethnicity. The characterization is contested by Israel and by those who argue that apartheid is a legally specific term that requires a specific intent that cannot be demonstrated. It is supported by Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, and a growing number of international legal scholars who have reviewed the full architecture of the system.

The debate about whether to use the word apartheid is, in some ways, less important than the reality the word is trying to describe. Whatever you call a system in which one population has full civil and political rights, freedom of movement, access to resources, and protection of civil law, while another population on the same territory has none of those things and is governed by military decree — that system is a moral catastrophe. The label matters less than the reality.

The Settlements Begin

Modern Israeli settlements in the hills of the West Bank — the physical transformation that began weeks after the 1967 war.

Within weeks of the 1967 war, the first Israeli settlements were established in the occupied territories. The settlement project is the subject of Part IX of this series, where it will receive the detailed examination it requires. But its origins in 1967 must be noted here because they establish a fact of critical importance: the settlements were not a later development that complicated an otherwise manageable occupation. They were present from the beginning, driven by the same religious nationalist ideology that the Six Day War had dramatically empowered.

The capture of the West Bank — biblical Judea and Samaria — was experienced by Israeli religious nationalists as divine confirmation of their ideology. The land that God had promised to the Jewish people in the Torah had been restored to Jewish sovereignty. The obligation, in this theology, was not to administer it temporarily but to settle it permanently. Gush Emunim — the Bloc of the Faithful — was founded in 1974 as the organized expression of this theology, and it drove the settlement enterprise with a religious fervour that secular Israeli governments found politically impossible to resist and occasionally found politically useful to encourage.

Every Israeli government since 1967 — Labour and Likud, left and right, hawk and dove — has presided over the growth of the settlement enterprise. Some have encouraged it more aggressively than others. None has seriously attempted to stop it. The result, fifty-eight years later, is over 700,000 Israeli settlers living in 279 settlements and outposts in the West Bank and East Jerusalem — a physical transformation of the territory that has made the two-state solution, which requires a contiguous Palestinian state in the West Bank, structurally impossible without a forced evacuation that no Israeli government could survive politically.

We will return to this in Part IX. The point here is that the settlements are not a complication of the 1967 occupation. They are its intended consequence, driven by an ideology that was present and politically active from the first days of the occupation and that has been allowed to operate, expand, and entrench itself for nearly six decades while the international community issued statements of concern and did nothing else.

What 1967 Changed Permanently

The Six Day War changed Israel’s relationship to itself, to the Palestinians, and to the Western world in ways that are still working themselves out.

Within Israel, it empowered the religious nationalist right in ways that have progressively transformed Israeli politics. The secular socialist Zionism of Ben-Gurion’s generation — pragmatic, state-building, willing to accept partition as the price of recognition — was gradually displaced by a maximalist territorial nationalism rooted in biblical claim. The process took decades but its trajectory was set in June 1967. The Israel of Itamar Ben Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich — the Israel that is governing Gaza’s destruction as this series is written — is the Israel that the Six Day War made possible, though it took another half century to fully arrive.

For the Palestinians, 1967 added the West Bank and Gaza to the territories lost in 1948, completing the dispossession that had begun with the Nakba. The Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza have now lived under Israeli military occupation for longer than the State of Israel existed before 1967. An entire civilization has been administered, controlled, and progressively dispossessed across three generations.

For the Western world, 1967 created the image of Israel that has dominated Western discourse ever since — the brilliant military power, the regional superpower, the indispensable American ally — and made it progressively harder to maintain the underdog narrative that had generated Western sympathy in the years immediately after the Holocaust. The Israel of 1967 was not the Israel of 1948. It was a military power that had just demonstrated its ability to defeat its neighbours comprehensively and was now administering a population of 1.5 million people with no democratic rights.

The world took several decades to fully absorb that transformation. The current collapse of Western public sympathy for Israeli conduct — visible in the street protests of 2023 and 2024 that dwarfed anything seen in previous conflicts — suggests the absorption is now complete.

The Occupation That Never Became Anything Else

There is a phrase used by Israeli politicians and their defenders that requires direct rebuttal: that Israel has “no partner for peace,” that every attempt to negotiate a resolution of the occupation has been rejected by Palestinian leadership, and that the occupation therefore continues because Palestinian rejectionism makes any alternative impossible.

The phrase contains some truth — Palestinian leadership has made catastrophic negotiating errors, most notably at Camp David in 2000, which we will examine in Part VIII. It also contains a fundamental dishonesty: it treats the settlements, which have grown continuously throughout every peace process, as though they are irrelevant to the possibility of a negotiated outcome.

You cannot negotiate a viable Palestinian state while simultaneously building the infrastructure that makes a Palestinian state impossible. You cannot tell a population that their state is coming while your government funds, protects, and incentivizes the settlement of 700,000 of your citizens on the land that state would occupy. You cannot claim to want peace while your ministers publicly declare that there is no Palestinian people, that the West Bank is not occupied, and that Palestinian population centres should be “wiped out.”

These are not fringe positions in the current Israeli government. They are the stated views of cabinet ministers holding portfolios that directly govern the West Bank. Itamar Ben Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich are not aberrations in the current Israeli political landscape. They are its defining feature — the logical endpoint of a process that began in June 1967 when Israel chose to occupy rather than negotiate, and to settle rather than withdraw.

The occupation that was described as temporary in 1967 is now the defining fact of Palestinian existence. It has produced the desperation, the radicalization, the armed resistance, and the civilian suffering that the current catastrophe in Gaza represents. It is not the only cause of October 7th — nothing with this many roots has a single cause. But any account of October 7th that omits fifty-eight years of military occupation is not an account of history. It is a brief for one side.

This series is not a brief for any side. It is an accounting. And the 1967 occupation’s fifty-eight-year ledger is one of the most consequential entries in it.
