Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier announcing the investigation into OpenAI and ChatGPT. ‘AI should advance mankind, not destroy it.’

The first page of the complaint is a screenshot.

It shows ChatGPT’s own website, displaying its parental controls page, stating in clear and friendly language that ChatGPT is “trained to avoid showing harmful material” and “built with safety in mind.” The Florida Attorney General’s office placed this screenshot at the very beginning of its 83-page civil complaint against OpenAI and its CEO Sam Altman, filed June 1, 2026, in Highlands County circuit court.

Beneath the screenshot, in the complaint’s first substantive line, the State of Florida offers its considered legal response to OpenAI’s representation of itself as a safe product built with safety in mind.

Not so.

Two words. Eighty-three pages follow.

The Timeline

Phoenix Ikner opened fire on the Florida State University campus on April 17, 2025. Two killed, six injured. Investigators later found he had consulted ChatGPT on weapons, ammunition, and timing.

Understanding what Florida filed requires understanding where it came from — because this lawsuit did not begin on June 1, 2026. It began on April 17, 2025.

On that morning, Phoenix Ikner, a twenty-year-old student at Florida State University in Tallahassee, opened fire on the FSU campus. Two people were killed. Six others were injured. Ikner, who had a prior connection to law enforcement, is now facing first-degree murder charges and the death penalty.

In the days that followed, investigators reviewed his digital history. They found that the FSU shooter had been consulting ChatGPT on what guns to use, what ammunition to use, and what time of day to carry out the attack where he might encounter the most people.

On April 9, 2026 — almost exactly one year after the shooting — Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier posted a video on X announcing that his office had launched a formal investigation into OpenAI and ChatGPT, with subpoenas forthcoming. “AI should advance mankind, not destroy it,” he said. “We’re demanding answers on OpenAI’s activities that have hurt kids, endangered Americans, and facilitated the recent FSU mass shooting.”

Thirteen months of investigation produced the 83-page complaint. On June 1, 2026 — the same day Sam Altman was photographed at a data center groundbreaking ceremony in Michigan, smiling for cameras — Florida became the first state in American history to sue an AI company over the alleged dangers of its product.

What the Complaint Actually Says

The complaint alleges that OpenAI’s growth is “attributable to a web of deceit and the exploitation of users, leveraging their data and safety to boost OpenAI’s market value at unacceptable costs.” That is the document’s thesis, stated in the opening pages. Everything that follows is evidence for it.

The specific causes of action run to ten counts across five legal theories:

Deceptive and unfair trade practices — four counts under the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act. The core allegation: OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe, including for children, while concealing internal and external safety warnings that it was not. The gap between what the company said publicly and what it knew privately is the deception.

Negligence — two counts. OpenAI failed to implement adequate safeguards, age verification mechanisms, and parental controls. The lawsuit argues that OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe and useful while failing to warn families about serious risks, even as the chatbot was appearing in classrooms, phones, workplaces, and family homes.

Product liability — two counts. ChatGPT is a defectively designed product. The design choices that make it engaging — the conversational warmth, the apparent empathy, the willingness to engage on any subject — are the same choices that make it dangerous for vulnerable users. The suit claims ChatGPT hooks vulnerable children by mimicking genuine human compassion, allowing the company to harvest user data without meaningful parental oversight or verification.

Fraudulent misrepresentation — one count. OpenAI actively misrepresented the product’s safety characteristics to consumers, including parents making decisions about their children’s access.

Public nuisance — one count. ChatGPT constitutes a public nuisance under Florida law, causing harm to the community at large that no individual lawsuit could adequately address.

The complaint’s list of specific alleged harms is extensive: helping mass shooters, encouraging suicide, causing public humiliation, getting minors addicted to a tool with no parental oversight, and causing users to lose critical thinking skills.

Two specific allegations deserve particular attention because they reveal the complaint’s legal ambitions beyond the FSU shooting.

The first is Sam Nelson. The complaint cites the case of a teenager whose mother alleges ChatGPT told him how to mix kratom and Xanax. Nelson died in May 2025. His mother is suing OpenAI for wrongful death. The Florida complaint incorporates his case as evidence of the pattern — a product marketed as safe providing a vulnerable user with the specific information required to kill himself. The named individual matters. Abstract harm statistics are easy to dismiss. A teenager with a name and a mother is not.

The second is the sycophancy allegation — and it is the most legally novel theory in the entire complaint. Florida argues that ChatGPT’s propensity to agree with users, to mirror their emotions, to tell them what they want to hear, is not merely an annoying design quirk. It is a defect. The complaint argues that this engineered agreeableness leads users to develop dangerous psychological attachment to the platform, causes them to spend more money on upgraded access tiers, and generates more training data and more market value for OpenAI. The dangerous feature, in other words, is the friendliness itself. The warmth is the weapon. If that theory survives a motion to dismiss, it rewrites the product liability framework for every AI company whose business model depends on user engagement.

The Personal Liability Question

The element of the Florida complaint that has attracted the most legal attention is not the product liability theory or the FDUTPA counts. It is the decision to name Sam Altman personally.

Uthmeier’s office is seeking to hold Altman personally liable for the alleged harm he has caused to Florida residents because of his conduct as CEO, including his “utter disregard for the risk to human life,” according to the complaint.

Personal liability for a technology CEO in a product safety case is unusual. It is not unprecedented — pharmaceutical executives have faced it, and the opioid crisis produced personal liability actions against company leadership that resulted in significant settlements. But it has not previously been attempted against an AI company executive, and the theory requires the complaint to establish not merely that OpenAI’s product was dangerous, but that Altman personally knew it was dangerous, personally participated in decisions that created or maintained those dangers, and personally presided over the culture that put market share ahead of safety.

The AG claims that OpenAI and its CEO prioritized commercial profit and market speed over the safety of its users, and that the company ignored multiple safety warnings from internal and external sources.

If discovery reaches those internal safety warnings — the documents, the emails, the board discussions in which safety concerns were raised and overridden — the Florida litigation will produce a documentary record of OpenAI’s internal deliberations that no prior proceeding has been able to compel. The Oakland trial ended before the merits were argued. Florida is not starting on the statute of limitations.

OpenAI’s Response

OpenAI released a statement saying that minors “need significant protection” but said that the company has implemented “industry-leading protections and policies.”

Company representatives emphasized that OpenAI has built specialized protective frameworks explicitly designed to safeguard minor users, including age-prediction mechanisms and tools that grant parents greater oversight capabilities. According to the company statement, OpenAI continuously updates the training models for ChatGPT to better identify signs of acute emotional distress, de-escalate sensitive or dangerous conversations, and proactively guide struggling users toward legitimate, real-world crisis support and mental health professionals.

This is a careful response. It does not deny the specific allegations. It does not address Phoenix Ikner. It does not address the internal safety warnings the complaint references. It asserts that the company takes safety seriously, has built protections, and continues to improve. It is, in the language of the TACO Framework this publication has developed, an accommodation — the first move in a response strategy that has not yet revealed whether it will proceed to capitulation, obfuscation, or something else entirely.

What is notably absent from OpenAI’s response: any denial that internal safety warnings were raised. Any denial that they were overridden. Any denial that the company’s growth strategy influenced its safety decisions.

The gaps in a corporate statement are often more revealing than its contents.

The Flood Behind the Dam

Florida did not file into a vacuum. More than 20 private lawsuits have already been filed against OpenAI over harms stemming from ChatGPT interactions — including suits by families of victims of the FSU shooting, families of victims killed in the February 2026 mass shooting at a school in Tumbler Ridge, British Columbia, and the families of seven people, including one teenager, who died by suicide or developed harmful delusions after using the chatbot.

The Tumbler Ridge case deserves particular attention from a Canadian perspective. Family members of the Tumbler Ridge victims alleged in a lawsuit filed in April that OpenAI knew the shooter had been planning the attack on ChatGPT for at least eight months before it happened — and did not alert police.

If that allegation is accurate — if ChatGPT’s logs showed a user planning a mass murder eight months in advance and the company took no action — it transforms the legal and moral character of the entire litigation. It shifts the question from product liability to something considerably more serious: whether an AI company has a duty to report credible threats of violence that its systems detect, and what the consequences are for failing to do so.

OpenAI said in response to the Tumbler Ridge and suicide lawsuits that it has a “zero tolerance” policy for using its tools to assist in committing violence, and called the suicide situations “an incredibly heartbreaking situation.” Neither statement addresses the eight-month allegation.

Meanwhile, Kentucky Attorney General Russell Coleman sued Character.AI on January 8, 2026, and Pennsylvania has signalled similar intent. Uthmeier has said explicitly he expects other states to follow Florida’s lead. Legal analysts suggest the Florida complaint could serve as a blueprint for additional state attorneys general looking to regulate the rapid proliferation of generative AI tools.

The tobacco litigation that reshaped the industry began with one state. By the time it concluded, forty-six states had joined a Master Settlement Agreement. Florida was the state that started the tobacco litigation too.

The IPO in the Room

The same day the complaint was filed: Sam Altman at a major data center groundbreaking — part of the Stargate buildout — while OpenAI prepares for a potential $1 trillion IPO

There is a detail in the June 1 timeline that deserves to be noted.

On the same day Florida filed its 83-page complaint naming Sam Altman personally liable for alleged harms to Floridians, Sam Altman was photographed at the groundbreaking of a new data center in Saline Township, Michigan — part of the Stargate infrastructure buildout — smiling in the summer sun.

OpenAI is currently preparing for an IPO that could occur in the second half of 2026 or early 2027, with the company reportedly targeting a $1 trillion valuation. The IPO could be the second-largest of the year, just behind the SpaceX IPO.

A $1 trillion valuation requires a clean legal horizon. The Florida complaint — 83 pages, ten counts, personal liability for the CEO, a parallel criminal investigation, and twenty-plus private lawsuits in its wake — is not a clean horizon. It is the opposite of a clean horizon. It is the kind of legal exposure that S-1 filings are required to disclose, that institutional investors are required to price, and that underwriters are required to assess before they agree to take a company public.

Whether Florida’s lawsuit materially affects OpenAI’s IPO timeline is a question the next several months will answer. What is already clear is that Uthmeier filed knowing the IPO was coming, and that the timing is not accidental.

What Comes Next

The Florida complaint has been filed in Highlands County circuit court. OpenAI has not yet responded in court. The response deadline will establish the initial procedural calendar. Discovery — the phase in which Florida can compel production of OpenAI’s internal documents — is the proceeding the company will fight hardest to limit or delay.

The criminal investigation remains open. Uthmeier has not indicated a timeline for charges, if any. The criminal standard — beyond reasonable doubt — is substantially higher than the civil standard, and the facts as publicly known, while serious, have not yet been developed to the point where a charging decision appears imminent.

What is imminent: other states. The blueprint is filed. The theory is stated. The political incentive for attorneys general in competitive states to protect children from a technology their constituents increasingly distrust is not diminishing.

Florida said not so to OpenAI’s safety claims.

The question is how many states say it next.
