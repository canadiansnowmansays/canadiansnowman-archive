Editor’s note: An hour after the Double-O onboarding packet for Robert Pattinson turned up in a printer tray at Amazon MGM Studios on Monday, November 16, 2026, a second document emerged from the same tray, still warm. It appears to be the writers’ room’s first memo on the villain for the twenty-sixth Bond film. It is reproduced below. The coffee ring on page two has not been.

MEMORANDUM To: Bond 26 Leadership, Amazon MGM Studios From: Creative (Villain Development) Re: Antagonist Shortlist, Draft One Classification: For Your Eyes Only. Do Not Forward to Goldtower.

1. The Problem

Congratulations on casting 007. He now needs someone to fight, and we must report a difficulty.

For six decades, the Bond villain was the one part of the franchise nobody had to fact-check. Dr. No kept a radioactive laboratory on a private island. Blofeld ran a global criminal syndicate from inside a hollowed-out volcano and stroked a white cat while doing it. Hugo Drax built a space station so that a hand-picked elite could repopulate the Earth. Max Zorin planned to flood Silicon Valley and corner the microchip market. Elliot Carver, a media baron, tried to start a war with China to lift his ratings.

At the time, these were fantasies. The writers’ room has now read the news, and regrets to inform leadership that reality has caught up and is lapping us. A media baron starting a war for ratings is no longer a plot; it is a programming strategy. A tech magnate cornering the chip market is a quarterly earnings call. A billionaire with a space programme and firm views on who should populate Mars is a Tuesday.

Our job, therefore, is no longer to imagine a villain the world has never seen. It is to find one the world has not seen this week. The proposals below are drawn from the morning headlines. Legal has asked that any resemblance to living persons be described as “uncanny” rather than “intentional,” and has since stopped returning our calls.

2. Considered and Rejected

Goldtower (Rejected). Considered first and dismissed fastest. A property developer who covers every surface he touches in gold leaf and cannot pass a building without putting his name on it. The writers’ room ruled him out within minutes: a gold-obsessed megalomaniac is Goldfinger, and Goldfinger came out in 1964. The premise is a relic of the Sixties, much like the gentleman himself, and audiences have seen it. Auric Goldfinger at least had a plan for Fort Knox; Goldtower merely wants to rename it after himself and charge admission. He also fails the menace test. He threatens, accommodates, capitulates and obfuscates, usually within forty-eight hours, which would leave Bond with nothing to do but wait for the capitulation and order another martini. Legal adds that he demanded top billing, final cut and his name on the Aston Martin. He has been offered a non-speaking cameo as the man the other villains keep phoning.

Several of us also recalled that in the original, Goldfinger cheats at golf. We could not find a way to make that feel fresh either.

3. The Shortlist

Moonraker (Remastered). In 1979, Hugo Drax plotted to fly a hand-picked elite into orbit and repopulate the Earth from space. The writers’ room points out that this is now a business plan with a launch calendar. The new villain owns the rockets, the satellites overhead, the social network where the plot is announced and the chatbot that writes the press release. The difficulty is pacing: by the time Bond reaches the launch pad, the rocket will have exploded on its own, and investors will call it a successful test.

Dr. No (Vaccine). A health minister who has replaced the laboratory with a podcast studio. His plot unfolds slowly, one cancelled study at a time, and his lair is decorated with diseases the twentieth century thought it had retired. Q Branch is developing a countermeasure. It is called a booster, and Bond will need two.

From Russia With Leverage. A former KGB officer who has been at war with his neighbour for nearly five years and has discovered that his most effective weapon is not a missile but a phone call to Goldtower. Every few months he produces a peace plan, which turns out to be his original demands in a nicer font. Bond’s mission is to intercept the plan before it is signed; the challenge is that it keeps arriving with an American cover letter.

The Man with the Golden Trophy. The head of a global sporting body who created a brand-new peace prize and presented the first one to Goldtower, live, with a medal and a certificate. Marketing is ecstatic. A villain handing an award to another villain is not a plot; it is a crossover event, and crossover events test very well.

The Secretary of War. A former weekend television host who renamed his department to sound more cinematic and summoned the generals to lecture them about push-ups. He is, in theory, the most dangerous man on the list. In practice, he keeps posting the battle plans in a group chat. M has asked to be added to it, as it is currently the most reliable intelligence source in Washington.

The Spy Who Billed Me. Submitted by our Toronto office. A wireless executive whose scheme is to charge the entire planet for next month’s service before this month’s has been delivered, then hold the world hostage with a cancellation fee. He has no army, no volcano and no cat. He does not need them. He has the fine print. Test audiences outside Canada found him confusing. Test audiences inside Canada left the theatre and called their carriers.

Lex Luthor (On Loan). Transferred from Casting, where he narrowly lost the role of Bond to the man he will now be fighting. Already bald, already plotting, already furious with 007 personally. Requires no rehearsal and no motivation.

4. Henchmen

The traditional henchman pool (steel teeth, razor-brimmed hats, unexplained Germans) has been retired. Henchmen will instead be recruited from the cabinet, whose members have demonstrated, at press conference after press conference, an unmatched ability to stand behind the boss, nod at everything he says and show no visible chagrin whatsoever. They work for nothing, take no initiative and will deny having been in the room. Casting has called them the most loyal ensemble in the history of the genre.

5. Lairs

Volcanoes are out; they are expensive to heat and the insurance is impossible. Locations under consideration include a gold-plated ballroom, a golf resort with an unusually forgiving scorecard, a launch pad that is mostly debris, and a television studio that has been told it is the Pentagon. The writers’ room notes that all four already exist, have already been photographed and can be visited by the public, which raises the question of what, exactly, the villains are hiding.

6. Recommendation

Creative recommends using the remaining seven at once, as a single coalition, meeting in a gold-plated ballroom borrowed from Goldtower, who has asked to be thanked in the credits. Test audiences in London found the premise wildly implausible. Test audiences in Toronto called it a documentary and asked whether it was based on a true story. We are still working out how to tell them.

The memo ends there. Across its cover page, in green ink, someone has written a reply in a firm hand that the writers’ room assumes is M’s: “Too realistic. Nobody will believe a word of it. Make one up.”

The writers’ room has been given until Friday to invent a villain more outlandish than the news. As of press time, the news was winning.

Satire. A companion to “Licence to Brood”; the villains are drawn from public reporting through November 2026.
