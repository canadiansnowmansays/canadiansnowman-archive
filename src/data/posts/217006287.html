A NOTE FROM THE CURATOR. The objects in this gallery were not, at the time of their creation, understood by anyone involved to be museum pieces. That is, historically speaking, the most reliable indicator that they eventually become the best ones. Visitors are reminded that flash photography is permitted, mockery is encouraged, and the gift shop is, appropriately, closed for renovation.

EXHIBIT 4.1 — Wall Fragment, Painted Concrete Accession No. 2031.014.1 · Acquired 2029, southern border, sector unspecified

A single panel of reinforced concrete, approximately six feet by ten, removed during the 2028–29 border infrastructure rationalisation. The graffiti on the visible face — layered, multilingual, added over several years by parties on both sides of the structure — was left uncleaned at the request of the acquiring committee, on the grounds that the writing was, in the committee’s words, “the only part of the wall that anyone actually wrote themselves.” Comparisons to a certain 1989 fragment now housed in a different national collection are, the curator notes, both obvious and intended.

EXHIBIT 4.2 — Bronze Nameplate, with Adhesive Residue Accession No. 2031.014.2 · Acquired 2029, Washington, D.C.

The plate reads, in its original casting, “The John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts.” A rectangle of adhesive residue, roughly nine inches by two, remains visible above the engraved text, where a second, temporary plate was affixed for a period of several months before being removed following a court order. Conservators have chosen not to clean the residue. It is, at present, the only part of the building’s recent history that has not been the subject of litigation.

EXHIBIT 4.3 — Stone Lion, Recovered from Storage Accession No. 2031.014.3 · Acquired 2030, a warehouse in Beltsville, Maryland

One of four lions originally commissioned for the base of the United States Triumphal Arch at Memorial Circle, removed from the final design after the Commission of Fine Arts determined the species was “not native to the United States.” The lion was placed in climate-controlled storage, where it remained for four years, awaiting a decision on its fate that nobody was ever assigned to make. It is, as of this gallery’s opening, still awaiting that decision. Visitors are asked not to disturb the lion, which the curatorial staff have come to regard with genuine affection.

EXHIBIT 4.4 — Commemorative Digital Asset, Framed Screen Capture Accession No. 2031.014.4 · Acquired 2029, a hard drive

A single frame from the price chart of a cryptocurrency token launched in January 2025, showing its valuation at peak and, in the same frame, roughly ninety seconds later. The museum has chosen to display the chart rather than the token itself, the latter being, per the acquisitions committee’s report, “not really a thing you can put behind glass, though several donors have asked.”

EXHIBIT 4.5 — Dividend Cheque, Uncashed Accession No. 2031.014.5 · Acquired 2029, a filing cabinet, provenance disputed

A cheque in the amount of five thousand dollars, drawn on an account whose funding mechanism was, at time of issue, still being explained to the public in three mutually inconsistent ways. The memo line is blank. The payee line is blank. The museum acquired the cheque un-cashed and has left it that way, on the theory that a blank cheque tells a more complete story than a cashed one ever could.

EXHIBIT 4.6 — Gilt Sample, Oval Office Trim Accession No. 2031.014.6 · Acquired 2029, a renovation dumpster, with permission

A four-inch swatch of gold-coloured moulding, removed during a 2025 redecoration and never reinstalled following a subsequent, further redecoration. Laboratory analysis confirms the gilt to be genuine gold leaf rather than paint, a finding the acquisitions committee has described, without further elaboration, as “on brand.”

EXHIBIT 4.7 — Structural Fragment, East Wing Accession No. 2031.014.7 · Acquired 2029, the White House grounds

A section of load-bearing brick from the East Wing of the White House, removed in October 2025 to accommodate a new ballroom whose final cost has been the subject of six separate revised estimates. The wing it came from housed, among other things, the office of the First Lady for the better part of a century. The museum has placed this fragment nearest the exit, not for any curatorial reason, but because the loading dock is closest to that door.

EXHIBIT 4.8 — ICC Resignation Letter Template, Unused Accession No. 2031.014.8 · Acquired 2029, a State Department printer tray

A single sheet, laser-printed, offering member states a pre-formatted template for withdrawing from the International Criminal Court, distributed following a September 2026 General Assembly address in which withdrawal was “called for” from the podium. The template was downloaded, according to server logs the museum obtained through a public records request, forty-one times. It was used zero times. The museum considers this the single most reassuring object in the gallery and has hung it accordingly, at eye level, beside the exit.

CLOSING NOTE. Gallery 4 will rotate periodically as new acquisitions become available, a process the curatorial staff anticipate will not be a problem.
