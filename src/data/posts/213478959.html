Stephen Gordon September 7, 2026

In November and December of 2020, Donald Trump told the country, in more than a thousand public statements across five weeks, that an American election could not be trusted. He named the mechanisms he said had failed him with precision: voting machines built by a company called Dominion, mail ballots processed in the middle of the night in Detroit and Philadelphia, dead people voting in Georgia, a secretary of state in that same state who would not, on a recorded phone call, find him the votes he needed. He filed more than sixty lawsuits to prove it. He asked his own vice president to overturn a certified result on the strength of it. On January 6, 2021, several thousand of his supporters walked to the Capitol because they believed him.

In 2025 and 2026, Donald Trump built, piece by piece, a set of federal levers over the same machinery he once said could not be trusted: the voting machines, the mail ballots, the state voter rolls, the district lines themselves. He did not rebuild trust in any of it. He built control over it. This series exists to place those two records next to each other, in the plainest and most specific terms the public evidence allows, and to ask a single question: what does a man do with the machinery of an election once he has spent four years insisting that machinery cannot be trusted, and has spent the years since regaining the power to redesign it?

That is the whole argument, stated once, before the five essays that will spend considerably more words proving it. This is worth doing carefully rather than as a single essay, because the honest version of this comparison requires two separately established factual records before the comparison itself means anything. Say only “Trump complained the 2020 election was rigged and now he’s rigging 2026” and the sentence is a punchline, not an argument — accurate in shape, unearned in substance, exactly the kind of claim a hostile reader can wave off as partisan shorthand. This series is not interested in the punchline. It is interested in whether the specific, documented, falsifiable claims Donald Trump made about 2020 point in a different direction than the specific, documented, falsifiable actions his administration has taken regarding 2026 — and if they do, in exactly which direction, and by exactly how much.

The structure, then. Part I will lay out the 2020 record on its own terms: not a general recollection that Trump claimed fraud, but the specific claims, named by name — Dominion, Sidney Powell’s “Kraken” affidavits, the Antrim County vote-flip theory, the Raffensperger call, the seven-state fake-elector scheme, the sixty-plus court losses (several before judges Trump himself had appointed), Attorney General William Barr’s own December 2020 finding that the Justice Department had turned up no fraud on a scale that would have changed the outcome, and the specific mechanisms — mail ballots, voting machines, state-run election administration — that those claims targeted.

Part II will lay out the mechanisms Trump’s administration has built for the 2026 midterms specifically: the mid-decade redistricting campaign Trump personally initiated in Texas and that has since spread to nine or more states; the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division, under Harmeet Dhillon, sending letters to election officials in all fifty states threatening criminal prosecution over voter-roll maintenance; the repurposed SAVE database that has already misidentified naturalized citizens as noncitizens in multiple states while producing, per one federal analysis, a documented total of forty-one voting-fraud prosecutions against a 2024 electorate of more than 150 million; and the SAVE America Act, the legislation Trump has pushed Congress to pass outright. Part III will turn from building the next election’s machinery to reopening the last one: the Justice Department’s nationwide demand for sensitive voter data from all fifty states, the FBI’s seizure of hundreds of thousands of already-triple-counted 2020 ballots from Fulton County, Georgia, and the open question of whether federal agents will appear at polling places in November 2026. The concluding essay will do the work the three middle essays deliberately withhold: it will lay the claims and the actions side by side, pair by pair, and let the reader see what changes and what does not.

What does not change, on the strength of the record assembled so far, is the machinery itself. The institutions Donald Trump named as corrupted in 2020 are, with almost no exceptions, the same institutions his administration has spent 2025 and 2026 trying to bring under direct federal control. Mail ballots were the mechanism he blamed for his loss; mail ballots are the mechanism a Postal Service rule now attempts to gate through a federal portal and a bar-code bottleneck. State voter rolls were where he said the dead and the ineligible cast ballots against him; state voter rolls are where a Justice Department division now threatens local officials with prosecution for declining to remove names a flawed federal database has flagged. District lines were not, in 2020, part of his complaint at all — but the same logic that treats an unfavorable outcome as evidence of a broken system extends easily to a district map that produces one, which may be why the redistricting campaign he started in Texas in the summer of 2025 was framed from the outset, in his own words, as necessary to winning seats he might otherwise lose. What changes is only the direction of the pressure. In 2020, the claim was that these systems had failed him. In 2026, the remedy is not to make these systems more trustworthy in some neutral sense. It is to make them answerable to him specifically, in advance, regardless of what a neutral standard would require.

This series is going to reach for a harsher word before it is finished, and it is worth saying so now rather than pretending otherwise: the concluding essay will name this pattern hypocrisy, and mean it as a specific, technical claim rather than a shrug or an insult. The word is being held back on purpose. Handed to a reader before the record is built, it invites exactly the shortcut this series exists to refuse — agree with the label first, check the specifics never. So the definition comes now, ahead of the word itself. Hypocrisy, precisely used, is not merely holding a belief that turns out to be wrong, and it is not merely changing one’s mind. It is applying one standard to a circumstance that disadvantages you and a different, more convenient standard to the identical circumstance once it advantages you instead — and doing so without acknowledging that the standard moved. That is a testable claim, not a character judgment, and it is the only kind of claim this series intends to make. The test is symmetrical by design: if the record shows Donald Trump applying a consistent standard to election administration across both periods — skeptical of centralized federal involvement in 2020, skeptical of it again in 2026, or trusting of state-level control in both years rather than only when the state in question is inconvenient to him — then the hypocrisy claim fails its own test, and this series will have to say so, in the concluding essay, in place of the word it spent three essays not using. This publication’s companion series, The Baseline Problem, was built on the same discipline in a different register: counting behavioral patterns rather than diagnosing them, because the counting is falsifiable and the diagnosis is not. This series borrows that same commitment to falsifiability and applies it to a different kind of pattern — not what a man says or does spontaneously, but what he claims a system requires when he loses, against what he builds into that system once he no longer has to accept an unfavorable count as final.

There is an obvious objection worth naming before Part I even begins, because ignoring it would be its own kind of intellectual laziness: every American president has favored election rules that help his own party, and every losing party in modern American politics has found something to say about the fairness of the process that beat it. Al Gore’s campaign contested Florida’s count in 2000. Stacey Abrams, after her narrow 2018 loss, argued for years that Georgia’s election administration under Brian Kemp had been structurally unfair to her candidacy. Neither example is equivalent to what this series will document, and the difference is not partisan convenience — it is scale, specificity, and consequence. Gore’s challenge ran through the courts and ended, by his own concession, within five weeks; it never produced a claim that the machines themselves had been rigged, and it never produced an attempt to overturn a certified result through the vice presidency. Abrams’s argument was about structural disadvantage built into ordinary administration — closed polling places, purge timing, voter-ID rules — pursued through advocacy and litigation, not through sixty-plus lawsuits alleging an invented criminal conspiracy, not through a phone call asking an official to manufacture votes, and not through a mob at a building where electoral votes were being certified. The distinction this series will hold itself to throughout is between contesting an outcome through the system and asserting, without evidence a single court ever accepted, that the system itself is criminally corrupted — and then, four years later, building federal levers over that same system on the strength of the identical, still-unproven premise.

None of what follows requires speculation about Donald Trump’s motives, his psychology, or his sincerity, and this series will make a point of avoiding all three. It requires only two adjacent, well-documented public records and an honest reader’s willingness to compare them. Part I begins with the record everyone remembers in outline and almost no one has seen assembled end to end: the specific claims, the specific courts that rejected them, and the specific officials — including members of Trump’s own administration — who said, on the record, that the claims were false. Part II follows with the record fewer people have connected into a single throughline: not a single dramatic order but a slow accumulation of redistricting maps, prosecution letters, database purges, and postal rules, each defensible in isolation and considerably harder to defend once placed beside the other three.

The concluding essay exists because the comparison, done honestly, does not need editorializing to make its point. It needs only to be made.
