Bottom Line Up Front: On September 4, 2026, President Trump threatened to halt trade with every country running a surplus against the United States unless the Federal Reserve slashes interest rates to “the LOWEST RATE of any country in the World.” To justify the threat, he invoked the Supreme Court’s February 20, 2026 ruling in Learning Resources v. Trump — the same 6-3 decision that struck down his IEEPA tariffs as unlawful — calling it “ridiculous and very costly” while simultaneously claiming it “strongly acknowledged” his “absolute right” to do exactly this. This is the Whack-a-Mole series’ fifth act, and the mallet has found a new target: not a customs statute, not a trading partner, but the Federal Reserve itself.

The Threat

As Tony Romm and Colby Smith reported for The New York Times, Mr. Trump’s September 4 morning began with genuinely good news: the August jobs report showed roughly 162,000 new hires, a labor market that has absorbed a trade war and an intensifying conflict with Iran without buckling. He posted about the number, credited his own stewardship, and then pivoted — in the same breath — to an ultimatum aimed at the one institution in Washington that still, on paper, answers to nobody.

Lower the rate, he said, or he would stop trading with every country running a deficit against the United States. That list is not short. It includes Canada and Mexico, most of the European Union, and China — which is to say, most of what fills American shelves and factories. He framed the threat as a matter of settled law, citing the Supreme Court’s tariff ruling as having “strongly acknowledged” that the president holds an “absolute right” to cut off deficit countries. He closed by telling the Fed’s “great new leader” to “get smart” and “BE PATRIOTS for a change.”

That “great new leader” is Kevin Warsh, the chairman Mr. Trump spent the better part of a year maneuvering into place. The irony of needing to publicly strong-arm your own handpicked appointee, four months into the job, is not one this series intends to let pass quietly.

The Man Trump Picked

Worth pausing on exactly how deliberately Mr. Trump built this appointment, because it makes Friday’s ultimatum land differently than ordinary Fed-bashing.

Trump nominated Warsh on January 30, 2026, explicitly to replace Jerome Powell — a chairman Trump had spent years attacking for being too slow to cut rates, and whose final months in office were shadowed by a Justice Department investigation into Fed building renovation costs that Senator Thom Tillis used to block Fed nominees until it was dropped. Once that obstacle cleared, Warsh’s path was smooth by comparison: confirmed to the Board 51-45, then to the chairmanship 54-45, sworn in May 22 with the Federal Open Market Committee immediately and unanimously electing him its chair. Every marker of the process signaled a president confident he had finally installed a partner rather than an obstacle.

But Warsh is not, by history or temperament, a natural instrument for what Friday’s threat demands of him. He made his name at the Fed nearly two decades ago as its youngest-ever governor and an inflation hawk who has since called for “regime change” at the institution over its 2021-22 inflation failure. He has argued publicly that the Fed talks too much — that tools like quarterly rate forecasts make it harder for officials to change course when the data demands it — and his own instinct at Jackson Hole was to refuse exactly the kind of forward commitment Mr. Trump is now trying to extract from him in public. Trump appears to have bet that a hawk with Republican credibility could sell a rate cut more convincingly than Powell ever could. What he got instead was a chairman whose entire professional identity rests on not being seen taking instructions on where rates should go.

That is the trap: Warsh cannot cut without looking like he capitulated to a trade ultimatum and abandoned the inflation-hawk reputation that got him the job in the first place. He cannot hold the line without becoming, within four months of his own confirmation, exactly the kind of Fed chairman Mr. Trump spent a year publicly humiliating Powell for being. There is no version of the next two weeks that leaves Kevin Warsh’s credibility fully intact, and Mr. Trump built the trap himself, one confirmation vote at a time.

The Ruling He’s Citing Is the Ruling That Beat Him

Readers of this series have been here before, but this installment’s twist deserves its own paragraph, because it is a genuinely new move even by this administration’s standards.

The Supreme Court’s February ruling did not expand presidential tariff authority. It did the opposite. In a 6-3 opinion authored by Chief Justice Roberts, the Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs at all — striking down the “Liberation Day” tariffs, the fentanyl-and-migration tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China, and every other IEEPA-based duty in one stroke. It was, by any measure, the single most consequential defeat of Mr. Trump’s trade agenda to date, reportedly exposing the administration to more than $160 billion in potential refunds.

Installments I through IV of this series tracked what happened next: rather than accept the ruling, the administration went statute-shopping — Section 122 within days, Section 301 within months, both explicitly described by this series as symptoms of a president who treats an adverse ruling as an instruction to find a different legal doorway rather than a boundary. What makes Friday’s threat different is that it doesn’t reach for a new statute at all. It reaches for the losing one, and asks readers to believe it said something it did not say.

There is no passage in Learning Resources that blesses a presidential power to sever trade with deficit countries as leverage over the Federal Reserve. The ruling is about statutory delegation under IEEPA specifically; it says nothing about Section 122, nothing about the Fed, and nothing remotely resembling an “absolute right” to use trade as a monetary-policy cudgel. What the ruling does contain — and this is likely the fragment being stretched past recognition — is the acknowledgment, standard in trade-law jurisprudence, that Congress has, through other statutes, delegated the president some tariff authority in some circumstances. That is a description of Section 122 and Section 301’s continued viability, not an endorsement of using either against the central bank.

Calling a ruling “ridiculous and very costly” while citing it as authority is not a contradiction this administration seems to notice. It may not be one it needs to notice, given how little the distinction has mattered to its base. But it is the cleanest illustration yet of the pattern this series exists to document: the law is not a constraint to be followed, it is raw material to be quoted selectively, in whichever direction is convenient that morning.

From Foreign Leverage to Domestic Leverage

The first four installments of Constitutional Whack-a-Mole all shared a structural feature: the target was always outward-facing. Canada, Mexico, the EU, China, importers waiting on refunds — the fight was between the executive and the courts over how much pain could be inflicted on foreign trade and domestic businesses. Friday’s threat moves the fight inside the government itself.

The Fed has held rates steady since December 2025 while it works out whether the recent bout of economic turbulence is transient or structural. Far from planning cuts, several sitting governors have spent the week signaling the opposite. Governor Christopher Waller said Thursday that a “hot” inflation report next week — the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases its Consumer Price Index data on September 11 — would push him toward supporting a rate increase, though he’d be open to holding steady if disinflation continues. Governor Michael Barr struck a similar note on Tuesday: continued progress buys the Fed time to wait, but insufficient progress means the Fed “should act decisively to raise rates.” Chairman Warsh himself, in his Jackson Hole remarks a week earlier, pointedly declined to offer forward guidance in either direction.

The clock on all of this is short and specific. The FOMC’s next scheduled meetings run this week, with the rate decision, updated economic projections and dot plot due at 2:00 p.m. today, followed by Warsh’s own press conference half an hour later. That is twelve days from Mr. Trump’s threat to the moment his handpicked chairman either delivers the cut publicly demanded of him, holds steady in open defiance of it, or — given Waller and Barr’s remarks this week — potentially raises rates entirely, turning what began as a demand for the “LOWEST RATE of any country in the World” into the opposite outcome, delivered on camera, twelve days after the ultimatum was issued.

In other words, the institution Mr. Trump is threatening to hold trade hostage over is not quietly deliberating a cut he can nudge along — it is actively weighing whether the next move is a hike, for the same inflation-fighting reasons the Fed was created to insulate from exactly this kind of political pressure.

The Institutional Collision

Central bank independence is not a courtesy extended to the Fed; it is the entire design premise. A president who can threaten a chairman’s monetary decisions with the fate of global trade has effectively made interest-rate policy a hostage negotiation, decided not by the inflation data due September 11 but by whichever principal blinks first. Mr. Trump has already spent much of this year trying to remove Fed officials who don’t share his view on rates — this newest maneuver simply swaps direct personnel pressure for a much larger blunt instrument: the entire architecture of American trade.

Should he actually follow through, the “deficit countries” list reads like a inventory of the American supply chain. This series’ companion coverage in The Great Canadian Trade War has already tracked what happens when Ottawa becomes collateral damage in a dispute that has nothing to do with Canada; a trade freeze invoked to pressure the Fed would put Canada, Mexico and the EU back in that position for a fight in which none of them are actually a party.

There is also the small matter of whether the president legally can do this. Section 122 authorizes a temporary import surcharge to address balance-of-payments crises — a narrow, time-limited emergency tool, not a standing lever for domestic monetary policy, and one already under a countdown clock this series has tracked since February. Section 301 requires an investigative record tied to specific unfair trade practices, not a Truth Social post about interest rates. Neither statute, nor the IEEPA ruling he is quoting, contains anything resembling authority to halt trade broadly as leverage over the Federal Reserve. If Mr. Trump attempts this, expect the same plaintiffs who won in February to be back in court within days — and expect this series to be covering Whack-a-Mole VI not long after.

History Has Been Here Before

This is not the first time an American president has tried to bend the Fed to an election-year or approval-rating timeline, and the last time it happened, the country is still paying it off in the folk memory of the 1970s.

Richard Nixon inherited a Fed chairman, William McChesney Martin, whose tight-money instincts he blamed for the 1969-70 recession, and replaced him with Arthur Burns — a man he expected, correctly, to be more pliable. As Planet Money’s Kenny Malone and Mary Childs reconstructed from the Nixon tapes, the pressure that followed was not subtle: private Camp David meetings, threats to pack the Fed’s board, and a White House aide who bragged privately that the administration had Burns’s monetary policy in a chokehold. Burns held out through 1971, warning Nixon at one point that flooding the banks risked “awful problems” the following year. By early 1972 — an election year — he had capitulated. Money supply growth more than tripled year over year. Unemployment fell, Nixon won a landslide, and inflation went on to hit double digits by 1974, with the Fed eventually forced to jack rates up to 13% just to break it.

The parallel is not exact — Nixon worked Burns quietly, over years, through private arm-twisting rather than public ultimatums backed by trade threats — but the mechanism is identical: a president who treats the central bank’s independence as an inconvenience to be managed rather than a structural feature to be respected, in service of short-term numbers he can point to before the next reckoning. The 1970s version of that story ended in stagflation. Mr. Trump’s version, should Chairman Warsh give ground the way Burns eventually did, would arrive with an added complication Burns never had to contend with: a simultaneous global trade freeze layered on top of an already-loosened monetary policy, at a moment when the Fed itself is debating whether to raise rates rather than cut them.

The Cautionary Tale That Isn’t Even Subtle

If Nixon’s story is the quiet, decades-old warning, Turkey’s is the loud, recent one — and it involves not persuasion but outright command, which makes it the closer analogy to a president publicly ordering a rate cut on Truth Social.

Recep Tayyip Erdogan has spent the better part of a decade insisting, against the entire discipline of economics, that high interest rates cause inflation rather than cure it — he has called them “the mother and father of all evil” and framed his low-rate campaign as an “economic war of independence.” To make that theory policy, he did not stop at persuasion. He fired three central bank governors in two years for the crime of raising rates, then stacked the monetary policy committee with allies who would deliver the cuts he wanted regardless of the inflation data in front of them.

The results are not ambiguous. The lira lost roughly half its value against the dollar through 2021 alone. Inflation, officially near 20% when the rate-cutting campaign began, blew past 36%, then 78%, then into the high double digits — by some independent estimates considerably worse than the government’s own numbers. Turks earning salaries in lira watched their purchasing power evaporate in real time; basic goods and even some imported medications became difficult to source. Markets stopped pretending the central bank was making its own decisions at all — one analyst’s assessment at the time was blunt: Erdogan was simply running the show, and the bank would produce whatever rate he wanted regardless of the underlying economics.

Turkey is not the United States, and the lira is not the dollar’s reserve-currency insulation. But the mechanism Erdogan modeled — a head of government who substitutes conviction for data, treats the central bank’s independence as an obstacle rather than a safeguard, and replaces anyone who won’t deliver the number he wants — is precisely the mechanism on display in Friday’s threat. The difference this time is that the lever isn’t a fired governor. It’s the entire architecture of American trade, aimed at a chairman who has been in the job four months.

The Ledger, Updated

Four statutory authorities invoked or discussed in this series to date: IEEPA (struck down), Section 122 (twice used, currently on its own clock), Section 301 (expanded to 59 countries as of the July 23 installment), and now — for the first time — no statute at all, just a bare assertion of presidential will dressed up in a mischaracterized court opinion. The mole has stopped bothering to find a new hole. It is simply declaring the whole lawn its own.

The tariff refund fight tracked in Whack-a-Mole III remains unresolved. The Section 122 clock tracked in Whack-a-Mole IV is still running. And now a fifth front has opened, aimed not at a trading partner or a court but at the one part of the federal government explicitly built to be deaf to exactly this kind of pressure. Whether Warsh’s Fed holds that line — or becomes the next institution absorbed into the president’s improvised legal architecture — is the question this series will be watching, with the September 11 inflation data as the first checkpoint and the September 16 rate decision as the deadline that actually matters.

The mallet found a new game. It’s still the same small hands.
