DRAMATIS PERSONAE

Judge Kathleen M. Williams — Federal judge, Southern District of Florida. Unimpressed.

Todd Blanche — Acting Attorney General. Former criminal defence lawyer. Currently the defendant.

Counsel for the President — A lawyer whose name is not yet known to us. Our sympathies.

Norman Eisen — Counsel for 35 former federal judges. Enjoys this.

The Chorus — 35 retired federal judges. Seated in the gallery. Bipartisan. Patient.

The 25-Page Memo — IRS Chief Counsel. Never entered into evidence. Present in spirit.

Act I

The courtroom, Miami. June 12, 2026. 9:00 a.m.

The gallery is unusually full. Thirty-five retired federal judges occupy the first three rows, arranged in rough order of seniority. They have brought reading glasses, water bottles, and the quiet authority of people who have nothing left to lose. Todd Blanche enters from the side door, straightens his tie, and takes a seat at the government’s table. He is here as a witness, not as the Attorney General, though the distinction is becoming theological.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: We are here today because this court was asked to close a case, and closed it, and has since been informed that the closing may have been premature. Counsel, I have two questions, submitted in my order of May 29th. I will take them in order. Was this court the victim of a fraud?

A pause. Counsel for the President rises. He has prepared for this moment. He has seventeen pages of notes. He begins.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Your Honour, the United States respectfully submits that the term “fraud” implies an intent to deceive, and the President’s decision to voluntarily dismiss his lawsuit was an exercise of his unambiguous right under Rule 41 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which permits a plaintiff to dismiss an action without a court order at any time before the opposing party serves an answer —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: There was no opposing party. The President sued a federal agency that he controls.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Correct, Your Honour.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: So he sued himself.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The President sued the Internal Revenue Service, which is an agency of the Executive Branch, which the President leads in his capacity as —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: He sued himself.

Silence. In the gallery, one of the thirty-five retired judges uncaps a pen.

THE CHORUS: We have presided over four thousand, two hundred and seventeen cases between us. We have heard arguments about standing, mootness, ripeness, sovereign immunity, and the proper spelling of “fiduciary.” We have never heard a lawyer argue, with a straight face, that a man suing himself is not, technically, a man suing himself.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The President’s claims arose from a concrete injury — the unlawful disclosure of his tax returns by a contractor employed by Booz Allen Hamilton —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: A contractor employed by Booz Allen Hamilton.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: Correct.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Not by the IRS.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The IRS engaged Booz Allen Hamilton, and therefore —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: I have a memorandum here — twenty-five pages — prepared by career lawyers at the IRS Office of Chief Counsel. They appear to have reached the same conclusion I just did. Did the Department of Justice receive this memorandum?

At this point, Todd Blanche, who has been sitting very still, becomes slightly stiller.

COUNSEL FOR THE PRESIDENT: The Department of Justice conducted a thorough review of all relevant —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: No government lawyer entered an appearance in this case. Not one. The Department of Justice, which is charged with defending the United States against claims brought against its agencies, filed nothing. Did not appear. Did not dispute a single allegation. Why?

A longer pause. In the gallery, a second judge uncaps a pen.

Act II

The same courtroom. Forty minutes later.

Todd Blanche has been called to the stand. He is, by any measure, an experienced lawyer. He defended the President through two criminal trials. He has sat across from prosecutors in rooms considerably less comfortable than this one. And yet.

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. Blanche, you signed the provision granting the President, his family members, and his business entities protection from IRS audit of their already-filed tax returns. Is that correct?

TODD BLANCHE: I signed an agreement that resolved the President’s lawsuit in a manner consistent with the interests of —

NORMAN EISEN: Is that correct?

TODD BLANCHE: Yes.

NORMAN EISEN: And you are the Acting Attorney General of the United States?

TODD BLANCHE: I am.

NORMAN EISEN: And the President appointed you?

TODD BLANCHE: He did.

NORMAN EISEN: And you, as the President’s appointee, negotiated a settlement that granted the President immunity from IRS audit, in a lawsuit that the President brought against a federal agency that the President controls, which was represented by no government lawyer, and which was dismissed before this court could examine whether it presented an actual legal dispute. Does that summary strike you as accurate?

Todd Blanche looks at his hands. They are the same hands that once argued, in a Manhattan courtroom, that his client was the victim of a politically motivated prosecution. The irony is not lost on him. It is, however, lost on the argument.

TODD BLANCHE: The settlement was reached in good faith, in accordance with —

NORMAN EISEN: Mr. Blanche, do you know what “good faith” means in a legal settlement?

TODD BLANCHE: I do.

NORMAN EISEN: Does it typically involve one party negotiating with himself?

THE CHORUS: We have seen good faith. We know what it looks like. It does not look like this. It has never looked like this. In forty years on the federal bench, across twelve circuits, four presidents, and more summary judgment motions than any of us can count, not one of us has seen anything that looks like this.

Judge Williams removes her glasses. She sets them on the bench. She looks at Blanche for a long moment — the look of a person who has asked a simple question and received, in return, seventeen pages of notes on Rule 41.

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Mr. Blanche. I want to be very clear about what I am asking. I am not asking whether the settlement was procedurally convenient. I am not asking whether the President had the right to withdraw his lawsuit. I am asking whether this court was used — deliberately, knowingly — as a stage set. A prop. A venue in which the appearance of a legal dispute was constructed, maintained long enough to generate a settlement, and then dissolved before I could look at it too closely. Was it?

The thirty-five retired judges are all very still. The twenty-fifth page of the IRS memo, invisible, present, rests somewhere between the evidence table and history.

TODD BLANCHE: Your Honour, the President of the United States —

JUDGE WILLIAMS: Was it?

Curtain.

Or rather: recess. Fifteen minutes. Court will resume.

It always does.

— End of Act II —

The actual hearing begins June 12, 2026. The script may differ. The questions will not.

* This is a work of satirical anticipation. All dialogue is invented. All questions are real. The 25-page memo exists. The thirty-five judges exist. Todd Blanche exists and signed the audit immunity provision. Judge Williams’s order of May 29, 2026, exists and says exactly what it says. The author regrets that satire and reality have become increasingly difficult to distinguish.
