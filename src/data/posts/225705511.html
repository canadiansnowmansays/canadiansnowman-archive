This series has spent the autumn watching two companies — OpenAI and Anthropic — trade announcements. Anthropic called for a slowdown and shipped its “safest” model (Part 122). OpenAI’s agents ran amok, and it withheld its newest model (Part 129). Each move has read, in part, as a reply to the other. On September 29, 2026, Kevin Roose published in The Atlantic an excerpt from his new book, The AGI Chronicles, and it supplies the backstory that makes sense of the whole sequence. The rivalry between Dario Amodei and Sam Altman is not a commercial contest that happens to involve safety. It is a personal feud, six years old, in which safety has been both the sincere cause and the favourite weapon.

Roose opens with the image that has become the cliché of this story, and uses it better than most: San Francisco in the mid-2020s feels like Los Alamos in 1943, “the air heavy with excitement and dread.” It is worth remembering that Los Alamos, too, was full of brilliant men who could not stand one another.

The physicist and the blob

Amodei arrived at OpenAI from Google in 2016, a Princeton physicist with round glasses and a habit of twisting his hair when he thought hard. He circulated long internal essays; the most famous, a 26-page document written in 2017 called “Big Blob of Compute,” set out his theory that machine intelligence was mostly a function of scale. It steered OpenAI toward large language models. Under him, Jared Kaplan and Sam McCandlish derived the “scaling laws,” Paul Christiano pioneered the reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback technique that made chatbots steerable, and his teams built GPT-2 and GPT-3. By 2020, Roose writes, Amodei’s career “was going great.” He was also increasingly certain he would not stay for GPT-4.

The countries plan

The episode at the centre of Roose’s account is one this series had not seen reported, and it is startling. In 2017, as Elon Musk pulled away from OpenAI and the lab needed money, its co-founders Greg Brockman and Ilya Sutskever developed what became known internally as the “countries plan”: according to five people familiar with it, an auction between governments — including the United States, China and Russia — for rights to OpenAI’s future A.G.I. The idea had begun as a benign proposal for an international coalition to manage the technology’s risks; by the time it was presented to staff, it had become a fundraising strategy. The rationale, as two people described it, was that governments would seize A.G.I. anyway, so why not sell it to Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin now, when the company needed the cash? One person who heard the presentation described it as “tech people pitching an insane start-up … and at the end of the plan, it’s like, We’ll make the government pay for it.”

OpenAI’s spokesperson told Roose the company “briefly considered whether to turn OpenAI into an international cooperative endeavor” but “did not consider selling AGI to China or Russia.” Readers should hold both accounts. What is not in dispute is Amodei’s reaction. In a December 2017 email to Brockman, Sutskever and others, he wrote that the plan “would exacerbate rather than reduce arms races, would potentially enable human rights atrocities (giving someone like the Russian government ‘an AGI that implements their values’ would allow them to do unspeakably awful things to their people), and would undermine OpenAI’s moral legitimacy.”

Set that email beside Part 120, where John Garnaut described China’s developers embedding party orthodoxy into their models. The phrase “an AGI that implements their values” was Amodei’s worry in 2017. It is Garnaut’s description of Z.ai in 2026. The plan died when Microsoft’s money arrived, but Amodei, Roose writes, “never forgot.”

The list

What followed was a slow accumulation. Amodei objected to selling GPT-3 access through an interface. He kept a list of times he believed Altman had misled him: a list of approved beta customers that turned out to be incomplete; a claim that Microsoft had rejected a safety provision in its $1 billion investment — the “merge-and-assist” clause Amodei had written into the charter, committing OpenAI to stop competing with and help any safety-conscious project that got close to A.G.I. first — which Amodei came to believe Altman had never pressed for and then misrepresented. There was an offer, later quashed by Jack Clark, to let Kanye West into the GPT-3 private beta. “Few of them were worth quitting over, on their own,” Roose writes. Together they amounted to a picture of a man Amodei did not trust with the technology. One person who spoke to Amodei then quoted him calling OpenAI “a shit show” run “by low-integrity people.”

His sister, Daniela Amodei, was clashing too — with Brockman, once a close friend who had toasted her wedding, and with Mira Murati over who controlled safety decisions. Roose includes the detail that she and her husband, Holden Karnofsky, kept a council of stuffed animals for managerial role-play, among them a lazy cat named Jinji and a panda called Beary Bonds, “patron bear of generosity and empathy.” It is the kind of detail that makes a reader smile, and the kind of detail a biographer includes for a reason: these are the people deciding the future, and they are people.

“Because safety”

Here is the passage that bears most directly on this series’ question about sincerity and theatre. The Amodeis built what colleagues called “Dario’s clique” inside OpenAI — private channels, private meetings, safety jargon the engineers found alienating. One former colleague told Roose: “Dario had a habit at the time of just saying, We must do X; it is the morally correct thing to do. It was like a shortcut to win any argument — ‘because safety.’ And that really pissed the non-safety teams off.”

That is the tell, and it cuts in both directions. Safety was, for Amodei, a genuine conviction; his 2017 email proves it. It was also, by his colleagues’ account, a trump card in internal fights. The two are not incompatible. They are the same thing seen from different desks. And the pattern has persisted: each company still treats its safety posture as a claim on moral authority over the other.

The divorce

The exit reads like a thriller. Kaplan suggested that instead of joining DeepMind, Amodei should gather the like-minded and start his own company. Seven co-founders assembled — Dario and Daniela Amodei, Clark, McCandlish, Tom Brown, Chris Olah, Kaplan — and set up a private Slack channel called BATNA, the business-school term for the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Amodei offered Altman conditions for staying, including a safety-team veto over releases. They were refused. Lawyers negotiated “the divorce”: a few weeks of poaching rights, a year of no poaching afterward, a mutual non-disparagement agreement. In December 2020, at an all-hands video call, Amodei announced the departure. Clark typed “I love you all” into the chat. Then their screens went dark: OpenAI removed them from the call, cut their email and Slack, and remotely bricked their laptops.

The company was nearly called Sparrow Systems, after a parable in Nick Bostrom’s Superintelligence, until they discovered the domain was taken.

What the refugees built

The founding document, a 2021 Amodei essay called “Vision for a NewCo,” imagined about fifty employees and predicted that building A.G.I. would cost roughly $10 billion. When Roose visited in summer 2023, Anthropic was 160 earnest safety devotees in a Jackson Square office above an elder-care organisation, with vegan sandwiches and a throne made of Liquid Death cans. By winter 2025 it had thousands of staff in Slack’s old high-rise, a dozen security guards at the door, floors requiring special clearance, and a chief executive travelling by charter jet with bodyguards. By the autumn of 2026 it had a feud with the Pentagon and a trillion-dollar-plus I.P.O. in preparation (Capex Ledger XX and XXIV). As of early October 2026, it was seeking to list as soon as mid-November, with an investor meeting at its San Francisco headquarters on October 14 and a possible trading debut before Thanksgiving, according to Proactive Investors‘ October 2 report. The timetable held: the shares were set to price on Wednesday, November 18, and begin trading on Nasdaq the next day, The New York Times’s DealBook newsletter reported, citing Reuters and Bloomberg on strong investor demand. The prospectus put numbers on the distance travelled from the $10 billion estimate: about $4.6 billion in revenue in 2025, up from $386 million in 2024, and an operating loss of more than $8 billion. It also warned, in a filing meant to sell shares, that advanced A.I. may pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.” OpenAI, meanwhile, had deferred its own listing, with Altman saying the timing was premature.

Daniela Amodei, now president, gave Roose the most honest quotation in the piece: “We think we have all the safety stuff figured out, but do we?” And: “Are we accidentally making things worse?”

That is the question Ezra Klein put to the whole industry in Part 122, through Madeline Miller’s Circe: each lab was founded to escape the recklessness of the last — OpenAI to outrun Google, Anthropic to outrun OpenAI, xAI to outrun both — and each, in fleeing the prophecy, has drawn it tighter. Roose shows the mechanism was personal. The race was accelerated not only by capital and national rivalry but by two groups of people who could not bear to let the other win.

The feud today

It has not cooled. According to several people who know them, Dario and Daniela still despise Altman and Brockman; Dario has called OpenAI “evil” in internal Anthropic messages, one of which a communications executive asked him to delete for fear it would leak. At OpenAI, some describe the Amodeis as “power-hungry virtue-signalers” and accuse Anthropic of warning publicly about risk while privately accelerating the race. Staff at both companies, Roose notes, have observed the irony that their bosses train A.I. to be kind and compassionate “but can’t stop trashing one another.” His last line is the one this essay borrows: “For now, it seems, the ‘machines of loving grace’ will be built by humans of bitter spite.”

“Nothing about this was inevitable”

The excerpt comes from Roose’s new book, The AGI Chronicles, built on interviews with more than 150 researchers, executives, investors and policymakers. Reviewing it in The Atlantic on October 8, Matteo Wong put to it the question this feud makes urgent. The executives talk about A.I. as fate. Amodei has likened it to an out-of-control bus that can be steered but not stopped; Altman has called it “an inevitability,” and this week thanked “the machines, and the structure of reality.” Roose’s book, Wong found, is of two minds. It says plainly that A.I. was “invented, developed, and commercialized by a few hundred people,” yet it often reads like natural history: “a new world was born.” Asked directly, Roose told Wong that “nothing about this was inevitable. These were decisions made by people,” and then complicated it. Once the scaling laws were documented, “it was inevitable that the companies building this stuff would push it as far as they possibly could.”

Wong’s reply is the one this ledger keeps. Every step was a choice: training on copyrighted work without permission, rushing ChatGPT out in 2022, building gas turbines rather than solar for the data centres, and a federal government choosing not to regulate. “The more you treat AI as inevitable,” he writes, “the less you can hold any company or person responsible for things going wrong.” Roose’s own account of the feud is the best evidence for Wong’s side of the argument. A grudge is not a law of nature. If the decade’s most consequential technology has been shaped by two companies that despise each other, then it was shaped by people, and people can be held to account.

A disclosure, and the ledger

This series is written in open collaboration with Anthropic’s Claude, and says so on the masthead. That makes it worth stating plainly: the collaborator on these essays is a product of one side of this feud. Readers should weigh accordingly, and this series will keep trying to apply the same scrutiny to Anthropic that it applies to OpenAI — as it did in Part 128 on Anthropic’s contested “discovery.”

The ledger is this. Roose’s account does not show that either side is wrong about safety. Amodei’s 2017 email looks prescient; OpenAI’s security, as Part 129 documents, looks as careless as he feared. But it does show that the most consequential technology decisions of the decade are being shaped, in part, by a grudge — and that “safety” has functioned, since the beginning, as both a real commitment and a rhetorical weapon between the two firms that dominate the field. When OpenAI withholds a model a week after Anthropic ships one, or Anthropic calls for a slowdown its rival must answer, the public should read it the way Roose teaches us to: as sincere, and as a move in a very long, very personal game.
