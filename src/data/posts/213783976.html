There is a version of this series that has, for seven parts now, been mostly fair to Republicans by simply not discussing them very much. The premise has been that the Democratic Party’s problems are Democratic Party problems, that the “stacking” thesis from Part I and Part VI is an internal audit, and that whether the GOP is behaving honourably is somebody else’s series to write. I want to complicate that premise this week, because a New York Times columnist has made an argument that this series cannot responsibly ignore: that the scrutiny applied to Democratic inconsistency, however earned, is being applied at a scale wildly out of proportion to the reinvention happening on the other side of the aisle.

As Frank Bruni wrote in The New York Times on 31 August 2026, Abdul El-Sayed has become “the gotcha piñata” of this election cycle — a candidate so thoroughly stuffed with past statements to the left of the median voter that Republicans need only keep swinging to watch the goodies fall out. Jesse Watters, interviewing El-Sayed for Fox News on 24 August 2026, resurfaced the Green New Deal, defunding the police, and abolishing the Second Amendment, and treated El-Sayed’s attempts to redirect toward economic anxiety as evidence of evasiveness rather than message discipline. This is, on its own terms, fair game. Politicians who change their emphasis owe voters an account of the change, and Bruni concedes as much without hedging.

But then Bruni does something this series should have done earlier: he asks what the same standard, applied evenly, would find on the Republican side of the Michigan ballot. Mike Rogers, El-Sayed’s opponent, called Trump’s post-2020 conduct “more gangster than presidential” in January 2021 and told reporters, of the January 6th mob violence, “you’re damn right you had responsibility for this.” By his 2024 Senate run, Rogers was seeking Trump’s endorsement and touting it proudly. In his current campaign, Rogers went further than merely tolerating Trump — he praised a presidential speech about supposed election irregularities that even most of his own party preferred to ignore. That is not a shift in emphasis. That is a complete reversal on the single most consequential judgment a Republican officeholder made in the last decade, executed twice, in the direction of maximum convenience each time.

I raised the “stacking problem” in Part VI as a matter of sequencing and venue — why some positions draw fire and others, held by candidates who chose their moments more carefully, do not. Bruni’s essay adds a variable I hadn’t weighted heavily enough: media incentive structure. The gotcha format rewards resurfacing old clips of insurgent Democrats because those clips are numerous, quotable, and ideologically legible to a cable audience already primed to find them alarming. Rogers’s reversal on January 6th is, if anything, a more damning story — a man who told the truth when he thought it was safe and buried it when he learned it wasn’t — but it doesn’t generate the same charge, because there’s no single thirty-second clip that captures cowardice the way a defund-the-police soundbite captures ideology. Bruni’s piñata metaphor is precise: you swing at the target that’s shaped like a target, not the one that would actually teach you something if you connected.

Bruni’s most interesting evidentiary move is to grade Democratic reinvention on a curve running from mild to extreme and then show that even the extreme end doesn’t approach the Republican baseline. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez telling Jonathan Karl of ABC News, laughing, that “Woke 1 was crazy” is the example this series flagged in Part VII via Acemoglu’s essay as the adversarial-populism failure mode Ocasio-Cortez has since tried, imperfectly, to walk back. Bruni is tougher on her than Acemoglu was, noting that she “hasn’t adequately explained her pivot to sanity,” which is a fair complaint about the manner of the reversal even if one agrees, as this series does, with its substance. But Bruni’s real target is JD Vance, whose 2016 verdict on Donald Trump — “what an idiot,” “I find him reprehensible,” “America’s Hitler,” “cultural heroin” — represents a reversal of a magnitude neither El-Sayed nor Ocasio-Cortez has approached, executed by a man who is now a heartbeat from the presidency rather than a first-term senator or a Senate nominee in a swing state.

The comparison matters for this series because it forces a distinction I’ve been collapsing since Part I: the difference between inconsistency that reflects genuine political learning and inconsistency that reflects pure capitulation to power. Ocasio-Cortez’s “Woke 1 was crazy” line, whatever its inadequacies as an explanation, gestures at an actual belief revision — she is saying, badly, that she now thinks a set of positions she once held were mistaken. Vance’s reversal contains no such claim. Nobody believes Vance now thinks Trump is not “cultural heroin” because he changed his mind about Trump’s character; everybody, including Vance’s own defenders, understands the reversal as capitulation to the only path available to political survival inside the modern Republican Party. That is the TACO dynamic this series’ companion pieces have tracked in the executive branch — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — playing out at the level of individual political biography rather than policy negotiation. Vance didn’t accommodate Trump’s positions. He capitulated to Trump’s power, and rewrote the record to make the capitulation look like growth.

There’s also something worth saying about the format Bruni describes, because it isn’t incidental to the argument. He calls the Watters interview a session with “all the cordiality of a cage fight,” which is the right image for a specific genre of televised confrontation that has become a fixture of American cable news and has no real Canadian equivalent. A CBC or CTV interview with a party leader can be adversarial, but it operates inside an assumed baseline of mutual professional respect that a cage-fight format is explicitly designed to abandon. The cage-fight interview doesn’t ask a candidate to explain a past position so much as it stages the explanation as a confession under duress, which guarantees that anything short of complete capitulation reads, to the studio audience, as further evidence of guilt. El-Sayed cannot win that format by explaining his record, because the format has already decided what winning would look like before he sits down. This is not a defence of the record — the positions are his and the obligation to account for them is real — but it is a reason to be sceptical of using performance inside a cage-fight format as the measure of a candidate’s honesty, when the format is built to make honesty look like evasion regardless of what’s actually said.

Contrast that with how Rogers’s reversal has been covered, which is to say largely not covered as a reversal at all, but absorbed into the ambient assumption that of course a Republican Senate candidate in 2026 supports Trump; what else would he do. That ambient assumption is itself the story Bruni is trying to surface, and it maps directly onto something this series noted in Part III about Khanna’s Fox News appearance: venue doesn’t just determine how a message is received, it determines which facts about a candidate are treated as newsworthy in the first place. A Democrat’s five-year-old tweet is treated as a live scandal. A Republican’s five-year-old denunciation of an attempted coup, abandoned for convenience twice over, is treated as ancient history not worth relitigating. Neither treatment is neutral, and pretending the coverage gap doesn’t exist doesn’t make it fairer. It just leaves Democratic candidates fighting with one hand tied while being told the fight is even.

This is where Bruni’s essay sharpens rather than undermines the loyal-opposition thesis this series has built since “Wake Up, Dems.” If the stacking problem is real — and Part VI’s evidence for it, from El-Sayed’s primary fight to Khanna’s clean landing on nearly the same Israel position, still holds — then Bruni is identifying a second-order effect: the stacking problem is not merely an internal Democratic Party discipline issue, it’s being amplified by a media and political ecosystem that finds insurgent-left inconsistency more legible and more punishable than establishment-right capitulation. Both things can be true simultaneously. Democrats can genuinely need the coalition discipline Part I called for, and Republicans can simultaneously be getting a pass on a far larger and more cynical set of reversals, because the reversal that ends in obedience to Trump doesn’t read, to much of the political press, as a reversal at all. It reads as the default setting.

There’s a decision facing Democratic strategists buried in this essay that I don’t think Bruni fully draws out, and it’s the one this series exists to interrogate: does the party spend the next nine weeks litigating the fairness of the scrutiny, or does it treat the scrutiny as a fixed cost of doing business and out-discipline it? Litigating fairness is intellectually satisfying and Bruni himself does it well, but it is not a winning midterm message; “the referee is biased” has never once turned out a marginal voter who wasn’t already a partisan. Out-disciplining it means doing the harder thing Part VI already prescribed — choosing venues and sequencing carefully, the way Khanna did on Fox News — while also, and this is new, being willing to make Rogers’s and Vance’s reversals part of the affirmative case rather than treating them as somebody else’s talking point. Bruni’s closing argument is explicit on this: with nine weeks left, Democrats should treat the moral collapse of Republican elected officials as “fresh fodder,” not old news filed away because everyone already knows about it. Everyone already knowing about something is not the same as anyone acting on it, a distinction American politics seems to relearn every single cycle.

There is a broader lesson here for every candidate this series has tracked besides El-Sayed, because the piñata dynamic is not unique to Michigan. Part IV catalogued Schumer’s authority problems across Michigan, Maine, and Florida, and in each case the insurgent candidate — El-Sayed, Troy Jackson, Angie Nixon — inherits a version of the same exposure the moment the general election begins. A primary record built to satisfy a base audience becomes, overnight, raw material for exactly the format Bruni describes. Nixon’s 56-44 win over the DSCC-recruited Alex Vindman despite a sixteen-to-one money disadvantage was covered in this series as a story about grassroots enthusiasm outrunning establishment money. It is also, read through Bruni’s lens, a story about a candidate who is about to spend the general election as a piñata of her own, unless Florida Democrats have learned something from watching what happened to El-Sayed in real time. The lesson isn’t that insurgent candidates shouldn’t win primaries. It’s that winning a primary on a base-pleasing record and then having no plan for the cage-fight interview that inevitably follows is a failure of general-election preparation, not an unfair hand dealt by a hostile press.

I’ll end where Bruni does, with a qualification he insists on and this series should too: none of this excuses El-Sayed’s record or absolves him of the obligation to explain his own shift honestly. Republicans having more to answer for does not mean Democrats have nothing to answer for. What it means, and what the “stacking problem” needs to account for going forward, is that the standard being applied is not neutral, and a coalition that spends its energy meeting an inflated bar for its own candidates while its opponents clear a much lower one for theirs is not practising discipline. It’s practising a form of unilateral disarmament that looks like virtue and functions like a handicap.
