In the summer of 1929, a young Canadian diplomat named Hugh Keenleyside travelled by steamship to Tokyo, where he would spend the next four years as Second Secretary at the Canadian Legation. He was 29 years old. He had grown up believing in the international institutions that the Great War had made necessary and the Paris Peace Conference had attempted to build — the League of Nations, the protocols of diplomatic engagement, the slowly emerging architecture of a rules-based world order. He had his doubts about whether these institutions were strong enough to hold. He went to Japan hoping they were.

Keenleyside was my ancestor. I mention him here because he is my personal thread into the larger story this series has been examining — the story of what it means for a creedal nation to export its idea of itself to the world, and what happens when the model loses faith in its own premise.

The America that Keenleyside watched from Tokyo in the early 1930s was, in some ways, the America this series has been describing: an extraordinary experiment in creedal nationalism, imperfectly executed, driven by contradictions it had not fully resolved, but still capable — at its best — of modeling something for the world that the world could not produce anywhere else. The proposition that all are created equal. The commitment to government by consent. The argument, still ongoing, about what the creed demanded and who it included.

He watched that America with admiration and with concern. The admiration was for what it claimed. The concern was for the gap between the claim and the practice — a gap that, in the 1930s, was tearing democratic institutions apart across the globe, as one country after another decided that the creedal model was too demanding, too uncertain, too slow, and that stronger medicine was required.

I think about Keenleyside often as I write about America from Toronto in 2026. The gap between the claim and the practice is, this year, as visible as it has been at any point since the Civil War. The institutions that sustained the creedal model are under deliberate assault. The narrative that explained the model has been abandoned by the institutions responsible for transmitting it. The birthday of the republic was celebrated with a UFC bout on the South Lawn and a garden of famous people without a theme.

And yet I find myself, at the end of this series, not despairing.

I want to explain why.

I. What Remains

The creedal nation is, by any honest accounting, in serious trouble.

The story is fractured. The three schools — mainstream, post-American, hyper-American — are at war with each other and none of them is winning in the sense of producing a shared framework adequate to the moment. The institutions responsible for transmitting the story — the schools, the universities, the civic organizations, the political parties — have largely failed at their mandate. Fourteen percent of American students are proficient in history. The football coach is in the classroom. The Senate voted 99-1 that there was no acceptable version of the national story, and has not revisited that verdict.

The political system is producing the most explicit anti-creedal argument since the Civil War — Vance’s “particular people,” Carl’s “heritage Americans,” the executive order that removes true information from national parks because it complicates the preferred mythology. The 250th birthday party is a symptom: a nation that cannot articulate what it is celebrating has lost, at some fundamental level, the ability to tell its own story.

I have made these arguments across nine parts of this series, and I stand behind them. The situation is serious. Jill Lepore is right that “everything ends, and this could be a part of what unravels it.”

But the situation is not hopeless. And the evidence for why it is not hopeless is more interesting than the evidence for why it is serious — because it comes not from the institutions that have failed but from the people those institutions were supposed to be serving.

II. The Hunger

The most important fact about the American public’s relationship to its own history is not the fourteen percent proficiency figure. It is the survey data that sits alongside it.

Large majorities of Americans, when asked directly, say they prefer history that challenges what they know over history that confirms it. They say, by nearly nine to one, that learning about other communities is at least as important as learning about their own. They say, overwhelmingly, that learning about past harms — even uncomfortable ones — is important. As Appelbaum documents, this is not a marginal or elite position. It is the default American position, expressed consistently across demographic and political lines.

This is the public that the institutions have been failing. Not a public that wants mythology. Not a public that wants condemnation. A public that wants the honest account — the whole journey, as the citizenship exam reader wrote to Jill Lepore. The story that includes the darkness and the light, the promise and the betrayal, the ongoing and unfinished work.

The hunger is demonstrated by what people actually do when given the opportunity. These Truths became a best-seller. Letters from an American reached millions of daily readers. The “No Kings” rallies drew crowds that were, by all accounts, not primarily ideological — they were people who had come to believe that something was being taken from them and who reached instinctively for the founding vocabulary to name what it was.

These are not the acts of a public that has given up on the creedal nation. They are the acts of a public that is hungry for someone to take it seriously — to tell the story honestly, to make the argument carefully, to treat the 250-year argument about the proposition as something worth engaging with rather than winning or abandoning.

The supply problem is real. The demand problem is not.

III. The Signs of Recovery

Johann Neem, who delivered the 2025 lecture that identified the three schools and their failures, was asked whether the mainstream school could survive. He was more hopeful than the diagnosis suggested he should be. “Realizing what we’re losing,” he told Appelbaum, “might enable us to remember the America we want.”

There are signs, scattered and insufficient but real, that this realization is underway.

The “No Kings” movement has done something that the academic left has largely failed to do: it has claimed the founding vocabulary for the process model. The protesters who carry copies of the Constitution are not claiming that the Constitution has been perfectly honored. They are claiming that it means something — that the founding argument is a real argument, with real stakes, and that they are participants in it. This is exactly the relationship to the national story that the mainstream school requires.

Progressive figures who spent the Trump first term suspicious of any appeal to national narrative — who saw the flag as a symbol of a history they wanted to indict rather than a claim they wanted to contest — have, in the Trump second term, rediscovered the utility of the founding argument. When Khizr Khan brandished a pocket Constitution at the 2016 Democratic National Convention and received a standing ovation from Bernie Sanders and Hillary Clinton supporters alike, he was demonstrating something the post-American left had forgotten: that the creedal vocabulary is a tool available to anyone who subscribes, and that subscribing does not require pretending the creed has been honored.

Historians, many of whom had spent decades deconstructing the national narrative, have begun to return to it. The success of Lepore’s These Truths was not merely commercial — it was a demonstration that serious, honest national history could find a large audience, that the public hunger was real, and that the academy’s retreat from national narrative had been a choice rather than a necessity. Other historians have noticed. The field has not transformed — the institutional pressures that pushed it away from national narrative remain — but the tide may be, incrementally, turning.

And naturalized citizens — the people who chose America, who know what the alternative looks like, who tend to be more patriotic and more trusting of institutions than native-born Americans — continue to arrive, continue to subscribe, continue to insist, by the act of choosing, that the creedal bargain is real and worth having. The woman who wrote to Lepore about her citizenship exam is not alone. She is, in her quiet way, the creedal nation’s most reliable argument for its own validity.

IV. What the Story Could Look Like

I want to be specific, as this series concludes, about what the story I am arguing for would actually say.

It would begin, as any honest account must, with the proposition — all are created equal, endowed with inalienable rights, governed only by consent — and with the audacity of announcing that proposition in 1776, when the men who announced it owned enslaved people, had displaced the Indigenous peoples on whose land they stood, and had no intention of extending it to the women who shared their households and their lives.

It would trace the argument — the long, imperfect, frequently reversed, ultimately ongoing argument about who the proposition included. The abolitionists who held the creed up against the practice of slavery and said: you cannot have both. The suffragists who held it up against coverture and said the same. The labor organizers who held it up against the company town and said the same. The civil rights marchers who walked across the Edmund Pettus Bridge and said, for the hundredth time in American history, the same thing: you said all. You meant some. We are holding you to the word.

It would not pretend that the argument has been settled. It has not. The distance between the creed and the practice remains, in 2026, substantial. The ongoing assault on voting rights, the concentration of economic power, the disparities in how the criminal justice system treats different communities, the explicit attempt to narrow the definition of who the creedal bargain applies to — these are not minor imperfections in an otherwise honored promise. They are the current front lines of the 250-year argument.

And it would not pretend that the argument has been hopeless. The franchise was extended. Slavery was abolished, at the cost of 620,000 lives, and the long struggle to honor that abolition has produced, imperfectly and incompletely, a country that is less racist than it was — not enough, not nearly enough, but less. Women vote. Workers have rights. The creed has been extended, generation by generation, to people who were told it did not apply to them. The argument has produced results. The results have been reversed, partially, by backlash. The argument has resumed. This is the structure of the story. It does not have a happy ending because it does not have an ending.

That is not a defect. It is the point. A nation built on a proposition must argue about the proposition. The argument is the nation. To participate in the argument — to know enough history to argue seriously, to care enough about the creed to hold it up against the practice, to refuse both the flattering mythology and the prosecutorial condemnation — is to be, in the most meaningful sense, American.

V. The View From the Border

I began this series as a Canadian — an outsider, a neighbor, a person who has spent fifty years watching the republic next door with a complicated mixture of admiration, concern, and the particular clarity that comes from being close enough to see clearly and far enough away to see whole.

I end it the same way.

What I see, from across the longest undefended border in the world, is a creedal nation in a moment of genuine crisis — not the kind of crisis that ends with a battle or an election, but the deeper crisis of narrative, the loss of the story that a creedal nation needs to hold itself together. The story has been abandoned by the academy, captured by the culture warriors, censored by an administration that confuses patriotism with mythology, and undertaught to a generation that does not have the tools to argue about it seriously.

This is a real crisis. I have not minimized it in these ten parts, and I will not minimize it here.

There is a historical parallel worth sitting with, one an American commentator reached for on the eve of this very anniversary. In 1850, Congress passed a set of compromises so morally compromised — a federal fugitive-slave law chief among them — that the poet John Greenleaf Whittier, mourning the senator who had championed it, wrote that when faith is lost, when honor dies, the man is dead. It was, by any standard, one of the low points of the republic's history. And yet the eleven years that compromise bought before the Civil War came were the years in which the North built the industrial and rail capacity that let it win the war when it finally arrived. The shameful bargain did not prevent the reckoning. It postponed the reckoning to a moment the country could survive it. I do not know whether this 250th anniversary's undignified spectacle — the algae, the arch, the cage match on the South Lawn — is this generation's version of that grubby bargain, buying time toward some reckoning not yet visible from here. No one living through a low moment can know what it is buying. But the possibility is worth holding onto, and it is, I think, the most honest form of hope available to an outside observer in 2026.

But I also see the hunger. The readers who make honest history a best-seller. The citizens who reach for the founding vocabulary when they feel something being taken from them. The naturalized Americans who chose the creedal bargain and have no intention of conceding that the bargain was fraudulent. The marchers who walked across a bridge in Selma sixty years ago and the protesters who have been marching, in their various ways, ever since — holding the creed up against the practice, insisting on the gap, refusing to accept that the distance is permanent.

The Founders made an audacious bet. They announced a proposition they could not fully honor and dared the future to hold them to it. Every generation since has had to decide whether to accept the dare — to take the proposition seriously enough to argue about it, to take the gap seriously enough to work to close it, to take the story seriously enough to tell it honestly.

The dare is still open. The proposition is still on the table. The argument is still there to be made, by anyone who subscribes to it.

O say, can you see?

The flag is still there. The question is whether it still means what it says it means.

The Battle Hymn has the answer we need: not a question about whether the symbol survived, but a testimony about what the eyes have seen. Mine eyes have seen the glory. Not the glory of uncritical celebration. Not the glory of managed mythology. The glory of an argument that has been going on for 250 years and has not, despite everything, been abandoned by the people it was made to include.

The story is still there to be told. The question — the only question, at 250 — is whether the tellers are willing to tell it honestly.

I believe they are. I believe the hunger is real. I believe the creedal nation has not yet exhausted its capacity to be what it claims.

From across the border, with complicated love, I am watching. And waiting. And still, after all of this, hopeful.
