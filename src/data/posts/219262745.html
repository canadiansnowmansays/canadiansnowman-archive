Every military has to guess what the next war will look like. Most get it wrong, usually by preparing for the last one. The institutions built to make that guess well are deliberately dull: scientific advisory boards, war colleges, futures offices, wargaming cells. They are staffed by people with security clearances, conflict-of-interest forms and very little celebrity.

On September 30, 2026, at Quantico, Pete Hegseth announced that the guess would now be made by three famous men, and introduced them from the stage.

The Performance

Hegseth pointed to three civilians in the audience: Elon Musk; Palmer Luckey, co-founder of the defence company Anduril, in a blazer over his signature Hawaiian shirt; and Newt Gingrich, the former Speaker of the House. He called them, in the New York Times’s account by Helene Cooper and John Ismay on September 30, 2026 (”’No Beardos, No Weirdos, No Wimps’: Hegseth Lays Out His Military Vision”), “three of our nations’ best minds.” They would lead a new effort called Project Meridian to study the future of warfare. Immediately after the speech, he said, he would meet them at a secure facility on the base.

As Ana-Maria Stanciuc reported for The Next Web on September 30, 2026, in “Project Meridian: Musk, Luckey and Gingrich to map future warfare,” the study’s scope runs, in the Pentagon’s phrase, from subterranean depths to the cislunar frontier: artificial intelligence, autonomy, directed energy, robotics and biotechnology. Hegseth said its job was to identify “the domains that we must conquer, and capabilities we must master.” It has 120 days. Its findings are due on January 28, 2027, in an unclassified public report with a classified annex.

The announcement landed exactly as staged. Fox News’s breaking-news post on X on September 30, 2026, led not with the study, the command or the deadline but with one name: Elon Musk would co-lead a new War Department effort on the future of warfare. Luckey and Gingrich followed in the second sentence, Emil Michael in a subordinate clause. The department’s own tagline came last: “An effort led by America’s best minds to study the future of warfare.” A study of the next war had been announced. What the country heard was a celebrity hire. In the same speech, he announced that a fifth of the military’s general and flag officer posts had been emptied or eliminated. That was not the breaking news.

In the same speech he announced a new four-star combatant command for drones and robots, the Autonomous Warfare Command, or AutoWarCom. “Drone warfare supercharged by SI-enabled targeting is the biggest battlefield revolution in generations,” he said. (”SI” here means superintelligence, a term that is doing a great deal of work in that sentence.)

The Paperwork

The subject is real. Begin with the part that deserves to be taken seriously, and with an awkward contrast. The Secretary’s ideal soldier, as described at Quantico, is young, lean, clean-shaven and tested for testosterone. The army that has done most to show what drone warfare looks like is Ukraine’s, and on the New York Times podcast “The Opinions” on October 7, 2026, the columnist David French pointed out that its soldiers are, by some estimates, in their late thirties or early forties on average, and that much of its drone war is run by people French cheerfully called nerds. His guest, the retired Special Forces officer Mike Nelson, called Ukraine a “lethal laboratory” and said the generals who had been trying to learn its lessons were among those purged. The future of war that AutoWarCom is meant to master may not look much like the men in the “This is War” T-shirts.

That said, drones and autonomy really are changing warfare, and the change is visible in Ukraine and in the Gulf. Gen. Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, made the case without theatrics on September 16, 2026. As Jon Harper reported for DefenseScoop in “Joint Chiefs chairman says U.S. forces must prepare to be ‘hunted’ by autonomous systems,” Caine told the Air and Space Forces Association that “we have to assume from now on that our formations will be hunted by autonomous systems, jammed across the spectrum, and tracked in real time.” That is a sentence a military can plan around. It is also a sentence the military’s own leadership was already saying, two weeks before the speech.

The money was already moving too. As Brandi Vincent reported for DefenseScoop on April 21, 2026, in “DOD moves to make its largest-ever investment in drones and anti-drone weapons,” the fiscal 2027 request seeks more than $70 billion for drones and counter-drone weapons, compared with about $16.5 billion in the 2026 request. The Defense Autonomous Warfare Group’s budget would rise from about $226 million to $54.6 billion, an increase of more than 24,000 percent. (Part II of this series explains why little of that request has actually been funded.)

The command. As Chris Gordon and Greg Hadley reported for Air & Space Forces Magazine on September 30, 2026, in “Hegseth Unveils New Autonomous Warfare Command in ‘State of the Force’ Address,” AutoWarCom is to be a four-star combatant command overseeing drones, AI systems and the command-and-control networks that tie them together, established by October 1, 2027. In the meantime an interim effort called Project Agincourt will be led by Owen West, director of the Defense Innovation Unit, and a Navy SEAL senior chief, Max Strasiser. No commander has been named. No budget was given.

A new combatant command is not trivial. There are currently eleven, each with a four-star officer, a staff of thousands and a headquarters. Creating one usually takes years of study and a fight with Congress. Announcing it in a speech, while the Army has no confirmed chief of staff (Part III) and the Pentagon is living on a stopgap budget (Part II), is a bold use of the future tense. It is also, as Tom Nichols observed in The Atlantic on October 1, 2026, in “Pete Hegseth, Idea Guy,” an odd move for a Secretary who, in the same speech, boasted of shrinking the senior ranks and who calls himself at war with the Pentagon’s bureaucracy. A new combatant command needs a four-star commander, deputies, a headquarters and a staff. The Secretary is eliminating senior billets with one hand and creating them with the other.

The three minds. Now to Meridian, and to its co-directors.

Palmer Luckey’s company, Anduril, is one of the fastest-growing defence contractors in the country. As Jon Harper reported for DefenseScoop on March 14, 2026, in “Army awards Anduril $20B contract with an eye toward counter-drone capabilities,” the Army gave the company a contract worth up to $20 billion through 2036, built around its Lattice command-and-control software, for counter-drone work. Anduril builds autonomous systems, the very technology Meridian is meant to assess.

Elon Musk’s SpaceX is the Pentagon’s dominant launch provider and an increasingly central supplier of military satellites. CNBC reported on May 29, 2026, under the headline “SpaceX wins $4.16 billion Space Force contract for threat-detection satellites,” that the company had won a major contract linked to Golden Dome, and Bloomberg reported on September 25, 2026 (”SpaceX-Focused Pentagon Contracts Leave Rivals Feeling Squeezed”) that competitors increasingly feel shut out. As early as May 1, 2025, Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Greg Casar asked the Pentagon’s inspector general to investigate Musk’s involvement in Golden Dome contracting. This series will not retrace Musk’s wider portfolio of power, which had a whole series of its own, The Musk Stack. The relevant point is narrower: one of Meridian’s three directors runs a company that would profit from almost any answer it gives.

Newt Gingrich is the odd man out. He was Speaker from 1995 to 1999, has written widely on history and the future, and has long styled himself a futurist. He has no defence business that the public reporting identifies. He also has no particular technical expertise in autonomy, directed energy or biotechnology. His qualification appears to be that he is Newt Gingrich.

What the reporting doesn’t say. The Next Web’s account notes that Pentagon chief technology officer Emil Michael will oversee Meridian through an unnamed partner organisation. The early reports did not say whether the co-directors will be paid, whether they will be special government employees subject to federal ethics rules, whether Meridian will operate as a federal advisory committee with public meetings and published minutes, or how conflicts of interest will be handled. Those are not technicalities. They are the difference between an advisory study and two contractors advising their customer.

The rules exist and are not obscure. The Federal Advisory Committee Act of 1972 requires that advisory bodies giving advice to federal agencies have balanced membership, publish notice of their meetings, and generally hold them in public. The ethics rules for special government employees, a category designed for outside experts who advise government part-time, cap their service at 130 days in any 365 and require financial disclosure and recusal from matters affecting their own interests. Musk held exactly that status in 2025, when he led the Department of Government Efficiency. Whether by design or by coincidence, a 120-day study fits comfortably inside the 130-day limit.

The established alternatives. The Defense Science Board has advised the Secretary of Defense on technology since 1956. Its members are vetted for conflicts, its studies are documented, and many are eventually published. The Defense Innovation Board was created in 2016 specifically to bring Silicon Valley thinking into the Pentagon, with the conflict-of-interest machinery attached. DARPA exists to imagine the weapons after next. The Defense Innovation Unit, whose director is now leading the interim work for AutoWarCom, exists to buy commercial technology quickly. Hegseth has chosen to create a new study alongside all of them, run by people with more fame than formal accountability.

The Bill

Procurement integrity. The Pentagon’s acquisition rules exist because of a long history of officials and contractors getting too close. A study led by the founders of two major suppliers, defining “the capabilities we must master” a few months before the next budget cycle, will shape requirements that companies then bid to meet. Even if every recommendation is impeccable, rival firms will reasonably ask whether the competition was decided before it began. The Bloomberg reporting suggests some are already asking.

The institutions passed over. The Defense Science Board, the Defense Innovation Board, the service labs, the war colleges and DARPA all exist, in part, to do what Meridian has been asked to do. They are slower and less glamorous, but they come with rules on conflicts of interest and a record of producing studies that are read years later. Choosing celebrities over institutions sends a message to the people who staff those institutions: the work that matters here is done by people who are famous.

The taxpayer. AutoWarCom, Meridian and the drone budget together represent tens of billions of dollars in prospective spending. Spent well, they could keep American forces from being the ones “hunted” in the next war. Spent through a process designed by interested parties, they could produce expensive systems built around one company’s software and another’s satellites. The taxpayer will not know which until long after the money is gone.

The troops. Caine’s warning was about the soldiers and sailors who will face cheap, autonomous, AI-guided weapons. They need the best possible answer to that threat, not the most marketable one. The point of an independent study is to tell the Secretary things he and his suppliers may not want to hear. A study led by his suppliers is less likely to do that, whatever its directors’ brilliance.

The Gingrich question. One question remains for the third director. If the purpose of including two technologists is expertise, and the purpose of including a former Speaker is political, then Meridian is partly a political project. That is not necessarily sinister. Gingrich knows how Congress works, and Congress will have to fund whatever Meridian recommends. But it means the study’s conclusions will arrive pre-packaged for a political campaign, in a year when the Secretary needs every ally he can find on Capitol Hill. A study designed to be persuasive is a different thing from a study designed to be right, and the troops who will fight the next war need the second kind.

The performance itself. It is worth pausing on the image. A Secretary of Defense, in wartime, introduces from the stage a defence contractor in a Hawaiian shirt, the world’s richest man and a former Speaker, and announces that he will now repair with them to a secure facility to discuss the future of war. It is a scene that would be cut from a film for being too on the nose. The Pentagon has spent decades building processes precisely so that the future of war is not decided by whoever the Secretary admires most. Hegseth has found a way around them in 120 days.
