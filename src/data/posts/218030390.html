The Poster Board

Midway through the morning of Tuesday, Sept. 29, 2026, in Room 216 of the Hart Senate Office Building, a United States senator stood beside a large printed exhibit and accused a former federal prosecutor of lying under oath about a basketball game.

The senator was Eric Schmitt, Republican of Missouri. The prosecutor was Jack Smith, the former special counsel who indicted Donald Trump twice in 2023, once over classified documents at Mar-a-Lago and once over the scheme to overturn the 2020 election, and who watched both cases dissolve when Trump won the 2024 election. Schmitt had text messages from Smith’s team about a trip Smith took in February 2024. He asked about it. Smith said he had no recollection. Schmitt then produced the poster board, which he said showed Smith travelling to Atlanta, home of Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis and her own case against Trump. The implication was a secret coordinating meeting between two prosecutors. “I think you’ve already perjured yourself,” Schmitt declared, and he called Smith a “villain” and a “total dirt bag,” as ABC News reported.

Senator Amy Klobuchar, Democrat of Minnesota, then asked Schmitt to put the poster back up so that the witness could actually read it.

Smith read it and cleared the matter up. He had not been to see the Atlanta Hawks. He had been to see the Iowa Hawkeyes, the women’s basketball team, playing the University of Maryland in College Park, a short drive from his desk in Washington. He had gone to watch Caitlin Clark. Klobuchar confirmed the fixture (”knowing my Midwestern sports,” she said) and told Schmitt that implying a man was in a different city, on the strength of a confused team name, and then calling him a dirtbag, deserved an apology. According to the New York Times’ live coverage of the hearing by Charlie Savage and Devlin Barrett (Sept. 29, 2026), Schmitt did not apologise. He said he stood by “dirt bag.”

By the time the hearing adjourned at 1:14 p.m., the Times’ Glenn Thrush observed that the Hawks exchange was drowning out everything else on social media, and that it risked undermining the whole Republican effort to destroy Smith’s credibility, because it showed the accusers being careless with basic facts. Four hours of prepared attack had been reduced to a sports-page correction.

Stephen Leacock could not have written the scene. His editors would have told him that no senator would be so careless, and that no audience would believe it.

The Verdict Came First

It would be comforting to put this down to one staffer’s bad afternoon with a search engine. The record doesn’t allow it.

Schmitt did not arrive at “dirt bag” by examining evidence on Sept. 29. He had said it nearly a year earlier. At a press conference in October 2025 on the FBI investigation known as Arctic Frost, Schmitt called Smith “a Stalin-like ‘show me the man, I’ll show you the crime’ prosecutor,” and then added, according to his own Senate press release: “He’s a dirt bag, and I hope he comes before [the Senate Judiciary] Committee.”

The order of events matters. The insult came first, in October 2025. The wish for a hearing came next. The hearing arrived eleven months later. The evidence was assembled last, and it turned out to be a college basketball schedule read carelessly.

“Show me the man, and I’ll show you the crime” is the line Lavrentiy Beria is said to have used as Stalin’s secret-police chief. It describes a prosecutor who starts with a target and works back to an offence. Schmitt used it to describe Jack Smith. Then, on live television, he showed exactly how that method works: pick the man, decide he is a dirtbag, and when the crime fails to appear, take a basketball game and see Atlanta in it.

The Machinery Behind the Insult

Taken alone, the Hawks episode would be a comic footnote. It matters because it is the public face of a process that could put Jack Smith in front of a grand jury.

The committee’s own framing gave the game away. Chairman Chuck Grassley titled the hearing “Oversight of Jack Smith’s Abuse of Authority and the Targeting of Republicans and Related Matters”, which supplied the answer before the witness was sworn in.

In March 2026, the president posted on Truth Social: “Deranged Jack Smith should be brought to Justice, NOW!!!” as Roll Call noted in its preview of the hearing. Since his return to office, Trump has said that he was once hunted and has now become “the hunter.”

In mid-July 2026, at Todd Blanche’s confirmation hearing, Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri asked the nominee whether he had considered investigating Smith for perjury. Hawley said he hoped Blanche would investigate Smith and “possibly” prosecute him. Blanche answered, “Yes, senator.”

On July 22, 2026, House Judiciary Chairman Jim Jordan sent Blanche a criminal referral, reported by ABC News’ Lauren Peller, Alexander Mallin and John Parkinson. It alleged that Smith knowingly made false statements in his closed-door deposition of Dec. 17, 2025. The supposed lie concerned toll records: the phone-company logs of calls to and from members of Congress in the days around Jan. 6, 2021, which Smith’s office had subpoenaed. Smith had testified that those subpoenas did not reach the content of anyone’s calls or messages. Jordan’s referral argued that Smith’s team did have some members’ message content, and so Smith had given a false impression.

Anyone who has worked inside a phone company, as I did for many years, knows what a toll record is and what it isn’t. It tells you which number called which, when, and for how long. It contains not one word of what was said. Smith’s answer about the toll records was true. The message content his team did hold came, as ABC’s reporting made clear, from the National Archives, which is a separate source reached by a separate route. The referral took two different things and treated them as one, so that a true answer could be presented as a false one.

Jamie Raskin, the ranking Democrat on the House committee, said the referral would be laughed out of court. Smith’s lawyers, Lanny Breuer and Peter Koski, said it showed “a chilling eagerness to weaponize the justice system.” Meanwhile, almost every prosecutor and line agent who worked for Smith has resigned or been fired.

By the time Smith sat down on Sept. 29, the hearing already had its title, the president had already demanded his arrest, the attorney general had already agreed on the record to consider prosecuting him, and a referral already sat on the attorney general’s desk. The poster board was supposed to supply the evidence those steps had gone ahead without.

The Rest of the Hearing

Schmitt was the loudest, but not the only one.

Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana questioned Smith about his wife’s politics. He suggested she had been bitterly disappointed by Trump’s 2016 victory and brought up her work on a documentary about Michelle Obama. “I’m not going to talk about my family here today,” Smith said. Kennedy then claimed that President Biden or his staff had told Attorney General Merrick Garland to prosecute Trump. When Smith said he knew nothing of that, Kennedy asked him whether he believed in the tooth fairy. The Times’ reporters noted that no public evidence supports Kennedy’s claim. The tooth fairy was the only party in the exchange with no documented connection to the case.

Schmitt also waved a second exhibit, a memo he described as a readout of a call in which the Justice Department’s Public Integrity Section objected to a superseding indictment against Trump. He said this proved Smith had overridden the section’s recommendation, contrary to his testimony. Smith said the section had objected to an earlier draft and approved the version that was filed. That makes two exhibits from Schmitt in one morning, and both fell apart once the witness was allowed to read them.

Senator Ted Cruz of Texas called the investigation a “fishing expedition” through hundreds of Republicans and demanded to know on what basis Smith had suggested that Republican senators might destroy evidence or intimidate witnesses. When Smith tried to explain that the concern was what Trump and his associates might do if the subpoenas became public, Cruz talked over him, loudly. Then he asked the question that summed up the whole Republican theory of the case: “Is it a crime to be a Republican?” “Absolutely not,” Smith replied. The answer took two words. It was the shortest exchange of the day, and the least useful to Cruz.

Senator Darline Graham, sister of the late Senator Lindsey Graham, gave the hearing its most personal moment when she asked about the subpoena for her brother’s phone records. Did Smith really believe Lindsey had been going to destroy evidence that wasn’t even in his possession? Smith answered that he believed her brother had been a patriotic public servant, that he was not a target, and that he would likely have been a witness in the case against Trump. It was a gracious answer, and it cut the ground out from under the idea that the phone-records subpoenas were a hunt for Republicans. The lawmakers whose records were sought were people who had been on the receiving end of Trump’s calls. They were not the accused. They were, in several cases, the evidence.

Dick Durbin, the committee’s senior Democrat, called the Republican case “petty, personal and political.” Given the day’s Hawks, Hawkeyes and tooth fairy, most of the room would struggle to dispute any of the three.

What Republicans Got Right

This series has always tried to give the other side its fair share, and the other side did have one real point on Sept. 29.

Senator Mike Lee of Utah pressed Smith on the nondisclosure orders that stopped the phone companies from telling lawmakers their call records had been subpoenaed. As a result, the lawmakers never had the chance to argue in court that their records deserved protection. Records unsealed in July 2026 at the request of the New York Times showed that investigators did not tell the court the numbers belonged to members of Congress. Justice Department policy did not require that disclosure at the time. The policy has since been changed to require it, which tells you the gap was real.

Lee and Democratic Senator Chris Coons have a bipartisan bill that would put stricter limits and more meaningful judicial review on such gag orders. It is a sensible bill, and it would have made a sensible hearing.

The larger constitutional claim is weaker. Republicans say the “speech or debate” clause barred Smith from obtaining their toll records at all, but no court has held that the clause reaches records collected from a phone company. The senators are entitled to argue for a broader reading. They are not entitled to treat it as settled law and then accuse Smith of breaking it.

Smith’s answer on why those lawmakers’ records were sought, repeated from his earlier testimony, is still the strongest part of his defence. His office wanted to corroborate calls made to members of Congress urging them to delay the Electoral College certification. “I didn’t choose those members,” he told the House. “President Trump did.”

Applying the Test

In Part 26, “The One That Was Real,” I set out a test for telling a genuine prosecution from a manufactured one. A real case predates the grievance, is built by career prosecutors across administrations, rests on the defendant’s own words, and tends to end in a plea. A manufactured case postdates the grievance, depends on handpicked prosecutors or a handpicked jurisdiction, and collapses when it meets a grand jury or a judge.

Jack Smith has not been charged, so the last stage of the test hasn’t been reached. The first three can already be scored.

Does it postdate the grievance? Yes. The grievance is that Smith indicted Trump. The demands to prosecute him began after that, and the specific perjury theory appeared only after Smith testified.

Senator John Cornyn of Texas tried to turn this test around. He argued that the “original sin” was Merrick Garland’s appointment of Smith three days after Trump announced his 2024 candidacy, which would make the case against Trump the one that postdated a political event. Smith’s answer was simple and correct: the investigations were already pending. Appointing a special counsel did not start them. It handed an existing inquiry to someone outside the political chain of command, precisely because the subject had just become a candidate. By the Part 26 test, that is what a real prosecution looks like. The investigation came first, and the candidacy came second.

Who built it? Not career prosecutors working independently. The referral came from the House Judiciary chairman. The commitment to pursue it came from an attorney general at his confirmation hearing, in response to a senator. The public demand came from the president in capital letters.

Does it rest on the target’s own words? In a way it does, and this is what makes the perjury theory attractive to the people pursuing it. Perjury is the one crime that can be manufactured almost entirely in the hearing room: bring a man in, question him for hours, and search the transcript for a sentence that can be misread. The Hawks poster was a live version of exactly that. It failed within minutes, in front of cameras, because the witness asked to see it.

The pattern belongs clearly in the manufactured column. The remaining question is whether it will reach a grand jury, and if so, whether the grand jury will be more careful with its basketball than Senator Schmitt was.

The Scorecard

Smith joins the list with this instalment. He is an unusual entry. The other targets were pursued because of who they were or what they said about the president. Smith is being pursued for the one thing no one disputes he did: his job.

What Smith Said

For all the noise, the witness was the calmest person in Room 216.

“I will not be silenced by the continued threats of prosecution from the president or others,” Smith said in his opening statement. He said he stood by his decision to charge Trump, made without regard to politics. Then he offered the sentence that his accusers can’t answer, only shout over: “If asked whether to prosecute a former president today, based on the same facts, I would do so regardless of whether that president was a Republican or a Democrat.”

When Grassley disparaged the agents and prosecutors who had worked for him, Smith defended them by name, as a group, and without qualification. He said they had been targeted illegally, fired illegally, and had their lives turned upside down, and he called that an outrage.

He said all this while partly gagged. Judge Aileen Cannon, the Trump appointee who dismissed the classified-documents case, has a standing order barring Smith and other officials from disclosing anything that might appear in his final report on that investigation. An appeals court is still considering a challenge to that order. So a man accused of hiding the truth sat there legally forbidden to tell part of it.

Smith told the committee that the rule of law is rarely destroyed all at once, and that it is usually worn down by attacks on the institutions and the public servants sworn to uphold it. He had just watched a small example of that happen in the same room.

A referral sits with Todd Blanche’s Justice Department. It turns a true answer about toll records into an alleged lie by merging it with a different source of evidence. It is the same mistake as the poster board, made with more care and on letterhead: look at the Iowa Hawkeyes and see the Atlanta Hawks. The next instalment of this series may well depend on whether anyone at the Justice Department is willing to read the poster before signing off on the charge.
