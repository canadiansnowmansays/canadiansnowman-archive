The column lists thirty-seven names. They are not famous. They are the names of people from this municipality who went and did not come back. Constance Hay knows all thirty-seven. The plaque is legible. Gerald confirmed it in writing.

Elsinore has a war memorial.

It stands in Confederation Park, near the bandshell, on the east side of the path that runs from the municipal dock to the Elizabethan Gardens. It is a granite column with a bronze plaque listing the names of Elsinore residents who died in the First and Second World Wars and the Korean War. The column is not tall. It is not architecturally distinguished. It was installed in 1952 by the Royal Canadian Legion and has been maintained, since 1987, by a volunteer committee that meets twice a year and whose treasurer is Gerald Fitch, who has held the position since 2003 and whose filing system for the committee’s accounts is considered, by the committee’s other three members, to be excessive in its thoroughness and therefore exactly right.

There are no statues in Confederation Park. There is no proposed garden of heroes. There is no list of figures whose likenesses should be rendered in bronze and arranged in thematic groupings for the edification of visitors. This is not because Elsinore lacks heroes. It is because nobody has proposed a garden of them, and because in the absence of a proposal the question has not arisen, and because Elsinore, in the general practice of municipalities that are not currently being advised by someone with slides, tends not to solve problems that have not yet presented themselves.

Bradley Fenn attended a municipal planning conference in Kitchener in September. He came back with materials.

The materials were from a presentation on “civic commemoration strategies in mid-sized Canadian municipalities,” which Bradley had attended in a breakout session and which had included, in its third section, a case study from the United States on the National Garden of American Heroes — a federal initiative to create an outdoor sculpture park in Washington featuring statues of significant American figures rendered in a style the executive order described as “traditional and realistic.”

Bradley had found the case study interesting. Not, he said, as a model to be replicated directly — the American context was specific and the figures appropriate for commemoration in the Canadian context would obviously differ. But as a framework for thinking about how Elsinore might consider its own commemorative landscape and whether the current approach — a single granite column in a park — adequately represented the full range of contributions made by Elsinore’s residents and associated figures across the municipality’s history.

He raised this at the October meeting of the Heritage and Cultural Assets Subcommittee. He had seven slides. This was fewer slides than usual, which the subcommittee’s chair noted internally as either a sign of restraint or a sign that Bradley had not yet finished developing the proposal, and which subsequent events would confirm was the latter.

Gerald had been to the National Gallery of Canada four times. He had been to the Art Gallery of Ontario twice. He had been to the Stratford Perth Museum eleven times, which was a number he could account for precisely because he had filed a note each time he visited an institution of cultural significance, not out of any particular reason but because filing notes was what Gerald did when things seemed worth remembering.

He was not opposed to civic commemoration. He had been treasurer of the war memorial committee for twenty-three years. He had attended the annual remembrance ceremony every year since 1989, in all weather, because the names on the column were the names of people who had been in this municipality and were no longer, and because showing up to acknowledge this seemed to him among the minimum requirements of civic participation.

He was not sure about a garden of heroes.

He did not say this at the subcommittee meeting. He said, when asked, that he thought the question of criteria would be important — specifically, what would qualify a figure for inclusion, who would determine the qualifications, and how the determination process would be structured to ensure that the list reflected the full range of the community’s history rather than the preferences of whoever was making the list at the time.

He said this in the measured tone of a man who considers it his job to identify the procedural question that contains all the substantive questions without requiring anyone to have the substantive argument before they are ready to have it.

Bradley said the criteria would be developed through a community consultation process.

Gerald noted this. He wrote down the word consultation. He filed it.

Alistair was not on the Heritage and Cultural Assets Subcommittee. He heard about the garden of heroes proposal from Wayne, who had heard about it from the subcommittee chair, who had told Wayne because Wayne was on the Tourism and Culture Committee and the chair thought Wayne would have views about the tourism implications of a civic sculpture garden.

Wayne did have views. His views were organised and numerical. He noted that the Stratford Festival already drew visitors for whom the appeal of Elsinore was primarily theatrical and literary, and that a sculpture garden featuring traditional and realistic bronze figures of historical figures could either complement this appeal — if the figures selected were thematically consistent with the Festival’s identity — or complicate it, if the selection criteria produced a list that visitors from international markets might find difficult to interpret as part of a coherent cultural identity.

He had a spreadsheet.

Alistair listened to Wayne’s spreadsheet presentation in the Festival Theatre parking lot. He listened to the part about thematic consistency. He listened to the part about international markets.

He said: “We already have a garden.”

Wayne said: “The Elizabethan Gardens.”

Alistair said yes.

He said the Elizabethan Gardens were not named after a person. They were named after a period, which was itself named after a queen who had been dead for four hundred years and who had no particular connection to Perth County, Ontario, but whose name had come to signify a style of cultivation that the original garden committee in 1965 had considered appropriate to the surroundings. The gardens contained flowers, paths, benches positioned at angles someone had decided were correct, and an implicit argument about what kind of beauty was suitable to a city with a Shakespearean theatre on its river.

He said he thought this was sufficient.

He went back inside.

The Elizabethan Gardens are not named after a person. The Festival is not named after a person. Ontario Street is named after a province. The Avon is named after a river in England by people who arrived and missed it.

Constance Hay’s grandfather had a street named after him. The street was not named after him because he had been a hero in any formal sense. It was named after him because he had built a significant portion of Wellington Street in the 1890s, and because when the street was given its name in 1927, the naming committee had decided that a man who had built things that were still standing was worth noting, and they had noted him by putting his family name on a cross-street two blocks from the houses he had built.

The cross-street was now called Hay Lane. It was a very short street. It contained six houses, none of which were particularly distinguished architecturally, and a recycling depot that had been there since 1994. Constance passed Hay Lane every morning on her way to wherever she was going and had no particular feelings about it in either direction. It was a lane. Her grandfather had built things on Wellington Street. These two facts coexisted without requiring each other to mean anything additional.

She attended the Heritage and Cultural Assets Subcommittee meeting at which Bradley’s garden of heroes proposal was discussed, because she attended every significant Elsinore meeting and because the notice had mentioned civic commemoration, which was a subject she had views on.

She did not speak until the floor opened. When the floor opened, she said one thing.

She said: “The column in Confederation Park lists the names of thirty-seven people from this municipality who died in service of their country. I would suggest the committee ensure those thirty-seven names are legible before adding any new names to the civic landscape.”

She sat down.

Bradley said the column was absolutely within scope and that any garden of heroes proposal would of course incorporate appropriate recognition of military service.

Constance did not respond. She had said what she had to say. She wrote one sentence in her notebook.

Afterward, Gerald retrieved the war memorial maintenance records from his filing system. He confirmed that the column had been cleaned in 2023 and that the bronze plaque was in good condition and fully legible. He filed a note confirming this under War Memorial — Maintenance — Current Status — Confirmed.

He had not been asked to do this. He did it because Constance had raised the question and because it seemed useful to know the answer.

A Note for the Record

The garden of heroes is a recurring idea. It appears when a government wishes to assert a particular account of national identity through the selection and rendering of specific figures — to say, in bronze and at public expense, that these people and not others represent what the country is and aspires to be.

The American National Garden of American Heroes was created by executive order in 2020, revived and expanded in 2025, and specifies that the statues must be rendered in a “traditional and realistic” style — meaning neoclassical — meaning the visual vocabulary of a specific European tradition that carries, for those who know its history, associations that the order’s authors may or may not have intended to invoke. The figures on the list include founding fathers, athletes, entertainers, and historical figures selected by an executive committee. Artists were not consulted on the style requirement. The style was specified in advance.

In Elsinore, the war memorial lists thirty-seven names. They are not famous. They are the names of people from this municipality who went and did not come back, and whose names are on a column in a park near the Avon River where the Easter egg hunt is held every spring and where Gerald Fitch laid sixty-two eggs on the Saturday before Easter and went to the Tim Hortons after and read the news because someone had to.

The Elizabethan Gardens are not named after a person. The Festival is not named after a person. Ontario Street is named after a province. The Avon is named after a river in England by people who arrived from England and missed it, which is either an act of imagination or of homesickness, and which has resulted in a small river in Perth County being the site of a world-class Shakespeare festival, which Leacock would have found exactly as absurd and exactly as moving as it actually is.

Bradley Fenn’s garden of heroes has not yet been approved. The consultation process has not been announced. Gerald has filed a note about the status of the war memorial plaque.

The plaque is legible. The names are there. Constance Hay knows all thirty-seven of them.

She has never been asked to recite them. She could.
