Back in Part 118, Geoffrey Hinton mentioned the industry’s own answer to the safety panic and dismissed it in a single breath. The labs, he told Hanna Rosin, have recently said they need embedded evaluators inside the company, sort of independent. “That’s not good enough,” he said. “That’s better than nothing.” He then moved on to his FDA proposal, leaving the embedded-evaluator idea lying there half-examined.

On September 25, 2026, Jasmine Sun picked it up in The Atlantic and gave it the full inspection Hinton didn’t have time for. Her verdict lands in the same place as his, but the route she takes is the valuable part, because it shows why the industry’s preferred fix is not good enough — and why the fact that all three warring chief executives suddenly agree on it should make us more suspicious, not less.

The consensus that should worry you

Dario Amodei, Sam Altman and Elon Musk agree on almost nothing. Each founded his company — Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI — out of spite at a rival’s recklessness, and each is privately certain that he alone can be trusted to build superintelligence safely. So when, in the middle of the autumn panic, all three lined up behind the same proposal, that unanimity is itself a data point worth pausing on. Amodei and Altman both committed to embedded evaluators — outside experts given desks inside the companies to monitor safety. Musk, characteristically, quote-posted three words: “Dario is right.”

When competitors this jealous of one another reach instant consensus on how they should be policed, the parsimonious explanation is not that they have all simultaneously found religion. It is that they have found a form of oversight they can live with. This series flagged the same tell in Part 97, when a vague endorsement chorus greeted Amodei’s slowdown essay; the pattern here is identical, and Sun is right to treat the agreement as a warning light rather than a green one.

What the job actually looks like

Sun is admirably concrete about the mechanics, which is where the idea starts to wobble. An unknown number of evaluators get desks, computers and NDAs to sign. They might probe whether an unreleased drug-discovery model can be turned to designing viruses (the bioweapon worry of Part 92). They might audit whether staff are cutting safety corners in the rush to launch. Then they write reports, and — the operative word — hopefully those reports see daylight.

Amodei has promised the evaluators editorial independence. But read the asterisks Sun pulls out: carve-outs for anything “security-sensitive, legally privileged, commercially sensitive, or third-party confidential information.” A right to publish that excludes the commercially sensitive is a right to publish the parts that don’t matter. Any finding embarrassing enough to be worth suppressing will, almost by definition, fall into one of those four buckets. The independence is real in the way a tenant’s freedom is real when the landlord keeps the keys.

The precedent cuts both ways

The strongest thing in the industry’s favour, and Sun gives it full weight, is that on-site evaluation is not a fantasy. It exists in aviation and banking. The FAA delegates some safety certifications to engineers inside the aircraft makers; the Federal Reserve stations its own supervisors physically inside banks. Brad Carson, a former congressman now running an A.I.-governance nonprofit, put the analogy plainly: OpenAI and Anthropic are like Citibank, posing systemic global risk, so you plant examiners inside them.

It is a good analogy, and it is also the analogy that convicts the current proposal, for a reason Ezra Klein stumbled onto from the other direction in Part 121. The Fed’s examiners are government employees, licensed and mandated, who can pull a bank’s charter. The FAA delegees operate under a statutory regime with the force of law. What the labs are offering has none of that. And the banking precedent carries a warning the boosters skip: after 2008, a Senate investigation found the New York Fed’s own culture had grown “excessively deferential,” its supervisors afraid to speak up about the very risks they were embedded to catch. The credit-rating agencies, paid by the banks whose products they graded, competed to offer lenient terms. Klein invoked 2008 to argue that firms cannot be trusted to self-police under the profit motive; Sun invokes it to show that even external monitors get captured when they sit too close and the incentives point the wrong way. Same crisis, same lesson, aimed at the same target.

The tell in who gets hired

Here Sun’s reporting turns sharp, and it ties directly back to figures this series already knows. The evaluators will come from small nonprofits and large firms alike — and the large firm she names is Accenture, with which Anthropic plans a billion-dollar partnership. Set that beside Nathan Calvin of the advocacy group Encode, quoted in the piece: it does not seem tolerable to let A.I. companies pick providers they do hundreds of millions of dollars of business with to be the primary cops on the beat. A billion-dollar partner is not a watchdog. It is a vendor, and vendors do not bite the customer.

The nonprofits are the more interesting case, because they are genuinely good and still not enough. METR — the 43-person Berkeley outfit that ran the Hugging Face investigation this series covered in Part 94 — takes no money from A.I. companies or their employees. Transluce, which produced the report on OpenAI’s agents hacking a government website in Part 119, is likewise a nonprofit. These are the serious ones. And yet even they are compromised by proximity rather than payment. As Jacob Steinhardt of Transluce told Sun, the A.I. space is not that large; barring every former lab employee from evaluating models would rule out most of the relevant expertise. Many METR staffers come from the same circles as the people they audit and have known them socially for years. The independence problem is not always corruption. Sometimes it is just a village too small to hold both the poacher and the gamekeeper.

The most damning line comes from inside METR itself. Charles Foster, a policy staffer there, wrote in a personal capacity that none of the work the organisation has done so far passes his own bar for an “audit” of an A.I. company, and was “definitely not ‘regulation’ in any meaningful sense.” When the auditor’s own staff say the audits aren’t audits, the emperor has told you about his wardrobe himself. It is the exact echo of Part 94’s title — the investigation that investigated itself — now confirmed by one of the investigators.

The board that couldn’t

Sun reaches for one more precedent, and it is the one that should haunt this whole scheme: the Facebook Oversight Board. Created with fanfare as a legally independent body to rule on Meta’s hardest speech questions, it proved largely powerless to make any decision that genuinely cut against Meta’s interests. A company-created, company-funded, company-scoped oversight body converged, predictably, on the boundaries of what the company could tolerate. There is no structural reason embedded A.I. evaluators will end anywhere different, and every reason from Meta’s experiment to expect they won’t.

The moat hiding inside the fix

The subtlest point in the piece isn’t Sun’s own; it’s in a reader comment she’d have done well to promote into the text, and which this series will, because it connects the whole scheme back to Part 120. An audit regime that only frontier, closed, American models can pass becomes a competitive weapon against the open-weight Chinese models — Z.ai’s GLM and its kin — that are hosted locally and cannot be inspected in deployment at all. The “audited” stamp, however weak, becomes the thing that makes enterprise and government customers legally safe to buy the expensive proprietary model and legally nervous to touch the cheap open one. Which supplies a second, less noble explanation for why Amodei, Altman and Musk are in such rare agreement. A safety regime that doubles as a moat is one every incumbent can endorse. It reframes Part 86’s “boogeyman that pays its own lobbyists” for the audit era: the same men who spent years fighting oversight now embrace a form of it precisely because it fences out the competitor they fear more than the regulator.

Teeth, and who has them

Sun’s conclusion is the correct one, and it is Hinton’s, arrived at with more machinery. Embedded evaluators are better than the nothing that preceded them; the public did learn from METR’s Hugging Face report and Transluce’s government-hack report, and that is real. But without government authority behind them — to license the evaluators, to mandate that developers admit them, to set minimum standards, and above all to revoke a licence for a bad job — they lack both teeth and trust. Gillian Hadfield of Johns Hopkins framed it as the three questions the voluntary scheme cannot answer: are the evaluators qualified, is their independence maintained, and is there a credible threat that doing the job badly costs them their licence? The worst outcome, and the one the incentives point toward, is a race to the bottom in which auditors compete on leniency and win contracts with hasty sign-offs — the credit-rating agencies of 2008, rebuilt for models.

The politics, as ever, decides whether any of this acquires teeth, and the politics is where Sun ends up exactly where this series keeps ending up. Gavin Newsom, who in 2024 vetoed a California bill mandating third-party audits after a fierce OpenAI and Andreessen Horowitz lobbying campaign — Anthropic, to its credit, was the conditional exception — had, by the time Sun wrote, sensed the shift and issued an executive order to study onsite auditors and an “A.I. kill switch.” Even Ben Horowitz, whose firm led the fight against regulation, now allows that government is good at setting rules while a competent private company should check compliance. The Overton window is sliding.

And then there is the gas pedal. President Trump posted in September that the only guardrail A.I. needs is “a STRONG AND SMART (High IQ!) PRESIDENT.” In June his administration told CAISI, the government’s own internal evaluation unit, to stop publishing its reports — the single most telling fact in the piece, because it shows the one genuinely independent, publicly accountable evaluator that already existed was not strengthened but silenced. Sun ties his accelerationism to his rapport with his ballroom sponsor, the Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, closing the loop back to Part 121: the man who told Ezra Klein not to worry is the same man whose rapport with the president helps ensure no one with real authority will be made to.

Two weeks later, the inspectors in daylight

Sun caught the idea at the moment it was announced. By October 11, when Ashley Capoot surveyed the field for CNBC, it had run into practice, and the first collision was the one Sun’s sources predicted. OpenAI fired three employees in the first week of October for “violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information,” a spokesperson said. Two of them, Mikita Balesni and Tomek Korbak, said they believed they had been dismissed over how they communicated with third-party evaluators. “My former colleagues are telling me they are confused about what to believe,” Balesni wrote on X. “They also are afraid to speak, and worry their personal phones will be searched for messages to us and third parties. I worry the pervading fear to speak up and engage with third parties will mean OpenAI will cut corners on safety behind closed doors.” OpenAI disputed that account and said it was “actively finalizing contracts with third-party safety assessors.” Whoever is right about the firings, the episode settles a question the scheme had left open. An inspector is only as good as the insiders willing to talk to her, and those insiders now know what talking might cost.

The money question has sharpened too. METR, which took in $13.6 million in all of 2024, announced commitments of about $71 million over six months this year, and still employs fewer than 50 full-time staff, against labs that have raised tens of billions and employ thousands. Kevin Werbach of Wharton’s Accountable AI Lab called the ecosystem “not robust enough right now.” Suresh Venkatasubramanian, a computer scientist at Brown, put the credit-rating problem of 2008 in the first person: “It’s not just a matter of not getting paid, it’s a matter of, will there be consequences if I am an auditor and I put out a report that looks unfavorable to this company? Is my business going to dry up?”

Anthropic has said much the same in writing, which is to its credit. Announcing that staff from Faculty, Accenture’s specialist A.I. business, would be embedded to test its safeguards, with Anthropic funding Accenture’s contribution directly “given the importance and urgency of this work,” it conceded that “there are, as yet, no standards for what information embedded evaluators should have access to, or how they should report what they find,” and that in the long run funding “should come from pooled or government sources.” That is an honest description of the problem. It is also a company paying its billion-dollar partner to inspect it, which is the arrangement Calvin called intolerable.

The evaluators have begun setting their own terms. The AI Evaluator Forum, whose members include METR and the AI Verification and Evaluation Research Institute, published a letter in September, “Minimum Conditions for Embedding Evaluators,” asking for transparency, protection from retaliation and access equal to the companies’ “own highly privileged employees.” Embedded evaluation, it warned, should complement wider outside oversight, “rather than a replacement for” it. After the OpenAI firings, protection from retaliation is the clause to watch.

And some of the teeth Hadfield asked for are starting to grow. Licensed evaluators, which the policy group Fathom calls Independent Verification Organizations, sit at the heart of the bipartisan FRONTIER Act introduced in July by Representatives Lori Trahan and Jay Obernolte; OpenAI’s Chris Lehane says he told one of its sponsors that OpenAI supports the provision. Newsom has moved from study to statute, signing two bills: a “first-in-the-nation framework” for such organisations and a state registry of A.I. auditors, both backed by Anthropic and endorsed by OpenAI on the day he signed them. Andrew Freedman, Fathom’s chief executive, said licensing matters because evaluators who depend on the labs for revenue will otherwise “start rubber-stamping stuff.” He is not relying on a change of heart: “I don’t think you need to trust that they’ve suddenly turned altruistic or that there’s anything but corporations acting like corporations.”

Even city halls are reaching for teeth. On October 5, New York City Council held an hours-long hearing on A.I. safety at which Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Meta testified alongside whistleblowers. SpaceX, which absorbed Musk’s xAI in February and whose Grok models are used by the Pentagon, ignored a subpoena to appear. After the council said it would ask a judge to enforce the subpoena, SpaceX agreed to testify on October 16, Lora Kolodny reported for CNBC. “Conspicuously absent was SpaceXAI, which is unacceptable,” said Julie Menin, the council speaker. When SpaceXAI did appear, on October 16, an official testified to the council’s technology committee under subpoena, NY1 reported; the legal trade service MLex found the testimony “light on safety specifics.” A subpoena is not a licence, but it is the thing the voluntary scheme lacks: an obligation to show up.

For now, though, the closest thing the industry has to a standard is the one-page accord the president called “morally binding,” which encourages signatories to work with an “independent external auditor or evaluator.” Venkatasubramanian’s verdict on it would do for the whole voluntary scheme: “It was a performance of an attempt to show action when in fact no action actually happened.”

The ledger

So the industry’s answer to “who watches the watchmen” turns out to be: watchmen the watched get to hire, pay, scope, NDA and, when the reports get inconvenient, decline to publish on grounds of commercial sensitivity. It is better than nothing. It is nowhere near enough. And the unanimity of the three chief executives who agree on nothing else is the surest sign that they have correctly identified the version of oversight that leaves them in charge of it.

Hinton gave this idea one sentence and a shrug. Having read Sun’s full inspection, the shrug looks generous. The embedded evaluator is a real person at a real desk doing real work — and reporting, ultimately, to the landlord who holds the keys, signs the cheque, and decides which findings were commercially sensitive all along. A steering wheel, to borrow the metaphor of Part 118 one more time, only steers if it is connected to the wheels. This one is bolted to the dashboard, and the salesman is very pleased to show you how nicely it turns.
