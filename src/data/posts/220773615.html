🖋 Wry, with a grave ending — because this is a story about the most playful idea in the history of mathematics, and about what a grateful nation did to the man who had it. There is also a short confession about the machine I write with. Dior attends, to demonstrate an unsolvable problem.

I count my essays, and every essay in this series rests on an assumption I have never stopped to examine: that a thing, once it is a number, can be counted. Essay 1,936 is the year someone examined it. A twenty-three-year-old fellow of King’s College, Cambridge, named Alan Turing sent a paper to the London Mathematical Society with a forbidding title — On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem — and in it he did two astonishing things at once. He invented, on paper, the machine that every computer on earth descends from. And he used it to prove that there are numbers no machine will ever be able to count.

The question from Göttingen

The problem in the title was a challenge laid down in 1928 by David Hilbert, the great German mathematician, and it is the most ambitious question this series has ever met. Hilbert asked whether there could be a mechanical procedure — a set of rules so precise that a clerk with no imagination could follow them — that would take any statement in mathematics and decide, in a finite number of steps, whether it was true or false. If such a procedure existed, mathematics would become a matter of bookkeeping. Every question would be answered by turning a handle.

Turing set out to answer it, and he realised that to say whether a mechanical procedure could do something, he first had to say exactly what a mechanical procedure was. Nobody had ever done that. So he did.

The clerk with a tape

Here is the part that belongs to this series more than any other, and it is why I have been looking forward to this number.

In 1936 the word “computer” did not mean a machine. It meant a person — very often a woman — employed to do long calculations by hand, at a desk, with pencil and paper, following instructions. Turing built his imaginary machine by watching, in his mind, a human computer at work, and stripping the work down to its bones. The person looks at one symbol at a time. She has a small number of states of mind — I am carrying a one, I am looking for the next digit. She follows a fixed rule: in this state, seeing this symbol, write that symbol, move one place left or right, and change to that state. That is all.

So Turing’s machine is an infinitely long paper tape divided into squares, a head that reads and writes one square at a time, and a short table of rules. Nothing else. No gears in particular, no electricity required. It is the clerk of essay 1,905 and the keeper of essay 1,835 and the scrivener of every essay in between, reduced to pure procedure — and Turing showed that this impossibly simple device could carry out any calculation that any human computer could, given enough tape and time.

Then he went one step further and described a single machine — the universal machine — that could read, from its own tape, the description of any other machine and imitate it exactly. One device, fed the right instructions, could become every device. That is the idea of the stored program. That is your laptop, your telephone, the machine in your car, and the one on which I am writing these words. All of them are Turing’s universal clerk, made of silicon instead of paper, running very much faster.

The numbers that cannot be counted

Now the proof, and it is the most surprising result in the series.

Turing pointed out that every one of his machines can be written down as a finite table of rules, and finite tables can be listed, one after another, and numbered: machine one, machine two, machine three, forever. So the numbers his machines can compute — the computable numbers — can be counted off in a list, like the essays in this series.

But the real numbers, all the points on a line, cannot be listed. A German mathematician named Georg Cantor had proved that decades earlier: there are, in a precise sense, more of them than any list could ever hold. Put those two facts side by side and the conclusion is inescapable. There are more numbers than there are machines to compute them. Almost every number on the number line — in a strict mathematical sense, nearly all of them — is a number that no machine, of any design, at any speed, given any amount of time, will ever be able to write out.

And there were questions, too, that no machine could settle. Turing showed that there is no general procedure that can look at an arbitrary machine and its tape and decide whether it will ever finish its work or run on forever. No clerk, however patient, can always tell in advance whether a calculation will end. Hilbert’s dream was impossible. Mathematics could not be reduced to turning a handle.

I have spent nineteen hundred essays arguing that the count is never merely the count — that every number has a choice behind it, and a cost. Turing proved something deeper and stranger: that the count itself has a boundary, and that the boundary can be found, exactly, by counting. It is the only proof in these files that measures the limits of measurement.

(One honest footnote, because this series keeps a priority ledger. An American logician, Alonzo Church, reached the same answer to Hilbert’s question by a different route and published it a few weeks before Turing. It was a dead heat, like Bell and Gray at essay 1876, and Turing handled it with grace: he went to Princeton to study with Church. Turing’s version won the future because his clerk with a tape was something you could actually build.)

Dior and the halting problem

The household contains a living demonstration of Turing’s unsolvable question. When the husky begins asking for a treat — a particular low, persistent remark at the kitchen door — there is no general procedure by which I can determine, in advance, whether the request will ever terminate. I have observed it run for a very long time. I have observed it stop abruptly for reasons I could not detect. I have tried to construct a rule that predicts its end and been defeated each time. Turing proved that no such rule exists for machines in general; Dior has proved it, independently, for huskies. She would have been a useful colleague at King’s.

The machine I write with

I said I would make a confession, and here it is. This series is written in open collaboration with an artificial intelligence, and I tell readers so on my masthead. The machine I work alongside is, in the most literal sense, a descendant of the tape in Turing’s head — a universal machine running instructions vastly more elaborate than he could have dreamed, but obeying, at the very bottom, the same rules about what can and cannot be computed. In 1950 Turing went on to ask, in another famous paper, whether a machine could think, and proposed a game to test it. I write about the answers people are giving to that question, under another dateline, nearly every week. I will only say here that it feels right, and a little dizzying, to stand at essay 1936 and acknowledge the ancestor of my own co-author. The keeper’s lineage in this series has always included the clerks. This one I owe a byline.

What the nation owed him

And now the grave ending, because the honest ledger requires it.

When war came, Turing went to Bletchley Park, where he did as much as anyone alive to break the German Enigma ciphers. The electromechanical machines he helped design there, counting through possible settings faster than any team of people could, let Britain read the enemy’s messages, and historians have argued that the work shortened the war by a long stretch — some say two years, though the precise figure is, as this series would expect, disputed. Untold numbers of lives were saved by a man doing arithmetic in a hut.

In 1952 that man was prosecuted by the British state for being gay. He was convicted of “gross indecency,” and offered a choice between prison and a course of hormone injections intended to suppress his desires. He chose the injections. His security clearance was withdrawn; the country whose codes he had broken a war for no longer trusted him with its secrets. In June 1954 he died, at the age of forty-one. The inquest called it suicide; some have since questioned the verdict.

It took the British government until 2009 to apologise, and until 2013 to grant him a royal pardon. Since 2021 his face has been on the fifty-pound note. A man who proved that some numbers can never be counted was, for a time, counted by his own country as a criminal. It is the cruellest miscount in the century so far, and the century has not yet reached its worst.

The closer

So 1936 is the year the count discovered its own edge: a clerk with a tape, a list of every possible machine, and a proof that the list can never be long enough. It gave us the computer and, with it, a humility about computing that the century promptly forgot.

The count moves on, three years, to a different kind of list. In the winter after Munich, a twenty-nine-year-old London stockbroker cancelled a skiing holiday, went to Prague instead, and began writing down the names of children. The next landmark is essay 1939, the year the war began, and the year one man’s list of names turned out to be worth more than his government’s, and more than mine.

Turing proved that some numbers cannot be counted. At 1939 we meet a man who counted to six hundred and sixty-nine, and then said nothing about it for fifty years.

See you at 1939.
