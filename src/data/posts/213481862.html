Second of five essays Stephen Gordon Sept 8, 2026

The claims arrived within hours of the polls closing and did not stop for five weeks. By the time Donald Trump left the White House on January 20, 2021, he had named, with unusual specificity for a man rarely credited with specificity, the exact mechanisms he said had stolen a second term from him: a voting-machine company called Dominion, a county in northern Michigan called Antrim, a secretary of state in Georgia who would not do as he asked, and seven states’ worth of paperwork designed to hand Mike Pence a legal pretext to ignore the voters entirely. Each of those claims produced a paper trail. None of that paper trail, four to six years later, has produced a single sustained finding that the 2020 election was stolen. This essay exists to walk through that paper trail in the order the claims themselves arrived, because the specific record — not the general memory of “Trump said the election was stolen” — is what Part II and the concluding essay in this series will need to stand on.

Start with Dominion, because it was the claim that eventually cost someone real money. Within days of the election, Trump allies including Sidney Powell and Rudy Giuliani alleged that Dominion Voting Systems’ machines had been used to switch votes from Trump to Biden, sometimes attributing the scheme to Venezuelan software with ties to the late Hugo Chávez. Powell filed a set of lawsuits — nicknamed the “Kraken” suits by her own allies — in Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, and Wisconsin, seeking to overturn results in all four states. Every one was dismissed. A federal judge in Michigan later sanctioned Powell and eight other attorneys for the filing, calling it an abuse of the judicial process; Powell was also referred for potential disciplinary action by state bar authorities. Years later, in the parallel Georgia racketeering case brought by Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis, Powell pleaded guilty to six misdemeanor counts related to a voting-system breach in Coffee County, Georgia, becoming one of several co-defendants in that case to accept a plea deal rather than contest the underlying facts at trial. And in April 2023, Fox News — the network that had given the Dominion conspiracy its widest platform — agreed to pay Dominion $787.5 million to settle a defamation suit, after a Delaware judge had already ruled, before the case ever reached a jury, that the specific claims aired about Dominion were false. A company does not pay out three-quarters of a billion dollars over an allegation that later turns out to be true.

Antrim County, Michigan, followed a similar arc from clerical mistake to conspiracy theory to formal repudiation. On election night, a human error in updating township tabulation software caused a brief, visible misreport of votes in the small, heavily Republican county — an error that was caught, corrected, and explained by county officials within days, and that had nothing to do with Dominion’s tabulation logic. The error became, in Trump allies’ retelling, proof of a nationwide vote-switching algorithm; a forensic report commissioned by a state legislator to examine the Antrim machines was cited widely on conservative media before Michigan’s own Republican-controlled state Senate committee published its own year-long investigation concluding that the vote-switching claims were unsupported by the evidence and had been pushed by people who, in the committee’s words, were “misleading the citizens of Michigan.” A county clerical fix became, briefly, a national conspiracy theory; it did not survive contact with an investigation run by members of the former president’s own party.

Then came Georgia, and the call. On January 2, 2021, Trump spent an hour on the phone with Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger, a fellow Republican, running through a list of claims — dead voters, shredded ballots, ballots scanned multiple times — that Raffensperger and his staff refuted point by point in real time. Near the end of the call, Trump made his request plain: “I just want to find 11,780 votes, which is one more than we have.” Raffensperger had already overseen a full hand recount of Georgia’s presidential ballots before that call took place. He declined. The recording became public days later, was cited in Trump’s second impeachment, and became a central piece of evidence in the Fulton County racketeering indictment that, in August 2023, charged Trump himself alongside eighteen co-defendants under Georgia’s RICO statute — the same call, transformed from a private request into a count in a criminal case.

The fake-elector scheme was the mechanism built to make results like Georgia’s irrelevant regardless of what any secretary of state said. In seven states Trump lost — Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, New Mexico, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin — eighty-four Republicans signed certificates falsely declaring themselves the state’s duly appointed presidential electors, with the goal of giving Vice President Pence a paper basis to reject the legitimate slates when Congress met to certify the vote on January 6. Prosecutors in Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, and Arizona have since brought criminal charges against participants in the scheme, including sitting and former state Republican Party officials; Wisconsin brought charges against the lawyers and campaign operatives who organized that state’s fake slate rather than the electors who signed it. The scheme’s own architect, attorney Kenneth Chesebro, wrote in a contemporaneous memo that he expected the plan would likely be rejected by the Supreme Court if it were ever tested there — meaning the plan’s own author understood, in writing, in real time, that it was not built to survive judicial review. It was built to survive one afternoon in the Capitol before anyone could review it at all.

That was the design; the sixty-plus lawsuits were the cover story running in parallel. By multiple independent trackers’ counts, Trump and his allies filed more than sixty post-election cases across a dozen states. All but one were dismissed, denied, dropped, or withdrawn; the single procedural win, a narrow Pennsylvania ruling, did not change the certified outcome and was later reversed on appeal. The judges who rejected these claims were not, disproportionately, Democratic appointees looking for a reason to rule against a Republican president — in one Georgia case, all thirteen votes cast across the litigation came from judges appointed by Republican presidents, twelve of them by Trump himself, and none of them favorable to Trump’s position. The most quoted of the rejections came from the Third Circuit, in an opinion written by Stephanos Bibas, whom Trump had personally placed on that court in 2017. Bibas’s opening line has outlived nearly everything else written about the 2020 litigation: “Free, fair elections are the lifeblood of our democracy. Charges of unfairness are serious. But calling an election unfair does not make it so. Charges require specific allegations and then proof. We have neither here.” Even Rudy Giuliani, arguing a related case before a different judge that same week, conceded on the record that the campaign’s Pennsylvania complaint was not, in his own words, “a fraud case.” The lawyer bringing the claim told the court, under questioning, that it wasn’t the claim he was bringing.

The Justice Department reached the same conclusion the courts did, from the inside — and Trump’s own cybersecurity agency reached it before the Justice Department did, at greater personal cost to the person who said so. On November 12, 2020, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, under a director Trump had personally appointed in 2018, issued a joint statement with state election officials of both parties declaring the election “the most secure in American history” and stating there was no evidence any voting system had deleted, lost, or changed a single vote. Trump fired that director, Christopher Krebs, six days later — by tweet, not through any formal process — calling the CISA statement “highly inaccurate” and reciting the same unproven claims about dead voters and machine glitches that courts would go on to reject in the weeks that followed. Krebs was a lifelong Republican whom Trump’s own White House had once championed for hardening election infrastructure against foreign interference; his removal was not a partisan disagreement resolved through normal channels, but the firing of an appointee for stating, in his official capacity, that the thing his job existed to verify had in fact gone as it should.

On December 1, 2020, Attorney General William Barr — a Trump appointee who had spent much of the preceding year defending the administration on nearly every other front — told the Associated Press that federal investigators had run down the specific fraud allegations state by state and found nothing that would have changed the outcome. Barr’s own account of what followed, published later in his memoir, describes an “awkward” and furious reaction from Trump once the interview ran. Barr did not walk the statement back. He resigned five weeks later, at the point when the administration’s own attorney general and the president who appointed him could no longer occupy the same page on the single question that mattered most to Trump personally.

Two smaller claims are worth naming specifically, because they illustrate how the broader campaign operated: assert a number, let it circulate, and abandon it quietly once someone checked. Georgia officials, responding to a Trump legal filing alleging that more than ten thousand dead people had voted in the state, ran the state’s actual death records against its voter file and found the true number in the dozens, not the thousands — a discrepancy the Georgia Secretary of State’s office would go on to repeat, under oath, in the Raffensperger call itself. In Pennsylvania, a widely circulated claim that the state had recorded more mail ballots returned than mail ballots requested — offered as proof of ballot-stuffing — was traced by state election officials to a reporting-lag artifact in how counties updated a public dashboard, not to any actual surplus of counted ballots; the claim was not repeated once the dashboard caught up. Neither correction reversed the broader narrative in the way it should have. Both corrections exist, on the record, from the election officials whose job it was to check.

The Fulton County racketeering case brought against Trump and his eighteen co-defendants has itself become a case study in how slowly accountability moves relative to a campaign calendar, which is a theme this series’ companion publication, When Democracy Fights Back, has tracked in a different context. Trump pleaded not guilty to all counts. Several co-defendants, including Sidney Powell and Kenneth Chesebro, took plea deals rather than face trial. The case itself has since stalled at the appellate level over a disqualification fight involving District Attorney Fani Willis’s personal conduct — a fight that has nothing to do with the strength of the underlying evidence and everything to do with whether Willis herself can remain the prosecutor bringing it. As of this writing, no trial date has been set, and no court has yet reached a verdict on the substance of the charges. That is worth stating plainly rather than treated as a resolution either way: the Georgia case remains unresolved procedurally, whatever the underlying facts show.

Every thread here — the machines, the county, the phone call, the fake paperwork, the courts, Trump’s own Justice Department, the two dead-voter and ballot-count claims that collapsed on inspection — terminates in the same place: a specific, falsifiable claim that was tested against evidence and did not survive the test. The claims did not simply fail quietly, either. On January 6, 2021, Trump told a crowd assembled at the Ellipse that Congress needed to “count only the electors who have been lawfully slated” — the fake-elector scheme’s own language, repeated back to the crowd as if it described the actual electoral count rather than the fraudulent one his own allies had manufactured — and urged them to march to the Capitol. Several thousand did. Congress’s certification of the vote was delayed for hours, five people died in connection with the events of that day and its aftermath, and more than 1,500 people have since been charged with federal crimes related to the riot. That is where the specific claims catalogued above ended up: not merely rejected in sixty courtrooms, but acted upon by a crowd that believed them, at a building where the actual, undisputed count was being finalized.

That is worth holding onto exactly as written, without embellishment, because the next essay in this series does not need embellishment either. It needs only the record — and if that record, once Part II is laid beside it, turns out to show Trump applying the same standard to federal power over elections in both directions, this series will say so plainly rather than force a conclusion the facts do not support. What Donald Trump built once he returned to office was not a correction to a system he had spent four years calling broken. It was direct federal control over the pieces of that system — the mail ballots, the voter rolls, the district maps — that had, in his telling, failed him the first time. Part II picks up there: not with claims this time, but with the machinery itself, and what a man who once said the count could not be trusted chose to do once he held the power to conduct it.
