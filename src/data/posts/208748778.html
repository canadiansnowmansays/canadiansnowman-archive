Dunhuang has appeared already in this series, twice and briefly — once in Part I as the westernmost of the four commanderies Emperor Wu established along the Hexi Corridor around 111 BCE, and again in Part IV as the site where a sealed cave full of Buddhist manuscripts was rediscovered in 1900. It gets a full installment of its own because the gap between those two facts is, in itself, one of the more remarkable transformations in the history of the route: a military tax-collection outpost founded to keep watch for Xiongnu raiding parties became, over the following millennium, one of the great repositories of religious art and manuscript culture anywhere on earth, and then became so completely forgotten that its most important cave sat sealed and unnoticed for roughly nine hundred years before a wandering Daoist caretaker happened to knock through a wall.

The geography explains why Dunhuang mattered before it explains anything else. The Hexi Corridor — that narrow strip of habitable land running northwest from the Chinese heartland, hemmed in by the Qilian mountains to the south and desert to the north — ends at Dunhuang, and what lies beyond is the Taklamakan, a desert whose name is popularly, if probably apocryphally, glossed as something like “you go in and you don’t come out.” No caravan crossed the Taklamakan directly. Travelers instead chose between a northern route skirting the desert’s edge along the foothills of the Tian Shan, or a southern route running along the base of the Kunlun range at the edge of the Tibetan Plateau, and both routes began at Dunhuang. Two gates west of the town — the Jade Gate and the Yang Pass, both of which survive today as eroded rammed-earth ruins standing in genuinely empty desert — marked the formal edge of Chinese administrative control, and Chinese poetry across many centuries treated passing beyond them as a kind of civilizational departure, the point at which a traveler left the known world for whatever lay past it. The Tang poet Wang Wei’s famous farewell lines about there being no old friends west of Yang Pass became, and remain, one of the most quoted sentiments in Chinese literature precisely because everyone understood what the geography meant.

A town positioned at that particular junction accumulates a very specific kind of population. Dunhuang was, for most of its history, home to a mixture of Han Chinese administrators and garrison soldiers, Central Asian and Sogdian merchant families with permanent trading interests in the town, Tibetan populations who periodically controlled it outright, Uyghur Turkic communities, Indian and Kashmiri Buddhist monks passing through or settling, and an assortment of travelers who had either just survived the desert or were about to attempt it. This was not multiculturalism as any modern polity would understand or celebrate it — nobody in Dunhuang articulated a philosophy of pluralism, and the town saw plenty of ordinary ethnic friction and periodic violent conquest. It was simply the practical consequence of a chokepoint: everyone moving between China and the west had to stop here, some of them stayed, and after a few centuries the resulting population was polyglot by default rather than by design.

The cave temples began in 366 CE, according to a later inscription, when a monk named Yuezun reportedly experienced a vision of a thousand Buddhas in the light on a nearby cliff face and carved the first meditation cave there. Whether or not the vision happened as described, the practical logic held: the cliffs outside Dunhuang were soft enough to carve, close enough to a wealthy town to attract patronage, and far enough from it to suit contemplative life. Over the following thousand years — a span running from the late fourth century through the fourteenth, through the Tang, the Tibetan occupation, the Uyghur and Tangut periods, and the Mongol conquest — monks and lay patrons carved and decorated something on the order of seven hundred caves into those cliffs, of which roughly four hundred and ninety contain significant surviving murals and sculpture. The total painted surface across the complex runs to tens of thousands of square metres, which makes the Mogao Caves not merely a significant Buddhist site but one of the largest concentrations of premodern painting surviving anywhere in the world.

What makes the murals genuinely valuable to historians, beyond their scale and their religious content, is their incidental documentary detail. Buddhist cave painting was not intended as social documentation, but the artists filled their compositions with the world they actually knew: merchants and their pack animals, foreign faces with distinctly Central Asian and Indian features, musicians with instruments whose construction can be reconstructed from the paintings, agricultural tools, architectural details of buildings that no longer exist anywhere, clothing, hairstyles, and in several notable cases, detailed depictions of caravans being robbed by bandits — a subject presumably chosen for its illustrative value in a parable about deliverance, but which incidentally records what banditry on the Silk Road actually looked like to people who had reason to worry about it. The murals also register, across the centuries, the shifting political control of the town: donor portraits painted into the lower margins of cave walls depict the patrons who paid for the work, and their clothing and titles change visibly as Chinese administrators give way to Tibetan officials give way to Uyghur nobility, providing an unusually direct visual record of who held power in a remote oasis town at various points across a thousand years.

The Tibetan occupation deserves particular attention, because it is the period in which Dunhuang’s manuscript production reaches its peak and because it complicates the assumption that foreign conquest necessarily interrupts cultural life. Tibetan forces took the Hexi Corridor during the chaos following the An Lushan rebellion, seizing Dunhuang around 786 CE after a siege, and held it for roughly six decades until a local Chinese uprising under Zhang Yichao expelled them in 848. Tibetan rule brought administrative disruption, tax obligations, and the resented requirement that some Chinese residents adopt Tibetan dress and hairstyle. It also brought enormous imperial Tibetan patronage of Buddhism, since the Tibetan empire had recently adopted the religion as a state project, and the result was a boom in copying, translating, and cave-building. A very substantial proportion of the Tibetan-language material in the Library Cave dates from this period, including copies of sutras produced in what appear to be organized scriptoria with quality-control systems — some manuscripts carry the names of the scribe, the reviser, and the checker. The oasis town went on being an oasis town, in other words, and its monks went on doing what monks do, with a different set of patrons paying for it.

That pattern of patronage is visible throughout the complex’s history and explains a good deal about which caves got built and how they were decorated. Cave sponsorship was expensive and public, which made it an ideal instrument for families seeking to display piety and status simultaneously — a mechanism familiar from chantry chapels in medieval Europe and functionally identical. The great local families of Dunhuang, the Zhang and later the Cao, sponsored the largest and most elaborate caves, installed portraits of themselves and their relatives in the donor registers along the walls, and in the Cao family’s case ran what amounted to a hereditary regional government for most of the tenth and eleventh centuries while maintaining a painting academy attached to the caves. What survives at Mogao is therefore not a neutral record of Buddhist devotion but a record of who in Dunhuang had money and wanted it known, which is arguably a more useful thing for a historian to have.

Then there is Cave 17, the so-called Library Cave, which is why most people outside the field of Buddhist art history have heard of Dunhuang at all. It is a small side chamber, roughly three metres square, opening off the corridor of a larger cave, and at some point in the early eleventh century — the exact date and reason remain debated — it was filled with somewhere between forty and fifty thousand manuscripts and paintings, walled up, plastered over, and painted with a mural that concealed its existence entirely. Why anyone did this is genuinely unresolved. One long-standing theory holds that the material was hidden in anticipation of an invasion, possibly by the Tangut Western Xia in the 1030s, though no invasion of Dunhuang in that period appears to have produced the kind of destruction that would justify the precaution. Another theory, argued persuasively by some specialists, holds that Cave 17 was not a hiding place at all but a genizah — a repository for sacred texts too worn or damaged to use but too holy to destroy, sealed once full in the ordinary course of monastic housekeeping rather than in any emergency. The contents lean toward the second explanation: a great deal of the material is fragmentary, damaged, duplicated, or otherwise the sort of thing a working monastery would retire rather than treasure.

Whatever the reason for the sealing, its effect was to preserve a cross-section of everyday textual life in a Silk Road town with a completeness that essentially nothing else from the period matches. The Library Cave held Buddhist sutras in overwhelming numbers, but also Daoist and Confucian texts, Nestorian Christian and Manichaean writings, Hebrew prayers, and material in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese, Uyghur, Tangut, and several other languages including some so poorly attested elsewhere that Dunhuang remains a primary source for reconstructing them. Alongside the religious material sat the sort of documents nobody preserves on purpose: loan contracts, land deeds, tax registers, divorce agreements, monastery account books, letters, school exercises written by bored students, medical prescriptions, and calendars. One frequently cited document is a model divorce agreement expressing the hope that a separating couple might each find happiness afterward — an unexpectedly gracious sentiment to find in an eleventh-century legal template, and precisely the sort of thing that never survives from most premodern societies because nobody thought it worth copying into a permanent record. And the cave contained the printed Diamond Sutra dated to 868 CE, complete with a colophon naming the man who commissioned it and the reason he did so, which makes it the oldest complete dated printed book known to survive anywhere — printed, it is worth noting, roughly six centuries before Gutenberg.

The story of what happened after 1900 is less edifying. Wang Yuanlu, the self-appointed Daoist caretaker who found the cave while clearing sand from a corridor, made attempts to alert Chinese provincial officials to the discovery and received, by most accounts, minimal interest and no resources. Aurel Stein arrived in 1907 with funding, an interpreter, and an approach that combined genuine scholarly interest with a straightforwardly acquisitive purpose, and departed with thousands of manuscripts and paintings for a payment that would be difficult to characterize as a fair market transaction under any reading. Paul Pelliot, arriving in 1908 with the considerable advantage of actually being able to read the material, spent three weeks in the cave by candlelight — there is a well-known photograph of him doing exactly this, surrounded by bundled manuscripts — and selected the items he judged most significant, which he took to Paris. Russian, Japanese, and later American expeditions took further material. By the time the Chinese government ordered the remaining contents removed to Beijing in 1910, the collection had been thoroughly dispersed, and it remains so: the Dunhuang manuscripts today are split principally between London, Paris, Beijing, and St. Petersburg, with smaller holdings elsewhere.

The scholarly community has, to its credit, addressed the practical consequences of this dispersal more effectively than most comparable cases. The International Dunhuang Project, established in 1994, has spent decades digitizing and cross-cataloguing the scattered holdings so that a researcher can now reconstruct on a screen a collection that no longer exists in one physical place, which is a genuine achievement and also a slightly melancholy one — the digital reunification stands as a monument to the fact that a physical reunification is not going to happen. The underlying dispute over ownership remains entirely live, and Dunhuang sits alongside the more famous contested cases in the ongoing argument about artifacts removed from their countries of origin during periods of asymmetric power.

The caves’ survival into the modern era owed a great deal to the same isolation that made them worth building in the first place, and that isolation has since become their principal conservation problem. Dunhuang’s extreme aridity is what preserved a thousand years of pigment and paper that would have rotted almost anywhere wetter, but the site now faces a set of pressures its builders never had to consider: wind-driven sand abrasion, salt migration through the rock as humidity fluctuates, and the accumulated respiration and body heat of very large numbers of visitors, each of whom raises the moisture level inside chambers that survived a millennium precisely by staying dry. The Dunhuang Academy, which has managed the site since 1944, has responded with strict visitor caps, rotating cave access, environmental monitoring, and an extensive digital reproduction program that allows tourists to see high-resolution facsimiles in a purpose-built visitor centre rather than crowding into the originals — a solution that satisfies almost nobody entirely but has measurably slowed the deterioration. It is, in its way, a fitting final chapter for a site whose entire history has been a series of accommodations to the desert: the sand nearly buried it, the dryness saved it, and now the only real threat to what the dryness preserved is the breath of people who have come to admire it.

What Dunhuang finally illustrates, for the purposes of this series, is how badly the intentions behind infrastructure predict its eventual consequences. Emperor Wu established a garrison there to secure a military corridor and collect customs revenue. The garrison made the town viable. The town’s position made it wealthy. The wealth funded a thousand years of religious art. The art complex included a storage room nobody thought remarkable, which was sealed for reasons that may have been nothing more than routine, and which consequently preserved the ordinary paperwork of a desert town in a state of completeness that no deliberate archival effort in the ancient world came close to achieving. Nobody planned any step in that sequence with the next step in mind. The most valuable thing Dunhuang produced was the thing nobody was trying to produce, which is, by this point in the series, beginning to look less like a coincidence and more like the route’s defining characteristic.
