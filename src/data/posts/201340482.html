Postmark: Burlington, Ontario. June 2026.

His parents raised him with a simple rule: every place you visit, leave it a little better than you found it.

Evan Budz is fifteen years old. He attends Frank J. Hayden Secondary School in Burlington, Ontario. He likes hiking, canoeing, and being outside. He is, by any reasonable measure, exactly the kind of teenager that adults describe approvingly as outdoorsy — which is to say, the kind who notices things.

On a camping trip some time ago, he noticed a snapping turtle.

He noticed how it moved — what he would later describe to Popular Science as “graceful, fluidic, and generally non-disruptive.” He noticed what the turtle was not doing. It was not churning the water. It was not scattering the fish. It was not announcing itself with turbulence and noise. It was simply moving through its environment, gathering information, leaving nothing disturbed behind it.

Evan Budz watched the turtle and asked a question that most people would not think to ask: why don’t we build robots that way?

That question, posed by a teenager in the Ontario wilderness with no laboratory, no corporate backing, and no reason to believe it would lead anywhere in particular, is where this postcard begins.

The Machine in the Pool

BURT — Bionic Underwater Robotic Turtle — was tested first in his grandparents’ backyard pool in Burlington. Just over eight feet deep. No currents, no murk, no wildlife, no complications. The controlled environment of a suburban backyard standing in for the coral reefs and freshwater systems that BURT was ultimately designed to monitor.

This is worth pausing on, not to diminish the achievement but to honour it. The gap between a backyard pool and the open ocean is enormous. Evan Budz knows this — he says so directly, with the refreshing intellectual honesty of someone who has not yet learned to oversell his work to a press release. The pool was a starting point. Lake Ontario was the next step. The real aquatic world, with its currents and debris and changing light and indifferent wildlife, remains the test that matters most and has not yet been fully passed.

But here is what the pool produced: a robot that weighs eleven pounds, swims on four biomimetic flippers — larger front pair for propulsion, smaller rear pair for steering and stability — carries a Raspberry Pi microcomputer and a front-mounted camera, follows autonomous search patterns without a tether, detects replicated coral bleaching with ninety-six percent accuracy, and runs for up to eight hours on a lithium battery with a solar panel available for extended operation.

It moves at approximately 0.5 miles per hour. The natural swimming speed of a turtle.

That pace is not a limitation. For environmental monitoring — for the patient, steady accumulation of data about what is happening in a fragile ecosystem before the damage becomes irreversible — 0.5 miles per hour is not slow. It is exactly right. Conservation work is not about speed. It is about presence. BURT can be present, quietly, for eight hours at a stretch, seeing what needs to be seen without becoming part of what needs to be worried about.

Why the Reefs Need Watching

Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor. They support approximately twenty-five percent of all known marine species. They protect coastlines from storm surge. They underpin fishing industries that feed hundreds of millions of people. The ecosystem services they provide have been valued at nearly ten trillion dollars annually — a figure that captures something of their economic importance while entirely failing to capture what they actually are, which is the most biodiverse marine habitat on the planet and one of the most ancient living systems on Earth.

They are dying faster than we can monitor them.

From January 2023 through mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress impacted eighty-four percent of the world’s coral reef area — the fourth global mass bleaching event on record, and by far the most extensive. The first such event, in 1998, affected twenty-one percent of reefs. The second, in 2010, reached thirty-seven percent. The third, from 2014 to 2017, hit sixty-eight percent. NOAA’s Coral Reef Watch program had to add three new alert levels to its bleaching scale in 2024 because the existing categories were no longer adequate to describe what was happening. Some reefs are now at risk of losing more than eighty percent of their coral to prolonged heat stress — a threshold that was previously theoretical.

The monitoring problem is acute. Comprehensive reef surveys require trained divers, surface vessels, and weeks of expensive fieldwork. The coverage is inevitably incomplete. A reef system can be deteriorating significantly between surveys, with the damage only discovered after the window for early intervention has closed. Satellite monitoring provides broad coverage but limited resolution. The gap between what researchers need to know and what current tools allow them to find out is wide, and it is costing reefs their lives.

BURT is a partial answer to that gap. A machine that can swim autonomously through reef systems, detecting bleaching events in real time, without disturbing the ecosystem it is studying — and doing so at a cost that does not require a research vessel and a crew — changes what early-warning monitoring looks like. One BURT cannot solve the problem. A fleet of them, as Budz envisions, begins to look like a solution.

Why Propellers Are the Wrong Answer

The standard approach to underwater monitoring is the remotely operated vehicle — a propeller-driven drone, tethered to a surface vessel, piloted by a human operator watching a screen. These are useful. They are also noisy, turbulent, and disruptive in precisely the environments researchers are trying to protect. A propeller-driven drone entering a coral reef system is a bit like driving a truck through a greenhouse to see how the plants are doing. You will collect data. You will also have changed what you were trying to observe.

Biomimicry — the discipline of designing technology by studying what three billion years of biological evolution has already figured out — offers a different premise. Nature is not particularly interested in noise or turbulence. Fish do not churn water. Birds do not announce their presence to prey. Turtles, as Evan Budz noticed on his camping trip, are extraordinarily good at moving through water without disturbing it.

The insight is ancient in principle and startlingly underused in practice. Leonardo da Vinci studied birds for decades, filling notebooks with sketches of wing mechanics and flight dynamics, because he understood that the birds had already solved the problem he was trying to solve. George de Mestral came back from a hike in 1941 with burrs stuck to his socks, looked at them under a microscope, and invented velcro. The history of human engineering is littered with moments when someone stopped and asked: what has nature already figured out that we keep ignoring?

Evan Budz asked that question at fifteen, watching a snapping turtle on a camping trip, and built an eleven-pound answer in his grandparents’ pool.

From Coral to Microplastics

Budz did not arrive in Phoenix, Arizona for the Regeneron International Science and Engineering Fair as an unknown. He had already won Best Project Innovation at the Canada-Wide Science Fair in Fredericton — a competition in which finalists qualify from approximately 25,000 competitors nationwide. From Fredericton, BURT travelled to Riga, Latvia, where in September 2025 it won first prize at the European Union Contest for Young Scientists, beating competitors from forty countries and earning approximately $11,500 in prize money. The trajectory — Fredericton to Riga to Phoenix — tells its own story about how scientific achievement compounds when the underlying work is genuine.

The BURT that won in Riga could detect coral bleaching and invasive species with ninety-six percent accuracy. It followed autonomous search patterns. It moved without disturbing what it was studying. It was already a significant piece of work.

The BURT that arrived in Phoenix in May 2026 was something more. In the months between Latvia and Arizona, Evan Budz had not stopped. He had added front lights for murky water conditions. He had added an ultrasonic transducer for obstacle detection. Most significantly, he had developed an entirely new capability: a 3D holographic camera system, powered by a custom-trained neural network, capable of detecting and classifying microplastics in real time.

Microplastics are one of the defining environmental crises of the early twenty-first century — particles so small they pass through conventional filtration, so widespread they have been found in Arctic ice and deep ocean trenches and human blood, so persistent that they will outlast everything alive today. Monitoring them requires either bringing water samples to a laboratory — slow, expensive, labour-intensive — or bringing the laboratory to the water. The conventional approach yields snapshots. BURT, in its 2026 iteration, yields continuous surveillance. It is a laboratory that swims, watching in real time, leaving nothing disturbed.

At Phoenix, competing against 1,727 finalists from 67 countries, Evan Budz won the $50,000 Gordon E. Moore Award for Positive Outcomes for Future Generations. The award is named for the Intel co-founder who formulated Moore’s Law — the observation that computing power doubles roughly every two years — and is given for work with meaningful long-term consequence for humanity.

The judges were not wrong.

The Canadian Thread

Here is something that the international coverage of Evan Budz’s achievement tends to mention briefly and then move past: he is Canadian, he is from Ontario, and he is fifteen.

This matters in a specific way that is easy to overlook. Canada has an extraordinary and largely unsung tradition of producing robotics innovation that punches well above its weight. The Canadarm — the robotic arm that flew on ninety NASA Space Shuttle missions beginning in 1981, that helped assemble the International Space Station, that made Canada a permanent partner in human spaceflight — began as a Canadian engineering project that nobody in the rest of the world expected to be as good as it turned out to be. The University of Waterloo’s RoboHub is the largest and most active robotics research cluster in Canada. Vancouver’s Sanctuary AI deployed the first commercially operational general-purpose humanoid robot in 2025. Burlington, Ontario produced Evan Budz.

There is a pattern here that is worth naming. Canadian robotics innovation tends to be quiet, purposeful, and more interested in solving the actual problem than in announcing that it is solving the problem. BURT is very much in that tradition. It does not announce itself. It swims at 0.5 miles per hour and watches and listens and reports back. It leaves things a little better than it found them.

His parents would approve.

What BURT Is Really Telling Us

The most important thing about BURT is not the ninety-six percent accuracy figure, though that is remarkable. It is not the $50,000 prize, though that is well-deserved. It is not even the holographic microplastics detection, though that represents a genuinely significant advance in environmental monitoring capability.

The most important thing about BURT is the design philosophy behind it.

Evan Budz did not start from the assumption that the existing approach to underwater monitoring was acceptable and then try to improve it incrementally. He started from the observation that a snapping turtle had already solved the movement problem in a way that existing technology had not, and he built backward from that observation. The result is a machine that is not only effective but is effective because it respects the environment it is designed to protect. Form follows function, and the function requires not disturbing what you are trying to observe.

This is rarer than it sounds. Much of the history of technological development in sensitive environments is a history of applying the wrong tool and then trying to manage the damage. We drill for oil with equipment that can fail catastrophically. We fish with nets that catch everything indiscriminately. We monitor coral reefs with drones that churn the water. The technology arrives before the ethical consideration of what the technology does to the place it enters.

BURT arrives after that consideration. The ethical question — how do I study this without becoming part of what I am studying? — is baked into the design from the first sketch. That is not a small thing. That is, arguably, the thing.

The Next Test

Evan Budz has said that part of his $50,000 will go toward further development of BURT. The next challenge, which he acknowledges directly and without spin, is the one that the backyard pool and the testing tank cannot solve: real aquatic environments, with everything that implies. Currents and debris. Changing light and visibility. Wildlife that does not cooperate with predetermined search patterns. Coral reefs, if BURT ever reaches them, are significantly more complicated than a grandparents’ pool in Burlington, Ontario.

There is also the question of scale. Evan Budz envisions a future in which fleets of BURTs patrol waterways continuously, building the kind of long-term data record that conservation researchers need but cannot currently obtain. One robot, however capable, covers limited ground. A fleet changes the nature of what is possible — not just for coral reef monitoring but for the broader freshwater systems that face their own crisis. Lake Ontario, where BURT has already been tested, is one of the most studied bodies of water in Canada. It is also one of the most stressed. Microplastics monitoring at the scale BURT makes possible would produce data that simply does not currently exist.

Both challenges — real-world robustness and scalable deployment — are engineering problems. They are hard, but they are the right kind of hard: specific, tractable, addressable by someone who has already demonstrated a remarkable capacity for solving specific and tractable problems in a grandparents’ pool in Burlington.

He is fifteen. He is in Grade 10 at Frank J. Hayden Secondary School. He has won the top prize at the Canada-Wide Science Fair, the European Union’s most prestigious youth science competition, and the world’s largest youth science fair. His university plans are, sensibly, undecided — though definitely STEM, he says.

The turtle is waiting. The ocean is waiting. The future, assembled from off-the-shelf parts and a camping trip epiphany, is swimming steadily toward both.

At 0.5 miles per hour. Right on schedule.
