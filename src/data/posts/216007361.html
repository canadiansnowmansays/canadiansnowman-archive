I. The Twelve Out of Ten

Regular readers of The Beijing Opera will remember where this began: Busan, South Korea, 30 October 2025, the first Trump-Xi meeting of the second Trump presidency, and a Truth Social post in which the President of the United States rated his own performance a twelve out of ten. It was, even by the standards of the genre, an unusually confident grade for a meeting that produced no signed agreements and a great deal of mutual flattery. But buried in the theatre of that self-assessment was the actual news: Trump invited Xi Jinping to visit the United States “at an appropriate time,” and the machinery of reciprocal summitry — the diplomatic custom by which a state visit is repaid with a state visit, like dinner parties among people who don’t much like each other but can’t afford to stop hosting — began to turn.

Seven months later, on 14 May 2026, Trump made good on the return leg of his own itinerary, arriving in Beijing for the summit this newsletter covered in five acts: the honour guard at the Great Hall of the People, the tour of the Forbidden City with the wives in tow, the banquet in the Golden Room, the “twelve out of ten” sequel review, and the Truth Social postscript in which Trump announced that America needed a ballroom of its own because, in his words, China had one and the United States therefore should too. That last detail seemed like a throwaway line in May. It is not a throwaway line anymore, and we will spend the whole of Part II on why.

Before that, though: the actual invitation. On 15 May 2026, during his visit to Beijing, Trump extended the reciprocal invitation — Xi would come to the White House in September. It took until 23 July for Trump to name the date publicly, telling reporters that Xi would arrive “toward the end of September... the 24th, I believe” — a phrasing that manages to sound both confident and improvised, which is more or less the house style of this administration’s diplomacy. The 24th held. It is now confirmed by both governments, it is nine days from the date I am writing this sentence, and if the choreography holds, Xi Jinping and his wife Peng Liyuan will arrive in Washington for his first state visit to the United States in more than a decade.

II. The Invitation Returned

It is worth pausing on what “returned” actually means here, because the word is doing more work than it looks like. This is not a novel encounter between strangers. Trump and Xi have now met three times in eleven months — Busan in October, Beijing in May, Washington in September — which is a pace of engagement between the leaders of the world’s two largest economies that has no real precedent in the post-Nixon era. Fifty years of American diplomats treated a US-China summit as a generational event, carefully staged and rarely repeated. This administration treats it more like a subscription service.

The Beijing summit produced what officials on both sides called, without apparent irony, “constructive strategic stability” — a phrase that means almost nothing and was clearly designed to mean almost nothing, alongside an ostensible alignment on keeping Iran away from nuclear weapons, Chinese purchases of American airline and agricultural products, and the paper architecture of a planned US-China Board of Trade and Board of Investment, as the Heritage Foundation catalogued in its own preview of the Washington summit. None of that is nothing, exactly. But none of it is the kind of durable agreement that changes the trajectory of a relationship, either, and that is precisely how the analysts I trust are framing September 24: not as a summit that will transform the underlying structure of US-China relations, but as what Heritage bluntly called a “maintenance check” on whatever was promised in May.

That is a useful frame for a Canadian writing about a bilateral relationship he has no vote in. A maintenance check is not nothing — you take your car in for one because the alternative is worse — but nobody expects the mechanic to hand you back a different car.

It is also worth noting, four months on, how selectively the May promises have actually been kept. Beijing pledged to buy at least $17 billion a year in US farm goods through 2028, prorated for this year — and through the first seven months of 2026 has bought $3.9 billion, excluding soybeans, barely above the tariff-war lows of a year earlier. Soybeans are the exception, and a telling one: purchases accelerated sharply in August, run through Sinograin, the state-owned enterprise that manages China's grain reserves, in a pattern one commodities analyst described simply as evidence "it's a geopolitical deal" rather than a commercial one. Corn, sorghum, wheat, cotton, beef, pork — all still languishing. Beijing appears to have concluded that one visibly kept promise, timed to land just before the summit, buys as much goodwill as fully honouring seventeen billion dollars' worth of ones nobody is tracking closely enough to notice the difference.

III. Fixing the Date

The date itself carries its own quiet subtext. Xi's visit lands five weeks before the US midterm elections, which is either a coincidence or isn't, depending on how much credit you're willing to extend an administration that has shown a consistent instinct for turning foreign policy into stagecraft with a domestic audience in mind. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer told reporters the two countries would make "some announcements on agriculture and non-tariff barriers" during the visit, without specifying what — the kind of promise that reads, to a skeptical outsider, like a placeholder for whatever turns out to be politically useful to announce three weeks out from voters going to the polls, and a curious one given that Beijing is already several billion dollars behind on the agriculture pledge it made the last time these two men shook hands.

The preparatory work has been underway for most of September. Bessent, Greer, and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng met in early September to discuss what Reuters described as “summit deliverables” — a phrase I find almost admirably candid, since it dispenses with the pretense that a summit produces anything other than deliverables, manufactured to order, on a schedule set well in advance of the actual meeting.

IV. Ghosts of 2015

Here is where the historical bookend gets interesting, and where a comparison across eleven years does more explanatory work than anything from this month's news cycle. The last time a sitting Chinese president made a state visit to Washington was September 2015, when Xi — five years into his tenure as general secretary, still consolidating power — arrived with a sizeable business delegation that included Alibaba's Jack Ma and Tencent's Pony Ma, alongside executives from major state-owned banks and firms. That 2015 visit produced a $38 billion agreement for 300 Boeing aircraft, the kind of splashy, countable deliverable that both governments could point to and call a win.

Xi is reportedly preparing to do it again. Two sources told Reuters in early September that Xi is assembling a large CEO delegation for the Washington trip — an unusual move, the report noted, given how rarely Xi travels abroad with corporate executives these days, and given how badly many of China’s marquee private-sector names have fared since 2020. Jack Ma himself all but vanished from public life after Beijing’s regulatory crackdown on the technology, education, and property sectors that began that year; the entire cohort of executives who might have joined a delegation like this one has spent five years learning that visibility is a liability in the current political climate. That Xi is willing to risk parading some of them through Washington again — even as the White House insists, a little oddly, that it “is not tracking a Chinese CEO delegation” — tells you something about how much Beijing wants this visit to read as a commercial thaw rather than a geopolitical stalemate.

It will need to read as a thaw against a much colder backdrop than 2015 offered. Washington has since imposed a 100 percent tariff on imported Chinese electric vehicles, banned the import of foreign drones, routers, and robots, forced the divestiture of TikTok’s US operations, and placed major Chinese technology firms on both the Commerce Department’s Entity List and a Pentagon blacklist. The Supreme Court’s February ruling striking down the China-specific tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act briefly threatened to unwind part of that architecture — before the administration simply replaced it with a 10 percent global tariff, in a manoeuvre this newsletter has tracked at length under a different masthead and will not re-litigate here. The point stands regardless: the 2015 Boeing deal happened in a relationship that, however tense, still ran on the assumption that trade was mostly separable from security. The 2026 version does not have that luxury, and both delegations know it.

V. The Keenleyside Test

My great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside spent a career watching Canada thread the needle between larger powers who assumed the world revolved around their disputes, and he had a rule of thumb worth borrowing here: pay less attention to what a summit is called than to what each side needs the other to believe by the time it’s over. Applied to September 24, that rule produces an uncomfortable answer. Washington needs Beijing to believe the tariff architecture built since 2020 is durable and non-negotiable, even while quietly hoping for agricultural purchases and a Boeing-sized headline before November’s midterms. Beijing needs Washington to believe the CEO delegation and the “constructive strategic stability” language reflect a genuine commercial opening, even while its own regulators continue treating the executives in that delegation as politically expendable the moment they attract too much attention. Both governments are performing sincerity toward audiences that include, but are not limited to, each other. That is not cynicism on my part; it is simply what a maintenance-check summit between two governments who don’t trust one another looks like from the outside, dressed in the language of partnership because the alternative language is too expensive to use out loud.

VI. The TACO Scorecard, Resumed

Readers of the original series will recall the analytical spine of The Beijing Opera: TACO — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — applied issue by issue to score how each side actually behaved against how it postured. It seems only fair to open a scorecard for the Washington leg before the summit happens, if only so I can be embarrassed by it in three weeks’ time.

On trade, both sides are pre-positioned toward Accommodate: the CEO delegation, the agriculture and non-tariff-barrier “announcements” Greer has already flagged, the general appetite on both sides for a win they can each sell domestically. On AI governance — the new item on the agenda that didn’t exist when Part I of The Beijing Opera was written — the early signals point toward Obfuscate, for reasons Part III of this series will lay out in more detail; a dialogue two governments agreed to launch in May has spent the intervening months struggling to agree on who should even be in the room. On Taiwan, the safest prediction is Threaten, quietly, through omission rather than declaration — which is a strange sentence to write, but Part III will make the case that silence is doing more diplomatic work this September than a direct statement would. And on the question of whether either government walks away having actually conceded anything of substance, my early money — subject to revision, as always, once the actual summit happens — is on Capitulate doing the least work of the four, because neither side has much appetite left to give this year.

VII. What a Second Act Has to Do

None of which answers the question a Canadian outsider is entitled to ask: what is this summit actually for? The honest answer, I think, is that it is for itself — a second act whose main function is to prove the first act wasn’t a fluke, to stabilise a relationship that both leaders have decided, for now, is more useful managed than escalated. Michael Kovrig’s framing from the Beijing summit still applies here with barely any modification: the danger was never that Trump would walk into a trap set by Xi, but that the theatre of summitry itself — the ballrooms, the honour guards, the state dinners — would come to substitute for the substance the two countries actually need to negotiate.

We will spend Part II on the most literal version of that theatre: an American honour guard rehearsing a welcome ceremony borrowed almost wholesale from the one China staged in May, a White House stripped of its shrouds and scaffolding just in time for company, and a president who has decided that if China has a ballroom, America needs one too — even though his won’t be finished for another two years, and Xi’s state dinner will have to be held in a construction zone.

The invitation has been returned. The choreography, it turns out, is being returned right along with it.
