I don’t suppose one person in a hundred, outside of Perth County itself, has ever heard of Elsinore, Ontario. Which is, if you mention it to anyone in Elsinore, either a problem to be solved or a condition to be preserved, depending on whether you are talking to Wayne or the Swan Warden.

I should say something about myself before I say anything about the town, since the narrator of a sketch about a small city is never quite as invisible as he pretends to be, and honesty about one’s position is usually the better part of the arrangement.

My name is Stephen Gordon. I grew up in London, which is the city forty-five minutes west on the 401 — the one that named its river the Thames and itself after London, England, and has spent a hundred and fifty years doing its level best to inhabit the comparison, which is more admirable than it sounds and funnier than London would prefer. I have lived near Elsinore for some years now, near enough that I know which bank the swans use in April and which table at The Avon Table is too close to the kitchen, far enough that I am not quite of the place, which is either a disadvantage or the condition of useful observation, and I have never entirely settled which.

My family has connections to this part of Ontario that run deeper than my own residence. My great-uncle was a Canadian diplomat of considerable distinction — a man who operated at the level of nations, who advised prime ministers, who understood that the relationship between countries is not an abstraction but a set of daily decisions made by specific people in specific rooms. His grandson apprenticed at the Festival in its early years and went on to a career of forty years across three countries, playing fathers and detectives and men of complicated history. He started on the stage at the south end of Ontario Street, learning what the work requires.

I write the Moonbeam Chronicle for the Elsinore Beacon, which the editor Norman Stalker publishes every Sunday morning. Norman told me we would try it for a month. That was eight months ago. He has not mentioned the original arrangement since, which is, in Norman’s editorial language, a renewal.

I find Elsinore funny and serious in equal measure, which is the only honest way to find anything worth writing about.

Elsinore is a city of thirty-three thousand people on the Avon River in southwestern Ontario, forty-five minutes east of London on the 401. It is named after the Danish castle in which Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, was informed by his father’s ghost that something was rotten and that someone would need to do something about it. The city’s founders chose this name in 1854. There is no record of their reasoning. There is, among those who live here, a settled conviction that the reasoning was sound and that the name has proved accurate in ways the founders could not have anticipated, which is either a tribute to their foresight or a comment on the general condition of civic life, and in Elsinore these have never been considered mutually exclusive.

You come into Elsinore from the 401 on County Road 8, which descends from the highway through a flat agricultural landscape that is, in the way of this part of the province in summer, almost aggressively productive. Field corn and soybeans grow in their rows with the patient industry of things that have been doing this for generations and see no reason to change. The occasional barn of the tall red variety that someone’s great-grandfather built to last stands, accordingly, still standing — weathered but undefeated, a quality Perth County admires in barns and people alike. The farmhouses sit back from the road behind windbreaks of cedar and spruce planted by people who understood that the wind here is a permanent feature of the arrangement and not a seasonal inconvenience.

There are mailboxes at the ends of long laneways. There are dogs that appear at the edge of properties and watch the road with professional interest. There are fields of standing wheat that move in the morning wind in a way that is genuinely beautiful, though nobody along this road describes it as beautiful because beauty, in Perth County, is understood to be a byproduct of function rather than a destination in itself, and the wheat is beautiful because it is doing what wheat is supposed to do, and that is sufficient.

The fields go on for some distance. Then Elsinore begins.

It begins, as most small Ontario cities begin, with a Tim Hortons and a set of traffic lights and a gas station and a sign that says WELCOME TO ELSINORE — FESTIVAL CITY. If you continue south on Ontario Street rather than turning toward the newer parts of the city, the town reveals itself in the way that towns on rivers reveal themselves: gradually, and then all at once, when you crest the slight rise before the street descends toward the water and you see it laid out below you — the old main street, the trees, and beyond them the silver-grey of the Avon moving between its banks in the summer morning light, unhurried and entirely indifferent to the existence of traffic lights.

The newer parts of Elsinore, to the north and west, are the parts that could be anywhere in the province. Their subdivisions have Elizabethan-adjacent names — Riverbend Estates, Festival Park Heights, Avon Meadows — the kind of naming that happens when a developer has encountered a city’s theme from a distance and is doing his genuine best. The people who live there are proud of the Festival in the way of people who are proud of something excellent that they attend once or twice a season when visitors come, which is a perfectly reasonable relationship to have with a world-class Shakespeare festival and should not be mistaken for indifference.

The old part of Elsinore, along Ontario Street and the streets that run toward the river, is the part that could only be here. The houses are Victorian and Edwardian, maintained with the stubborn care that characterises the relationship between certain Ontarians and certain houses — a relationship of mutual obligation that neither party entered into lightly and neither intends to abandon. The front porches are furnished with chairs that indicate ongoing use rather than decorative intent. In the evenings there are people on them, as there have been for a hundred years, watching the street in the unhurried way of people who have decided this is a reasonable thing to do with an evening.

The trees are large. Maples mostly, their canopies meeting over the road. On Wellington Street, the oaks have been growing since before anyone now living was born, and they have developed, over that time, certain opinions about the sidewalk that the city manages through a series of accommodations representing, in miniature, the relationship between institutions and the things that outlast them.

The gardens of the old streets are the gardens of people who have been gardening for a long time and have stopped trying to impress anyone. They smell, in late June, of something that is very specifically an old Ontario garden in summer — a combination of warm earth and roses past their best that people who grew up near these gardens spend the rest of their lives recognising in other places and never quite finding in the same form.

The Avon River runs through the middle of Elsinore the way rivers run through the middles of small Ontario cities: with complete indifference to the city’s opinion of itself, at whatever depth the season provides, pausing for no committee and acknowledging no permit. It is not a large river. It is not a famous river. It has been running through Perth County since before Perth County was Perth County, which gives it a certain perspective on municipal affairs that it does not share and has not been asked to share and would not share if asked, being a river.

In summer the Avon is green and slow and dappled, moving between the Elizabethan Gardens on one side and the willow trees on the other. The light on the water in the early morning — when the mist is still rising from the surface and the willows are silver-grey in the half-dark and the swans are moving on the far bend with the composed authority of creatures who have been here longer than the Festival and the name Elsinore and fully intend to remain longer still — this light is the thing that Alistair, the Festival’s Artistic Director, has been looking at from his office window for nineteen years. He wrote about it once, in a note to himself he has not shown anyone. It is one of the reasons he titled this, his farewell season, This Rough Magic. He has not explained this to the committee. Some things are not for committees.

In spring the river runs higher and faster, carrying the melt from the fields to the north, and the Swan Warden is on the bank before first light noting the levels and the downstream implications. She has been reading this river for twenty-two years. She can tell you what it is thinking before it has finished thinking it.

In autumn the river slows and the leaves come down from the maples along Wellington Street and the light goes the particular gold of this part of the province in October — a gold that painters have been attempting to capture since the Group of Seven and which the river receives every year with the equanimity of something that has seen a great many attempts.

The swans arrived in the 1970s, when the city acquired a breeding pair as a contribution to the Elizabethan theme. They have since established themselves with the self-assurance of creatures who received an invitation and decided to regard it as a permanent and non-negotiable arrangement. They hiss at tourists who approach too closely, which Wayne’s Literary River Tour laminated route map addresses in a footnote under WILDLIFE ADVISORY. The swans regard this as a technicality. They use the south bank near the second municipal bench in April, for reasons that the Swan Warden has documented in twenty-two years of notebooks and that the swans have not explained.

The Elizabethan Gardens run along the south bank of the Avon between the pedestrian bridge and the municipal dock — tended, intimate, with benches positioned at angles that someone, thirty years ago, determined were correct. They have proved correct in the way that good decisions about where to put a bench prove correct: quietly, over many years, without announcement.

The Festival has been running for seventy years. It draws visitors from across Canada, from the United States, from the United Kingdom, and, increasingly, from South Korea, for reasons that will become clear in due course. The people of Elsinore regard it with the particular pride of a community that has decided it deserves something excellent and then, through sustained effort and occasional stubbornness, produced it. They are not wrong to be proud. They are occasionally, in the specific way of communities that have decided what they are, a little much about it.

This is not a criticism. It is a description.

Ontario Street runs parallel to the river for six blocks, from the traffic lights at the north end — where the Tim Hortons occupies the ground floor of a building that was, in sequence, a hardware store, a dry goods emporium, a pharmacy, and a video rental outlet before Tim Hortons arrived in 2001 and established what appears to be a permanent tenure, which nobody objects to because the coffee is reliable and the parking is free and the window table on the right has a clear sightline to the Festival Theatre marquee six blocks south — down to the Festival Theatre at the south end, where the street meets the river and the whole composition achieves something that city planners in larger cities spend considerable money attempting and occasionally manage.

The six blocks contain: McKelvie’s Bakery, which opens at five-thirty and can be smelled from the pedestrian bridge on a still day, the smell being specifically of cinnamon and bread in proportions that several people have described as the smell of Elsinore itself; Harrington’s Hardware, operated by three generations of Harringtons, which sells what Perth County farms require with the calm authority of a store that has always known its customers; the Elsinore Beacon office, whose windows the editor last cleaned in a year he cannot precisely recall; a gift shop called The Gilded Avon, which sells Festival merchandise, Perth County honey, and items decorated with swans, the existence of which the Swan Warden is aware and regarding which she has said nothing; and The Avon Table, which opened four years ago and serves food that people who have been to Toronto describe as surprisingly good and people who haven’t describe as very good, the distinction being, as Norman Stalker once wrote in the Beacon, “a question of what you think you are being surprised by.”

There is a municipal parking lot behind the Tim Hortons that serves as the informal meeting ground of Elsinore: a place where conversations happen that are too important for a committee and too informal for a restaurant, and where the Festival’s Artistic Director has delivered some of his finest statements to audiences of one.

The Festival Theatre sits at the south end of Ontario Street, facing the river — solid, mid-century, clearly important without announcing it. It does not make a statement about itself the way buildings designed to be photographed make statements. It makes a different statement: the statement of a building that has been full of people for seventy years, that does not need to announce its importance because its importance is in the use.

The marquee faces Ontario Street and can be read from the Tim Hortons window table on the right. It currently reads: THIS ROUGH MAGIC — 2026 SEASON. It has said exactly that since April and will continue saying it until November first. After November first, the Artistic Director will have put down the staff, and the question of what the marquee says next will belong to someone else.

Around Elsinore, as far as you can see from the pedestrian bridge on a clear morning, is Perth County: the fields, the farms, the concession roads running at right angles in the grid the surveyors laid down in the 1820s and that the county has followed ever since, because when something works and the land is flat and the reasons for deviation are insufficient, you follow the grid. The silos catch the early light. The grain elevators of the smaller towns — Millbank, Milverton, Listowel — are visible as vertical declarations in the horizontal landscape.

This is the land that grows things. It has been growing things since before the grid and will grow things after whatever comes next, which is either reassuring or irrelevant depending on your relationship to the long view. In Perth County most people have that relationship whether they have chosen it or not, because the land makes it available every morning.

Elsinore sits in the middle of it all: on its river, beside its Festival, beneath its maples. Thirty-three thousand people who have, collectively and individually, decided that this is where they are. Some were born here. Some arrived. Some arrived and discovered they had, without quite meaning to, stopped leaving.

It is that kind of place.

Something is always, gently, rotten.

The Avon runs through it regardless.

Welcome to Elsinore.
