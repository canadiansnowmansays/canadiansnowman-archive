Postmark: White Sands Missile Range. A laser locked onto a target, holding track, when a civilian aircraft strayed one degree into its field. The system went dark on its own, before anyone in the control room fully understood why.

Part XVI of this series, The First Robot War, reported on the offensive side of the drone war Ukraine has been fighting since 2022 — the FPV quadcopters, the Shahed loitering munitions, the machine-on-machine engagements that turned a battlefield into the world’s fastest-moving robotics laboratory. This postcard reports the other half of that same war: the defensive scramble, now well underway on American soil as much as any front line abroad, to answer a problem that has no clean solution yet, and that every military on Earth is racing to solve before someone solves it against them first.

The arithmetic that breaks the old defense

The problem, stated as plainly as the people fighting it state it themselves, is a matter of cost asymmetry rather than raw capability. As one detailed 2026 military analysis lays it out, a single Iranian-designed Shahed-pattern loitering munition costs roughly twenty thousand dollars to build. The interceptor missile a conventional air-defense system fires to bring one down — a Stinger, in the cheapest common case — runs to roughly a hundred and twenty thousand dollars per shot. By early 2026, Ukrainian and Russian forces were collectively launching an estimated five to ten thousand drones a week in offensive operations, a volume against which traditional air defence, however capable, is not being defeated technically. It is being defeated by arithmetic: no country can sustain firing six-figure interceptors at four-figure drones indefinitely, no matter how reliably each interceptor works.

This is the same cost-asymmetry logic this series found underneath the combat-robotics postcard of Part XIX and the police-robot postcard of Part XXIII, applied here at the scale of a shooting war rather than a garage hobby or a subway platform: the side that can make its weapon cheaper than its opponent’s defence wins the economics of attrition, regardless of who wins any individual engagement. Solving that arithmetic, rather than building a better missile, is the actual engineering problem every counter-drone program in this postcard is trying to answer.

The laser with a Star Wars pedigree

The most promising answer, on the evidence available in 2026, is a weapon with a genuinely strange origin story. As Al Jazeera’s own reporting on the field traces it, much of today’s high-energy laser technology descends directly from the United States’ 1980s Strategic Defense Initiative — the program popularly nicknamed Star Wars, conceived to shoot down Soviet ballistic missiles from space, and generally remembered as an expensive Cold War fantasy that never achieved its original goal. One of the program’s participating firms, the Australian company Electro Optic Systems, took the tracking and beam-control expertise it had developed for that failed missile-defense dream and repurposed it, first to track satellites, and then — after watching Houthi rebels attack Saudi Arabia with drones in 2018 — to shoot down exactly the kind of small, cheap aerial target the original Cold War program never anticipated. The company has grown from a few dozen employees to five hundred, with seven hundred projected by the following year, and secured contracts with the Netherlands, South Korea, and other governments now racing to field the same capability. A defence technology built to counter intercontinental ballistic missiles has found its actual, profitable market four decades later, shooting down machines that cost less than a used car.

The physics of the defeat mechanism is straightforward, even if the engineering to deliver it reliably is not. As one detailed industry account explains, a high-energy laser must hold its beam steady on a single vulnerable point of a small, often manoeuvring quadcopter — a propeller arm, a motor housing, a battery compartment — for long enough, typically several seconds, to burn through it, while wind, smoke, dust, rain, fog, and the beam’s own jitter all work against maintaining that lock. High-power microwave weapons take a different approach entirely, transmitting radio-frequency energy intended to scramble or physically damage the electronics inside a target — the flight controller, the datalink, the GPS receiver — rather than burning a hole through the airframe. Neither approach is a complete answer on its own; both are best understood as one layer in what the field calls a layered defence, alongside older tools like jamming, GPS spoofing, net guns, and, when all else fails, ordinary ammunition.

The base on American soil

What makes this postcard belong as much to the American homeland as to any foreign battlefield is the announcement that anchors it. As Stars and Stripes reported, the Pentagon named five American military bases — Naval Base Kitsap in Washington, Fort Bliss and Fort Huachuca in the Southwest, and two Air Force installations, Grand Forks and Whiteman — to field an operational pilot program of exactly these directed-energy weapons, defending against what officials describe plainly as unlawful and adversarial drone activity over domestic military infrastructure. The joint task force overseeing the effort, established in 2025 specifically to consolidate the government’s response to this threat, reported to Congress that troops deployed along the southern border alone dealt with dozens of small drone incursions using dedicated countermeasure kits, and that installations nationwide logged 350 detections across a hundred different sites in a single recent year. This is not a hypothetical border-of-the-future scenario. It is a defence posture already being built, on bases most Americans could name, against a threat that has moved from foreign battlefield to domestic airspace faster than most people have noticed.

The Pentagon’s own swarm answer

Directed energy is not the only defensive bet on the table, and the Pentagon’s own largest counter-drone initiative reveals a second, complementary strategy this series has now traced through several other territories: fighting a swarm with a swarm. The Department of Defense’s Replicator initiative aims to field thousands of autonomous systems across every service, including dedicated counter-drone platforms, backed by more than three billion dollars in the fiscal year 2026 defence budget earmarked specifically for anti-drone capability. The individual pieces read like a roster of every armed service adapting the same underlying insight in its own idiom: the Army’s Directed Energy Short-Range Air Defense system mounts a fifty-kilowatt laser directly on a Stryker armoured vehicle; the Marine Corps has fielded a vehicle-mounted system called MADIS that combines sensors, electronic warfare, and kinetic weapons in one package; the Navy has installed its own sixty-kilowatt laser, called HELIOS, aboard a destroyer. DARPA, meanwhile, is separately funding research into AI-guided detection and autonomous engagement through a program called Sprint — the same autonomy-under-time-pressure problem this series found in the asteroid-mining robots of Part XII, here compressed from the minutes a communication-lag allows into the fractions of a second a saturating drone swarm demands.

This is worth pausing on, because it marks a genuine escalation past the debate this series covered in Part XVI. There, Ukrainian commanders insisted, nearly unanimously, that the final decision to kill must remain human — a line held, as far as this series could establish, by deliberate choice rather than technical necessity. A counter-drone system defending against a genuine mass-drone saturation attack faces a harder version of the same dilemma: if fifty drones arrive within seconds and a human operator can meaningfully evaluate perhaps five of them in that window, the system either engages the remainder autonomously or it does not engage them at all. Whether that pressure quietly erodes the human-in-the-loop principle this series has found held so firmly elsewhere is, as of this writing, an open question — and one worth watching as directed-energy and autonomous-engagement systems move from White Sands test ranges to operational deployment on ships, vehicles, and fixed installations worldwide.

The safety story worth telling in full

There is one detail from this year’s testing that this postcard wants to linger on, because it is the rare counter-drone story that ends in genuine reassurance rather than escalation. As DefenseScoop’s detailed account of a White Sands test recorded, during an evaluation of the Army’s Multi-Purpose High Energy Laser system, a civilian aircraft on approach to Albuquerque — far from the test range, and entirely unrelated to the exercise — happened to pass through a narrow, one-degree angle that the laser was holding track on for its simulated target. The system shut itself off. Not because an operator noticed and intervened, but because the safety architecture built into the weapon recognized the aircraft’s presence and disabled the beam automatically, before the people running the test had even registered what had happened. One official present called it a tipping point for the entire evaluation — the moment that demonstrated to the Federal Aviation Administration, watching closely as it always does when a weapon is proposed for use anywhere near American airspace, that the system’s inhibit function actually worked exactly as designed, unprompted, under real conditions rather than a scripted demonstration.

This is worth setting beside every other honest-limits section this series has written, because it inverts the usual pattern. Most of the technologies this series has examined earn their caution paragraph by falling short of a promise — the marathon-winning robot that could not stand back up, the recalibration delays in the surgical humanoid, the thread-retraction issues in the first Neuralink patient. Here, the caution paragraph is a success story: a weapon system built to burn a hole in a small flying machine chose, entirely on its own initiative, not to fire near an airliner it was never aimed at in the first place. The subsequent FAA and Pentagon safety review confirmed exactly this finding — that with proper safeguards, the system posed no added risk to commercial air travel — and that finding, not any successful drone kill, was what actually cleared the technology for wider deployment.

The proliferation of this technology beyond American and Israeli borders is itself worth a line, because it follows the same pattern this series traced through the humanoid supply chain in Part VIII: once a capability proves itself, it does not stay contained to the country that developed it. British defence firm MBDA secured a contract worth more than four hundred million pounds to deliver its own DragonFire laser system by 2027, and is separately cooperating with a German counterpart on that country’s parallel program; France has assembled its own coalition of domestic defence firms pursuing the identical capability. Israel’s own Iron Beam system, developed by Rafael and Elbit Systems under a five-hundred-million-dollar contract, extends a lineage this series’ coverage of Middle Eastern drone conflict has already touched — a defensive answer built, in no small part, in direct response to the same Houthi drone attacks that first convinced Electro Optic Systems to repurpose its satellite-tracking lasers. Every major military power capable of the engineering is now building some version of the same weapon, on roughly the same timeline, which is itself the clearest evidence available that the underlying problem — cheap drones overwhelming expensive defences — is recognized as universal rather than particular to any one theatre of conflict.

What the ray gun actually protects

From the balcony, the counter-drone story completes something this series left open in Part XVI. The offensive drone war in Ukraine demonstrated, with brutal clarity, that cheap, mass-produced flying machines can overwhelm expensive, precisely engineered defences through sheer economics. This postcard’s answer — a weapon whose ammunition costs a few dollars in electricity per shot rather than six figures per missile — is the first serious attempt this series has found to break that arithmetic rather than simply lose to it more slowly.

A Canadian, whose country shares the longest undefended border on Earth and has spent the last several postcards watching this series document how quickly a defensive technology can migrate from one theatre to another, should watch this file closely. The same directed-energy systems now guarding Fort Bliss and Naval Base Kitsap are, by design, meant to be interoperable and vehicle-agnostic — buildable onto a robot, a Stryker, or any future platform a defence ministry chooses, in whichever country eventually buys the technology once its American originators finish proving it works. The ray gun that spent four decades failing to shoot down Soviet missiles has finally found honest work, and the honest work, this time, appears to come with the safety switch built in from the start.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived, held its aim, and then — for exactly the right reason — refused to fire.
