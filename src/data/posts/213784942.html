Every essay in this series so far has been about Democrats deciding what to do with people already inside the party — how to discipline the insurgents, how to talk to the base, how to apologise to the constituencies a candidate’s own past comments alienated. This instalment is about the opposite problem, and in some ways the more interesting one: what happens when the party goes looking for converts, actual former Republicans willing to run under a Democratic banner, and finds that the same reversal-scrutiny asymmetry Part VIII diagnosed in Frank Bruni’s column doesn’t disappear when the reversal runs in the other direction. It just changes who gets to make the accusation.

As The Washington Post reported on 30 August 2026, Democrats are fielding a slate of candidates this cycle who are running not despite their Republican pasts but because of them — arguing that having once been inside the conservative coalition gives them a credibility with distrustful and independent voters that a lifelong Democrat can’t easily manufacture. The most prominent of these is David Jolly, the party’s nominee for Florida governor, a former one-term Republican congressman from the Tampa area who spent the Trump years building a national profile as one of the most consistent Republican-turned-critic voices in American politics. But the pattern isn’t confined to Florida. The Post also names Janelle Stelson, a former television news anchor running in Pennsylvania, and Rebecca Bennett, a Navy helicopter pilot running in New Jersey, as part of the same recruitment strategy — candidates whose biographies are built around having left the Republican coalition rather than never having belonged to it.

Jolly’s case is the richest of the three, partly because his reversal has been unusually well-documented in real time rather than reconstructed after the fact. He was a sitting Republican member of Congress in 2016 when he took to the House floor to call on then-candidate Trump to drop out of the presidential race, and he balked publicly at both the proposed “Muslim ban” and the Access Hollywood tape during that campaign. He lost his seat that November to Charlie Crist — a detail with genuine dramatic irony, since Crist was himself a Republican-turned-independent-turned-Democrat, and Crist’s own two failed gubernatorial runs under the Democratic banner, in 2014 and against Ron DeSantis by nineteen points in 2022, are now the exact precedent Florida Republicans are using to discredit Jolly before he’s cast a single general-election vote. By 2018, Jolly had published an op-ed arguing flatly that “you can’t be never-Trump and be a Republican,” which is either a statement of principle or an admission of exile depending on which party you ask, and by April 2025 he had formally registered as a Democrat and begun building toward the governor’s race. He won the Democratic primary on 18 August 2026 to become the party’s first notable nominee for a seat Democrats haven’t held in more than two decades.

The Republican response, delivered by Florida GOP Chairman Evan Power, is worth quoting in full because it is almost a mirror image of the “gotcha piñata” dynamic Bruni’s column, discussed in Part VIII, argued was applied unevenly against Democrats. “No matter the issue, David Jolly has been on all sides of it,” Power said. “Floridians won’t be swayed. Under Republican leadership, Florida enjoys low inflation, fiscal responsibility, and a thriving economy... We aren’t going backward with a flip-flopping political relic.” The Crist comparison is doing the heaviest lifting in that attack, because Crist’s failed 2022 run gives Power a concrete, recent data point suggesting Florida voters have already rejected exactly this profile once, by a landslide margin, making Jolly’s candidacy easy to file under precedent rather than evaluate on its own record.

Here is where this series has to be honest about a distinction Part VIII drew between Ocasio-Cortez’s and JD Vance’s reversals, because it applies with even more force here. Bruni’s argument, and this series’ extension of it, was that not all reversals are the same kind of reversal — that a politician revising an actual belief is doing something categorically different from a politician capitulating to power while pretending the capitulation is growth. Jolly’s reversal is about as clean a case of the former as American politics currently offers. He denounced Trump from the floor of the House in 2016, while it cost him meaningfully within his own party, and he has held that position with total consistency for a decade, culminating in a formal party switch made not for immediate electoral advantage — registering as a Democrat in a state Republicans have controlled for over two decades is not, on its face, a shrewd career move — but seemingly because his stated beliefs no longer had a party home. Whatever else one wants to say about Jolly’s chances in November, “flip-flopping political relic” is a strange way to describe someone whose position on the single most consequential question in American politics has been unchanged since 2016. Power’s attack borrows the vocabulary of inconsistency to describe a decade of remarkable consistency, which is either a rhetorical trick worth calling out directly, or a genuinely different standard than the one Bruni applied to Vance, since Power is at least targeting an actual party switch rather than laundering a private grudge into a public loyalty oath.

That said, this series shouldn’t let Jolly, or Democrats recruiting candidates like him, entirely off the hook either, because the Crist precedent Power is leaning on is a real and uncomfortable data point, not merely a talking point invented for the occasion. Crist’s 2022 loss to DeSantis by nineteen points wasn’t a fluke of a bad candidate; it’s some evidence that Florida voters specifically have already had the “former Republican running as a Democrat” pitch made to them once, recently, at the top of the ticket, and rejected it decisively. Whether Jolly’s version of that pitch lands differently depends on variables this series can’t fully settle from the outside — DeSantis’s term-limited departure changes the shape of the race, Jolly’s decade of consistent anti-Trump positioning is a sharper story than Crist’s more meandering ideological path, and the political environment nationally, per Part IX’s polling data, currently favours Democrats on affordability in a way it didn’t in 2022. But “Florida voters have tried this exact profile before and said no” is not nothing, and Democratic strategists betting on Jolly, Stelson, and Bennett as a genuine electoral strategy rather than a media-friendly novelty need an answer to it beyond “this time is different,” which is the answer every campaign gives right up until the moment it isn’t.

There’s a connective thread here back to Part V’s account of the Democratic Governors Association, and it’s a detail the Post’s reporting on Jolly makes explicit: strategist Christina Freundlich Hyers, who has managed winning campaigns for Democratic Govs. Andy Beshear of Kentucky, Steve Bullock of Montana, and Gina Raimondo of Rhode Island, is a visible surrogate for Jolly’s campaign. Beshear — the same governor whose “talk like a normal human being” line anchored the DGA section of Part V, and who now co-chairs the DGA alongside Whitmer — lending an ally to Jolly’s Florida operation is a meaningful signal about where the party’s most electorally successful red-and-purple-state apparatus thinks its resources are best spent. Hyers’s own framing to POLITICO is worth citing directly because it restates this series’ entire coalition-math thesis in a single sentence: “people are not focused on the letter in front of your name — they want people they trust who do what they think is right and put them first.” That is, almost verbatim, the argument Rob Sand is testing in Iowa with his near-total de-emphasis of his own party label, discussed in the expanded Part V — except Sand was born a Democrat quietly downplaying it, and Jolly is a converted Republican making the same de-emphasis explicit and biographical rather than tonal. Both bets rest on the same underlying claim: that partisan identity is a weaker predictor of a 2026 vote than trust in the specific person asking for it. If that claim is right, recruiting genuine converts isn’t a gimmick. It’s the most literal possible test of the big-tent thesis Part VII built from Daron Acemoglu’s essay, because a convert isn’t merely tolerated inside the tent the way a wavering swing voter might be — he’s actively vouched for by the party as proof the tent has room.

Stelson and Bennett are worth a closer look on their own terms, because the Post’s framing of them as a slate rather than isolated candidacies is itself part of the strategy, and the two of them test the convert thesis in a lower-stakes, more congressional register than Jolly’s statewide gamble. Stelson spent decades as a familiar television news anchor in central Pennsylvania before running for Congress, which gives her a version of the credibility Rob Sand borrows from his auditor’s office in Iowa: a public reputation built on being trusted to deliver information neutrally, now being redeployed in a partisan context where that trust is the entire argument for why voters distrustful of both parties might make an exception for her specifically. Bennett, a Navy helicopter pilot running in New Jersey, offers a different kind of biographical credibility — military service is one of the few remaining categories of experience both parties still compete to claim rather than dispute — and her candidacy suggests Democrats are betting that a service record can do for a Republican-district recruit roughly what it has long done for veterans running as Republicans in Democratic-leaning suburbs: signal competence and country-first instincts that outrun a party label. Neither Stelson’s nor Bennett’s Republican past appears, from the available reporting, to be nearly as ideologically load-bearing as Jolly’s decade-long, floor-speech-documented break with Trump; they read more as candidates who happened to hold a different registration once and have since found a more comfortable political home, which may make them less vulnerable to the “flip-flopping relic” charge precisely because there’s less flip to point to. That’s a real asset in districts that don’t need a redemption narrative so much as a candidate who simply isn’t carrying the Republican brand’s current baggage.

If that claim is wrong, though, the risk isn’t merely that Jolly loses. It’s that a losing convert campaign becomes its own kind of evidence, cited by the next skeptical strategist as proof that former Republicans can’t actually deliver the voters they’re recruited to deliver, which would set the big-tent strategy back further than simply never having tried it. This is the structural tension this series flagged in Part VII in the abstract and now gets to watch play out concretely: Acemoglu’s argument that a coalition needs to hold adversarial energy without alienating the people caught in its blast radius sounds clean in an op-ed and considerably messier when the person testing it is a former GOP congressman whose own party’s most damaging line against him is a decade-old, well-earned reputation for genuine belief revision that Republicans are nonetheless trying to recast as opportunism. Whether Florida voters can tell the difference between Jolly’s decade of consistency and Crist’s more improvised political journey is, in the end, exactly the kind of general-election test this series has been arguing the party needs and has mostly avoided giving itself. In November, for the first time in this series, it will actually get one.
