The photograph used in this White House X post is magnificent.

There is the President, captured in heroic three-quarter profile against a darkened background, the lighting arranged to suggest a man shouldering the burdens of history. Beside the image, his words: JD’s team has already caught “hundreds of billions of dollars of fraud.” Find half the fraud going on in this country — and they will — and America gets a balanced budget. Very soon. The official White House account frames it simply: “Rampant fraud is being identified, prosecuted, and extinguished across America under the Trump Administration.”

I read this several times. I made tea. I read it again.

What a revelation. The deficit — that stubborn, decades-long, bipartisan affliction that has consumed economists, blue-ribbon panels, and at least three generations of earnest congressional interns — turns out to have been fraud all along. Not structural imbalances. Not the compounding cost of two unfunded wars, several rounds of tax cuts, and an aging population. Fraud. Identified by JD. Problem essentially solved. One is almost embarrassed for the economists.

But I want to linger on JD’s achievement for a moment, because it deserves closer examination than it has received. The Vice President and his team have, the President tells us, already caught hundreds of billions. We are invited to imagine the scope of this operation — the diligence, the forensic rigour, the sheer acreage of fraud mapped and catalogued. What exactly did JD find?

Well. Among the fraud that was identified, prosecuted, and extinguished was a $1.776 billion scheme in which the Justice Department, at the direction of the White House, attempted to divert nearly two billion dollars of taxpayer money into an “Anti-Weaponization Fund” — a pool drawn from the federal Judgment Fund, which Congress appropriates for the government to pay legitimate legal claims. This fund would have compensated individuals deemed to be victims of “lawfare and weaponization” by past administrations. The administration’s allies celebrated. Several participants in the January 6th Capitol attack announced their eligibility. Two Capitol Police officers who had actually defended the building that day sued to block it, describing it in a lawsuit that deserves to be framed as “the most brazen act of presidential corruption this century.”

Courts blocked it. Senate Republicans — half the conference, by Ted Cruz’s count — threatened to vote with Democrats to kill it. By June 1st, the administration was backing away, citing a federal judge’s ruling with the pained dignity of a White House that had not, in fact, expected anyone to object.

The $1.8 billion fraud, in other words, was caught. Identified. Extinguished. Just as the President promised.

By JD.

Who works for Trump.

Who authorized the fund.

The circle, as they say in geometry, is complete.

Now, to be scrupulously fair, the scheme did not arrive from nowhere. It grew from a seed planted on January 29th of this year, when President Trump — suing in his personal capacity, as the filings took pains to specify, lest anyone confuse the President of the United States with the President of the United States — filed a $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS and Treasury Department. His complaint: a former IRS contractor had leaked his tax returns to the New York Times and ProPublica in 2019. The contractor went to prison for five years. The President, concluding that five years was insufficient compensation for his suffering, decided the American taxpayer owed him an additional $10 billion.

Senator Ron Wyden called this “a shameless, disgusting act of corruption” and noted that while Trump was loudly defending taxpayer privacy in his own case, his Treasury Department was simultaneously sending tens of thousands of individual tax records to immigration enforcement agents. The height of hypocrisy, Wyden said. He was not wrong, though in this administration the height of hypocrisy tends to be a moving ceiling.

The lawsuit settled on May 18th, producing the Anti-Weaponization Fund described above, along with its swift and ignominious collapse. But here is where the story acquires its most instructive detail, and where the fraud-hunters reveal the precise shape of their blind spot.

The day after the settlement was announced — quietly, as these things tend to happen — a separate one-page addendum appeared on the Justice Department website. Signed by acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, it declared that the IRS is now “forever barred and precluded” from auditing President Trump, his family, his sons, and the Trump Organization. Past returns. Current filings. Future examinations. The whole ledger, permanently sealed. The document releases, waives, and acquits. It is, in its quiet way, a masterpiece of the genre.

Legal scholars noted with some urgency that this immunity had nothing whatsoever to do with Trump’s lawsuit, which concerned the leak of his returns, not the review of them. A full audit could reveal, some estimated, that Trump owes upwards of $100 million in back taxes — or alternatively that he is not the wildly successful businessman he has long claimed to be. Either revelation would be inconvenient. The addendum ensures neither will occur. A former deputy attorney general observed that the DOJ’s own policies prohibit settling matters not referred to the department, and that granting sweeping immunity to third parties who weren’t even plaintiffs violates those policies. “This kind of sweeping immunity,” he said, “is unprecedented and yet another abuse of power.”

The slush fund is dead. The immunity lives.

So let us take the full inventory. The administration that gutted IRS enforcement, fired experienced auditors, and stripped the agency of resources needed to pursue complex returns — that same administration is now the one informing us that fraud is being identified, prosecuted, and extinguished across America. The fraud-hunters operate with impressive thoroughness everywhere except one address, one family, one set of books that will, by legal decree signed on a single page and posted to a government website the day after the announcement in the hope that no one was paying attention, never be opened.

Elon Musk flew in with a chainsaw. He fired twenty thousand federal workers and called it efficiency. He had an acronym and a social media platform and a very dramatic sense of theatre. In the end none of it was necessary. JD just needed a team. The fraud was, it turns out, closer to home than anyone advertised.

Perhaps the heroic photograph was prepared before anyone had fully reviewed the sequence of events. Perhaps the one-page addendum permanently exempting the First Family from IRS examination was a clerical error of the sort that happens when one is moving very fast and catching very much fraud. Perhaps the most reliable indicator of where the fraud actually lives is not, in fact, the precise location the investigators have permanently and legally declined to look.

Perhaps.

The balanced budget is coming. JD’s team is on it.

The mirror has been legally exempted from examination. Forever. In writing. Filed quietly, the day after the announcement, in the hope that no one would notice.

Someone noticed.
