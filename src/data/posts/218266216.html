“He loves war.” — President Donald Trump on his Secretary of Defense, slapping him on the biceps, 2026

I. The Dispatch: Quantico, September 30, 2026

The troops arrived about forty-five minutes early, while country music played over the speakers. As the Washington Examiner’s reporter inside the room noted, roughly 600 of them filed into the auditorium at Marine Corps Base Quantico on Wednesday, September 30, 2026. Every one held a small black book titled The War Department, handed out at the door. It collected Pete Hegseth’s notable speeches as Secretary, and it opened with a foreword by President Trump.

According to the New Republic, in “’No Beardos, No Weirdos’: Hegseth Goes on Bizarre Rant to Silent Crowd,” the foreword promised that American soldiers would not be sent into “dumb wars.” It was distributed in the seventh month of a war with Iran.

The day had started earlier, outside the Pentagon. The Secretary led the same 600 troops in push-ups, stretching and a morning run, the way he might have as an Army captain twenty years ago. The service members had swapped their issued workout gear for the “This is War” T-shirts Hegseth wears in the workout videos his staff posts online. That detail comes from Greg Jaffe’s analysis for the New York Times on September 30, 2026, “Hegseth Talks Tough as Republican Doubts About His Leadership Grow.” If a man’s wardrobe can be a policy statement, this one was.

The address was billed as the “State of the Force.” It came exactly one year after Hegseth stood on the same Quantico stage and lectured more than 800 generals and admirals about fat, beards, rules of engagement and “toxic ideological garbage.” Last year’s audience was mostly senior officers, and he spoke at them. This year’s audience was entirely junior: captains and staff sergeants, the E-6 and O-3 ranks from whom the next generation of generals will be chosen.

The content was mostly familiar. “We are no longer the woke department or the weak department,” he said. Then he gave what he called a “simple translation”:

“No fatties, no tr*nnies, no beardos, no weirdos, no wimps, no radicals. Just warriors.”

Elsewhere he promised “the patriotic cowboys are in, with testosterone testing on top,” and told the room that “diversity is not our strength.” He boasted about the purge of the senior officer corps, citing a newspaper’s tally as if it were a performance review:

“The number crunchers say we fired 10 percent of the generals and admirals across the force, and that number sounds about right.”

As Joe Gould and Hamed Ahmadi reported for the Washington Sun in “Hegseth Is Cutting the Number of Generals and Admirals by 20%,” he then added a further 10 percent cut to general-officer positions. “So, yes, 20% reduction across the board,” he said. “Now the media calls that a purge. I call it accountability.”

Then he pointed at the few reporters at the back of the auditorium. He called them “anti-American losers in our media,” said their coverage of the Iran war was helping the enemy, and reached for the heaviest word in the civic vocabulary: “It’s treason, actually. I mean, I hate to have to say it, but it has to be said.” He has not taken a reporter’s question about that war at a Pentagon briefing since early May.

The last part of the speech was a list of new initiatives:

  • A four-star Autonomous Warfare Command for drones and robotics, which the Pentagon is already calling “AutoWarCom.”

  • A “Fortress America” plan to take military bases off the civilian power grid.

  • An Office of Religious Affairs reporting directly to the Secretary.

  • A competition among the fifty states to host a vast new base for up to 25,000 troops withdrawn from overseas. He did not say which countries would lose them. “We’re talking hundreds of thousands of acres,” he said. “Think a fortress.”

  • A new futures project called Project Meridian. He introduced its three civilian leaders from the stage as “three of our nation’s best minds”: Elon Musk, the Anduril co-founder Palmer Luckey (in a blazer over his Hawaiian shirt), and Newt Gingrich.

Accounts differ on how the room received all this. The New Republic’s headline described a silent crowd. Helene Cooper and John Ismay, in their New York Times report the same day (”’No Beardos, No Weirdos, No Wimps’: Hegseth Lays Out His Military Vision”), recorded that the Secretary finished to a standing ovation.

That ovation is where this series begins. A year ago, the generals and admirals sat stone-faced and kept their applause to themselves. That restraint is the old, unwritten etiquette of a military that serves whichever party wins. Duke’s Peter Feaver, who has studied civil-military relations for decades, told the Times that last year’s senior officers showed by their silence that they were nonpartisan and would still carry out lawful orders. This year the Secretary did not change his speech. He changed his audience.

II. Where Things Stand, as of September 30, 2026

What follows is a snapshot. It will date, and it is meant to. Readers arriving later should treat it as the starting position against which the rest of the series measures events.

The war. The United States and Israel began the war with Iran seven months ago. At least 18 American service members and thousands of Iranians have been killed. In one strike on the war's first day, an American bomb hit a school in southern Iran, killing more than 160 civilians, most of them children, by the count in Representative Thomas Massie's articles of impeachment; the New York Times puts the toll above 175. Hegseth declared victory within weeks. At a Pentagon briefing on March 19, 2026, he said that “never in recorded history has a nation’s military been so quickly and effectively neutralized.” On March 31 he announced a “decisive military victory.” Iran still has its missiles and drones and still fires them at its neighbours and at tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. Wednesday’s speech described the war as one “that every red-blooded American is proud of.” According to U.S. Central Command, as cited by the Washington Sun, it had cost at least $43.6 billion by early September.

The magazines. The war has used up the munitions the United States needs to protect its troops and allies in the Middle East and to deter China or Russia. Defence analysts quoted by the Times estimate that rebuilding those stocks will take two to three years.

The money. The administration has asked Congress for $1.5 trillion for defence. Even Republicans concede it will not pass in its current form. Until something does, the Pentagon runs on last year’s budget of about $890 billion. That shortfall will be covered by taking money from barracks repairs, training and procurement to keep 50,000 troops in the Middle East. Trump’s Golden Dome missile shield will probably stall. The Pentagon’s recent $67 billion request for Iran operations ran to seven pages. The Iraq and Afghanistan supplementals usually ran past one hundred.

The chairs. Hegseth has fired or forced out the Army’s chief of staff, the Army secretary and the Navy secretary. Republican senators are refusing to confirm his replacements. The country’s largest military service, and its Navy, have no Senate-confirmed civilian leadership.

The purge. He has fired more than 80 generals and admirals or blocked their promotions. Senator Jack Reed told the Senate, as the Spokesman-Review reported, that nearly 60 percent of the senior officers Hegseth has fired are women or Black, groups that make up fewer than 20 percent of generals and admirals. He has never explained an individual firing.

The Hill. Senator Thom Tillis cast a key vote to confirm him. He now says Hegseth has not built productive relationships “even with Republican members.” Senator Joni Ernst says he “works very little with Republican senators.” Representative Don Bacon, a retired Air Force general, says the firings are “not decent” and that Congress was not “treated honestly” about withdrawing brigades from Poland and Romania. A Republican congressman is leading an impeachment drive. If Democrats take the House on November 3, 2026, they will not need a Republican to lead it.

The voters. Gas and food prices have risen with the war. Mike Rogers, the Republican Senate nominee in Michigan and an early supporter of the war, now says it “needs to end, and end quickly.”

The boss. Through all of this Hegseth keeps his most important supporter. “Trump really likes Hegseth, and I think that’s based on style,” Todd Harrison of the American Enterprise Institute told the Times. As Hegseth himself told the troops, the President likes to greet him with two words: “Pete, central casting!”

III. What This Series Is

This writer has covered Pete Hegseth before, but always as part of someone else’s story. In The Ministry of Truth he appears as the man who barred photographers over unflattering pictures, made his own press office appointment-only, and kept the boat-strike death toll quiet. In the Iran Crisis war diary he is one of the war’s narrators. Neither series asks about the man himself, so here is that question.

Trump’s original endorsement was that Hegseth “loves war.” Loving war is not the same as running a department of three million people, a budget approaching a trillion dollars, and a nuclear arsenal. That job is mostly administration: budget justifications, confirmation calls, munitions procurement schedules, the patient handling of 535 members of Congress who control the money. None of it makes good social media video.

This series’ argument is that the performance has replaced the job. The push-ups, the T-shirts, the slogans, the victory declarations and the stage at Quantico are not decoration on top of the work. They have become the work. Meanwhile the parts that cannot be filmed — the seven-page budget request, the vacant service secretaryships, the empty magazines, the senators who stopped returning calls — pile up unattended.

Each essay follows the same three movements:

  • The Performance. What he said, staged or posted: the claim as delivered.

  • The Paperwork. What the record shows: the budget lines, the votes, the numbers, the testimony.

  • The Bill. Who pays for the gap between the two: troops, allies, taxpayers, the officer corps, or the nonpartisan military itself.

A note on boundaries. Hegseth’s war on the press stays in The Ministry of Truth. Battlefield events stay in the Iran Crisis diary. This series covers the job itself: personnel, money, Congress, command, and the difference between the image and the output. When stories cross over, the essays will say so and link across.

The series also has a calendar. The midterm elections on November 3, 2026 are the hinge. If Hegseth survives them with a Republican House, the series follows a Secretary who has outlasted his critics. If he doesn’t, it follows an impeachment. Either way the series ends when he leaves office, by his choice or the President’s, or when he becomes the first Secretary of Defense to serve out a full term while at war with the Congress that pays for his war. Between the planned essays, news-pegged instalments will open with a short, dated Since Last Time section, so readers who come late can catch up.

One last point, since the Secretary raised it. Reporting on a war that the government will not report on itself is not treason. It is the job he gave up when he stopped holding briefings. This series will do some of that work for him.
