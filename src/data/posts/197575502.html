Sam Altman in 2016, when he openly discussed prepping for survival with guns, gold, and a bolt-hole in Big Sur. Nine years later, his San Francisco home has been targeted by Molotov cocktail and gunfire — early “warning shots” of AI populism.

“I prep for survival,” Sam Altman confessed in 2016. “I have guns, gold, potassium iodide, antibiotics, batteries, water, gas masks from the Israeli Defense Force and a big patch of land in Big Sur I can fly to.”

Last month, a Molotov cocktail was thrown at Altman’s San Francisco home. A few days later, the home was attacked again, this time by gunfire.

The writer Jasmine Sun called this “AI populism’s warning shots.” The phrase is precise in ways Sun may not have intended. A warning shot is not an endpoint. It is a signal that something is coming — that the gap between the people making the decisions and the people living with the consequences has become too wide to ignore, and that at least some of the people on the wrong side of that gap have decided to say so in the most direct language available to them.

This series has spent thirty-five parts documenting the governance failure from the inside — the dissolved safety teams, the deleted AGI clause, the Pentagon contracts, the court that tried to apply a nineteenth century doctrine to a twenty-first century technology because no twentieth century law was written to cover the gap. This essay documents the response from outside: the democratic backlash that the industry prepared for least and understands worst.

What Ted Chiang Saw in 2017

In 2017 — two years after OpenAI was founded, before Musk left, before Amodei and six colleagues walked out, before ChatGPT — the science fiction writer Ted Chiang published an essay on BuzzFeed News about how Silicon Valley imagines superintelligence.

The fear at the time was the paperclip maximizer: a godlike AI that decides the purpose of existence is the manufacturing of paperclips, making everything else — including humans — irrelevant. Serious people took this seriously. Musk was warning the National Governors Association that AI was “a fundamental risk to the existence of human civilization.”

Chiang looked at the paperclip maximizer and saw something different. “This scenario sounds absurd to most people,” he wrote, “yet there are a surprising number of technologists who think it illustrates a real danger. When Silicon Valley tries to imagine superintelligence, what it comes up with is no-holds-barred capitalism.”

The paperclip maximizer is not a story about a rogue AI. It is a story about what happens when optimization is pursued without constraint, without democratic accountability, without any mechanism to say: not that, not at that cost. Silicon Valley recognized the danger in the abstract. It did not recognize that the danger was already present in the concrete — in its own business model, in its own race dynamics, in its own five-person leadership structure making civilizational decisions without democratic input.

Nine years later, Chiang’s observation has aged into something close to prophecy. The United States spent more money building AI infrastructure last year than building single-family homes. It built ten times as many data centers as the next largest builder. It invested more than twenty times as much in AI as the world’s next largest investor. The five people running the five companies at the centre of this investment — Sam, Dario, Elon, Mark, and Demis — are all already billionaires or close to it, on trajectories that will expand their wealth and influence exponentially as, around them, anti-elitism multiplies.

Science fiction writer Ted Chiang, whose 2017 essay reframed the paperclip maximizer not as rogue AI, but as “no-holds-barred capitalism” — an optimization process without democratic constraint. His observation has aged into prophecy.

Only ten percent of Americans told the Pew Research Center last year they were more excited than concerned about AI. Fifty percent said they were more concerned than excited. The yawning gap between those numbers is the space into which an entire society is being asked to tumble without consent.

The Faces of American Oligarchy

The new faces of American oligarchy: the small group of billionaires and near-billionaires (Sam, Dario, Elon, Mark, Demis) now steering civilizational decisions on work, war, medicine, and the future itself. Illustration by Khoa Tran. Photos from Getty Images.

David Wallace-Wells, writing in the New York Times, offers the frame that connects the paperclip maximizer to the Molotov cocktail.

The AI companies, he writes, have become “the new faces of American oligarchy” — a fearsome concentration of economic and social power producing a self-compounding pattern of extreme inequality. The list of people in charge is so short that most Americans know them by first name. They are reassuring the public, in the same weeks they are testifying in Oakland and signing Pentagon contracts, about the future of work, the future of war, the futures of medicine and companionship and coding. It is hard, Wallace-Wells notes, to escape the impression that these same people are basically in charge of everything now.

Dean Ball — architect of the Trump administration’s original AI policy, speaking at a conference staged by the Palantir Foundation at Yale — offered what may be the most chilling description of where this leads. AI, he said, is “this giant acid vat” that will dissolve the mediating institutions most Americans see as “society.” His prediction: “It will not be AI in government. It’s going to be AI as governments.”

The only income bracket with hopeful views of AI for their day-to-day lives, according to a recent Quinnipiac poll, was those making over $200,000 per year. The people most likely to benefit from AI are the people already benefiting from the economy it is accelerating. The people most likely to be displaced — whose jobs involve the cognitive tasks AI performs at near-zero cost — are the people least represented in the rooms where its governance is decided.

This is not an abstract concern about inequality. It is a description of a specific mechanism by which the governance gap becomes self-perpetuating: the entities that have the most to gain from ungoverned AI development are the same entities that now hold the knowledge, the infrastructure, and the political access that governance would require. This argument was made in Part 27 through Brynjolfsson and Hitzig’s analysis of Hayek. It is being confirmed, in real time, by polling data, by the Oakland trial, and by the Meta employee who typed “How do we opt out?” and was told by the company’s CTO: “There is no option to opt-out on your corporate laptop.”

Inside the Vat

Meta's internal AI transition, as reported by the New York Times, is the most detailed available portrait of what Dean Ball's acid vat looks like from the inside.

Mark Zuckerberg has staked the future of his company on AI. To fund the bet, Meta is tracking the keystroke, mouse movement, and screen activity of its 78,000 employees — without an opt-out — to train its AI models on “how smart people use computers to accomplish tasks.” It has introduced internal dashboards measuring employees’ consumption of AI “tokens,” creating competitive pressure around AI use that has produced, according to internal accounts, people making agents to find agents, and agents to rate agents.

It laid off 8,000 people on May 20.

Some employees have shared nihilistic memes. One read: “It do not matter.” Three internal websites are counting down to the layoffs, one with the header “Big Beautiful Layoff” — a reference to Trump’s domestic policy bill.

The CTO who told employees there was no option to opt-out of keystroke tracking is Andrew Bosworth. He received more than 100 angry and surprised emojis in response and replied: “This data is very tightly controlled. This will not be a leak risk.” The employees who are being asked to train their own replacements while their data is captured without consent are, in the Tyrangiel framing examined in Part 35, the people the counterculture is trying to protect. They are also the people the governance failure has left most exposed.

“There is no playbook for AI in the workplace yet,” said Leo Boussioux, a professor of information systems at the University of Washington. That sentence — spoken about one of the largest technology companies in the world, which has been working on AI for years — is the Asimov Gap applied to corporate governance.

The Numbers That Changed

The polling shift is the story most underreported in AI coverage, and Wallace-Wells has the sharpest data.

In September 2025, Americans were roughly ambivalent about data center construction in their communities — voters were two points more likely to support new construction than oppose it. By February 2026 — four months later — they were 24 points more likely to oppose it. That is a 26-point swing in public opinion in four months on a single infrastructure question.

Northern Virginia is ground zero. Between 2023 and 2025, voters there swung 69 points against data center construction — from 45 points in favor to 24 points against. This is in Loudoun County, where data centers are expected to generate nearly half of all local tax revenue in 2027: $1.3 billion of the $2.9 billion the county expects to collect. The communities most economically dependent on AI infrastructure are the communities most opposed to building more of it.

Data Center Alley, Loudoun County, Virginia

The Quinnipiac finding — only the $200,000-and-above bracket has hopeful views of AI — maps directly onto the Brynjolfsson finding from Part 27: AI disproportionately displaces the cognitive tasks performed by college-educated workers in the middle of the income distribution. The people most exposed to displacement are the people least likely to benefit from the investment. The people most likely to benefit are the people in the courtroom in Oakland arguing about a founding promise.

Paul Tudor Jones — the billionaire hedge fund manager who told CNBC in the same week as the trial that the US was “late already” on AI regulation — described a conference of AI experts and model makers at which 80 percent of participants supported regulation, up from 20 percent the previous year. The leader of one of those companies told him he was surprised the industry wasn’t regulated yet. The industry that spent years arguing that regulation would kill innovation is now, by a four-to-one margin, asking for it.

The Lieu bill is still about deepfakes.

The Race That Has No Finish Line

Wallace-Wells offers a reframing of the AI race that is analytically important for where this series is heading.

The race as typically described — between leading companies, between companies and regulators, between American and Chinese industry — presumes a finish line. A point past which progress proceeds so rapidly that any advantage is extended over time. AGI. Artificial superintelligence. Recursive self-improvement. Anthropic’s Jack Clark predicted this week that fully independent recursive self-improvement might be less than two years away.

But less than two years ago, Bay Area venture capitalists were already asking each other whether they could “feel the AGI.” The finish line keeps moving.

The political scientist Jeffrey Ding calls the actual dynamic a “diffusion marathon” — the speed and shape at which new AI models spread out into the world beyond the lab, finding users and uses, hitting human bottlenecks and real-world obstacles. In this view, the state-of-the-art achievements of world-class models matter less than who is using AI and for what. Six months from now, there will be an open-source version of Mythos — perhaps not quite as capable but much cheaper to produce — that many more users around the world will have access to and be able to customize to their own purposes.

This is what AI people mean when they describe AI as a general-purpose technology, like steam engines or electricity or the internet. The inventors and entrepreneurs who developed those technologies made enormous fortunes and disrupted much of the world they inherited. But none of them retained absolute control over those technologies for very long, let alone over the long-run future they unleashed.

The robber barons are still with us, in the sense that we live in their shadows. But we are not their serfs.

The question Wallace-Wells leaves open — and that this series has been building toward — is whether AI will be different. Whether the combination of speed, concentration, and capability that distinguishes this technology from steam and electricity will allow the five people running the five companies to retain a degree of control that the railroad barons never achieved.

The answer depends on governance. Not the governance that currently exists — which is a nineteenth century doctrine applied by one judge in an Oakland courthouse — but the governance that does not yet exist. The laws that Parts 1 through 35 have been arguing are necessary and Parts 36 through whatever will continue to argue are possible.

AI Populism as Democratic Signal

Sam Altman told The Atlantic in 2023 that “jobs are definitely going to go away, full stop.” Earlier this month he wrote “jobs doomerism is likely long-term wrong.” The venture-capital firm Andreessen Horowitz published an essay the same week declaring the job apocalypse a “baseless fantasy.” The industry has coordinated a messaging pivot. Whether the pivot reflects new evidence or new polling is not specified. The people receiving the messaging live in a country where, as Stanford law professor Nathaniel Persily put it, “they’re not convinced they’re going to be the winners, and they base that conclusion on the history of technology over the last 20 years.”

The Molotov cocktail and the gunfire at Altman’s home are not a governance proposal. They are not even, in most meaningful senses, a political movement. They are data — evidence that the distance between the people making the decisions and the people living with the consequences has become legible to at least some of the people on the wrong side of it.

The data center protests are a governance proposal, in the sense that people are showing up at county commission meetings and funding recall campaigns and demanding that the extraction of their water, their power grid, and their community character be subject to democratic approval. The Northern Virginia 69-point swing is a governance proposal, in the sense that it is a democratic signal that has not been received by any legislature in a form that produces enforceable rules. Maine passed the country’s first statewide data center moratorium in May 2026 — subsequently vetoed by the governor, but the legislative vote is a first. A record number of proposed data center projects were canceled nationally in the first quarter of this year, as Heatmap reported. A guide called “How to Stop a Data Center,” written by a Michigan group, has circulated widely; demonstrating outside local officials’ homes, it notes, has been an effective organizing tactic. After someone shot 13 rounds at an Indianapolis councilman’s house and left a note reading “NO DATA CENTERS,” the council introduced a measure allowing officials to keep their addresses private. The researcher Yannick Veilleux-Lepage, who studies technology and terrorism, wrote in April 2026 that AI “generates the structural conditions historically associated with the onset of political violence.” Local officials who approved data center construction have become, he wrote, “proxies for the same structural anger” as national figures who are unreachable.

The 55 percent of Americans who see AI as a force for harm, and the 50 percent who are more concerned than excited, are a governance proposal, in the sense that democracies are supposed to translate the preferences of majorities into policy. That translation has not occurred. The industry’s lobbying apparatus — Greg Brockman’s super PAC, the Palantir Foundation conference, the revolving door between AI companies and the Pentagon — has ensured that the translation mechanism is jammed.

This is not new. It is the oldest story in American political economy. Concentrated interests defeating diffuse ones. The people who benefit most from a policy paying for the political infrastructure to maintain it. The robber barons said regulation would destroy industry. The tobacco companies said regulation would destroy the economy. The AI companies are using the same playbook, as Bernie Sanders noted at Georgetown in November 2025, with the same confidence that democratic response will arrive too late.

The difference, as this series has been arguing since Part 1, is speed. The railroad barons had decades before democratic governance caught up with them. The AI industry may not. The technology is moving faster than any previous general-purpose technology. The governance gap is closing faster than any previous governance gap — not because the institutions are moving faster, but because the consequences are becoming visible faster. The Molotov cocktail, the 69-point swing, the 80 percent of AI insiders asking for regulation, the former OpenAI board member calling from the witness stand for stronger government oversight — all of this is happening within three years of ChatGPT’s launch.

The window is not closed. The Asimov Gap is not a verdict. It is a description of where we are, not where we are going.

The Asimov Frame

Isaac Asimov, whose robot stories explored ordinary people’s resistance to machines that changed the conditions of their existence without their consent. The Asimov Gap asks whether today’s resistance has arrived in time.

Asimov’s robots were not resisted by philosophers or regulators. They were resisted by ordinary people who encountered them in ordinary life — people who found that the machines, despite the Three Laws, were changing the conditions of their existence in ways they had not consented to and could not control.

The farmer in Wolcott, Indiana — examined in Part 32 — who sued to halt a data center 300 yards from his farmhouse and funded a campaign to unseat the three county officials who approved it, would have recognized Asimov’s resistant humans immediately. So would the Northern Virginia residents who swung 69 points against the infrastructure that generates nearly half their county’s tax revenue. So would the Meta employee who asked “How do we opt out?” and received the most honest answer the AI era has produced: there is no option.

The AI populism that is here — and that no one was ready for — is not coherent enough to be a movement. It is not organized enough to be a party. It is not unified enough to be a coalition. It is, at this moment, a collection of grievances finding each other: farmers and musicians and pastors and safety researchers and board members and hedge fund managers and Meta employees counting down to their own layoffs on websites they built themselves.

Asimov’s stories always asked not whether the resistance was right, but whether it was in time.

The question remains open. The trial has ended. The gap continues.
