There is a moment in most careers when a person finally explains what the job is. Todd Blanche’s came on Fox News Sunday, on November 8, 2026.

Five days earlier, voters had handed both chambers of Congress to the Democrats. Representative Robert Garcia of California, in line to chair the House Oversight Committee, had told the administration to “lawyer up.” The attorney general of the United States had a reply. “If that’s what this new incoming leadership wants to do, we are ready,” he said, as Rainier Harris and Tony Czuczka reported for Bloomberg. “And if you work in this administration, you have a lawyer. It’s me, and we will defend you.”

The same morning, on CNN, he addressed the other side. “I would be very worried if I was a member on that committee,” he said, according to Andrew Pantazi of Axios.

So the arrangement is now on the record. To the people about to be investigated, Mr. Blanche offers counsel. To the people about to do the investigating, he offers a warning. It has been a long time since the two halves of a job description fit so neatly into one Sunday morning.

I. The Order

It began, as these things now do, with a post.

On Saturday, November 7, President Trump accused Democrats on Truth Social of threatening administration officials and Republican donors with investigations and impeachment. “This is extortion at the highest level, and they should be held accountable for this,” he wrote. He singled out Senator Elissa Slotkin of Michigan. “The imprisonment should be against these EXTORTIONISTS.” And then came the sentence that would once have been a scandal in itself: “By copy of this statement, I am ordering the Department of Justice to look into this criminal act, immediately!”

Readers will notice the form. In September 2025, Mr. Trump addressed a public post to “Pam,” demanding action against his enemies. In October 2026, as Part XI recorded, he said of the Alaska certification suit: “I’m the one that said do it.” Now the instruction arrives as a press release with a legal flourish, “by copy of this statement,” as if the Attorney General were a clerk being copied on a memo. After a half-century in which presidents kept up at least the pretence of distance, this one has found the most efficient channel available. He tells the department what to investigate in front of everybody.

What crime? Nobody has said. Federal extortion, under the Hobbs Act, generally concerns obtaining someone’s property through force, fear or the abuse of official position. Promising to hold a hearing does not obviously obtain anyone’s property. Asked on CNN whether his department would investigate Democratic lawmakers, Mr. Blanche did not identify a statute, Axios reported, nor did he confirm that an investigation had begun. Pressed on whether this could be a crime, he said, as Newsweek reported: “It depends on what’s said. So yes, it could be.” The department’s statement was a model of the genre: “We don’t comment on the existence or non-existence of investigations.”

That is how the thing is done. No investigation need exist. It is enough that no one can be sure one doesn’t.

II. Ruin Their Lives

Mr. Blanche’s grievance deserves to be quoted in full, because it is the most remarkable sentence he has spoken in office.

The president, he told Fox, is “angry” about “these outright threats to come after people, to investigate people, to ruin their lives, to lawyer up.”

Consider who is saying this. This series and its sister series, The Enemies List, have spent more than a year documenting exactly that process. Federal agents searched the home of Representative Ilhan Omar on October 12. A Washington grand jury refused on October 27 to indict Cassidy Hutchinson, whose offence was testifying. James Comey has now been charged three times. In the final days before the midterms, the department sent about 20 target or preservation letters to Democrats and about 100 subpoenas in the Alaska matter, as The New York Times reported.

As for “lawyer up,” this department did not merely suggest it to Americans. It made it compulsory. Rachel Maddow, in the excerpt discussed in Part XII, concedes that Trump’s Justice Department has managed to “cost them money in legal fees,” even where it failed to convict. Lawyering up is what has happened to nearly every name on the list.

Mr. Blanche also told Fox that Democratic leaders “are not allowed to just go around threatening existing members of the administration, to go around threatening companies.” One wonders what he imagines a target letter is.

And Senator Slotkin, of all people. In February, a federal grand jury in Washington rejected the department’s attempt to indict her and five other Democratic lawmakers over a video telling service members they could refuse unlawful orders. Ordinary citizens have already told this department once that it had no case against her. The president’s new post is, in effect, a motion to reconsider.

III. What Is Fair in It

There is a defence of Mr. Blanche, and it should be put as strongly as it can be.

The Justice Department does represent federal officials. It is routine. Mr. Blanche has said there is “nothing unique” about saying so, as The Washington Sun reported, and on the narrow point he is right. Administrations of both parties have resisted congressional subpoenas, and they have used department lawyers to do it. In 2024, when House Republicans held Merrick Garland in contempt, the Biden Justice Department declined to prosecute its own attorney general.

Nor is every word from the other side a model of constitutional sobriety. “LAWYER UP,” posted in capitals on X, is a taunt, and a congressional committee that begins with its verdict will deserve the scepticism it gets. Democrats ran on corruption, and they are entitled to investigate it. They are not entitled to treat the hearing room as a victory lap, and some of their own new members are saying so. Senator-elect Adam Hamilton of Kansas told the Sun he would be “deeply disappointed” if Congress focused chiefly on the administration.

Mr. Blanche also said the words a defender would want him to say. “I welcome oversight,” he told CNN’s Dana Bash. “Oversight is exactly something they should be doing.” And he added: “You have free speech in this country. You are allowed to be critical of President Trump, of me, of this administration.” He then explained that “at some point you have to draw the line.”

All of this is a fair account of where the line usually sits. The trouble is the sentence he put after it. “I would be very worried if I was a member on that committee” is not a statement about legal representation. There is no version of a lawyer’s duty to a client that includes telling the opposing party to be frightened of him personally.

IV. Who Is the Client?

The real question is the one Mr. Blanche’s own words raise. “It’s me.” Who, exactly, is he representing?

Federal regulations answer this. Under 28 C.F.R. § 50.15, department representation of a federal employee is available when the conduct reasonably appears to have been within the scope of employment, and when the attorney general determines that representing the person is in the interest of the United States. Those are the two tests. Note who gets to apply the second one.

Now look at what Democrats have said they will investigate. According to Bloomberg, the potential targets include the Trump family’s stock trading and cryptocurrency ventures, private companies’ funding of the White House ballroom, immigration enforcement, and the handling of the Epstein files. Immigration policy is plainly official business. A family’s crypto income is rather less so. Mr. Trump told 60 Minutes, “I devote almost no time to my net worth,” and “I have a lot of cash.” Mr. Blanche told Fox that the attempt to investigate Mr. Trump and his family “did not work then, and it won’t work now.”

He has some history on this question. At an earlier Senate hearing, when Senator John Kennedy asked whether he and the president were friends, Mr. Blanche answered, “I’m his lawyer,” before correcting himself: “Was his lawyer — and now I’m the deputy attorney general,” as The Daily Beast recounted. The correction, it now appears, was the error.

Representative Garcia put it simply on CNN. “All he wants to do is protect Donald Trump, is protect Donald Trump’s Cabinet and administration officials. That is not his job, I want to remind him.” Marc Elias, the election lawyer and himself a named target of the department’s Alaska letters, made the same point on his live stream that evening. An attorney general who reads about corruption in the president’s circle, he said, might be expected to open investigations, “not become their de facto criminal defense lawyers.”

V. Defence Counsel and Prosecutor

Here is what makes the Sunday declaration more than bluster.

When a witness defies a congressional subpoena, the House can vote to hold that person in contempt, and the statute sends the matter to the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia. Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro both went to prison because a Justice Department agreed to carry out that step. The next one will not.

So consider the position Mr. Blanche has now described. A subpoena goes to Stephen Miller, the new White House chief of staff, who, in the words of Mediaite‘s headline, “already has a lawyer.” Mr. Miller’s lawyer advises him. If he refuses to appear, the House refers him for prosecution, to a department run by Mr. Miller’s lawyer. Who, in the same interview, has told the committee members making the referral that they should be very worried, and who may be looking into them on the president’s written order.

Congress has other tools. It can go to court, as it did with Donald McGahn, and wait about two years. It has the power of the purse. It has inherent contempt, a power that has not been used in nearly a century. And it can impeach, which, as the Sun reported, is exactly what Mr. Blanche is complaining about. “They’re talking about impeachments,” he said, in the tone of a man reporting a gas leak.

Impeachment is in Article I of the Constitution. The Speech or Debate Clause is there too, protecting what members do in the course of their legislative work. It does not cover everything a member says on television, which may be why Mr. Blanche’s phrase was so careful: it depends on what’s said. Still, an administration that has lost an election is now suggesting that the winners’ announced plans for oversight may be criminal. In the phrase Axios used, that “threatens a constitutional crisis over the separation of powers.”

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

We have had our own fight about whether a government can refuse Parliament. In 2021, when the House of Commons ordered the Public Health Agency to hand over documents about the Winnipeg laboratory, the Attorney General of Canada went to Federal Court against the Speaker. Parliament summoned the agency’s president to the bar of the House to be admonished, the first time in more than a century that a non-member had received that treatment. It was an unedifying spectacle on all sides.

But the Attorney General of Canada did not go on television to advise the MPs on the committee to be very worried. The order to prosecute did not arrive by social media. And at no point did anyone suggest that a member promising to ask hard questions had committed extortion.

In August, Mr. Blanche declined to pledge that the department would act independently of the White House. In the autumn he said that he works for President Trump. Now he has given the rest of the address. If you work in the administration, you have a lawyer, and you know his name. If you are the Congress of the United States, you have been told to be careful.

It would be useful if someone could tell the American people who, in all of this, is representing them.
