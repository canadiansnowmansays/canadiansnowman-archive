by Stephen Gordon

I. Fourteen Weeks of Nothing, Apparently

The last instalment of this series appeared in mid-June. It dealt with the Situation Room, the room where the United States once planned the killing of Osama bin Laden, being turned into a crisis centre for managing the president’s old friendship with a convicted sex offender.

Since then, if you have been following the American networks, you could be forgiven for thinking the Epstein story simply ended. There have been wars and tariffs. There has been a state visit from Xi Jinping. There has been a fight over which news organisations are allowed through the White House gates. Jeffrey Epstein has drifted to the bottom of the rundown, somewhere below the weather.

I will confess that I let him drift too. That was a mistake, and this essay is the correction.

Because the Epstein story did not go quiet between June 16 and September 25, 2026. It changed rooms. It left the briefing room and the House floor, the places with cameras, and moved into places where the cameras rarely go: a federal courtroom, a committee vote, a docket entry filed at dinner time, the unanswered inbox of European prosecutors.

Readers of this series know the framework I keep returning to: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. For most of 2025, we watched the first three stages play out in public. Trump threatened, Congress pushed, and in November 2025 he capitulated and signed the Epstein Files Transparency Act after the House passed it 427 to 1.

What we have been watching since June is the fourth stage. Obfuscation is the quietest of the four. It does not need a podium, and it prefers that nobody be standing at one.

So let us take a tour of the quiet rooms.

II. Room One: The Briefing Room

On September 18, 2026, Donald Trump announced he was banning three news organisations (CNN, MS NOW and Politico) from the White House. The next day, their reporters were turned away at the gate.

As CNN reported on September 20, ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox News and NBC responded with a rare joint statement and declined to send a replacement crew to fill CNN’s pool slot, which left the television pool not covering the president at all. Asked about it, Trump briefly seemed unsure which of his bans was meant before settling on “the ban on the free press,” as Klaus Marre recounted for WhoWhatWhy. Marre proposed that the remaining reporters ask about nothing but Epstein until the ban was lifted. Nobody did.

On September 24, U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly granted a temporary restraining order returning the three outlets to the pool, writing that the ban was most likely unconstitutional, as Jason Weisberger noted for Boing Boing.

The ban may be on hold, but notice what the week it lasted accomplished. The press corps spent it covering its own access instead of the president’s record. The White House did not need to win that fight. It only needed to start it.

III. Room Two: The House Floor

The second quiet room is the one where Epstein accountability was actually won the first time.

In July 2026, Representatives Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna introduced the Epstein Files Transparency Act II. The original law ordered the release. The sequel is about enforcement: it would give victims, members of Congress and state attorneys general the standing to sue the Justice Department when it fails to comply, and it would require unredacted records to be provided to victims who ask for them, as the Deseret News explained on September 21.

The bill stalled in committee, as bills the Speaker does not like tend to do. So Massie reached for the same tool he used last year, the discharge petition, which forces a floor vote once 218 members sign it.

On the House floor at the start of September, as Erin Kelly reported for Spectrum News, Massie said the government was still withholding more than three million files a year after the president signed the first law. By September 3, the petition had 106 signatures. By September 17, Massie and Khanna announced commitments for all 218. Four Republicans had joined every Democrat but two: Massie himself, Ralph Norman and Nancy Mace of South Carolina, and Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania. The two missing Democrats were home sick and have pledged to sign on the first day back, according to the Washington Sun.

The first day back is the whole problem.

Speaker Mike Johnson had already sent the House home. He cut the legislative session short by a day, shortly after Massie filed a privileged resolution to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth that would otherwise have forced a vote that week. The House is now in its pre-election recess. It is not scheduled to return until after the midterm elections on November 3, 2026.

Consider what that means. The Epstein story was not pushed off the House floor by some other headline. It was scheduled off.

Every member of the House will now face voters without having cast a recorded vote on whether the Justice Department should be made to obey a law that passed 427 to 1. The vote will come in the lame-duck session, when defeated members have nothing to lose and re-elected members have two years to let voters forget.

There is a small consolation. MS NOW’s Maddow Blog has counted eight successful discharge petitions in the current Congress; before Johnson became Speaker, only two in this entire century had reached 218. A Speaker who has to send the House home to avoid a vote is not in control of his House. He is in control of the calendar, which is a smaller thing.

IV. Room Three: The Mailbox

The third quiet room is not in Washington at all. When the files came out, police and prosecutors in Europe began following leads the American Justice Department would not. To pursue them, they need American cooperation. As Maegan Vazquez reported from Berlin for the Washington Post on September 9, some of those agencies say that a year after the first release, the Justice Department still has not responded to their requests for help. Not a refusal, which would be a story. Silence, which is not.

V. The Room That Stayed Open

Which brings us to the one room the administration could not empty: the courtroom of Senior U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan in Washington. Sullivan is a Reagan appointee. I mention it only to save a certain kind of commentator the trouble of calling him a partisan.

The case was brought by Katie Phang, a lawyer and independent journalist, who sued the Justice Department for violating the Epstein Files Transparency Act. Three moments tell the story.

The concession. On June 25, 2026, Sullivan granted Phang a preliminary injunction ordering redactions removed from key records, as The Hill reported that day. In that ruling he noted that the Attorney General had conceded he was in violation of the Act. The Justice Department’s position was not that it was obeying the law. It was that disobeying it was reasonable.

The defence. Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward justified the redactions by arguing that victims’ messages, read without context, “can appear disturbing on their face,” and that the department had protected victims’ identities “even in instances where the victims eventually became complicit or engaged in reprehensible activity,” as Mira Bhakta reported for The Hill on September 17. I leave readers to decide how comfortable they are with a Justice Department that, asked why it is hiding records about the abuse of girls, starts describing some of those girls as complicit.

The deadline. On September 16, Sullivan warned Todd Blanche, who has run the Justice Department since Pam Bondi’s firing in April, that civil contempt was premature but not off the table, as Axios reported. Among other things, he ordered Blanche to deliver handwritten FBI notes to the court for private review by 11 a.m. on September 24. Those notes record interviews conducted in 2019 with a woman who told the FBI that Trump and Epstein abused her when she was a teenager, as Lisa Rubin and Julianne McShane reported for MS NOW. Trump has denied any wrongdoing. The order did not make the notes public. It asked only that a federal judge be allowed to read them.

The department filed a notice of appeal the next night.

On the deadline itself, the only public filing from the department was a routine Answer at 7:50 p.m. that did not mention the notes. The next day, September 25, the department said it had complied after all. According to MeidasTouch, a Justice Department lawyer wrote in an email that Blanche had delivered the unredacted notes to the judge under seal, along with material supporting three disputed victim redactions. Nothing on the public record lets anyone check that. As Katie Phang pointed out, the public cannot tell whether Blanche supported one of those redactions, all three, or none. Sullivan had also refused, on September 22, to pause a separate order to review foreign-language records. The department then asked the D.C. Circuit, which has temporarily frozen that order while it considers a longer stay.

This is what obfuscation looks like in a courtroom. Not defiance, which is dramatic and reportable, but a procedural shuffle designed to make the next hearing boring. Boring is the strategy.

VI. The Contempt That Lands on the Wrong Desk

There was one moment of genuine bipartisan unity in these weeks, and it deserves its place in the record.

Leon Black, co-founder of Apollo Global Management, paid Epstein $158 million between 2012 and 2017, according to a review Apollo itself commissioned, which described the payments as fees for tax and estate advice. He sat for a voluntary interview with the House Oversight Committee in June and walked out before it ended. Subpoenaed to return under oath and to hand over any nondisclosure agreements, he instead sued the committee on September 3.

On September 15, the Oversight Committee voted 41 to 0 to recommend contempt. The next day, as NPR reported, the full House held him in contempt by unanimous consent, the first time it has done so to any witness in the Epstein inquiry. Black denies any wrongdoing or knowledge of Epstein’s crimes.

A unanimous House. Republicans and Democrats. In an election season. On Epstein. For about an afternoon, it was news.

Now follow the referral. A contempt of Congress citation goes to the U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia, who decides whether to prosecute. That office works for the Justice Department. The Justice Department is run by Todd Blanche. Todd Blanche is the man a federal judge has spent the summer warning about contempt over the Epstein law.

The House has handed its Epstein contempt case to the one official in Washington with the most personal reason to let it sit in a drawer. I do not say he will. I say the arrangement would have made Stephen Leacock put down his pen and pour himself a drink.

VII. The Scorecard

  • Files still withheld, by Massie’s count: more than three million

  • House votes on the Transparency Act II before the midterms: zero

  • European requests for help answered, according to the agencies making them: none reported

  • Epstein contempt referrals sent to the official facing Epstein contempt himself: one

VIII. A Canadian Observation

I am told, often, that Americans have moved on from Epstein. That voters care about grocery prices and gas, and not about a dead financier and his address book.

The evidence says otherwise. A YouGov poll reported by Newsweek, taken between February 27 and March 2, 2026, found only 26 per cent of Americans approved of Trump’s handling of the Epstein investigations, while 57 per cent disapproved. Sixty-eight per cent believed the government was covering up what it knows. The public has not moved on. The coverage has.

That is why the quiet rooms matter with the midterms weeks away. Silence is not the absence of a story. It is a decision someone made. The recess was a decision. The press ban was a decision. The unanswered letters from Europe are a decision. The appeal filed the night after a judge’s order was a decision.

From up here, what strikes me most is how much effort the silence takes. It took an early recess, a banned network, a protective appeal and a docket full of paperwork to produce fourteen weeks in which, apparently, nothing happened.

The networks may have gone quiet. That is their decision. Mine is to keep writing it down, one quiet room at a time, until November 3, and after.

Because the women at the centre of this story were told to stay quiet too. And a great many powerful men found that arrangement very convenient.
