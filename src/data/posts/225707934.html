This ledger opened, back in Part I, with a hedge fund called Situational Awareness: an A.I.-focused fund whose giant leveraged bet on A.I. stocks blew up over the summer and was saved when Kenneth Griffin’s Citadel bought much of its portfolio. At the time the story was the collapse. On September 30, 2026, Matthew Goldstein and Rob Copeland reported in The New York Times on what came after. The fund survived, the banks mostly got their money back, and the question has moved from what happened to one fund to what is building up across the whole market.

Details that might have given a lender pause

The Times begins with the due diligence that apparently never happened. When Situational Awareness began trading in the summer of 2024, its founder, Leopold Aschenbrenner, was 24 and had never so much as interned at an investment firm. A German-born Columbia graduate, he was known mainly for having been fired by OpenAI, reportedly for sharing company secrets, and for the essay he published two months later, the one that gave the fund its name, predicting A.I.’s inevitable rise and musing about raising money to profit from it.

The firm had eight employees. Four of them managed the investments. There was no team dedicated to investment risk; the chief compliance officer split his time between his apartment in Reno, Nevada, and the San Francisco office. He was replaced in June, a month before the losses began.

None of this stopped Goldman Sachs, Bank of America and other large banks from lending the fund tens of billions of dollars. On some of the riskiest trades it paid a larger-than-normal spread over base rates, which made it a lucrative client. Encouraged, the fund asked several lenders to raise its leverage from four times its investors’ capital to ten times, according to a banker involved.

It was a good year to ask. Prime brokerage, the business of lending to hedge funds, drove the banks’ record profits in the spring and summer of 2026; at Goldman, equity-financing revenue rose as much as 91 per cent in the second quarter. The banks were not reluctant lenders. They were competing to lend.

The summer, and the rescue

When some of the fund’s positions fell sharply over the summer, the banks did what lenders to leveraged investors always do: they asked for their money back. To raise it, Situational Awareness sold about $20 billion of stock to Citadel at a discount. The market dropped quickly, then recovered.

“To some degree, the system worked,” the Times concludes, and that is fair. The banks’ losses appear to have been minimal because a rival fund with deep pockets stepped in. But this ledger has seen that pattern before, and the history is not reassuring. In 1998, Long-Term Capital Management, a fund run by some of the most celebrated minds in finance, borrowed so heavily that its failure threatened the banks that had lent to it; the rescue was organised by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The rescue worked then too. What followed was not more caution but a decade of rising leverage that ended in 2008. A rescue that works mainly teaches the market that rescues arrive.

Tyler Gellasch of the Healthy Markets Association put the lesson in one sentence: “A confident genius with a lot of leeway from his investors, and leverage from his counterparties, can be one of the most dangerous things in finance.”

$3.7 trillion

The single fund is not the point. The point is the number behind it. As of mid-2026, according to the Treasury Department, hedge funds had borrowed nearly $3.7 trillion from banks, the highest in more than a decade and more than triple the figure at the start of the pandemic in early 2020. Some of that finances short positions, bets that prices will fall. Most of it finances bets that stocks will keep rising.

That borrowing is part of why they have risen. By the end of September 2026, the S&P 500 had set 27 record closes for the year and gained 158 per cent, including dividends, since 2020. Leverage is an accelerator in both directions: it enlarges gains on the way up and forces sales on the way down, and in a market where many large investors are borrowing to hold the same kind of stocks, one fund’s forced selling can push prices down far enough to trigger the next fund’s margin call.

Frank Smets, head of economic analysis at the Bank for International Settlements, the central bankers’ bank, cited the fund’s near-failure in mid-September as another reminder of the risks. Reuters reported on September 14, 2026, as the BIS published its quarterly review, that he had named the specific worry: “What we are most concerned on in this (AI) area is the rapid increase in debt and leverage.” By the BIS’s count, technology firms’ borrowing from private-credit lenders had grown from $22 billion, or 22 per cent of that market, in 2010 to more than $1 trillion, or 44 per cent, by 2025. Part X of this ledger traced how the hyperscalers’ bond issuance was pushing up borrowing costs for everyone. Part XXIII, drawing on Bethany McLean, asked whether A.I. had become too big to stop. Situational Awareness is the mechanism by which a correction in A.I. stocks would spread: not just through the companies building data centres, but through the leveraged funds that own their shares and the banks that lent the funds the money.

Looking away

The S.E.C. opened an investigation within weeks of the sell-off and subpoenaed Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, Citi and JPMorgan Chase about the trades they financed. The banks and the commission have declined to comment, and an investigation is not an accusation. JPMorgan has all but cut the fund off. Morgan Stanley’s talks about lending it billions more broke down for a while and, as of the Times’s report, had resumed, and other banks were still lending to it.

Nor had the fund stopped trading. Quartz reported on September 11, 2026 that in late August and early September it had bought options on Advanced Micro Devices, Bloom Energy, CoreWeave, SK Hynix and SanDisk, and on a memory-chip exchange-traded fund: the same chips-and-power thesis, now expressed through instruments that carry their own built-in leverage. Before the summer, Quartz noted, the fund had used as much as 400 per cent leverage and had posted returns of more than 1,000 per cent from its launch in July 2024 to the end of June.

At the same time, and this is the detail that should worry people most, the administration is moving to reduce what regulators can see. After 2008, hedge funds were required to report their overall borrowing to securities regulators, precisely so that someone could measure the kind of risk that Situational Awareness exposed. On April 20, 2026, the S.E.C. and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission jointly proposed easing some of those reporting rules. The proposal would raise the threshold for filing Form PF, the confidential report private funds make to regulators, from $150 million in private-fund assets to $1 billion, and raise the threshold for the more detailed reporting required of large hedge-fund advisers from $1.5 billion to $10 billion. The agencies said about half of current filers would no longer have to file, while coverage of more than 90 per cent of private-fund gross assets would be kept. “Prior amendments to Form PF have led to overly burdensome disclosure requirements for advisers, distracting them from their core investment functions,” said Paul Atkins, the S.E.C. chairman. Then, on August 31, 2026, a month after the fund’s fire sale, the two agencies postponed again, from October 1, 2026 to July 1, 2027, the compliance date for the expanded Form PF reporting they had adopted in February 2024, to spare filers “certain potentially significant costs” while the April proposal was considered. Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democrat on the Banking Committee, said the change “would make these already shadowy markets even more opaque, creating blind spots for financial regulators that could harm their ability to respond after major market events.”

The Asimov Gap has documented the same reflex applied to the technology itself: a one-page voluntary pledge in place of oversight, and an industry proposal to choose its own inspectors. Here it is applied to the money. The response to a near-miss is to make the next near-miss harder to see.

Back for more

The fund did not stay chastened for long. Its quarterly filing with the S.E.C., made public on November 16 and showing its holdings at the end of September, revealed that it had moved from a broad range of A.I. shares into mostly call options, contracts that rise in value if a stock goes up and carry leverage of their own, Samuel O’Brient reported for Business Insider on November 17. Its biggest position, 28 per cent of the portfolio, was SharonAI, an Australian company that rents out A.I. computing power; Bloom Energy, Nebius, SanDisk and a memory-chip fund made up most of the rest. The bets were placed before A.I. and technology shares fell from their early-October highs. By the time the filing became public, SharonAI’s shares were down 54 per cent from October 1.

Aschenbrenner has also begun talking about borrowing again. On November 18, the Financial Times reported that he had approached investors and prime brokers about a relaunch, informally called Situational Awareness 2.0, that would use leverage and could take outside money as early as 2027, as Startup Fortune summarised. After July, the fund had told its investors it would stop borrowing to amplify its bets. JPMorgan ended its lending relationship in September, and the fund has held talks with Jefferies about a new prime-brokerage account. No relaunch has been announced.

A stake in the collaborator

The fund’s wealthy investors, who had about $10 billion committed at the time of the Times’s report, appeared to be staying. A.I. stocks rebounded after the summer, even as, the Times notes, “a flurry of A.I. executives called for regulation to slow down advances in the technology.” And Situational Awareness kept its stake in Anthropic, which the Financial Times valued at about $5 billion before the listing. On November 19, Anthropic went public at $83 a share, valuing the company at more than $1.5 trillion, as The New York Times reported; this ledger examined the offering as it was being prepared in Part XVIII.

Readers of this newsletter know why that matters here. These essays are written in open collaboration with Anthropic’s Claude. Anthropic’s listing is the event that could turn the fund’s summer into a triumph, and the fund’s history is a reminder of how much of the A.I. boom is financed on borrowed money by people who believe, as Aschenbrenner’s essay did, that the outcome is inevitable. A company whose chief executive has called for an industry slowdown has become the best asset of a fund built on the premise that nothing will slow down.

By the Times’s count at the end of September, Situational Awareness had 25 employees, three of them working on compliance. It has, in its own terms, learned its lesson. Gellasch doubts the rest of Wall Street has learned the same one. “Compliance officers and regulators will likely spend years asking how the risks grew so quickly,” he said.

The answer, when they find it, will probably be the one it always is. Everyone could see the leverage. It was profitable, the thesis was fashionable, and the person asking for more was described as a genius. A fund named Situational Awareness is a fine joke for a satirist. For a regulator trying to see less, it is also a warning.
