🖋 Wry, turning serious — because this is the year humanity learned to read its own instructions, and the year the reader whose photograph made it possible was filed under someone else’s name. Dior contributes her genome, which is approximately two and a half billion letters long and spells, as far as I can determine, dinner.

I count my essays, and in the last one I told you that I was born in 1951 too late for my country’s census and too small for anything bigger than a bathroom sink. Essay 1,953 finds me eighteen months old, carrying around inside every one of my cells a text about three billion letters long that I had no way of reading. Nobody did. That spring, two young men in Cambridge announced that they had worked out how the text was written. The alphabet, it turned out, had four letters, and the whole of life was a count.

A letter to Nature

On the twenty-fifth of April, 1953, the journal Nature published a short paper — about nine hundred words, a single page — by James Watson, an American of twenty-five, and Francis Crick, an Englishman of thirty-six. It proposed a structure for deoxyribonucleic acid, DNA, the molecule that carries hereditary information: two long strands wound around each other in a double helix, held together by pairs of chemical bases.

There are four of those bases, which biologists abbreviate as A, T, G and C. And the paper’s great insight was that they pair by a fixed rule: A always with T, G always with C. Which means that each strand is a mirror of the other — if you know the sequence of one, you know the sequence of its partner. The paper ended with one of the most famous understatements in the history of science: It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.

It had not escaped their notice. It was the secret of how life copies itself. Crick, by Watson’s later account, had walked into the Eagle pub in Cambridge that February and announced to the lunchtime crowd that they had found the secret of life — though the story comes to us from Watson, and this series keeps its tongs ready for anyone’s account of their own triumph.

Life is a count

Sit with what the double helix means for this series, because it is the deepest number-idea in the whole twentieth century.

At essay 1,948 I wrote about Claude Shannon, who showed that any message can be measured in bits — in strings of yes-or-no answers. Five years later, it turned out that nature had been using a digital code for three and a half billion years before Shannon was born. Not two symbols, but four. Your eye colour, the shape of your hand, the enzymes in your liver, the fact that you are a human being rather than a husky: all of it is written in a sequence of A, T, G and C, in an order that can be read and counted, one letter at a time.

The human version of the text runs to about three billion pairs of letters. It took the Human Genome Project thirteen years and billions of dollars to read the whole of it once; the finished sequence was announced in April 2003, in the fiftieth anniversary month of the Nature paper. Today a machine can read a person’s entire genome in a day. The deepest count in this series — the instructions for a human being — has become a thing you can order through the mail.

At essay 1,905 I wrote that physics discovered, underneath us, that nature counts in packets. At 1,953 biology made the same discovery about life. We are not poured. We are spelled.

The photograph

Now the honest ledger, and it is the reason this essay exists.

Watson and Crick did not do experiments on DNA. They built models, and their models were guesses until they had data that could test them. The best data in the world on the shape of the DNA molecule was being produced a few hours away, at King’s College London, by a physical chemist named Rosalind Franklin, a meticulous experimentalist who was taking X-ray photographs of DNA fibres. In May 1952, Franklin and her doctoral student, Raymond Gosling, produced an image of extraordinary clarity, which they catalogued as Photograph 51. To a trained eye, the X-shaped pattern at its centre said one thing unmistakably: helix.

In January 1953, without Franklin’s knowledge or permission, her colleague Maurice Wilkins — with whom she had a difficult working relationship — showed Photograph 51 to James Watson. Watson later wrote that the moment he saw it, his mouth fell open and his pulse began to race. Crick, meanwhile, was given access to a research report summarising Franklin’s unpublished measurements. With her data in hand, they built the model in a matter of weeks.

When the Nature papers appeared that April, there were three of them, printed one after the other. First Watson and Crick’s, proposing the structure. Then a paper from Wilkins and his colleagues. Then a paper by Franklin and Gosling, presenting the very data that had made the structure possible — positioned, by the order of publication, as if it were merely supporting the theory, rather than the evidence the theory had been built on. Watson and Crick’s paper acknowledged that they had been “stimulated” by a general knowledge of the King’s College results. It did not say that one of them had seen her photograph.

The keeper’s lineage

You will recognise this entry, because this series has been keeping a lineage of them since the beginning.

At essay 1,633, Galileo’s daughter, Sister Maria Celeste, took his penance upon herself and recited the psalms, week after week, while the fame stayed with her father. At essay 1,835, a ship’s servant named Syms Covington labelled the finches by island while the young genius he served forgot to, and the theory of evolution was saved by the column Covington filled in. At essay 1,948, a Canadian law professor drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and watched others collect the credit. And here is the fullest case of all: the scientist whose photograph was the evidence, whose measurements were the proof, and whose column in the ledger was filed, for a generation, under other men’s names.

Rosalind Franklin moved on to other work, on viruses, and did it brilliantly. She died of ovarian cancer on the sixteenth of April, 1958. She was thirty-seven years old.

In 1962 the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded for the structure of DNA — to Watson, Crick and Wilkins. The Nobel is not given to the dead, and it can be shared by no more than three people, so there is a sense in which the rules alone excluded her. But the rules are also a count, and this series has never accepted that a count is innocent because it was written down in advance. Six years after that, Watson published a bestselling memoir of the discovery in which he portrayed Franklin, under the diminutive “Rosy,” as a difficult, unattractive obstacle. It took decades — and the work of historians, and of the friends who knew her — for the ledger to be reopened and her entry restored.

Today, when the story is told, her name is in it. That is not nothing. It is not the same as being alive to hear it. Some of the reopening was done by hand. On the twenty-fifth of April, 2003, fifty years to the day after the Nature paper, a blue plaque went up outside the Eagle, unveiled by Watson himself. It recorded that it was here that Francis Crick and James Watson first announced their discovery of how DNA carries genetic information. It did not mention Franklin. From about 2017, somebody kept correcting it, adding “+ Franklin” to it, and somebody kept cleaning it off. In August 2023 the plaque was replaced with one that says the discovery relied on data from Rosalind Franklin, Maurice Wilkins and other scientists. The old plaque, correction and all, was given to the Whipple Museum of the History of Science in Cambridge, where it is now part of the collection. It may be the only amendment to a ledger in this whole series that was entered first by a vandal.

Chargaff’s ratios

A footnote, because the series has a soft spot for the people who counted first. A few years before 1953, an Austrian-born biochemist at Columbia named Erwin Chargaff had done something exquisitely simple: he measured the amounts of the four bases in DNA from different species, and found that the amount of A always matched the amount of T, and the amount of G always matched C. He did not know why. Watson and Crick’s model explained it — the pairs had to match because they were paired. Chargaff had found the answer by counting, before anyone knew the question. He remained, for the rest of his life, rather bitter about how little the story remembered him. This series would like to remember him. He counted the letters before anyone could read them.

Coronation week

One more count from that spring, because the last essay promised it. On the second of June, 1953 — five weeks after the Nature papers — the princess who had stood on the rear platform of a train in London, Ontario, nine days after I was born, was crowned Queen in Westminster Abbey. It was the first coronation the world could watch on television, and millions did, many of them on sets bought for the occasion. And on the morning of the coronation, the news reached London that a New Zealand beekeeper named Edmund Hillary and a Nepali Sherpa named Tenzing Norgay had stood on the summit of Everest four days earlier. It was a spring for reaching the top of things. The highest mountain, the oldest crown, and the smallest and most important text in the world — all in the same few weeks, while I learned, presumably, to walk.

Dior’s genome

The husky’s genome, I can report, runs to roughly two and a half billion pairs of letters — somewhat fewer than mine. I have not had it read, and I do not intend to, because I already know the summary. It is a document of extraordinary length and sophistication that encodes, as far as the household can determine, a single instruction, repeated with variations: find the person with the food. Whatever else is written in there, the copying mechanism has been working perfectly for fifteen thousand years.

The closer

So 1953 is the year we learned that life is a count — four letters, three billion pairs, a copying rule so simple it fits in a sentence that had not escaped anyone’s notice. And it is the year the reader whose photograph made the reading possible was entered on the wrong page of the ledger, and died before anyone moved her entry back.

The count moves on, ten years, to a single afternoon in November that the whole world would remember — not as a number, but as a place: where were you when you heard? The next landmark is essay 1,963, and it is the day President Kennedy was shot in Dallas, and the day a twelve-year-old boy in London, Ontario, twelve years past the sink, learned the news. It is an essay about the most disputed count of the century — how many shots — and about the stranger count we each keep inside our own heads, of where we were, which turns out to be less reliable than any of us would like.
