In the summer of 1787, fifty-five men gathered in Philadelphia to repair a broken government and ended up inventing something that had never quite existed before.

They did not set out to do this. The Constitutional Convention had been called to fix the Articles of Confederation, which had proven so inadequate to the task of governing a new nation that the country was effectively dissolving — states ignoring federal law, commerce grinding to a halt, veterans of the Revolution going unpaid, the whole rickety enterprise threatening to collapse into thirteen quarrelling republics or, worse, back into the orbit of European power.

What they produced instead was a document that staked a nation’s existence not on territory, not on ethnicity, not on shared religion or common ancestry, but on a set of propositions. Propositions that they could not fully live up to. Propositions that contradicted, in their application, almost everything they claimed in their declaration. Propositions that would require two and a half centuries of argument, amendment, war, and unfinished struggle to even partially redeem.

And yet: propositions. An idea. A creed.

The political philosopher’s term for what America became is a creedal nation — a country that defines membership not by who you are but by what you believe. You do not have to be born American. You have to subscribe. You have to accept, or at least profess acceptance of, a set of founding truths: that all are created equal, that rights are inalienable, that government derives its authority from the consent of the governed. Subscribe, and you belong. Refuse, and you are outside — regardless of where you were born.

This was, by any historical measure, a genuinely radical idea. It had almost no precedent. And it carried within it, from the very first moment, both the seed of America’s extraordinary strength and the mechanism of its potential unravelling.

A small note for readers keeping the medieval essay count with me: this is Substack essay 1,276 — five hundred years, on the nose, before 1776. I did not plan it, but I am happy to take the coincidence as an omen. It is worth knowing, too, that the actual year 1276 was itself a small case study in institutional collapse: Rome went through four popes in twelve months, the conclave rules broke down so completely that the cardinals had to rebuild the system from scratch. An institution unable to agree on who spoke for it, five centuries to the year before a nation staked its whole existence on an idea. I don’t want to overwork the symmetry. But I don’t want to ignore it either.

I. The Audacious Bet

To understand what was radical about the creedal nation, it helps to understand what came before — and what surrounded it.

Every other significant nation-state in the world at the time of the American founding was built on something other than an idea. England was England because of the English — a shared history, a shared church, a shared monarchy, a shared sense of being English that was prior to any political arrangement and did not require any document to sustain it. France was France because of the French. The German states were German because of the Germans, long before Germany existed as a political unit. These were ethnic or cultural nations — what political scientists call ethnic nationalism — and their identity did not depend on agreement about anything so abstract as a proposition. You were English or you weren’t. The question was biological, not philosophical.

America said: we will try something different. We will be a nation of believers rather than a nation of ancestors. Anyone, from any corner of the earth, can come and subscribe to the creed and become one of us.

President Reagan, who understood the rhetorical power of this idea better than most, made the point repeatedly. “Anyone, from any corner of the Earth, can come to live in America and become an American,” he said. The Irishman who told President John Quincy Adams that he planned soon “to become a native” — a joke Adams reportedly loved — was expressing something that would have been nearly incomprehensible in any other country of the era. In England, you could live for fifty years and remain Irish. In America, the creed said you could arrive this year and belong next year, if you chose to.

The phrase "the American idea" is often used as though it were self-evident, ambient, always available. It has an origin, and the origin has a date and a controversy attached to it. It was given its first systematic definition in 1850, by a Unitarian minister named Theodore Parker, in a speech to an abolitionist convention. Parker did not treat the phrase as a compliment. He treated it as a demand. Quoting the Declaration's claim that all men are created equal, he insisted that the only political arrangement consistent with that claim was democracy — a government of all the people, by all the people, for all the people. Lincoln would tighten Parker's sentence into three matched phrases thirteen years later, at Gettysburg, without crediting the minister who wrote it first. It's a small theft, but a revealing one: the phrase America uses to describe its own government was minted, in the first instance, as an argument against slavery, As David Frum and Jeffrey Goldberg discussed on The Atlantic's David Frum Show, the phrase had genuine "bite" in Parker's mouth — foreign policy included, since the same speech condemned the Mexican-American War and the planned annexation of Cuba. The American idea, in its first systematic articulation, was not a celebration of what the country already was. It was an indictment of what it had not yet become.

Theodore Parker, 1850: The man who first systematically defined ‘the American idea’ — not as a celebration, but as a radical demand that the creed actually apply to everyone.

The historian Johann Neem, who came to the United States from Mumbai at the age of three and grew up eating masala dosas and Thanksgiving turkey in equal measure, describes this as “pretty astounding.” He means it as a genuine compliment, not an ironic one. The claim that someone from South Asia could arrive in America and become American — fully, legitimately, without asterisk — was, and remains, a claim unlike any other nation has made with comparable consistency. Most countries make the claim in theory and revoke it in practice. America has made the claim in practice often enough, and with sufficient institutional backing, that it became a genuine part of the national identity rather than merely a recruitment slogan.

This is the audacious bet at the heart of the American experiment. Not that the creed would be perfectly honored — the Founders knew, or should have known, that it wouldn’t be. But that the creed would be real enough, and the commitment to it durable enough, that each generation would face pressure to extend it further: to the next excluded group, the next community told the proposition didn’t apply to them, the next Americans who had to fight to be recognized as what they already were.

The room where the proposition was born: Fifty-five delegates in Philadelphia, summer 1787, tasked with fixing a failing confederation but instead inventing a creedal nation.

And here is where the proposition stopped being words on a page.

On October 17, 1781, at Yorktown, George Washington dictated and signed a short note to Charles Cornwallis, the British lieutenant general commanding the southern campaign. It is not a stirring document. It is the prose of a man arranging the end of a war — terse, procedural, businesslike. But in it Washington wrote of his “ardent desire to spare the further effusion of blood,” and with it he set in motion the surrender that converted the United States from a claim into a fact. The idea declared in Philadelphia five years earlier had, until that moment, no force in the world. A proposition is not a country. Yorktown is where the proposition acquired an army’s worth of consent and a defeated empire’s signature.

But an army does not raise itself, and the consent Yorktown registered had been building for a decade in places far less famous than Philadelphia. As the historian T.H. Breen has argued in The Atlantic, the conventional shortlist of Founders — Washington, Jefferson, Adams — leaves out the thousands of ordinary Americans whose local committees, some with only a dozen members and no previous political experience, spent the years before 1776 debating, organizing, and gradually talking themselves into revolution. By one estimate, more than ten thousand men who had, in a Massachusetts reverend's words, "not before been honored with the confidence of the people," sat on these committees. When the Continental Congress finally moved toward independence, its own delegates acknowledged they could do so only because, as they put it, "the voice of the people drove us into it." The proposition that would be tested at Yorktown had already been ratified, informally and repeatedly, in towns most of the signers never visited. The audacious bet was not made by five men in a room. It was made by a mass movement that then found five men willing to write it down.

There is a detail here that I cannot resist, because it is precisely the sort of thing that makes a Canadian smile into his coffee. That letter is, as I write this, going on display as NBC News reports for the first time in London — at The National Archives in Kew, as part of a British exhibition titled, with no apparent irony, “Revolution 250: America’s Independence Story.” Cornwallis carried the note home to Essex after the war; it sat in the family papers until 1880 and has belonged to the British state ever since. The curator describes it as the moment “Britain finally accepts the reality of the declaration of independence drafted five years earlier.”

So consider the geometry of it. The document that converted the American idea into the American nation is preserved, lovingly, behind glass, in the capital of the country it defeated. And in the country it created, the idea itself — the creed, the proposition, the thing the document was for — has become, as we saw in Part I, the word that clears a room. The mother country curates the relic. The child can no longer say what it means. There is a lesson in that, and it is not a flattering one: a nation founded on an idea is only ever as durable as its willingness to keep saying the idea out loud.

II. The Promise Made in Bad Faith

The creed was announced, in 1776, by men who did not believe it applied to half the people they could see from their windows.

This is not a radical observation. It was obvious at the time. Abigail Adams told her husband John to “remember the ladies” when constructing the new government, and he wrote back a letter that manages, across nearly 250 years, to remain breathtaking in its complacency. The ladies were not remembered.

Mark Twain, no one's idea of a sentimentalist about his own country, made the point more bluntly than most historians dare to. As Ron Chernow noted in The Atlantic, Twain believed 1865, not 1776, was liberty's true birthdate in America — "for there was slavery before." He proposed, with the particular cruelty of a man who meant every word, that the Declaration should have been honestly rewritten: "All white men are created free and equal." Twain was writing in the late nineteenth century, decades before the creed's promise would be extended, however incompletely, to the people his sarcasm was defending. His edit is not a historical footnote. It is the plain-language version of the argument this section is about to make at length.

Twain's rewrite assumes we agree on what "equal" meant in the first place, and that assumption deserves a second look. As Teresa Bejan has written in The Atlantic, the word carried at least eight distinct definitions in Samuel Johnson's 1755 dictionary, several of which flatly contradict one another when applied to politics — "in just proportion" pulls toward something like what we would now call equity; "upon the same terms" pulls toward strict procedural sameness. The Declaration's "self-evident" truth was never as self-evident as the phrase insists. The word had already been weaponized once before Jefferson borrowed it: by the English Leveller John Lilburne, a distant cousin of Jefferson's, who in 1646 declared that "every particular and individual man and woman that ever breathed" was equal in "power, dignity, authority, and majesty" — a claim radical enough to get him imprisoned by the House of Lords. Jefferson inherited Lilburne's phrase and, by the end of his life, owned hundreds of people to whom he apparently did not consider it to apply, at least two of whom were, in a coincidence no novelist would risk, named Lilburn. The ambiguity was not a bug introduced later by bad-faith interpreters. It was present at the coining. Much of what this series calls the argument between the three schools is, at bottom, an argument about which of Samuel Johnson's eight definitions of equal the creed was actually promising.

Enslaved people were not merely excluded from the creed — they were constitutionally instrumentalized by it. The Three-Fifths Compromise, which counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for purposes of congressional apportionment, gave the slaveholding states greater political power than they would have had if enslaved people had simply been ignored. The creed said all men are created equal. The Constitution said the political power of the men who owned other men would be amplified by the number of people they owned. Both things were true simultaneously, and no one in Philadelphia pretended otherwise. They simply did not resolve the contradiction.

Indigenous peoples were denied citizenship on their own land. Women were, under the legal doctrine of coverture, merged into the personhood of their husbands at marriage — they ceased, legally, to exist as individuals. The franchise that the young republic extended to white men was, as Appelbaum notes, extraordinarily broad by the standards of the time — more than half of adult white men could vote, while in England only about one in seven could. Within a few decades, perhaps eighty percent of adult white men, and ninety percent in some states, had the right to vote. This was a genuinely unprecedented democracy. It was also a democracy that excluded, by design and by law, the majority of the people living under it.

The history of America is, in very large part, the history of excluded people demanding that the creed mean what it claims to mean — and the resistance those demands have always encountered. The abolitionists. The suffragists. The labor organizers. The civil rights marchers. The LGBT Americans who insisted that inalienable rights did not have an asterisk. Each of these movements made essentially the same argument: you said all. You meant some. We are holding you to the word all.

This is what the mainstream school of American history, the tradition that Neem calls the largest and still most widely held interpretation of the American story, identifies as the central and animating drama of the republic. Not a story of uninterrupted progress — the backlashes have been as real as the advances, and some of the most significant expansions of the creed have been followed by violent retrenchments. But a story of argument: an ongoing, unfinished, frequently bloody argument about whether the proposition is going to mean what it says.

That argument is not a flaw in the American design. It is the design. A nation built on a proposition must argue about the proposition. The argument is the nation.

III. Why Creedal Nations Are Uniquely Fragile

Here is where the audacious bet becomes genuinely dangerous.

An ethnic nation can survive a fractured narrative about its own history. Two German nationalists can disagree violently — about Bismarck, about the Kaiser, about the meaning of 1945 — and still be, unambiguously, German. Their Germanness does not depend on their agreeing about German history. It predates the history, in a sense. It is prior to any particular telling of the story. Blood and soil do not require consensus.

A creedal nation cannot do this. As Appelbaum argues in The Atlantic, a nation bound together by a shared understanding of its own story begins to come apart when the story comes apart. The creed requires a narrative. The narrative requires enough shared agreement about what the story means — what the proposition has demanded, what it has produced, where it has been honored and where betrayed — to function as a common reference point. Without that shared reference point, the creed becomes a collection of words that different groups interpret in incompatible ways, and the words stop doing the work of holding anyone together.

This is precisely what has happened. The three schools Neem identifies — mainstream, post-American, hyper-American — do not merely disagree about emphasis. They disagree about whether the story is worth telling at all, and if so, to whom it belongs. The post-American school says the story is a white supremacist story and should be decentred. The hyper-American school says the story is a heritage American story and should be protected from those who would complicate it. Neither school produces the kind of shared narrative that a creedal nation needs to reproduce itself across generations.

What fills the vacuum is not neutrality. It is competing mythologies, each more interested in the culture war than in the country. And a country that loses its shared narrative does not simply become neutral — it becomes available to whoever tells the most compelling story next, regardless of whether that story is true.

Thomas Chatterton Williams, writing in the same Atlantic issue, identifies the logical endpoint of this process in the white identitarian movement — people like Jeremy Carl, who argue that America is not a creedal nation at all but a “particular place” with a “particular people.” Vice President Vance made the same argument explicitly last year, scolding a mayoral candidate for saying he was “proud of our country even as we constantly strive to make it better.” In Vance’s telling, that kind of conditional pride — the mainstream school’s patriotism, love earned through honest reckoning — represents insufficient gratitude. America, in his view, is not an idea to be subscribed to. It is a heritage to be inherited.

This is not a new argument. It is, in fact, the argument the creedal nation was invented to supersede. The Founders — imperfect, compromised, frequently dishonest about the gap between their stated principles and their practiced lives — chose, nonetheless, to define their new republic by a proposition rather than an ancestry. They did this because they knew, or some of them knew, that a nation defined by ancestry had a ceiling. It could only ever be as large as its founding ethnic group. A nation defined by an idea could, in principle, grow without limit. Subscribe, and you belong.

Vance’s “particular people” argument is a retreat from that founding choice — a decision to trade the infinite expandability of the creedal model for the false security of an ethnic one. It is also, as the evidence of 250 years makes clear, a strategic catastrophe. Every group America has eventually included — the Irish Catholics, the Chinese railroad workers, the Jewish refugees, the Black Americans who fought for a country that denied them its promise — has made America stronger in ways that exclusion never has. This is the argument of The American Contradiction, a companion series published here, and the evidence for it is overwhelming.

IV. The Immigrant Test

There is a reliable test for the health of a creedal nation, and it is simpler than any political science metric.

Watch how it treats the people who chose it.

Naturalized citizens — people who were not born American but chose to become American — tend, as a group, to be more patriotic than native-born Americans. They tend to be more trusting of American institutions. They tend to be more likely to believe that the world would be better off if people elsewhere resembled Americans. This makes sense: they subscribed. They made a deliberate choice. They know, from lived comparison, what the alternative looks like.

Neem, who came from Mumbai at three, describes the claim of American citizenship as something that has become harder to hold as the national story narrows. “I’ve seen a change on both ends that makes it harder for someone like me to be an American,” he told Appelbaum. The post-American left no longer believes that immigrants should assimilate into a national culture tainted by white supremacy. The hyper-American right views immigrants’ very presence as a threat to the national culture it seeks to protect. Between them, the people most invested in the creedal bargain — the people who chose it — are finding the bargain increasingly contested.

The ultimate test of the creedal nation: Those who chose it. Naturalized citizens often display the fiercest attachment to the proposition — because they subscribed deliberately.

This is the tell. A creedal nation that cannot extend its creed to the people who subscribed to it is not a creedal nation having an argument about its proposition. It is a creedal nation losing faith in its own premise.

The woman who wrote to Jill Lepore after reading These Truths — who had bought the book to study for her citizenship exam and kept reading, enthralled, and wrote to say she finally felt she belonged “because I understand the whole journey that is the story of this land and this people and these ideas and this country” — that woman understood the creedal bargain intuitively. She understood that subscribing to America required knowing the story. Not the flattering version. The whole journey. The massacres and the movements. The promise and the betrayal and the long, unfinished work of making them match.

She passed the test the commission room failed.

V. What the Bet Still Requires

Two hundred and fifty years in, the audacious bet is under more pressure than it has been since the Civil War tested whether a nation conceived in liberty and dedicated to a proposition could endure.

It is not obvious that it can. The institutional erosion is real and documented. The narrative collapse is real and, I would argue, at least as dangerous. A country that cannot tell its own story to its own children — that has allowed the transmission of the creed to be captured by football coaches and culture warriors on both flanks — is a country that is failing the most basic requirement of a creedal nation: reproduction. Not biological reproduction. Ideological reproduction. The passing of the proposition from one generation to the next with enough fidelity and enough honesty to sustain the argument.

The argument is, remember, the point. The creedal nation does not require agreement. It requires a shared commitment to arguing about what the proposition means — and a shared understanding that the argument has stakes, that it matters, that it is about something real.

What it cannot survive is the abandonment of the argument itself. The retreat into competing mythologies that do not engage each other. The post-American refusal to tell the story because the story is too painful. The hyper-American refusal to tell it honestly because honesty is too threatening. The institutional cowardice that pulled history out of the curriculum because no one could agree on what to test.

The Founders made a bet. It was imperfectly executed from day one, in ways that cost millions of people their freedom, their land, their lives. But the bet itself — that a nation could be built on an idea, that the idea could be transmitted across generations, that anyone who subscribed could belong — was one of the genuinely radical things human beings have attempted in the long history of organizing themselves into polities.

At 250, the question is not whether the bet was worth making. It was. The question is whether enough Americans still understand what was bet — and what it costs to lose.

That is what this series is about.
