In the last days of the campaign, the phones of left-leaning Alaskans lit up with text messages. They urged a vote for Eric Hafner, the only Democrat among the four candidates for Alaska’s one seat in the House of Representatives. Mr. Hafner, the texts said, supported Medicare for All and the Green New Deal, and would fight back against Donald Trump and his enablers. The independent in the race, Bill Hill, was, they said, a closet conservative.

The texts left a few things out. As Anna Griffin reported for The New York Times on 14 November 2026, Mr. Hafner is serving a 20-year federal sentence at the prison in Otisville, New York, about 4,351 miles from Anchorage. He has never been to Alaska. The Constitution bars him from serving even if he wins, because a member of the House must live in the state that elects him. The candidate the Democratic Party was actually backing was Mr. Hill. And the texts carried no information about who had sent them.

Mr. Hafner took nearly 8 per cent of the vote.

Part XI of this series, “Heads We Win, Tails Don’t Count,” described how Todd Blanche’s Justice Department went to federal court to freeze the result of Alaska’s Senate race, but only if the Republican lost, over a second Dan Sullivan whom Democratic operatives had helped onto the ballot. The court threw the case out. The department kept investigating. This part is about the other spoiler on the same ballot, and about the department’s complete lack of interest in him.

I. The Prisoner’s Campaign

Mr. Hafner is not a new figure in American elections, just an unusual one. In 2016, “the Rev. Dr. Eric Hafner” finished second in the Republican primary for Hawaii’s Second Congressional District. In 2018 he ran as a Democrat for a House seat in Oregon, another state he had never lived in. Between 2016 and 2018, according to court records cited by the Times, he threatened to kill lawyers, police officers and judges in Monmouth County, New Jersey. In 2022 he pleaded guilty to two counts of making interstate threats and one of making false bomb threats, and received 20 years.

In 2024 he paid the $100 fee to run for Alaska’s House seat from his cell. He won fewer than 500 votes in the primary, and would have gone no further, except that several Republicans who finished ahead of him dropped out to help Nick Begich, the Republican challenger. That moved Mr. Hafner into the general election against the Democratic incumbent, Mary Peltola. Alaska Democrats sued to keep him off the ballot, worried that voters would be confused. Republicans fought to keep him on, and won. He drew 3,417 votes, about one per cent, not enough to decide Ms. Peltola’s defeat.

This year he filed again, on 10 May, on another handwritten form mailed from prison. Independent groups, fearing confusion, spent nearly $500,000 urging Alaskans not to rank him; their advertisements said the D beside his name stood for “doing time.” Somebody else spent an unknown amount on the opposite message. No outside group has reported a cent of spending in his support.

With the count still going on, Mr. Begich has 47.8 per cent and Mr. Hill 41.7. Eric Croft, the chairman of the Alaska Democratic Party, does not believe a man in a New York prison won 8 per cent in Alaska on his own. “That becomes a dirty trick that worked,” he said. Carol Gray, a political scientist at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, offered a simpler explanation: most of those votes probably came from Alaskans angry at Republicans and willing to back any Democrat on the ballot. Both explanations can be true at once. A ballot designed to catch angry Democrats, and texts designed to steer them, would work best together.

II. The Family Business

Mr. Begich has denied any involvement. The only clear Republican fingerprints, the Times found, are on the campaigns of the candidate’s mother.

Carol Ann Hafner ran as a Democrat this year in Alaska’s nonpartisan Senate primary, the same primary in which Senator Dan S. Sullivan was seeking a third term. She was backed by $346,000 in advertising from Fair Deal Alaska, a super PAC funded almost entirely by committees tied to Senate Republicans and to Senator Sullivan himself. She won less than one per cent and did not advance. She says that no Republican recruited her or her son, denies any connection to the advertising bought on her behalf, and describes the politics of both as “progressive through and through.”

The paperwork tells a more complicated story. Mother and son both use the same South Dakota mail-forwarding service as their campaign address. When Mr. Hafner filed, in his own hand, for a Florida congressional special election last year, he listed ancillary committees called “MAGA Republicans Victory Fund,” “Secure the Border Florida” and “America First Florida Always.”

Ms. Hafner, for her part, does not much care who paid for her son’s texts. “If something lands in your lap, you’re not going to say ‘no thanks,’” she said. She also sees a way around the constitutional problem with his candidacy. “Somebody needs to tell President Trump that Eric has earned a pardon.”

III. The Other Investigation

Now set this beside what the Justice Department has been doing about the other spoiler.

The Democratic scheme deserves its full weight, and this series gave it that in Part XI. As Kellen Browning reported for The New York Times on 28 October, aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and an Ohio operative travelling under an assumed name ran what they called the ballot composition project. They searched Alaska’s voter rolls for men named Dan Sullivan, found a retired teacher in Petersburg, and cheered in a group chat when the courts kept him on the ballot. It was cynical, and it was organised by people who ought to have known better.

The department’s response has been thorough. Grand jury subpoenas were issued on primary day in August. Dozens more followed on 29 October. In the last days before the election, the department sent about 20 target or preservation letters to Democrats, including the elections lawyer Marc Elias, and about 100 subpoenas in the Alaska matter, as The New York Times reported in its election-day coverage. On election morning, Mr. Trump said Mr. Elias should go to jail. Before the polls closed, Mr. Blanche and the homeland security secretary, Markwayne Mullin, had both promised to investigate the election itself. Two days later, the president ordered the department to investigate Ms. Peltola’s campaign.

On the Hafner texts, there is no public sign of any interest at all. The Times notes that sending them without saying who paid may have broken federal election law, depending on how many went out and how they were sent. Federal law requires political committees, and anyone spending money independently to support a candidate, to report that spending and to identify themselves on many kinds of communication. Mass texting is a grey area, and it is quite possible that whoever sent these messages stayed on the right side of the line. But that is exactly the kind of question a grand jury exists to answer. In August, the department did not wait to find out whether the Democrats’ operation had broken a law before issuing subpoenas. It started with subpoenas.

Nor has anyone in Washington asked about Fair Deal Alaska. Part XI argued that recruiting spoiler candidates is ugly but generally legal, and that the line which matters is money and coordination. It pointed to Florida, where a former state senator was convicted in 2024 of campaign finance violations for paying a decoy candidate to run. By that test, Alaska’s Republicans have at least as much to explain as its Democrats, and their money is already on the public record: $346,000 from a super PAC funded by committees tied to the incumbent senator, spent to promote a “Democrat” in his own primary. Nobody has suggested that this was illegal. The point is that nobody in Mr. Blanche’s department appears to have asked.

IV. The President’s Rule

The president, without meaning to, has supplied the clearest test of the department’s even-handedness.

On 5 November, two days after the vote, Mr. Trump posted that the votes cast for the other Dan Sullivan, together with those for a fourth candidate, “should be immediately given to the REAL Dan Sullivan, and the Election is over.” An analysis by The New York Times found that most of the other Sullivan’s voters who named a second choice had picked Ms. Peltola, not the senator. The president’s rule would have reassigned their votes to the man they chose not to support.

Apply the president’s rule to the House race, though, and something awkward happens. If the votes of a spoiler belong to the major candidate he was put there to hurt, then Mr. Hafner’s nearly 8 per cent belongs to Mr. Hill. Added to his 41.7, that would put Mr. Hill at about 49.7 per cent on the first count, ahead of Mr. Begich at 47.8. Nobody in the administration has proposed it. Nobody will.

Alaska, of course, already has a system for handling spoilers, and it is the right one. Under ranked-choice voting, the last-placed candidate is eliminated and each ballot moves to the voter’s next choice. If a Hafner voter ranked Mr. Hill second, Mr. Hill gets the vote. If the voter ranked no one else, the ballot is exhausted. Preliminary data suggest that only about 45 per cent of Mr. Hafner’s votes will transfer to Mr. Hill, and that nearly half will be exhausted. Mr. Begich is likely to win. That is what the voters chose, and it should stand. It is also the logic behind Chief Judge Sharon Gleason’s ruling when she threw out the department’s Senate suit on 1 November: there is no way to investigate a voter’s intent without breaking the secret ballot, so the ballot the voter marked is the only record of what that voter meant.

V. The Count Goes On

Meanwhile, the Senate count kept moving in one direction. By 13 November Ms. Peltola had 49.8 per cent and a lead of nearly four points, and an analysis by The New York Times found her on track to win once ranked choices were counted. In the end she didn’t need them. On the evening of 17 November, with 209,003 ballots counted, she crossed 50 per cent of first-choice votes, as James Brooks reported for the Alaska Beacon, and the Associated Press called the race. Her final margin was more than 12,000 votes, well over the roughly 4,700 that went to the other Dan Sullivan, as Kellen Browning and Emily Schwing reported for The New York Times. The ranked-choice tabulation the state had scheduled for the next day was never required. Part XV looks at what that majority does to the Justice Department’s case.

If Ms. Peltola wins, she will be the first Alaska Native in the Senate. She will also arrive under a cloud that the Justice Department spent three months building: the subpoenas, the target letters, the failed lawsuit, the president’s order. None of it changes how Alaskans voted. All of it is designed to make some of them doubt the result.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

Canada has its own form of ballot stacking, and it is more theatrical than Alaska’s. In the federal election of 28 April 2025, the ballot in Pierre Poilievre’s Ottawa-area riding of Carleton listed 91 candidates, most of them recruited by the Longest Ballot Committee, a group protesting the electoral system. Mr. Poilievre lost the riding. When he ran again in a by-election in Battle River–Crowfoot that August, the ballot listed more than 200 names, and Elections Canada had to design a special ballot on which voters wrote in their choice.

People were furious, and there has been plenty of argument since about tightening the rules for getting onto a ballot. What nobody proposed was to investigate the voters, or to ask the RCMP to determine which of the 91 names each elector in Carleton had really meant to mark. The argument has been about the rules, which would bind every party equally.

That is the standard Alaska deserves. Both parties stacked its ballot this year. As Alaska’s Democrats like to point out, nobody’s hands are clean. But only one party’s hands are being fingerprinted, and the department doing the fingerprinting is run by a man who announced on Fox News Sunday, on 8 November, that if you work in this administration, he is your lawyer.
