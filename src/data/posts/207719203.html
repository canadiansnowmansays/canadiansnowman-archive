I. The Lever That Got Put Down

Readers of this series already know the history, but it’s worth putting the whole sequence in one place, because it’s short and it matters. In March 2025, Ontario imposed a 25 per cent surcharge on electricity exports to Michigan, New York and Minnesota. It lasted one day. It raised roughly $260,000. Trump responded by threatening to double steel and aluminum tariffs, and within twenty-four hours Ford suspended the surcharge as a goodwill gesture — an “olive branch,” in his own framing — after a call from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. It has stayed suspended ever since, “on the table” by Ford’s own repeated description, but never actually back on it.

Sixteen months later, with a 50 per cent tariff on Canadian autos, dairy and alcohol now signed, no USMCA exemption attached, and the White House confirming on background that liquor bans and dairy quotas are exactly what provoked it, I think the case for putting that lever back to work is stronger than it’s been at any point since Ford first picked it up.

II. Why It Worked in a Day

The liquor ban has now run sixteen months. It cost American producers real money — this series has tracked the DISCUS and wine export numbers — but it never produced anything like the reaction the electricity surcharge did in twenty-four hours. That’s not a coincidence; it’s economics. A shopper in Toronto who can’t find Kentucky bourbon can reach for a different bottle. A household in Rochester or Detroit cannot simply choose a different electrical grid on a hot July afternoon. Electricity is the one export Ontario controls that American consumers genuinely cannot substitute away from on short notice, and Washington’s own panic in March — a tariff threat within hours, a phone call the same day — is the clearest evidence anyone’s produced that they know it too.

III. What the Olive Branch Actually Bought

Here’s the part worth sitting with. The whole rationale for standing down in March was that de-escalation might buy calmer waters — that keeping the bigger tool in reserve was wiser than using it, because provoking Washington further wasn’t worth the risk. Sixteen months on, we have the results of that experiment. The provocation happened anyway. Washington didn’t need Ontario to reinstate a surcharge to arrive at a 50 per cent tariff with no USMCA carveout — it got there on its own, over a Section 301 bill named after the country it targets and, quite possibly, over a weekend of anger about wildfire smoke. The goodwill gesture didn’t prevent the worse outcome it was suspended to avoid. It just meant Ontario walked into that outcome having already put its most effective tool down.

IV. The Honest Case Against

I don’t want to pretend there isn’t a real argument on the other side, because there is. Reinstating the surcharge risks feeding exactly the narrative Greer has already used against Canada — the “only two countries that retaliated” framing this series flagged back in Part XIII — and hands Washington a fresh talking point about Canadian escalation just as the USMCA review process limps forward. There’s also a fair question about whether a province should be the one wielding a tool this blunt while Ottawa is nominally the one negotiating the broader file, and whether Ontario utilities and grid operators want the operational uncertainty of an on-again, off-again export surcharge. These aren’t trivial objections, and I’d rather name them than pretend the case is one-sided.

V. Why It Tips Anyway

But the objection that mattered most in March — that restraint might spare Canada something worse — has already failed on its own terms. Whatever Ontario was trying to avoid by standing down happened regardless. That doesn’t leave much of a reason left to keep the lever holstered, and it leaves an obvious one to pick it back up: it is, provably, the single measure that has ever made Washington move fast. Everything else in this trade war has been absorbed, endured, or slow-walked through committee. The electricity surcharge is the one exception on record, and it sat unused through sixteen months of escalation that the olive branch was supposed to prevent.

VI. Whose Decision This Actually Is

Worth noting for anyone assuming this sits with Carney: electricity exports are provincial jurisdiction. This isn’t a federal lever Ottawa has to authorize — it’s Ford’s, sitting exactly where he left it in March. Given his response to today’s tariffs — tariff for tariff, dollar for dollar, no hesitation — reinstating the one measure he already knows works fast would be a fairly small step from where he’s already standing. Whether Carney would welcome that, given his more measured instincts across this whole series, is a separate question, and one this series will be watching for as closely as anything else.
