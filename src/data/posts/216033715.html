At two o’clock this afternoon, the Federal Reserve announced what everyone with a Bloomberg terminal and a functioning inflation forecast already knew was coming: a quarter-point increase, lifting the federal funds rate to a range of 3.75 to 4 percent. It is the first rate hike since July 2023. All twelve voting members of the Federal Open Market Committee supported it. Not one dissent, from a chairman who inherited a committee that split three ways against him just two months ago.

Read that unanimity carefully, because it is doing more work in this story than the number itself.

In July, when the Fed held rates steady, three regional bank presidents — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis, Lorie Logan of Dallas — voted against Kevin Warsh, arguing the committee should already be raising. That was a chairman four months into the job losing control of the room on his signature issue. Wednesday’s clean sweep is the opposite signal, and Wall Street understood it as such before the ink was dry: the ten-year Treasury yield, which had crossed 5 percent for the first time since 2007 earlier this week, slipped back to around 4.95 percent in the hours before the announcement. The S&P 500 ticked up. This is the “credibility” the bond strategists had been demanding of Mr. Warsh all month, delivered in a single unanimous vote.

But credibility, in this particular telling, is a trap of Mr. Warsh’s own construction. Go back to Jackson Hole, Wyoming, four weeks ago. Addressing the Fed’s annual policy retreat, the man who built his reputation as a hawkish critic of his predecessor’s caution told the assembled economists he “would be hard pressed to describe broad financial conditions as restrictive,” and warned the Fed would have “work to do” if underlying inflation didn’t ease “clearly and at sufficient speed.” That is not a data-dependent hedge. That is a chairman nailing his colours to the mast in front of an audience that writes down every word. When August’s inflation numbers came in hotter than expected, as they did, he had left himself no room to do anything but hike. Charles Evans, who ran the Chicago Fed for sixteen years, put it plainly in a New York Times interview ahead of the meeting: had Mr. Warsh offered more “framework guidance” — more of the ordinary scenario-sketching his predecessors indulged in — he would have had the flexibility to justify either outcome. Instead he talked himself into a corner and then, this week, walked into it. Jonathan Pingle, a former Fed staffer now at UBS, was blunter still: “a certain amount of credibility is on the line after throwing down the gauntlet.”

So: the gauntlet was his own. Nobody threw it at his feet. He picked it up in Jackson Hole and threw it at himself, and Wednesday was him honouring the challenge.

This is where the series’ running preoccupation with institutional independence gets an unusually clean test case, because the man being tested this time isn’t a cabinet secretary or an inspector general who can simply be fired for the inconvenient finding. Donald Trump handpicked Kevin Warsh for precisely the outcome that did not happen this week. At Davos in January, months before the appointment was formalized, Mr. Trump complained about the pattern he’d seen before, in his first term, with Fed chairs who told him what he wanted to hear and then raised rates the moment they got the job. “They’re saying everything I want to hear,” he groused. “They get the job, and all of a sudden, ‘Let’s raise rates a little bit.’” He said this as a grievance. It is now, four months into the Warsh chairmanship, a prophecy fulfilled by the man he appointed to prevent it.

It’s tempting to read this as the TACO pattern in miniature, except run through an institution that — so far — hasn’t capitulated. Mr. Trump threatened, in a social media post earlier this month, to “stop trading with countries with which we have a deficit” unless the Fed lowered rates — an ultimatum so large in scope, if acted upon, that it would restructure global trade to punish American borrowers for the sin of high interest payments. The White House spent the following days doing what it does best when a threat needs quiet burial: Kevin Hassett, the National Economic Council director, told CNN the administration would “100 percent” respect whatever the Fed decided, then told CNBC the day before the vote that “we’re going to respect the process.” That is the Obfuscate phase arriving before the Accommodate or Capitulate phase ever had to. The threat gets aired, the institution proceeds to do the opposite of what the threat demanded, and the White House retroactively reframes the whole episode as respect for process it never actually promised to honour. Whether Mr. Trump follows through on choking off trade with deficit countries — a threat that would inflict enormous self-harm on the American consumers this series has spent four addenda tracking — remains, as of this writing, an open thread. Readers of Constitutional Whack-a-Mole know how this administration treats threats that go unfulfilled: not as bluffs to be embarrassed by, but as ammunition to be reloaded for the next grievance cycle.

The other pattern worth flagging is Mr. Warsh’s continuing refusal to participate in his own institution’s central forecasting tool. The Fed’s quarterly Summary of Economic Projections — the “dot plot” that maps all nineteen policymakers’ individual rate forecasts — came out Wednesday alongside the decision. Mr. Warsh did not submit a dot. He didn’t in June either. This is not an oversight; it is doctrine. He has called the dot plot a form of forward guidance that ties the Fed’s hands, and has said openly he wants no part of the “fine-tuning business” it represents. The irony, four addenda into this series’ running argument about institutional transparency, should not need much underlining: a chairman appointed in part to restore public trust in an overleveraged, overextended fiscal system has made his signature reform the withholding of the one document that would let outsiders check his math. The other eighteen officials still filed their dots. Most penciled in another quarter-point increase by December — the meeting after next, conveniently scheduled for after the midterms, since October’s gathering falls just days before Americans vote and nobody at the Eccles Building wants to be accused of thumbing the scale. The projections themselves are not comforting reading regardless: inflation is expected to end the year at 3.7 percent, core inflation at 3.4 percent, and the 2 percent target — the number the Fed has chased for half a decade now — is not projected to arrive until 2029.

There is a genuinely strange mechanism buried in all of this, one that Joe Rennison flagged for the Times and that deserves its own moment in this series, because it is the kind of perversity the whole “Thirty Seven Trillion” project exists to notice: by raising the rate it directly controls, the Fed may end up lowering many of the rates ordinary Americans actually pay. The logic runs like this. A hike, delivered credibly and unanimously, tells bond markets the central bank is serious about inflation even when the president appointed the chairman specifically to avoid that outcome. Serious central banks earn lower long-term inflation expectations. Lower long-term inflation expectations mean investors demand less compensation for holding thirty-year paper. Lower demanded compensation means lower long-term yields — the yields that actually set mortgage rates, the ones that matter to a family shopping for a house rather than a hedge fund shopping for basis points. Credibility, in other words, is the only tool left in the box that doesn’t require Scott Bessent to personally buy back government debt, which — readers of the Fourth Addendum will recall — he has already tried, and which has done essentially nothing to bring long yields down. The Treasury secretary spent a chunk of last week defending an unprecedented $5.2 billion self-purchase of Treasury debt maturing in the coming decade, called it his “dream,” said he had “asymmetric information,” declared himself “the house now.” The house, this week, watched the ten-year yield still sitting north of 4.9 percent regardless of how many bonds he personally repurchased. Mr. Warsh, by simply doing his job in the way central bankers have always understood the job, may have accomplished in one unanimous vote what Mr. Bessent’s interventions could not.

Mr. Warsh is also not fighting this battle alone, whatever it feels like from inside the Eccles Building. The Bank of Japan meets Friday and is widely expected to raise its own rate, from 1 to 1.25 percent, continuing a reversal of the ultra-loose Abenomics policy that defined Japanese monetary policy for over a decade. Koichi Hamada, one of the architects of that original strategy, told the Times he has now flipped to recommending “monetary constraint” — a reversal Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s government has not accepted, as she continues to lean on the central bank for low rates while running record fiscal deficits, the same affliction this series has spent five addenda diagnosing at home. Scott Bessent’s own history here is worth a beat: in 2012, as a hedge fund manager at George Soros’s firm, he visited Mr. Hamada at Yale, absorbed the Abenomics playbook, and built one of the most profitable trades of his career shorting the yen against it. He now tells Japanese officials, as he did at the G20 finance meeting in Asheville this month, to abandon the very reflationary approach he once profited from betting on. The European Central Bank, for its part, raised rates last week for the second time since the Iran war began. What Wednesday’s hike actually represents, then, is not an isolated American drama between one stubborn chairman and one furious president — it is the world’s major central banks converging, almost in lockstep, on the same conclusion: that an energy-shocked, debt-heavy global economy no longer has room for the easy money that defined the 2010s. Kevin Warsh just happens to be doing it with the most politically exposed microphone in the building.

None of this resolves anything, which is rather the point of an addendum rather than a conclusion. The bond market’s verdict on Wednesday’s hike will take days, not hours, to fully register. Mr. Trump’s trade threat sits unresolved, a loaded gun left on the table by a president who has shown no particular reluctance to fire loaded guns at his own economy. October’s meeting, deliberately or not, arrives too close to the midterms for the Fed to act without being accused of interference either way. And December looms as the next test of whether Wednesday’s unanimity was a genuine institutional reset or a chairman who simply hadn’t yet run out of room to manoeuvre around his own rhetoric.

What is worth banking, for now, is this: the man Donald Trump appointed to lower interest rates raised them instead, unanimously, at a moment when doing so directly contradicted an explicit and recently repeated presidential demand. Whether that reflects genuine Federal Reserve independence reasserting itself, or simply a chairman who talked himself into a box at Jackson Hole and found honouring his own bluff was politically cheaper than eating it — is a distinction this series intends to keep watching for, addendum by addendum, as the debt clock keeps running well north of the number in its title.
