I. Fidelity

Special Agent Dennis Okoro arrived at work on Tuesday morning at 7:48 a.m., twelve minutes ahead of schedule, because Dennis Okoro had been twelve minutes ahead of schedule every working day of his twenty-three-year career at the Federal Bureau of Investigation.

He badged in. He nodded at Cheryl, who worked the lobby desk. He took the elevator to the fourth floor. He sat down at his desk in the Insider Threat Operations Unit, adjusted his monitor to the angle he preferred — eleven degrees, he had measured — and opened the case file that had been assigned to him the previous Friday.

The subject was a journalist.

Dennis read the file. Then he read it again. Then he set it down, aligned it with the corner of his desk, and stared at the wall for the amount of time it takes a man of considerable professional discipline to decide that he will, in fact, do what he has been told.

The case concerned a reporter at The Atlantic named Sarah Fitzpatrick, who had published a story about the FBI director. The story alleged that the director had been absent from the building for extended periods, had been observed in states of visible intoxication, and had on at least one occasion been unreachable behind a locked door to a degree that prompted his security detail to request breaching equipment. The story cited more than two dozen sources.

Dennis noted — because Dennis was thorough — that the case file did not allege that Fitzpatrick had published classified information. It did not allege that she had stolen government property. It did not allege that she had committed any crime recognizable to Dennis, who had investigated actual insider threats for eleven of his twenty-three years at the bureau, threats that typically involved people who were, in some technical sense, inside.

Sarah Fitzpatrick had never worked for the federal government. She did not hold a security clearance. She was not a contractor. She was, by every metric the Insider Threat Operations Unit was designed to evaluate, an outsider.

Dennis opened a new document. He typed the date. He began his preliminary assessment.

On the corner of his desk, placed there before he arrived by someone whose identity he did not investigate because a man can only investigate so many things at once, sat a bottle of Woodford Reserve bourbon. It was engraved with an FBI badge and the words “Kash Patel FBI Director.” Beneath the name, a small but unmistakable dollar sign replaced the letter S.

Dennis moved the bottle to his filing cabinet. He continued typing.

II. Bravery

The briefing was at ten.

Dennis had been to many briefings in his career — briefings about counterintelligence operations, about domestic terrorism networks, about foreign agents cultivating sources inside defence contractors. He had once sat through a four-hour briefing about a janitor at the Pentagon who had been photographing documents with a camera concealed inside a thermos. That briefing had been, in its way, thrilling.

This briefing was about a woman who had called people on the telephone and written down what they said.

The unit supervisor — Dennis will call him Brian, because Brian is the sort of name that belongs to a man who delivers unreasonable instructions in a reasonable tone — explained the parameters of the investigation. The objective was to identify Fitzpatrick’s sources. Current and former FBI officials who had spoken to her. Congressional staff. Anyone inside the government who had described the director’s conduct to a reporter.

Dennis raised his hand. This was unusual. Dennis was not, by temperament, a hand-raiser.

“Are we alleging that classified information was disclosed?” Dennis asked.

Brian said they were not, at this time, making that allegation.

“Are we alleging that any federal statute was violated by the publication of this article?”

Brian said the investigation would determine what statutes may have been implicated.

“So we are investigating a reporter for publishing a story in order to determine whether the story should have been investigated?”

Brian said that was one way to characterize the matter, and that another way to characterize it was that the Insider Threat Operations Unit had been assigned a case by the director’s office and that the case would be pursued in accordance with bureau protocols.

Dennis asked how the sources would be identified.

Brian explained that personnel with access to the relevant information — current agents, support staff, anyone in the director’s operational orbit — would be subject to polygraph examination. The polygraphs would be administered by the bureau’s own polygraph unit, on the bureau’s own employees, to determine which of them had spoken truthfully to a reporter about their director’s conduct.

Dennis noted — because Dennis noted things — that the bureau was now prepared to deploy the polygraph twice in the same quarter: once to identify federal employees who had described, to a journalist, what they had witnessed their director doing, and once to identify whichever federal employee had taken a bottle of bourbon from a table at Quantico. He did not write down which of these the director appeared to consider the greater institutional betrayal, because Dennis had already learned one lesson today about what not to put in notebooks.

He did write something, though. What he wrote was: They know they are not supposed to do this.

He underlined it. Then he crossed that out too, because Dennis had been twelve minutes early every day for twenty-three years, and a man who is twelve minutes early does not leave evidence of any kind.

On the briefing table, between the coffee urn and a stack of interdepartmental routing forms, sat a case of Woodford Reserve bourbon with FBI insignia. Brian did not reference it. No one referenced it. It sat there the way institutional rot sits in any organization — visible, unmentioned, and gradually accepted as furniture.

III. Integrity

The Quantico visit had been on the schedule for weeks, and Dennis could not, in good conscience, say it was the strangest day of his career, because his career now included investigating a journalist for the crime of journalism, and that had rather recalibrated the scale.

The seminar was a training exercise. UFC athletes — professional fighters — were teaching mixed martial arts techniques to FBI recruits. Dennis did not know whose idea this was. He suspected it was the sort of idea that emerges when an organization’s leadership has time on its hands, perhaps because the leadership is periodically unreachable behind locked doors.

A fighter whose neck was wider than Dennis’s thigh demonstrated a chokehold on a recruit who had, until recently, been studying forensic accounting. The recruit tapped out. The fighter released him. Everyone applauded.

On a table near the mats, next to a tray of protein bars and a sign that read “QUANTICO TACTICAL TRAINING — ATTENDEES ONLY,” sat a row of personalized bourbon bottles. One of them was missing. Dennis learned, through the reliable internal channels that exist in any workplace, that the director had threatened to polygraph his staff over the missing bottle.

Dennis considered this. In the briefing that morning, he had learned that the bureau would polygraph its own employees to determine who had spoken to a journalist about the director’s conduct. Now he was learning that the bureau would also polygraph its own employees to determine who had taken a bottle of bourbon from a training seminar.

Two polygraphs. One to find people who told the truth. One to find a bottle of whiskey.

Dennis did not record which of these investigations the director appeared to consider more urgent. He no longer needed to. The answer was on the table, twelve bottles across, engraved in gold, with a dollar sign where the S should be.

He excused himself. He went to the men’s room. He looked at himself in the mirror for slightly longer than the task required.

Then he went back to his desk and continued typing.

Coda: The Second Story

Dennis went home at 5:47 p.m. — thirteen minutes early, a deviation he attributed to the cumulative effect of the day and not to any erosion of professional standards.

He ate dinner. He watched part of a hockey game. He opened his laptop to check whether anything had developed on the case.

Something had developed on the case.

Sarah Fitzpatrick had published a second story. This one was about the bourbon.

Dennis read it. He read about the personalized bottles. He read about the Ka$h branding. He read about the DOJ aircraft transporting cases of whiskey. He read about the UFC seminar at Quantico. He read about the polygraph threat over the missing bottle. He read about the retired agent advising current employees to “run from him.”

Dennis closed the laptop.

He opened it again.

He opened a new file.

He typed the date.

The director of the FBI was being investigated by his own bureau — in the sense that a reporter who had described the director’s conduct was now the subject of a case file, which meant that the director was, in practice, using the bureau’s investigative apparatus to investigate coverage of himself, which meant the bureau was, in a manner of speaking, investigating itself on behalf of a man who branded bourbon with a dollar sign in his own name.

Dennis typed his preliminary notes. They were thorough. They were procedurally correct. They would withstand any audit.

On his filing cabinet, the bottle of Woodford Reserve caught the light from his desk lamp. Ka$h Patel FBI Director.

Dennis looked at the bottle. He looked at the file. He looked at the article on his screen — the second article, the one about the bourbon, written by the woman he was investigating, published on the same day the investigation became public, which meant she had responded to the threat of federal prosecution by committing another act of journalism.

The FBI’s motto is Fidelity, Bravery, Integrity.

Dennis could not, at this moment, identify which of those words described his own conduct. He had been faithful — but to the wrong directive. He had not been brave. And his integrity was, for the first time in twenty-three years, a matter he preferred not to examine.

One of those words, however, described Fitzpatrick’s conduct with precision.

Dennis knew which one. He suspected Fitzpatrick did too.

He saved the file. He closed the laptop. He went to bed twelve minutes ahead of schedule, because that is what Dennis Okoro did, and if the reasons for doing it no longer made the sense they once had, well.

A man needs something to hold onto when the bottles start showing up everywhere.
