On November 7, 1874, days before that year’s midterm elections, Harper’s Weekly printed a cartoon by Thomas Nast called “The Third-Term Panic.” A donkey dressed in a lion’s skin roams a menagerie, braying about a phantom danger, and the other animals bolt. Among them is a vast, frightened elephant labelled “The Republican Vote,” stampeding toward a pit. It was the first time anyone had drawn the Republican Party as an elephant.

Nast chose the animal for its size, its strength and its dignity. He also drew it panicking, because that was his point: a creature that large has nothing to fear from a braying donkey, and yet it can be scared into trampling everything in its path.

One hundred and fifty-two years later, on the eve of another set of midterms, the elephant is stampeding again. This time it is not running from the scare. It is the one doing the braying.

Elephants are big. They don’t need to be bigots too.

This series began in June 2025 with a simple argument. Every group America has marked as “unassimilable” — Irish Catholics, Chinese labourers, Jewish refugees, Italians, Japanese Americans — eventually became part of the national success story, and every exclusion cost the country something it later had to win back at a premium. In August, Part 18 recorded Indiana’s lieutenant governor, Micah Beckwith, telling Americans they needed “permission to hate again.” Ten weeks later, the president of the United States granted that permission from a rally stage. The Republican Party’s closing message of the 2026 campaign now reads like a revision quiz on the whole syllabus.

The Closing Argument

Adam Serwer laid out the pattern in The Atlantic on November 2, 2026. Facing an unpopular war in Iran, tariff-driven inflation and a corruption record that writes itself, Republicans have reached back for the oldest tool in the shed: fear of the neighbour who prays differently or looks different.

The sharpest example is Michigan. There, Republican Mike Rogers is running against Democrat Abdul El-Sayed for the Senate. Rogers has said, without evidence, that El-Sayed was bought off by the Muslim Brotherhood. Vice-President J. D. Vance accused him of anti-white racial hate. At a rally in Port Huron on October 25, as David A. Graham reported in The Atlantic the next day, President Trump called El-Sayed a “jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member.” Trump said that if El-Sayed won, Christmas itself would be in danger. He added that El-Sayed would be the only senator who had to pass through a metal detector to enter the chamber.

Graham noted the history the president skipped. The only time members of Congress have had to pass through metal detectors in the Capitol was after Trump’s own supporters sacked it on January 6, 2021. El-Sayed has disavowed the Brotherhood. No one has produced evidence tying him to it, or to anything resembling jihad. Graham pointed out that if such evidence existed, a Justice Department that has shown no shyness about political prosecutions would presumably be using it.

The same message was running in other states:

  • Ohio: Senator Bernie Moreno asked a rally whether El-Sayed knew that Jesus came for our salvation, as though Michigan’s Senate seat came with a catechism.

  • Texas: Governor Greg Abbott accused his opponent, Gina Hinojosa, a Catholic, of supporting Sharia law because a Muslim civil-rights group endorsed her.

  • Halloween: Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins posted a mock “SNAP fraudster” costume that Serwer described as a racist stereotype about Black Americans.

  • Kansas: Trump called Democrats the party of hostile Third World migrants.

Trump did not leave the message to implication. At Port Huron he praised Rogers as “apple pie” and “central casting.” As Graham observed, that is not a dog whistle when everyone can hear it.

Who Gets Into the Tent

For forty years, the Republican Party has described itself as a big tent. It is, in fact, a big tent. The question is who gets past the usher.

You get in if your name is Vivek Ramaswamy. You are the Ohio-born son of Indian immigrants and a practising Hindu, running for governor of Ohio. Peter Wehner reported in The Atlantic on November 1, 2026 that your campaign pledged $4 million to air an ad featuring the estranged mother of your opponent, Amy Acton. That mother stayed married to the man indicted in 1978 for raping Acton when she was twelve. Ohio’s Republican governor, Mike DeWine, called the ad deeply troubling. Trump called it hitting pay dirt. Vance said he was disgusted, but by the Republicans complaining about the ad, not by the ad itself. You are still in the tent.

You do not get in if your name is Abdul El-Sayed. You are the Michigan-raised son of Egyptian immigrants, with a medical degree from Columbia and a doctorate from Oxford, and you served as Detroit’s health director. You are running as a Democrat and polling competitively in a swing state.

Both men’s parents came from somewhere else. Both men practise a faith that is not Christianity. The difference is the party, and, the president has made plain, the name. Trump has mocked El-Sayed’s full name, Abdulrahman Mohamed El-Sayed, for its Muhammad. Graham drily noted that the president’s own middle name is John.

That is the American Contradiction in a single campaign season. Belonging is conditional on loyalty to the faction, not on citizenship, achievement or the Constitution.

The Crocodiles of 1871

The man who invented the Republican elephant would recognise this closing argument. He helped write an earlier version of it.

Nast was himself an immigrant. He was born in Landau, Bavaria, in 1840, arrived in New York at the age of six, and was baptised a Catholic. As an adult he became one of the most effective anti-Catholic propagandists in American history. In “The American River Ganges,” published in Harper’s Weekly on September 30, 1871, he drew Catholic bishops as crocodiles crawling ashore to devour American schoolchildren, while Irish politicians blocked the children’s escape.

Fourteen years after Nast drew his crocodiles, a sixteen-year-old from the same corner of Germany stepped off a ship in New York. Friedrich Trump left Kallstadt, a wine village in the Palatinate a few dozen kilometres from Nast's Landau, in 1885. He made his money in the Klondike. When he tried to resettle in his homeland in 1905, the Bavarian authorities ordered him out, ruling that he had emigrated to dodge military service. He didn't even claim to have bone spurs. The president who now asks whether a Michigan-raised doctor can ever be truly loyal is the grandson of a German immigrant his own homeland would not take back. He also springs from the same Palatinate soil as the man who gave the elephant its first outing. We now know why Donald Trump loves the word "nasty" so much. (The etymologists will object that "nasty" is older than both men by four centuries. The etymologists are not invited to this paragraph.)

The charge against Catholics was the charge now made against Muslims: their true loyalty lay with a foreign authority. For Catholics it was the Pope in Rome; for Muslims it is a shadowy Brotherhood. Their religious law would supplant the Constitution. They could never fully be Americans.

Al Smith heard that charge in 1928, when he ran for president as a Catholic and lost amid warnings that the Vatican would govern from Washington. John F. Kennedy heard it in 1960, until he went before the Greater Houston Ministerial Association on September 12 and reminded them of Article VI of the Constitution: “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.”

Greg Abbott’s Sharia smear therefore carries a particular irony. He is a Catholic governor accusing a Catholic opponent of loyalty to foreign religious law, which is the very accusation once levelled at his own church. One hundred and fifty years ago, Nast’s crocodiles wore mitres. The costume has changed, but the creature hasn’t.

Columbia’s Answer

Here the story becomes more interesting than a simple morality tale, because Nast also drew the antidote.

In “The Chinese Question,” published on February 18, 1871, a defiant Columbia, the female personification of the United States, shields a Chinese man from an armed mob. The wall behind them is plastered with the slurs of the day. Columbia’s words are printed beneath: “Hands off, gentlemen! America means fair play for all men.”

Nast drew dozens of cartoons defending Chinese immigrants. When Republican Senator James G. Blaine championed Chinese exclusion, Nast went after Blaine with the same ferocity he had used against Boss Tweed. Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act anyway, and President Chester Arthur, a Republican, signed it in May 1882. Two years later, Nast and Harper’s Weekly broke with the party over Blaine’s nomination and backed the Democrat Grover Cleveland.

So the elephant’s own creator contained the whole contradiction this series has traced. The same pen produced the crocodile bishops and Columbia’s fair play. The same artist who drew a party built on emancipation watched it sign the first federal law to bar an entire nationality, and he walked away from it.

The question the elephant faces in November 2026 is the one Nast faced in 1884: which of his drawings does the party mean to live by?

Never Speak to Them Again

One feature of this closing argument deserves attention, because it is how bigotry always behaves once it is let loose. It does not stay with its first target.

At the same Port Huron rally, Graham reported, Trump turned from El-Sayed to Michigan’s Jewish voters. Any Jewish person who supported El-Sayed, he said, should be ashamed, and others should “never speak to them again.” Graham observed that once contempt for one community is mainstreamed, it quickly spreads to the next.

The record bears him out. The 1924 Johnson-Reed Act, built on the same “unassimilable” logic used against the Chinese, sharply cut Jewish and southern and eastern European immigration. Fifteen years later, it helped ensure that the MS St. Louis, carrying more than nine hundred Jewish refugees, was turned away from American shores.

A Canadian writing this owes his readers some honesty. Canada turned the St. Louis away too. During those same years, an unnamed Canadian official reportedly answered the question of how many Jewish refugees the country should accept with three words: “None is too many.” Ottawa interned Japanese Canadians just as Washington interned Japanese Americans. Canada’s present advantage in attracting the talent America now drives away is not a matter of national virtue. It is a lesson learned the hard way and, for the moment, remembered.

Not an Aberration

It would be comforting to treat the final week of 2026 as a lapse, a campaign gone sour under pressure. The record does not allow it.

In June 2019, Graham and three colleagues assembled an oral history of Trump’s bigotry for The Atlantic. It runs from the Justice Department’s 1973 housing-discrimination suit against the Trump family’s company to “very fine people on both sides” in Charlottesville. One thread in it is directly relevant to Port Huron. Trump’s political career did not begin with birtherism. It began with his 2010 campaign against an Islamic cultural centre in lower Manhattan, which first gave him a national audience on the right. When he turned to Barack Obama’s birth certificate the following spring, he wondered aloud on a radio programme whether the document might list Obama’s religion as “Muslim.”

The Muslim card is not new to this campaign. It was the first card in the deck. Fifteen years later, the dealer is still playing it, and the party is still letting him.

The Fall Guy

There is a simpler way to describe what this closing argument is. It is a search for someone to blame.

Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire reported in The Atlantic on November 1, 2026 that Trump, angry that the Iran war has damaged Republican prospects, has been asking allies about the performance of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and General Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs. He is, in their words, searching for a scapegoat.

Here is the method at its most transparent. Gas prices are high, so the answer is a Muslim doctor’s name. The war is going badly, so the answer is whichever general told the president what he wanted to hear. The man at the top is never the cause. The cause is always someone else: someone foreign, someone disloyal, someone who can be made to carry the weight.

Separately Fleeced

In October 1892, the Georgia populist Tom Watson wrote that poor Black and white farmers were “kept apart that you may be separately fleeced of your earnings.” Serwer recalled the line, and the tragedy that followed: Watson later abandoned his own insight and became a notorious racist and antisemite. The scam he described proved more durable than the man who exposed it.

El-Sayed answered the president in terms Watson would have recognised. Graham reported that he told his own rally that the attacks pointed at his name and his faith, but “my name and my faith are not the reason you can’t afford your gas.”

The voters may be listening. Wehner reported that a Republican poll in Ohio found the Ramaswamy ad backfiring, with those who had seen it saying, by a margin of two to one, that it made them more likely to vote for Acton. That is one state and one ad. It is also the first sign in some time that the stampede has limits.

The Coda

Every exclusion this series has examined ended the same way. The Irish became mayors and then a president. The Chinese Exclusion Act was repealed in 1943, and the Senate formally expressed regret for it in 2011. Japanese American internment produced a national apology and reparations in 1988. Catholics stopped being crocodiles and became the largest single denomination in the country, including, as it happens, the governor of Texas.

The pattern does not guarantee the outcome on November 3, 2026. It guarantees the verdict of history, which tends to arrive late and charge interest.

Nast drew the elephant for its size, its strength and its dignity. He drew it panicking because he understood that even the largest animal in the menagerie can be frightened into stampeding over its own platform. The party that wears his elephant has spent this campaign trying to frighten voters about a Michigan doctor, a Catholic woman in Texas, Somali neighbours in Minnesota and Black families on food assistance.

It has another option, and Nast drew that one too. It is Columbia, standing between the mob and the man it has chosen to hate, with her hand raised: America means fair play for all men.

Elephants are big. They don’t need to be bigots too.
