The iconic 1988 leaders’ debate: John Turner confronts Brian Mulroney. ‘I believe you have sold us out.’ One of the most dramatic moments in Canadian political history.

In 1988, Canadians fought a federal election over a single question: should Canada tie its economic future to the United States through a free trade agreement?

It was the most consequential economic debate in Canadian history since the railway — another instance of the country’s destiny being decided by the terms of its relationship with the continent it shares. And like the railway debates of the 1870s, it split the country along lines that were not simply economic but existential, touching the deepest questions of Canadian identity: what does it mean to be sovereign when your economy is integrated with a neighbour ten times your size? Where does partnership end and dependency begin? And what happens when the partner turns hostile?

Brian Mulroney’s Progressive Conservatives argued that free trade would unlock prosperity. Canada had a resource-rich economy and a skilled workforce, but it was constrained by a domestic market too small to achieve the economies of scale that global competition demanded. Free trade with the United States would give Canadian firms access to a market of 250 million consumers, drive efficiency, attract investment, and modernize industries that had grown complacent behind tariff walls. The argument was classical economics, and it had the support of most of the business establishment and many of the country’s leading economists.

John Turner’s Liberals argued that free trade would surrender sovereignty. That it would hollow out Canadian manufacturing, making the country a resource colony for American industry. That it would erode the policy space Canada needed to maintain its social programs, its cultural institutions, and its regulatory independence. That it would, in Turner’s memorable formulation from the leaders’ debate, sell out the country with “the stroke of a pen.”

The debate performance was one of the iconic moments in Canadian political history. Turner, looking Mulroney directly in the eye, accused him of having betrayed Canada’s independence. “I happen to believe that you have sold us out,” he said. “I happen to believe that once you—” and here Mulroney interrupted, and the two men argued, face to face, on national television, about the future of the country. It was the closest Canadian politics has come to genuine drama — two leaders, two visions, one question that would determine the country’s direction for a generation.

Mulroney won the election. The Canada-United States Free Trade Agreement took effect on January 1, 1989. NAFTA — the North American Free Trade Agreement, which added Mexico — followed in 1994. And for roughly twenty-five years, the bargain appeared to work.

The numbers told a story of integration and growth. Canadian exports to the United States surged. The auto industry, already deeply integrated across the border through the Auto Pact of 1965, became fully continental. Parts crossed the border multiple times during assembly. Supply chains that had once been national became seamless — a car assembled in Oshawa might contain components manufactured in Michigan, Ohio, and Puebla, crossing borders so frequently that the concept of a “Canadian-made” or “American-made” car became largely meaningless.

Investment flowed. American companies expanded their Canadian operations. Canadian companies expanded south. The energy sector deepened its continental integration — Alberta oil flowed to American refineries; American capital flowed to Alberta oil sands. The softwood lumber trade, perennially contentious, was at least governed by a framework for managing disputes. The border, for goods and most services, became something closer to an administrative formality than a genuine barrier.

The standard of living rose. Canadian consumers benefited from lower prices on imported goods. Canadian producers benefited from access to a larger market. The economic relationship between the two countries became, by most measures, the most productive bilateral trading relationship in the world.

And beneath the prosperity, a dependency was growing that would not become fully visible until it was weaponized.

The cost of integration was structural, and it accumulated slowly.

By 2025, roughly seventy-five percent of Canadian exports went to the United States. No other major economy on earth was so concentrated in its trade dependence on a single partner. The Canadian economy was not merely integrated with the American economy; it was, in many sectors, a subsidiary of it. The auto industry could not function without cross-border supply chains. The energy sector could not easily redirect pipelines that had been built to flow south. The agricultural sector depended on American markets for everything from beef to canola to softwood lumber.

This concentration was, in good times, a tremendous advantage. Canada had preferential access to the world’s largest consumer market. The proximity, the common language, the shared business culture, the integrated financial systems — all of it made the Canada-U.S. trading relationship not just productive but almost frictionless.

In bad times — in the time of Trump — the integration became a weapon.

When a president who views trade as a zero-sum competition and allies as adversaries decided to impose tariffs on Canadian goods — steel, aluminum, dairy, lumber, auto parts, and eventually a broad swath of Canadian exports — the economic architecture that Mulroney had built became precisely the instrument of coercion that Turner had feared thirty-seven years earlier.

Canada could not easily redirect seventy-five percent of its exports. You cannot reroute a pipeline overnight. You cannot rebuild a supply chain in a fiscal quarter. You cannot replace a market of 330 million consumers with alternative customers when your entire economic infrastructure has been optimized, over three decades, for continental integration.

The free trade bargain had delivered prosperity. It had also delivered dependency. And dependency, in the hands of a hostile partner, is not an economic relationship. It is leverage. It is a weapon. It is the economic equivalent of the lapels that Johnson grabbed — except that the grip is tighter, the consequences are broader, and the damage, if the grip is not loosened, is structural and potentially permanent.

John Turner, who lost the 1988 election and whose political career ended with that loss, lived to see his warnings validated. He died in 2020, before the worst of the Trump tariffs, but the trajectory was already visible. The man who said “you have sold us out” was, in the most important respect, right — not about the economics of free trade in normal times, which delivered the benefits its proponents promised, but about the vulnerability that came with them. Free trade worked as long as both partners played by the rules. When one partner decided the rules were for suckers, the country that had organized its entire economy around the partnership discovered that it had no fallback position.

This is the context in which Mark Carney’s trade war must be understood. Carney is not merely standing up to Trump. He is attempting to rewrite a bargain that has defined Canadian economic policy for nearly four decades. The pivot away from American markets — the diversification of trade toward Europe through CETA, toward the Asia-Pacific through the CPTPP, toward bilateral arrangements with individual partners — is not a rhetorical exercise. It is a structural reformation of the Canadian economy, undertaken under emergency conditions, by a country that has spent a generation building economic infrastructure optimized for a continental relationship that has suddenly turned hostile.

The Okanagan wine over California. Prince Edward Island over Florida. The “Buy Canadian” campaigns. These are not just consumer choices. They are the early expressions of an economic nationalism that Canada has not practiced since before free trade — a deliberate, conscious effort to reduce dependency on a partner that has proven itself unreliable.

Mark Carney’s era of trade diversification: Canada actively working to loosen the continental embrace after decades of deepening integration.

Whether it will work is an open question. Economic nationalism is easier to practice in speeches than in supply chains. The integration is deep, and disentangling it will take years, not months, and will involve real costs — higher prices, disrupted industries, lost jobs in sectors that cannot quickly pivot. But the direction is set, and the political will — driven by a population that is genuinely, viscerally angry at being treated as a vassal state by a president who jokes about annexation — appears to be real.

The continental embrace that Mulroney initiated and that three decades of Canadian governments deepened is being loosened. Not abandoned — the geography makes complete disengagement impossible, and no serious person proposes it. But loosened, deliberately and with intent, by a country that has decided the cost of dependency is higher than the cost of diversification.

Turner was right. Mulroney was also right. Both things can be true. Free trade delivered prosperity and created vulnerability. The task now is to keep the prosperity and reduce the vulnerability, and that is the work of a generation, not a single parliamentary term.
