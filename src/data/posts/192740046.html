American motorists wait in long lines at a gas station during the 1973 Arab oil embargo. The embargo quadrupled oil prices in months and created widespread shortages. For the first time since World War II, Americans directly experienced the fragility of the energy foundation underlying postwar prosperity — a fragility that would shape U.S. strategy in the Middle East for the next half-century and re-emerge dramatically in 2026.

On the evening of August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon addressed the American people in a televised speech and announced, almost in passing, that the United States was suspending the convertibility of the dollar into gold.

It was, in its quiet way, one of the most consequential decisions in the history of the post-war international order. The Bretton Woods system — the monetary architecture assembled in 1944, which had underpinned the stability of the global economy for a quarter-century and in which Canadian diplomats including Hugh Keenleyside had played their part — was, at that moment, effectively over. The world had been running on a fixed exchange rate system anchored by the dollar’s convertibility to gold at $35 an ounce. Nixon ended it unilaterally, without consulting America’s allies, because the Vietnam War’s costs had drained American gold reserves to the point where maintaining the peg was no longer feasible.

He called it the “Nixon Shock.” The Europeans called it something less polite.

Two years later, in October 1973, the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries — OPEC — imposed an oil embargo against the United States and the Netherlands in retaliation for their support of Israel during the Yom Kippur War. The price of oil quadrupled in three months. Gas lines stretched around blocks in American cities. The economy staggered into stagflation — the combination of rising prices and stagnant growth that Keynesian economics had declared impossible and that the 1970s made familiar.

These two events — Nixon’s suspension of gold convertibility and OPEC’s oil embargo — are usually treated as separate crises of the 1970s. They were, in fact, two dimensions of the same structural transformation: the moment when the post-war international economic order, built on American dominance and dollar stability, began to fracture — and when the energy-power nexus that is now being weaponized in the Strait of Hormuz was first fully revealed.

Understanding what happened in 1971 and 1973 is essential to understanding why, in 2026, a war about Iranian nuclear weapons became a war about oil prices, and why the closing of a 21-mile-wide waterway between Iran and Oman can threaten the global economy with recession.

Bretton Woods and the Dollar’s Throne

To understand the Nixon Shock, you must first understand what Bretton Woods built.

In July 1944, as Allied forces were consolidating their gains in Normandy and the end of the Second World War was becoming foreseeable, 730 delegates from 44 nations gathered at the Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the post-war international monetary system.

The conference was dominated by two men: John Maynard Keynes, representing Britain, and Harry Dexter White, representing the United States. They disagreed about almost everything. Keynes wanted a genuinely international currency — he called it the bancor — managed by a global central bank, with the burden of adjustment shared between creditor and debtor nations. White wanted a system centered on the dollar, with the United States in the dominant position.

White won. The United States was, in 1944, the world’s largest economy, the world’s largest creditor, and the only major economic power whose industrial base had not been damaged by the war. It held approximately two-thirds of the world’s monetary gold reserves. The dollar’s claim to be the anchor of the international monetary system was not ideology — it was arithmetic.

The system that emerged from Bretton Woods fixed the dollar to gold at $35 an ounce, and fixed all other major currencies to the dollar at adjustable pegs. The International Monetary Fund was created to provide balance-of-payments support to countries in difficulty. The World Bank was created to finance reconstruction and development. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was created to manage the reduction of barriers to international commerce.

This was the economic architecture of the post-war order — the complement to the security architecture of NATO and the political architecture of the United Nations. It was designed to prevent the competitive devaluations, trade barriers, and financial instability that had characterized the 1930s and that were widely believed to have contributed to the conditions that produced the Second World War.

For twenty-five years, it worked. The post-war economic boom — les Trente Glorieuses, the French called it, the Thirty Glorious Years — was the most sustained period of economic growth in human history. Western European and Japanese living standards rose dramatically. International trade expanded. The institutions Bretton Woods created became the frameworks through which the global economy was managed.

But the system had a structural vulnerability that Keynes had identified in 1944 and that proved, ultimately, fatal. The Triffin dilemma — named for the Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin, who formalized it in 1960 — held that any country whose currency served as the global reserve currency faced an irresolvable contradiction. To supply the world with the reserves it needed to conduct international trade, the reserve currency country had to run persistent balance-of-payments deficits — it had to spend more abroad than it earned. But persistent deficits would eventually undermine confidence in the reserve currency’s stability, which would trigger runs on its gold backing and collapse the system.

This is precisely what happened. The Vietnam War’s costs — combined with President Johnson’s refusal to raise taxes to pay for both the war and his Great Society social programs — produced the deficits that drained American gold reserves. By 1971, foreign central banks held more dollars than the United States held gold to back them. The system was mathematically insolvent.

Nixon’s solution was not a negotiated reform of the system — the kind of multilateral process that Keynes would have preferred and that the post-war order’s architects would have pursued. It was a unilateral act: close the gold window, let the dollar float, and present the rest of the world with a fait accompli. Treasury Secretary John Connally — Nixon’s combative Texan — summarized the American position to the Europeans with characteristic bluntness: “The dollar is our currency, but it’s your problem.”

It was the Nixon Shock’s most honest statement. And it was, in embryonic form, the same attitude that would eventually produce Trump’s “you’ll have to start learning how to fight for yourself” to NATO allies in March 2026.

The OPEC Embargo and the Energy Revelation

If the Nixon Shock revealed the vulnerability of the dollar-centered monetary order, the OPEC embargo revealed something more fundamental: that the post-war economic boom had been built on a foundation of cheap energy whose price was set not in Washington or London but in Riyadh and Tehran.

The 1973 oil embargo was triggered by the Yom Kippur War — the Egyptian and Syrian surprise attack on Israel on October 6, 1973, and the American airlift of military supplies to Israel that followed. OPEC’s Arab members, led by Saudi Arabia, cut oil production and embargoed exports to the United States and the Netherlands. The price of oil, which had been approximately $3 a barrel before the embargo, rose to nearly $12 a barrel by January 1974. In inflation-adjusted terms, it was the largest commodity price spike in recorded history to that point.

The consequences were immediate and severe. American gasoline prices doubled. Heating oil shortages forced thermostats down across the Northeast. The Nixon administration imposed speed limits of 55 miles per hour, daylight saving time year-round, and restrictions on gas station hours. The Christmas lights went out on the White House.

But the longer-term consequences were more significant than the immediate disruption. The oil shock revealed three structural realities about the post-war order that had previously been obscured by the assumption of cheap energy.

The first was the vulnerability of the advanced industrial economies to energy price shocks. Western Europe and Japan were almost entirely dependent on imported oil from the Middle East. The United States was less dependent — it was still a significant domestic oil producer — but the interconnectedness of global energy markets meant that price shocks anywhere were felt everywhere. The comfortable assumption that energy was simply a commodity, available in unlimited quantities at reliably low prices, was shattered.

The second was the political power of the oil-producing states. OPEC’s members — Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Venezuela, Libya, and others — had for decades accepted the terms set by the major Western oil companies: the “Seven Sisters” that controlled production, refining, and distribution across most of the world’s major oil fields. The 1973 embargo demonstrated that the producing states could set the terms instead. The transfer of pricing power from the companies to the states — the nationalization of oil industries that had been underway across the developing world since the 1950s — was now complete.

The third, and most consequential for our story, was the centrality of the Persian Gulf to the global energy system. More than half of the world’s known oil reserves were concentrated in the states surrounding the Persian Gulf — Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, the UAE, and Qatar. The geography of the world’s energy supply was fixed in ways that the geography of almost no other commodity was. You could find coal on five continents. You could find iron ore across the world. But petroleum — in the quantities the global economy required — was concentrated in one of the most politically volatile regions on earth.

This geographical concentration created a structural vulnerability that no amount of conservation, efficiency improvement, or alternative energy development could quickly eliminate. It meant that American strategic interests in the Middle East were not, as Robert Kagan correctly notes, primarily about Israel or terrorism or ideology. They were about oil — about ensuring that the energy supplies on which the global economy depended remained accessible and affordable. Every American military engagement in the Middle East from 1973 forward — the Carter Doctrine, the Gulf War, the Iraq War, the Iran war of 2026 — has been shaped, at its foundation, by this energy geography.

The Petrodollar System: Turning Crisis Into Leverage

Henry Kissinger meets with Saudi leaders during 1974–1975 shuttle diplomacy. In the aftermath of the oil embargo, Kissinger helped negotiate the petrodollar system: Saudi Arabia would price oil in U.S. dollars and recycle revenues into American Treasuries in exchange for security guarantees. This replaced gold with oil as the anchor of dollar dominance.

Nixon’s response to the breakdown of Bretton Woods and the OPEC shock was, in retrospect, one of the most ingenious pieces of financial statecraft in American history. It turned a potential catastrophe — the end of dollar dominance — into a new form of dollar dominance.

The mechanism was the petrodollar system, negotiated primarily by Henry Kissinger and Treasury Secretary William Simon in 1974-1975. The deal, struck with Saudi Arabia and subsequently extended to other Gulf states, had two components.

First, Saudi Arabia would price its oil exports exclusively in US dollars. Since Saudi Arabia was the world’s largest oil exporter, and since OPEC broadly followed Saudi pricing, this meant that any country that wanted to buy oil — any country in the world — would need to first acquire US dollars. The demand for dollars was therefore not merely a function of American trade and investment but of the global energy economy. As long as oil was priced in dollars, dollar demand was structural and permanent.

Second, Saudi Arabia would recycle its oil revenues — the “petrodollars” — into US Treasury bonds and other dollar-denominated assets. The money that flowed from the world to Saudi Arabia for oil would flow back to the United States as investment in American government debt. This recycling mechanism provided the United States with a reliable source of financing for its government deficits — deficits that would only grow in the decades ahead.

The elegance of the arrangement was extraordinary. The United States had just abandoned the gold standard, which should have undermined confidence in the dollar. Instead, it replaced the gold anchor with an oil anchor. The dollar was no longer backed by a metal in a vault at Fort Knox. It was backed by the world’s need for energy — a need that was, if anything, more fundamental and more durable than the need for gold.

This is why the dollar has remained the world’s dominant reserve currency for fifty years after Nixon closed the gold window. Not because of American economic management, not because of American institutional strength alone, but because oil — the commodity on which the global economy runs — is priced in dollars. As long as that is true, every country in the world has a structural need for dollars, and the United States can finance its deficits at uniquely favorable terms.

The petrodollar system also explains why the Strait of Hormuz matters so much more than its 21-mile width suggests. The strait is not just a shipping channel. It is the physical chokepoint through which the dollar’s most important anchor — the Gulf oil that gives the dollar its structural necessity — must pass. When Iran closes the strait, it is not merely disrupting energy supplies. It is threatening the foundation of dollar dominance. It is doing, through geography and military pressure, what no economic competitor has managed to do through finance: challenging the mechanism that gives the United States its unique privilege of printing the world’s reserve currency.

This is what Ghalibaf meant, when he called the strait “sovereign Iranian territory.” And this is why Trump’s response was so visceral and so disproportionate to what any conventional military calculus would justify.

The Carter Doctrine and Its Consequences

President Jimmy Carter delivers the 1980 State of the Union address announcing the Carter Doctrine: any attempt by an outside force to control the Persian Gulf would be regarded as an assault on vital U.S. interests and would be repelled by military force if necessary. This formalized the permanent American military commitment to Gulf oil security that has shaped every major engagement since, including 2026.

The 1973 oil shock produced one more consequential legacy: the Carter Doctrine of 1980.

In his State of the Union address on January 23, 1980 — delivered in the shadow of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan and the Iranian revolution that had deposed the Shah and installed Ayatollah Khomeini — President Carter announced that the United States would use military force, if necessary, to prevent any outside power from gaining control of the Persian Gulf region.

“An attempt by any outside force to gain control of the Persian Gulf region will be regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States of America, and such an assault will be repelled by any means necessary, including military force.”

The Carter Doctrine was the formal institutionalization of what the petrodollar system had made structurally necessary: American military commitment to the security of the Persian Gulf. It established the principle that was never explicitly articulated but was always understood — that American military power in the Middle East existed primarily to ensure the flow of oil, and that any threat to that flow was a threat to American vital interests.

Every American military action in the Gulf region since 1980 has operated under the Carter Doctrine’s framework. The Gulf War of 1991 was its most explicit application: when Saddam Hussein’s invasion of Kuwait threatened Saudi oil fields and Gulf oil flows, the United States assembled the largest military coalition since the Second World War and expelled him — not because Kuwait was a democracy or because Saddam was uniquely evil, but because the petrodollar system required Gulf oil to flow freely.

The Iran war of 2026 is, in this light, not an aberration but an extension. The United States went to war, ostensibly over nuclear weapons, in a country whose control over the Strait of Hormuz made it the single greatest structural threat to the petrodollar system. The nuclear rationale was real — but it was not sufficient on its own to explain the urgency, the scale, and the willingness to absorb the economic costs. The strait was always the deeper motivation.

And Iran, by closing the strait, has demonstrated something that no adversary had managed to demonstrate in the fifty years since the petrodollar system was established: that the dollar’s oil anchor can be threatened from the water as well as from the oil field. The Strait of Hormuz has become the pressure point on which the entire architecture of dollar dominance rests.

Henry Kissinger would recognize the mechanism. He helped design it. He would not be surprised by what has happened. He would simply note that the architecture he built was more fragile than it appeared, and that fragility had always been its most carefully concealed feature.

What the Pivot Built and What It Left Exposed

The petrodollar system was a work of strategic genius. It preserved American monetary dominance after the end of Bretton Woods, provided the United States with unparalleled access to cheap financing, and embedded American strategic interests in the Persian Gulf in ways that made them structural and permanent rather than contingent and political.

But it also entangled American power in a region that American values — democracy, human rights, the rule of law — made deeply uncomfortable to manage. Saudi Arabia, the anchor of the petrodollar system, was an absolute monarchy with no elections, no free press, and a record on women’s rights and religious freedom that was incompatible with the liberal values the post-war order was supposed to embody. The United States supported it anyway — because the alternative was to lose the oil anchor on which dollar dominance depended.

This entanglement produced the hypocrisy that became one of the most powerful arguments of America’s critics: that the country which claimed to stand for democracy and human rights was the primary external supporter of some of the most repressive governments on earth. Osama bin Laden drew his most potent argument not from theology but from this contradiction — the presence of American troops on the sacred soil of Saudi Arabia, the land of the two holy mosques, in service of a monarchy that had made itself dependent on American military protection.

The 1973 oil shock planted the seed of 9/11. It planted the seed of every subsequent American military engagement in the Middle East. And it planted the seed of the Iran war of 2026 — a war that is, at its foundation, about who controls the chokepoint through which the dollar’s oil anchor must pass.

The order’s founding logic — that the benefits of American leadership should be broadly shared, that American power derived its durability from its service to a common good rather than merely to American interests — was partially betrayed by the petrodollar system. It preserved American monetary dominance at the cost of entanglement with regimes whose values were incompatible with the principles the order was supposed to embody.

Canada felt this entanglement from a particular angle. The 1973 oil shock hit Canada’s oil-importing provinces — Ontario and Quebec — as hard as it hit the United States, while Alberta’s oil-producing economy boomed. Pierre Trudeau’s National Energy Program of 1980, which attempted to redistribute oil revenues from producing to consuming provinces and assert Canadian ownership over the energy sector, produced a fury in Alberta and a genuine constitutional crisis that Canadians are still processing. It also produced a sharp confrontation with Washington, which viewed the NEP as a threat to American investment in Canadian energy — a reminder that even the most intimate of American alliances was ultimately subordinate to the economics of oil. Keenleyside’s careful architecture of Canadian-American partnership, built on the premise that the two countries’ interests were more complementary than competitive, ran directly into the reality that energy made them competitors as well as allies.

The bill for the petrodollar system’s contradictions has been accumulating since 1973. In the spring of 2026, it is being presented.
