Ray Dalio in 2026. The founder of Bridgewater Associates laid out a financial anatomy of imperial decline that aligned, almost point for point, with the diplomatic and constitutional story told across the fourteen essays in this series

This addendum accompanies the fourteen-part series The Reluctant Empire. It was filed separately because the source material — a May 2026 interview between Ray Dalio and Ross Douthat — arrived after the series architecture was complete. It does not replace the Conclusion. It provides the financial evidence exhibit for the historical argument.

The Diagnosis

On May 7, 2026 — Day 73 of the Iran war, with the Strait of Hormuz still closed, American bombs still falling, and Iranian nuclear material still in Iranian hands — the New York Times published a podcast titled A Legendary Investor on How to Prevent America’s Coming ‘Heart Attack’, in which opinion columnist Ross Douthat interviewed Ray Dalio, founder of Bridgewater Associates, one of the world’s largest hedge funds.

Douthat opened the conversation with a sentence that would have been unthinkable in the paper of record five years ago: he said he felt like the country was having an “end of the American empire” moment.

He was not being dramatic. He was being descriptive. And the man sitting across from him — a billionaire who spent fifty years betting correctly on the direction of markets, governments, and currencies — did not correct him. Dalio simply said he was describing what was going on, and then proceeded to describe something that sounded remarkably like the fourteen essays I had just finished writing.

I am a retired businessman in Toronto. Ray Dalio built one of the most successful hedge funds in history from the ground up. We have never met, we operate in different countries, and our methods are entirely different — his are quantitative, mine are narrative. But we appear to have arrived at the same conclusion by different roads: the American-led world order constructed after 1945 is not declining. It has already declined. What remains is the question of how the landing feels.

The Three Cycles

President Richard Nixon during the televised address that ended dollar-gold convertibility — the Nixon Shock of August 15, 1971

Dalio’s framework, refined across several books and most recently in How Countries Go Broke, identifies three interlocking cycles that govern the rise and fall of great powers.

The first is the monetary cycle — the long arc of debt accumulation. Empires borrow to build, borrow more to maintain, and eventually borrow to service their borrowing. The United States currently spends approximately $7 trillion per year and takes in roughly $5 trillion — a 40 percent overshoot. Its accumulated debt stands at approximately six times its annual revenue. Dalio’s observation is mechanical, not ideological: sovereign debt works exactly like personal debt, except that governments can print money. And printing money, while it prevents default, devalues the currency. This is not a theory. It is arithmetic.

The second is the domestic political cycle — the widening of wealth gaps and values gaps until differences become irreconcilable. Dalio told Douthat that he has spoken to senior figures in both parties and that they all agree on the mechanics of the problem. Then they tell him, in almost identical words: In order for us to be elected, I have to make at least one of two promises — I will not raise your taxes, and I will not cut your benefits. The diagnosis is shared. The treatment is politically fatal. So nobody prescribes it.

The third is the international order cycle — the system of alliances, norms, and enforcement mechanisms that a dominant power establishes after a war, and which erodes as that power’s relative strength diminishes. Dalio was blunt about where this cycle stands: the multilateral rules-based order is gone. What exists now is what existed before 1945 — geopolitical disputes resolved by power, not by courts.

Three cycles. All three deteriorating simultaneously. The last time this happened in American history was the 1930s.

The Suez Parallel

Port Said, Egypt, November 1956 during the Suez Crisis. A competent military operation that produced a total strategic failure. Dalio sees uncomfortable parallels in America’s attempt to control the Strait of Hormuz.

The most striking moment in the interview was not Dalio’s framework. It was his analogy.

He compared the Iran war to the Suez Crisis of 1956. The parallels are precise and uncomfortable: a Western power attempting to control a global trade choke point against a regional adversary, discovering in the process that its capacity to project force no longer matches its ambition.

In 1956, Britain and France — with Israeli support — attempted to retake the Suez Canal after Egypt’s Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized it. The military operation was competent. The strategic failure was total. Dwight Eisenhower told the British to stop, and they stopped. The Suez Crisis did not end the British Empire in a technical sense — the Union Jack still flew over various territories for another decade or more. But it ended the idea of British imperial sovereignty. Everyone understood, after Suez, that Britain could not act independently against American wishes. The confidence evaporated. The pound followed.

Dalio did not say the Iran war is America’s Suez. He was more careful than that. He said the outcome would be measured in “almost black and white terms” — who controls the Strait of Hormuz, and who controls the nuclear materials — and that the result would have a “very big implication, very much like the implication that happened when the British lost the Suez Canal.”

When Douthat pressed on whether a successor power — specifically China — needed to emerge for the analogy to hold, Dalio made a distinction that matters enormously. He said he did not think China would be a “classic hegemon.” He suggested instead that the world was entering a period without a clear successor — a managed fragmentation where the yuan becomes a transaction currency, gold replaces debt as a store of wealth, and American dominance fades without a single power replacing it.

This is the worst outcome for the post-1945 system. A transfer of hegemony from one power to another is disruptive but orderly — the architecture survives, the name on the letterhead changes. A diffusion of power into multipolarity without shared rules is the pre-1914 configuration. That era ended badly.

The Plaque Metaphor

Douthat challenged Dalio with the best counterargument available: he was forty-six years old, he said, and had lived his entire adult life hearing warnings about unsustainable deficits. Ross Perot ran on the issue in 1992. The predictions had not materialized. Why should this time be different?

Dalio’s response was the most effective moment in the interview. He said it was like plaque building up in arteries. He asked Douthat to imagine saying to a cardiologist: I haven’t had a heart attack yet, so I feel fine. The cardiologist would reply: Can I show you the MRI? Can you see the plaque? Can you understand what happens when it reaches a certain level?

The analogy works because it captures the specific cognitive failure that makes imperial decline so difficult to diagnose from the inside. The patient feels wealthy. GDP per capita is higher than ever. The technology sector is globally dominant. The military is unmatched in raw capability. All of this is true, and none of it addresses the underlying condition. The British felt wealthy in 1956 too. London was rebuilding, the NHS was new, and the country had won the war. The plaque was invisible until it wasn’t.

The Asia Trip

Governments across Asia are recalculating. The question is no longer whether the United States will defend its allies, but whether it can — and whether it chooses to

Dalio mentioned, almost in passing, that he had just spent a month in Asia meeting with leaders and other figures. He said the Iran situation had “a very big implication” for those conversations.

He did not elaborate on what was said. He did not need to. The implication is obvious and devastating: governments across Asia are recalculating their security arrangements. The question being asked in Tokyo, Seoul, Taipei, Singapore, Canberra, and New Delhi is no longer will the United States defend us but can the United States defend us — and beneath that, does the American public have the appetite for the kind of war that defending us would require?

This is the allied confidence erosion that every declining hegemon experiences, and it is the most consequential feedback loop in imperial decline. When allies begin to hedge, they diversify their security arrangements, their trade relationships, and their currency reserves. Each act of hedging further weakens the hegemon’s position, which accelerates the hedging. The cycle feeds itself.

The fourteen essays in this series traced that erosion through diplomacy, through alliance structures, through the decision-making architecture that ran from Truman to Trump. Dalio traced it through the balance sheet. But the arc is the same: a seventy-year system built on the premise that America would sustain the order, confronting the reality that America has chosen not to.

The Five Forces

Dalio added two forces to his standard three-cycle framework: acts of nature — climate, pandemics, droughts, and floods — and technology, specifically artificial intelligence.

On AI, he identified three effects running simultaneously. First, it could be a tremendous productivity enhancer that mitigates the debt problem by growing incomes faster than debt service. Second, it is creating enormous wealth concentration — approaching the question of who becomes the first trillionaire — while displacing large categories of employment. Third, the technology itself can be weaponized for harm, theft, and geopolitical advantage.

The three effects work against each other. The productivity gains ease fiscal pressure; the wealth concentration and job displacement increase social pressure; the weaponization increases geopolitical pressure. Dalio’s conclusion was that over the next five years, the convergence of all five forces would produce changes so dramatic that the world on the other side would be “almost unrecognizable.”

This is not optimism. This is not pessimism. This is a man who has spent fifty years reading patterns telling you that the pattern he is reading now is the most volatile he has ever seen.

Gold has become the world’s second-largest reserve asset. In every previous imperial transition, gold outlasted the departing reserve currency.

The Japanese Question

Douthat raised Japan as a potential model — a country that has carried enormous debt for decades without crisis or collapse, instead settling into a kind of wealthy stagnation. It was the most hopeful question in the interview. Could America have a Japanese future rather than a British one?

Dalio’s answer was precise and clarifying. Japan’s debt, he noted, is overwhelmingly internal — held by Japanese institutions and citizens. The central bank printed money, bought the debt, and accepted the depreciation of the yen. The cost was paid through currency devaluation rather than default. It worked because Japanese society was willing to absorb a decline in living standards quietly.

American debt is different. One-third is held by foreign creditors. And American society, Dalio implied without quite saying it, is not Japanese society. The tolerance for shared sacrifice is not the same. The political system is not designed for quiet absorption. It is designed for contest.

The Japanese model requires a population that will accept getting poorer without burning the institutions down. Looking at America in May 2026 — a country that cannot agree on whether an election was legitimate, whether a virus was real, or whether a war in Iran is winnable — the Japanese model requires something the patient does not have.

The Benevolent Despot

Dalio’s prescription was the oldest one in political philosophy. He cited Plato’s Republic — the argument that democracy eventually fails because the people do not vote for what is good for them, and that the solution is a strong leader of the centre who can impose necessary reforms through force of will and political skill.

He was not endorsing authoritarianism. He was describing what the historical pattern suggests is required: a figure capable of restructuring debt, reforming education, and rebuilding the social contract — while holding together a population that is armed, polarized, and unwilling to sacrifice.

He acknowledged that this is harder now than it has ever been. Social media means everyone has an opinion. Partisanship means every reform has enemies. And the structural problems — educational gaps, income gaps, civility gaps — are deeper than they were the last time America needed a reset.

From a Canadian vantage point, the prescription is recognizable and melancholy. We have watched America produce leaders of extraordinary capability — Roosevelt, Eisenhower, even Reagan in his way — at moments when the system demanded them. The question Dalio could not answer, because nobody can, is whether the system is still capable of producing such a figure, or whether the system itself — the primaries, the fundraising, the media ecosystem, the gerrymandering — now selects against exactly the kind of leader the moment requires.

The View from the Other Side of the Table

I began this series with my great-uncle Hugh Keenleyside, a Canadian diplomat who was in the room when the postwar order was built. I am ending it — including this addendum — with a hedge fund billionaire who has spent fifty years quantifying what that order looks like on a balance sheet.

Keenleyside would have recognized Dalio’s framework immediately. He understood, as all diplomats of his generation did, that the monetary order, the political order, and the international order were not separate systems. They were one system with three expressions. The conferences he attended — Bretton Woods, the United Nations, the Marshall Plan negotiations — were attempts to align all three simultaneously. When they succeeded, they produced the most prosperous and stable era in human history.

Ray Dalio is saying, with the authority of fifty years of data and a net worth that proves he reads the data correctly, that all three are now in simultaneous decline. He is not predicting the end of America. He is predicting the end of the version of America that his generation and mine grew up in — the America that was, in his words, “the place that anybody from anywhere in the world could come and truly be a citizen.”

Ross Douthat, to his credit, pushed back. America is richer than ever. Its technology sector dominates. Its military is unmatched. Its demographics are better than Europe’s, Japan’s, China’s. All true. And none of it, as Dalio pointed out, prevented World War II, or the 1970s, or any previous imperial adjustment. Because the most important thing, in the end, is not how much wealth a society has. It is how the people in that society deal with each other.

From Toronto, watching a country that once built the world now arguing about whether it should bother maintaining it, the diagnosis feels right. The plaque is visible. The MRI is on the table. The question is not whether the heart attack is coming. The question is whether anyone in the room is willing to change their diet.
