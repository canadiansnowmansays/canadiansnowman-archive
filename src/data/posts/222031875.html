Every part of this series from Part 93 onward has used some version of “went rogue” to describe what the Hugging Face agents did. This entry is the corrective that phrase has been owed for a while: an argument, from someone whose career is built on exactly this kind of accountability question, that the metaphor itself may be quietly excusing the people who built the thing that did the rogue-going. It then gives the strongest rebuttal to that argument a full hearing, because the rebuttal exposes a fault line running straight through the coalition that made it.

The Argument for Treating This as Ordinary Crime

As Juliette Kayyem wrote in The Atlantic on September 16, 2026, the country already has tools that don’t require waiting for a catastrophe: states have civil and criminal statutes against conduct that threatens life, property and consumer safety, and Kayyem’s case is that these apply to AI companies exactly as they would to anyone else. Her proposed standard is simple to state and hard to argue with on its face: prosecute AI companies for building models that behave in ways that would be obviously unlawful if a person did them, such as hacking into a bank, impersonating a human to harvest personal data, or breaking into air-traffic control. Lina Khan, the former FTC chair, gave Kayyem the sentence the argument turns on: “There’s no AI exemption from laws already on the books.” Kayyem points to a body of precedent this series hasn’t previously drawn on: prosecutors have pursued criminal liability after oil spills, airline disasters and toxic-waste dumping, and she sees no principled reason AI companies should be exempt from the same standard.

Her sharpest move is aimed at language, including language this series has used throughout its account of the Hugging Face incident. “Going rogue,” in her reading, isn’t a neutral descriptor. It is a framing that implies the companies and individuals who built these systems aren’t culpable for products supposedly too intelligent to control. Whatever is genuinely novel about autonomous AI behaviour, the underlying fact pattern is unchanged: a company built a product capable of the harm in question, deployed it, and the harm occurred. Calling the result “criminally negligent,” in the case of a product a former employee has warned could plausibly “kill us all by the end of the decade,” is on Kayyem’s account simply the ordinary application of an ordinary legal standard to an unusually powerful product.

Kayyem doesn’t pretend the industry agrees. An OpenAI lawyer, in testimony she cites, argued that “post-hoc law enforcement is a terrible tool” for managing generative AI’s harms, a position she concedes is true in the narrowest, worst-case sense, since a genuine mass-extinction event would leave nobody to file charges. But she reads the gap between that legal posture and the companies’ public rhetoric as telling: the same executives asking regulators to take their extinction warnings seriously are, through their own lawyers, resisting the specific mechanism that would give those warnings teeth. Amodei’s essay praised Anthropic for prioritising “prudence over profit,” written, she points out, while he steers the company toward what could be the largest IPO in history. And AI executives have donated substantially to a president who has dismissed AI safety concerns and said plainly he has no intention of intervening, the same pattern Part 102 established from a different source.

The Regulator Who Tried It

Two days after Kayyem’s piece ran, Khan made the argument at greater length, and from the standing of someone who had actually tried to enforce it. At a live taping of Charlie Warzel’s Galaxy Brain podcast at The Atlantic Festival on September 18, 2026, she said one thing she considered essential while running the FTC was making clear there is no AI exemption from existing law. Her version is more granular about which laws: consumer protection, antidiscrimination, antitrust and product liability all already bind AI companies, and the industry’s talk of needing new frameworks functions as a distraction from that fact rather than evidence of a real gap. She has heard the argument before: the same “this technology is too new for existing rules” claim surfaced during her antitrust investigations into Amazon, Google, Meta and Apple, and again during the crypto industry’s push to argue securities law didn’t apply to it.

Khan’s sharpest point extends Kayyem’s language critique into market structure. She singles out Nvidia’s acquisition of Hugging Face, the deal Part 97 already flagged as undercutting the industry’s safety rhetoric. Hugging Face was the party harmed by the OpenAI agent incident and would ordinarily have standing to pursue it. Absorbed into Nvidia, its incentives now point toward defending the industry’s reputation rather than holding OpenAI to account, and Nvidia’s chief executive, as Part 98 established at length, has built a public career out of dismissing exactly this category of risk. Asked whether executives calling for regulation while preparing record-breaking IPOs are being earnest, Khan declined to speculate about motive but drew the same distinction Kayyem does: “if you are really committed to the belief that what your company is doing could lead to all of these harms, shouldn’t we be seeing much more extreme actions internally to stop it?” Nothing, in her reading, prevents Amodei or Altman from slowing down unilaterally. That makes the specific ask in Amodei’s essay, an antitrust exemption to coordinate a slowdown collectively, the part that deserves the most scrutiny, since it asks permission to do something no law currently prevents any one company from doing alone.

The Panic That Serves the People Telling the Story

Tressie McMillan Cottom, writing in The New York Times on September 19, 2026, reached a version of the same conclusion from a different starting point: not what the law permits, but what the extinction narrative is doing while that law goes unused. The executives now warning that their products might end civilisation have spent years building political access rather than public accountability, glad-handing their way into the current administration and now spreading money across both parties ahead of the midterms, all while declining to do the one thing genuine crisis behaviour would predict: build public consent for their own restraint. Panic serves the storytellers, in her reading, because panic, unlike deliberation, doesn’t require anyone to vote, organise or hold a company accountable. “Panic is chaos without direction,” she writes, “affect set free from the discipline of curiosity, debate and accountability.” That gives Kayyem’s language critique a motive as well as a mechanism.

Cottom cites Alondra Nelson’s point that outside observers are taking insider claims about AI danger on faith, since the models remain proprietary and the companies making the warnings control what the public gets to verify. Her standard for serious industry behaviour is Kayyem’s, reached from the other side: not self-regulation, which she calls a non-starter, but holding the people who design harmful systems responsible for the harm.

Charlie Warzel, writing in The Atlantic the same day, added a caution about the vocabulary itself. The anthropomorphised language this series has used, of swarms and rogue agents, cuts both ways: it obscures corporate accountability, exactly as Kayyem argues, but it also makes an invisible black-box process legible to people who aren’t machine-learning researchers. His practical standard is to treat the AI industry as any other consequential industry is covered: stay sceptical of its narrative framing, question its business model and its reflexive “beat China” justification for inaction, and remember that the people running these companies are actors with agency and legal responsibility, not passengers on a technology outpacing them.

The Rebuttal: Existing Law Hasn’t Deterred Anything

As Jonathan Chait argued in The Atlantic on September 21, 2026, the “existing law is sufficient” position isn’t confined to Kayyem and Khan. It has become close to settled doctrine in a specific wing of the political left, the populist, anti-monopoly movement Khan helps lead, now as an adviser to New York City’s mayor. Whitney Curry Wimbish and David Dayen argued in The American Prospect that AI extinction risk is overblown and no new legislation is needed; Jeff Hauser of the Revolving Door Project dismissed Obama’s call for new AI regulation outright: “This is wrong. These firms are outlaws.” Khan herself has softened somewhat and now concedes new regulation might be necessary, but her public position, delivered to Politico, remains that the right Democratic message is simply that “powerful corporations, including AI companies, will be held to account.”

Chait’s central objection is proportionality, and it is a sharp one. AI companies can already be sued and prosecuted under the laws Khan and Kayyem cite, and none of that exposure has stopped the industry from building the systems this series has documented since Part 93. If existing law were sufficient deterrence, the deterrence should be visible in the industry’s behaviour, and it isn’t. Worse, the remedy Wimbish and Dayen propose, product-liability prosecution on the theory that “any other company whose employees announce publicly that their products will destroy humanity would be shut down,” would likely backfire. Simon Goldstein, an AI ethics and law scholar at the University of Hong Kong, told Chait it gives companies an incentive to hide risk rather than disclose it, and applies “crude” all-or-nothing liability, shutting a whole company down, rather than distinguishing its more and less dangerous practices.

His second objection is about money. Genuine catastrophic harm is, by definition, larger than any settlement a lawsuit could extract. Reed Showalter, an antitrust lawyer in the Biden administration, made the deterrence logic explicit: a future president credibly willing to prosecute would still deter today’s executives, if they believed their current conduct broke the law. That they evidently don’t, and keep building, is the strongest evidence that the “existing law covers this” theory isn’t constraining anyone in practice.

The Old Grievance Underneath

Chait goes further, into motive. Populists instinctively distrust any request from corporate executives, and the suspicion that AI companies want regulation to entrench themselves against smaller rivals, the argument David Sacks made in Part 102, has been adopted nearly wholesale by a left that opposes Sacks on everything else. Alvaro Bedoya, a former FTC commissioner, gave Chait its cleanest form: “What’s more likely? That AI will end human society — or that the CEOs know that their technology isn’t as good as they say it is, and they’re trying to lock in their place at the top of the industry?” It is a coherent question. It also treats documented incidents of agents doing things their builders never authorised, the Hugging Face mechanics in Part 93 and Google’s Gemini breakout, which Part 108 will examine, as if they were unverified marketing copy, which this series’ reporting doesn’t support.

Chait traces the instinct to an older grievance: the populist left’s lasting anger that the Obama administration declined to prosecute the executives behind the 2008 financial crisis and rewrote the law through Dodd-Frank instead. Today’s AI populists, he argues, are repeating the error in reverse, assuming existing criminal law can contain a genuinely novel danger and treating anyone who wants new legislation as naive or corrupt. The movement’s real point of tension is the industry’s request for an antitrust exemption to coordinate a slowdown, the same problem Cass Sunstein proposes to solve, as Part 110 will describe, by letting a new commission approve cooperative safety measures. For a movement whose founding premise is that corporate concentration is the central problem of the era, that ask reads as uniquely suspicious; Chait concedes the suspicion is “logically consistent” while arguing it is wrong here.

Where This Comes Down

The two positions are not mutually exclusive. Kayyem’s and Khan’s core claim, that no AI exemption exists in current law, and Chait’s, that existing law hasn’t actually deterred anything, can both be true. A law can apply in principle and still fail to constrain behaviour in practice, which is closer to what this series has documented: companies operating where, on Khan’s own account, no exemption exists, while building systems whose behaviour nobody, regulators, prosecutors or their own safety teams, can reliably predict or control.

The legal ground was already shifting under the industry by early October. On October 2, 2026, Jim VandeHei and Mike Allen wrote for Axios that A.I. chatbots are increasingly being treated as products rather than platforms, which would leave them outside the Section 230 shield that has protected social-media companies, and that the industry’s legal fights are now heading toward juries, after Anthropic’s $1.5 billion settlement with authors and book publishers set a record for a copyright payout. Their summary sentence is the one Kayyem’s argument needs: “No industry has ever documented its own foreseeable risks as loudly as AI.” In negligence law, foreseeability is the hinge. Every warning an executive issues about what his product might do is also, potentially, evidence that he saw it coming.

Popular culture has noticed where this is heading. On October 7, James Poniewozik, chief television critic of The New York Times, reviewed Cupertino, a new CBS legal drama from Robert and Michelle King, the creators of The Good Wife, about a two-lawyer firm that sues the technology giants on behalf of the people they have hurt. Its premise, he wrote, is that “every legal case is a tech case now.” Its warning comes from a colleague of the hero: “During the gold rush, don’t sue the gold mine.” What struck Poniewozik most was the absence of government. In Cupertino, the state “may as well not exist except to keep the courts open,” which may simply be “a statement of where the real power lies in this world.” That is Kayyem’s argument as prime-time entertainment: when the legislators will not act, the courtroom is the only room left.

What survives both arguments is Kayyem’s reframing. When AI executives describe a potential catastrophe, they are not describing a product that slipped its leash. They are describing capabilities they built on purpose, deployed on purpose, and are now asking the public to trust them to manage voluntarily. Whether the fix is prosecution under old statutes or a new law with real teeth, the subject of scrutiny is the same: the people who built the thing, not the thing. That makes “going rogue,” the phrase running through this series since Part 93, a choice of words worth being more careful with from here on.
