I. The Rehearsal

On Tuesday, 15 September 2026, a Fox News correspondent posted a short video clip that told you almost everything you need to know about how this summit is being staged. Service members from several branches of the US military, dressed in ceremonial uniform, stood in formation on the White House grounds, running through a drill — an honour guard rehearsal, unmistakably built to mirror the welcome Beijing gave Donald Trump four months earlier, when a People’s Liberation Army honour guard performed in the square outside the East Gate of the Great Hall of the People while Trump watched and, later, called the marching “incredible.”

There is something almost too on-the-nose about a superpower rehearsing another superpower’s choreography for its own head of state, and I want to be careful not to overstate the novelty — honour guards are a standard fixture of any state visit, and the United States has hosted plenty of them without anyone accusing the Pentagon of imitation. But the timing and the framing here are doing real work. Trump’s public reaction to the Beijing ceremony in May wasn’t a diplomatic nicety; it was genuine, repeated enthusiasm, amplified across both his personal and the official White House social media accounts with a volume of photographs that read less like routine diplomatic documentation and more like a man who’d seen a show he wanted to put on himself. Four months later, the rehearsal footage suggests he’s getting his wish.

II. The Shrouds Come Off

The honour guard is not the only piece of stagecraft racing toward completion this month. Restoration work on the White House’s North Portico — the building’s iconic front columns, first completed in 1830 under Andrew Jackson — has been underway since June, the columns hidden under drapes and scaffolding since July while crews addressed what officials described as age and weather damage, alongside security upgrades and new doors on the Pennsylvania Avenue side. On the weekend of 5 September, workers removed the shrouds; by Tuesday the 8th, the scaffolding itself was coming down. The Washington Post’s framing of the timing was blunt and, I think, correct: Trump is preparing to show off his renovation work the moment Xi Jinping arrives to see it.

That is worth sitting with for a moment, because it inverts the entire logic of the Beijing summit’s staging. In May, China’s advantage was depth of history — Xi walked Trump through the Forbidden City and the Temple of Heaven, sites the Ming and Qing dynasties built centuries before either man’s country existed in its current form, and the message, delivered without saying it aloud, was continuity: we have been governing for a very long time, and we intend to keep doing it. Washington cannot compete with that kind of civilizational depth on command, so it has done the only thing available to it on a five-month timeline: renovate. The North Portico restoration is being finished in a hurry specifically so that Xi’s visit becomes the unveiling. It is stagecraft in a genuinely different register — not “look how old and enduring this is,” but “look what we just built for you.”

III. “China Has a Ballroom, and So Should the USA”

The line comes from a Truth Social post Trump wrote on 15 May 2026, the day after the Beijing banquet, accompanied by a photograph of himself and Xi walking outside the Great Hall of the People. “China has a Ballroom, and so should the U.S.A.!” he wrote, adding that the project was “under construction, ahead of schedule,” with an opening date he pinned, for the first time publicly, at September 2028.

That sentence is the entire thesis of this essay in miniature, and I don’t think I’m overreading it to say so. The White House ballroom — a $200 to $400 million project (reporting has varied on the final figure), 90,000 square feet, seating up to 650 people, more than triple the capacity of the existing East Room — was announced in July 2026 with backing from Trump and a group the administration called “patriot donors.” Construction required demolishing the East Wing, the building that had housed the First Lady’s offices since 1902, and that demolition proceeded before the usual regulatory review process for alterations to historic federal buildings had run its course — a sequencing problem that has since produced multiple lawsuits from preservation groups and, according to several former White House officials and presidential relatives, real anger at how the East Wing was treated. One construction worker was injured in an incident at the North Portico earlier this month and taken away by ambulance; the White House has offered little detail beyond calling the portico work “standard restoration.”

None of that will be resolved by 24 September. The ballroom itself — Trump’s answer to the Great Hall, the building he explicitly framed as China envy in his own words — will not open until roughly September 2028, two years after Xi’s state visit and conveniently timed to land near the very end of Trump’s second term. Which means the actual state dinner for Xi Jinping, the marquee social event of the entire visit, has to happen in whatever existing space the White House can offer, inside what is functionally a construction zone. Trump gets to extol the ballroom project to his guest of honour. He does not get to show it off finished. Xi, whose own government hosted Trump in a hall completed under the Qing dynasty and expanded in the 1950s to seat thousands, will instead see the excavation for the American equivalent, mid-build, behind a fence.

IV. The Dump and the Prop

Trump’s own language about the White House before he began renovating it is worth recording plainly, because it tells you how he understands the building’s function. In a Sunday post earlier this month, he described the White House as having been “in very [bad shape]” — a “dump,” in his word — before his renovations began. Whatever the merits of that assessment architecturally, it reveals the same instinct that produced the ballroom, the flagpoles he paid $100,000 of his own money to install in June, the magnolia tree he had removed as a safety hazard, and the North Portico’s rushed restoration: the White House, for this president, is not primarily a workplace or a historic trust held for future occupants. It is a stage set, and like any stage set it can always be improved before the next scene. One piece of commentary on the renovation, admittedly from a source with more attitude than rigour, put it about as well as I could manage myself: the White House “isn’t just a building. It’s a stage, a symbol, and now, apparently, a construction site.”

I’d add only this. In Beijing, the stagecraft pointed backward, toward five hundred years of continuous governance. In Washington, it points forward, toward a building that doesn’t exist yet, financed partly by donors, delayed past the visit it was ostensibly timed for, and photographed mid-construction as the best evidence available that something is, at least, under way. Both are performances. Only one of them is finished.

V. The South Lawn, Then and Now

It is worth remembering what the last Chinese state visit to Washington actually looked like, if only to measure how far the theatrical ambitions have grown. In September 2015, Xi’s arrival ceremony with Barack Obama was staged, as these things usually are, on the South Lawn: a formal arrival, a review of the troops, a nineteen-gun salute, the standard grammar of American state visits that had served every administration back to Eisenhower’s. It was dignified, unremarkable by design, and entirely uninterested in competing with anything China had staged for an American president. Obama’s White House was not, at that point, in the business of measuring its ceremonial output against Beijing’s.

Eleven years and one Trump presidency later, that comparison has become the entire point. The honour guard rehearsal isn’t simply “a welcome ceremony,” in the generic sense the phrase usually carries — it is a specific, documented attempt to reproduce the visual grammar of the PLA’s Great Hall performance, right down to formation and dress. Whether that improves on the 2015 baseline is a matter of taste; whether it represents a change in what American state ceremony is for is not really debatable. The South Lawn used to be where America received a guest. It is now, at least for this one visit, where America auditions.

VI. What the Lawsuits Are Actually About

I don’t want to let the ballroom litigation pass as a footnote, because it is the part of this story that will outlast the summit by years regardless of how 24 September goes. The lawsuits filed after the East Wing’s demolition are not, at bottom, arguments about aesthetics or even about the ballroom itself — plenty of past presidents have altered the White House complex, and McCrery Architects, the classical design firm Trump selected to lead the project, has a defensible pedigree for this kind of work. The objection that has stuck, voiced by preservation groups and echoed by former officials and even members of the Trump family, is procedural: that a structure serving as the First Lady’s office since 1902 was torn down before the review process historic federal buildings are supposed to receive had actually concluded. Process objections rarely generate the same headlines as aesthetic ones, but they tend to be the ones that hold up in court, and they are the ones likeliest to still be working their way through the system long after Xi Jinping has gone home and this series has moved on to whatever comes next on the calendar. Stagecraft photographs well. Procedure is what actually gets adjudicated.

VII. What the Rehearsal Can’t Fix

An honour guard can be drilled into readiness in a matter of days. A ballroom cannot. That mismatch is, in miniature, the whole difficulty of staging a rival superpower’s welcome ceremony on borrowed choreography: the parts that are easy to copy — the uniforms, the formation, the marching — get copied quickly and will very likely look impressive on 24 September. The parts that actually took China centuries to build, and that Trump’s own ballroom project has spent barely more than a year trying to replicate, are not available on a five-month notice period no matter how many donors sign the cheques.

Xi will walk into a White House with fresh columns and a rehearsed honour guard, and he will almost certainly be polite about all of it, the way an experienced guest is polite about a host’s home renovation regardless of what he actually thinks of the result. Whether the choreography reads as confidence or as compensation will depend less on how well the honour guard marches than on what gets said — and left unsaid — in the rooms where the actual business of the summit happens. That is where Part III of this series goes next: the quiet channel between Scott Bessent and He Lifeng, where an AI dialogue that once had its own dedicated track has been folded, almost apologetically, into an economic meeting that already had enough on its agenda.
