India has 22 official languages, six major religions, thousands of castes, 28 states that function as quasi-nations, and a political environment that divides citizens along every conceivable axis of identity. It has almost no shared cultural experiences — no Thanksgiving, no Super Bowl, no single narrative that the entire country participates in simultaneously.

Except two.

When India plays Pakistan in cricket, a billion people stop what they are doing and watch. When a major Bollywood film opens, it plays in every state, every language region, and every socioeconomic stratum simultaneously. These are the only two cultural forces in India that cut across every line the country draws within itself — caste, religion, language, region, class. They are not trivial. In a country where everything else divides, the things that unite are load-bearing.

This essay examines cricket and Bollywood not as entertainment but as infrastructure — the cultural binding agents of a nation that has almost no other shared space.

I. Cricket: The Religion That Actually Unifies

Cricket was brought to India by the British, which makes it, like the English language and the railway system, a colonial inheritance that the colonized have repurposed beyond the colonizer’s wildest imagination.

The British played cricket in India as a mark of civilizational superiority — the game was, in the colonial imagination, a manifestation of English character: fair play, discipline, the stiff upper lip. Indians were allowed to watch. Eventually, Indians were allowed to play — first in segregated teams organized by community (Hindus vs. Muslims vs. Parsees, a structure that tells you everything about how the Raj organized identity), and then, gradually, in mixed teams that began beating the British at their own game.

India’s first Test cricket victory against England came in 1952, five years after independence. The symbolism was not lost on anyone. The former colony had mastered the colonizer’s sport and defeated the colonizer on the field. Cricket in India has never been merely a game since.

What makes cricket unique as a binding agent is its reach. Football is regional — popular in Kerala, Goa, the Northeast, and West Bengal, but not a national obsession. Kabaddi is traditional but niche. Hockey was once India’s sport — India won eight Olympic gold medals in field hockey — but it has been eclipsed. Only cricket penetrates every corner of the country. A Test match or a World Cup game is watched in Gujarati living rooms and Tamil tea stalls and Punjabi truck stops and Kashmiri houseboats. The Indian Premier League — a Twenty20 franchise league launched in 2008 — is now the most valuable cricket league in the world, valued at over $16 billion, and its teams are organized by city, not by religion, caste, or language. When the Mumbai Indians play the Chennai Super Kings, the competition is sporting, not sectarian. This is not a small thing in a country where nearly every other form of organized competition — elections, university admissions, government jobs — is structured around identity.

The IPL has also become a vehicle for India’s soft power. It attracts players from every cricket-playing nation, is broadcast globally, and has turned Indian cricket into a commercial juggernaut that generates more revenue than most national cricket boards combined. The Board of Control for Cricket in India is the richest sporting body in the cricket world, and its financial leverage has reshaped the global governance of the game. India does not merely play cricket. It owns cricket — economically, culturally, and in terms of the sheer volume of eyeballs it delivers.

And then there is Pakistan. An India-Pakistan cricket match is not a sporting event. It is a geopolitical psychodrama watched by over a billion people, most of whom are experiencing emotions that have nothing to do with batting averages. The Partition wound, the four wars, the nuclear standoff, the family connections that still span the border — all of it is compressed into a cricket match that lasts a few hours and carries the emotional weight of decades. When India wins, the celebration is national catharsis. When India loses, the mourning is proportional. When the two teams play in a World Cup, the television audience regularly exceeds one billion viewers — the largest audience for any single sporting event on earth.

My roommates watch cricket. Of course they do. Rikesh follows the IPL with the analytical intensity of a man who works in IT — he tracks statistics, evaluates player performance, and has opinions about auction strategy that he shares whether or not I have asked. Gurneet watches with the passionate investment of a man whose identity is woven into the game — Punjab has produced some of India’s greatest fast bowlers, and Gurneet takes this personally. When India plays, the kitchen television is on, the chai is poured, and the five languages in my house reduce to one: the language of cricket, which requires no translation.

II. Bollywood: The Dream Factory

If cricket is India’s shared religion, Bollywood is its shared mythology.

The Hindi film industry — based in Mumbai, formerly Bombay, and nicknamed Bollywood in a portmanteau that its practitioners find both flattering and reductive — produces more films per year than Hollywood. It sells more tickets. It reaches a larger audience. And it does something that no other cultural product in India can do: it tells stories that people across the entire subcontinent watch, discuss, sing along to, and incorporate into their emotional lives regardless of where they come from or what language they speak at home.

The Bollywood formula — and it is a formula, refined over decades — combines romance, melodrama, music, dance, comedy, and moral resolution into a package that runs roughly three hours and includes four to six song-and-dance sequences. Western critics sometimes dismiss this as unsophisticated. They are missing the point. The formula works because it addresses, in a single product, the emotional needs of an audience that spans billionaires and rickshaw drivers, Hindi speakers and Tamil speakers, urban sophisticates and village farmers. The songs become the soundtrack of Indian life — played at weddings, blasted from auto-rickshaws, hummed in kitchens, and memorized by people who may not speak Hindi but who know every word of the latest hit.

Bollywood’s cultural power lies in its reach and its inclusiveness — or at least its historical inclusiveness. For most of its history, Hindi cinema was the one space in Indian public life where Muslim and Hindu creative talent collaborated without friction. The three Khans — Shah Rukh Khan, Aamir Khan, and Salman Khan — are Muslim actors who have been the biggest box-office draws in a Hindu-majority country for over three decades. The greatest music directors, lyricists, and playback singers in Bollywood history include Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs, Christians, and Parsis. A.R. Rahman — born Dileep Kumar, converted to Islam — composed the music for Slumdog Millionaire and is India’s most celebrated film composer. The industry’s creative ecology was, for decades, a living proof of concept for India’s secular ideal: talent mattered more than identity.

That consensus is under pressure. The rise of Hindu nationalism has produced a parallel trend in Hindi cinema: films that celebrate Hindu civilizational identity, that cast Muslims as villains or threats, and that align with the BJP’s cultural project. The Kerala Story, which claimed that thousands of Hindu and Christian women in Kerala had been converted to Islam and recruited by ISIS, was a box-office hit and a propaganda vehicle. The Kashmir Files, which dramatized the exodus of Kashmiri Hindus in the 1990s, was promoted by Modi himself. These films are commercially successful and ideologically pointed. They represent a departure from Bollywood’s traditional role as a space of inclusive fantasy and a turn toward cinema as a weapon in the culture war.

The three Khans — aging, still bankable, but increasingly cautious about expressing political opinions — are the canaries in the coal mine. Shah Rukh Khan, the most famous Indian on earth outside of Modi, has been targeted by Hindu nationalist groups, accused of anti-national sentiments, and subjected to social media campaigns calling for boycotts of his films. That the biggest movie star in the country — a man whose films have been watched by more human beings than those of any actor in history — must navigate his own identity with the caution of a diplomat tells you something about the direction of Indian public life.

III. Beyond Bollywood

Bollywood is not the only Indian film industry, and its dominance is being challenged.

The Telugu film industry — based in Hyderabad and sometimes called Tollywood — produced RRR, which won an Academy Award for Best Original Song in 2023 and became a global phenomenon. The Tamil industry — Kollywood — has produced some of the most innovative and politically engaged cinema in Asia. Malayalam cinema — from Kerala — has been described by international critics as the most consistently excellent film industry in the world on a per-capita basis. Kannada, Bengali, Marathi, and Punjabi cinema all have vibrant traditions.

The rise of regional cinema and streaming platforms has fragmented the audience that Bollywood once monopolized. An upper-middle-class viewer in Bangalore is as likely to be watching a Korean drama on Netflix or a Malayalam thriller on Amazon Prime as a Hindi blockbuster. The shared cultural space that Bollywood provided is narrowing — not disappearing, but narrowing — at the same moment that the political pressures on that space are intensifying.

This matters for the argument of the series. If cricket and Bollywood are among the few cultural forces that bind India together across its internal divisions, then the fragmentation of one and the politicization of the other represent a loss of binding capacity at precisely the moment when binding is most needed.

IV. The Soft Power Machine

Together, cricket and Bollywood constitute India’s most effective soft power — more effective than diplomatic summits, more persuasive than foreign aid, more visible than military hardware.

The Indian diaspora is held together by these two forces more than by any political institution. In my Toronto kitchen, the IPL and the latest Shah Rukh Khan film are reference points that Rikesh, Gurneet, and Ugyen share — Ugyen, who is Bhutanese, watches Bollywood films in Hindi and follows cricket because South Asian cultural space extends well beyond India’s borders. In diaspora communities from Southall to Scarborough to Jackson Heights, the screening of a major Bollywood release or the broadcast of an India cricket match functions as a communal gathering — a moment of shared identity in countries where Indian immigrants are otherwise dispersed across neighbourhoods, workplaces, and social networks that may have little in common.

The soft power extends beyond the diaspora. Bollywood films are watched across the Middle East, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa. Cricket connects India to the Caribbean, to Australia, to England, to Pakistan — connections that exist outside the framework of diplomacy and that operate at the level of emotion rather than strategy. When India hosts a World Cup or when a Bollywood film breaks records in markets from the Gulf to Malaysia, India is projecting an image of itself that no foreign ministry could manufacture: vibrant, creative, emotionally generous, and fundamentally attractive.

Whether that image survives the politicization of both cricket and cinema is an open question. The BCCI’s relationship with the BJP government is close — critics say too close. Bollywood’s turn toward Hindutva-aligned content threatens the inclusive fantasy that made it universal. The soft power machine runs on the same fuel as the democracy: pluralism. If the fuel changes, the machine produces something different.

V. What the Kitchen Watches

I asked Rikesh once why cricket mattered so much. He looked at me the way you look at someone who has asked why breathing matters. “It is the only time,” he said, “when everyone is Indian.”

He is right. And that is exactly why it matters for a series about democracy. Democracy requires a demos — a people who recognize themselves as belonging to the same political community. In most countries, that recognition is built on shared language, shared history, shared ethnicity. India has none of these. What it has is a constitution, a railway system, a cricket team, and a film industry that — at their best — tell a billion people that they belong to the same story.

The story is under revision. The revisionists want a narrower plot, a simpler cast, a single language, and a hero who looks like them. The question is whether the audience will accept the revision or demand the original.

In my kitchen, the original still plays. The IPL is on. The chai is poured. And for a few hours, the five languages, the three countries, the competing claims on the spice shelf — all of it dissolves into the shared spectacle of eleven men in white trying to hit a red ball with a piece of willow.

It is not enough to hold a country together. But it is not nothing.
