Every so often a manufacturer releases two products in the same week that are ostensibly built for the same purpose — registering displeasure — and yet perform so differently that a side-by-side becomes not just useful but necessary. This week, the White House’s Office of Presidential Feelings shipped exactly such a pair. We acquired both units through standard channels (they were simply broadcast, repeatedly, on every major network) and ran them through our lab.

PRODUCT ONE: THE UMBRELLA MODEL

Marketed use case: A federal prosecutor determines, after reviewing the evidence, that a case she filed against a 67-year-old retired Olympic canoeist for vandalizing the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool doesn’t hold up, and drops it, citing shoddy contractor work rather than criminal damage.

Deployment time: Under two hours. Jeanine Pirro filed her motion on a Friday. By Saturday the president was on Truth Social disagreeing “100 percent.” By Monday, in the Oval Office, on camera, unprompted, he had upgraded to calling her a woman who “choked” and “folded like an umbrella.” We would like to note for the record that an umbrella is an inanimate object with no legal training, and the comparison, while vivid, does not hold up under peer review any better than the original vandalism case did.

Volume: Extremely loud. Public, sustained, multi-platform, and delivered with the kind of personal wounded-ness typically reserved for reviews of restaurants that seated you near the kitchen.

Root cause of malfunction, per manufacturer: An “extremely unfriendly” judge. Not, notably, the underlying fact pattern, which suggested the pool’s lining had been botched during installation rather than clawed apart by a passing cyclist.

Consumer Reports rating: ★★★★★ for sheer performative commitment. ★☆☆☆☆ for proportionality, on the grounds that the underlying offense here is a cracked pool liner, and this unit responded as though someone had defaced a Rembrandt.

A NOTE FROM OUR ENGINEERING DIVISION

Our lab flags one additional finding, cross-referenced against the ongoing pattern log our sister publication keeps under the name The Baseline Problem — which, to be clear, counts documented behaviors rather than issuing any diagnosis. Both units this week showed the same recurring manufacturing quirks that log has tracked for nineteen prior installments. The Umbrella Model’s insult didn’t arrive once and stop; it perseverated — “choked,” then “folded like an umbrella,” then repeated substantially unchanged across at least three separate public appearances over two days, the way a scratched record repeats a bar rather than moving on to the next one. The underlying vandalism claim itself was a confabulation in the clinical sense the Baseline log uses the word: not a lie exactly, but a story assembled and then defended past the point the facts could support it, complete with capital letters for emphasis, as if volume were a substitute for evidence. And the insult arrived with zero apparent filter — unprompted, on camera, about a sitting U.S. Attorney, in a register most manufacturers reserve for enemies rather than employees. Whether any of that belongs on a spec sheet is a question above our pay grade. We note it here only because a pattern log exists for a reason, and this week gave it two more entries.

PRODUCT TWO: THE SKILLET MODEL

Marketed use case: A sitting congressman is accused, in police reports spanning a two-year custody battle, of holding a gun to his ex-wife’s head, throwing scalding water from a stovetop skillet onto her body, shoving her into a wall, and fracturing his toddler daughter’s collarbone. His own former father-in-law — a sitting Republican senator — publicly states that the congressman fails “any basic standards of character” required for office and needs professional help before being allowed near people.

Deployment time: We could not get a stopwatch on this one, because there was, functionally, nothing to time. The unit was delivered privately, over the phone, on a Monday night, and consisted of the words “things aren’t looking good,” in reference to the congressman’s polling. Earlier that same day, in public, on camera, the manufacturer had described the congressman as “a good person” and said he’d let “the families” sort it out — a phrase our lab was unable to distinguish from the phrase a landlord uses about a shared driveway dispute.

Volume: We had to turn our instruments up considerably to detect this one at all. At its loudest recorded moment, the unit generated a Speaker of the House saying things “don’t look good right now” before pivoting to a monologue about “decorum” that used the word without ever risking a verb.

Root cause of malfunction, per manufacturer: Not investigated by the manufacturer. The manufacturer’s own family members did the investigating, informally, over roughly two years, before going public out of what they described as no longer being able to keep the matter private.

Consumer Reports rating: We are declining to issue stars here, on the grounds that a five-point performative scale implies the product was, at some point, actually deployed, and the evidence suggests this unit mostly stayed in the box.

A SECOND NOTE FROM OUR ENGINEERING DIVISION

The Skillet Model’s quiet is worth logging too, because the Baseline pattern log has a category for it: suggestibility, the tendency for whichever voice spoke to the manufacturer most recently to become, briefly, the operative one. Here the last voice in the room wasn’t a family member with two years of documentation. It was Miller himself, on the phone, denying everything — and the manufacturer’s own polling instincts, which is what actually produced the words “things aren’t looking good.” Not the allegations. The numbers. A unit capable of loud, sustained, personally wounded output when its own narrative is at stake registered nothing louder than a consultant’s memo when someone else’s child was, allegedly, the one who got hurt. That’s not a contradiction in the product. Per the Baseline log, it’s the same mechanism running in the direction it usually runs: toward whatever protects the manufacturer, and quietly past whatever doesn’t.

We do not, as a matter of policy, editorialize about which of these products should concern a consumer more. We will simply note that one of them involved a pool, and one of them involved a household appliance being used as the alleged instrument of a documented injury to a small child, and only one of them generated the phrase “folded like an umbrella” from the office of the President of the United States.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: Why does the Umbrella Model make so much noise for what appears to be a modest structural defect?

A: Our engineers believe the Umbrella Model is calibrated not to the size of the underlying problem, but to whether the underlying problem was ever attached to the manufacturer’s own personal narrative. A pool the manufacturer publicly championed, turning out to be a construction failure rather than a crime, registers as a five-alarm event. We regret that we cannot offer a more satisfying technical explanation, because there isn’t one.

Q: Is the Skillet Model defective, or is it simply not designed to activate under these conditions?

A: Both, possibly. Our testing suggests the unit is fully capable of loud, public, sustained operation — see: the Umbrella Model, above — but appears to require, as a precondition, that the manufacturer himself feel embarrassed. Allegations involving a third party’s physical safety do not, on the evidence available, meet this threshold on their own.

Q: Does the manufacturer offer a bundle discount if I purchase both products together?

A: Functionally, yes — you are already receiving this bundle whether you ordered it or not. It arrives automatically, roughly once a week, and there is currently no way to opt out.

Q: What is the return policy on a congressman?

A: Unclear. As of this writing, the item remains listed as “not withdrawing” and the manufacturer has declined to process a return, citing an ongoing internal review that the item itself requested.

A NOTE ON THE WARRANTY

Both products, notably, come with the same warranty structure: whatever happens, the manufacturer reserves the right to decide, after the fact and entirely on his own authority, which incident was actually serious. Readers hoping for a consistent standard applied evenly across both cases — the same volume for the same magnitude of offense — should adjust their expectations accordingly, and possibly consult a different manufacturer entirely, one that does not appear to sell its loudest model exclusively to people who have embarrassed it personally.

PRODUCT RECALL NOTICE (VOLUNTARY, PARTIAL, UNLIKELY)

This bulletin is issued for informational purposes only and does not constitute an admission that either unit is currently being recalled, considered for recall, or will ever be recalled, by anyone, at any point.

Affected units: The Skillet Model, serial number Ohio’s 7th District, manufactured across at least two relationships spanning five years, with a documented prior incident (Grisham, 2021, settled 2023) that the manufacturer’s own quality control process failed to flag as a pattern until a family member outside the company raised it.

Known defects: Per multiple independent inspectors — a police report, a custody court, and a sitting United States senator who happens to be the unit’s own former father-in-law — the Skillet Model has been associated with a gun held to a head, scalding water, a shove into a wall, and a fractured collarbone belonging to a child too young to file her own report. The manufacturer’s official position is that no agency has ever substantiated these findings and no criminal charge has ever been filed, a distinction our legal department confirms is real, and also not remotely the same thing as a clean bill of health.

Corrective action: None issued. The unit remains on shelves. A House Ethics Committee review has been initiated at the unit’s own request, which our compliance team notes is roughly equivalent to a defendant filing his own subpoena.

Consumer guidance: If you are Republican leadership and are currently in possession of this product, our advice is the same advice we’d give about any item with a known, repeating defect pattern and no corrective action on file: say something, or don’t, but at least stop describing the silence as an ongoing investigation.

A NOTE ON AVAILABILITY

The Skillet Model remains, as of this writing, in active use. Its subject has declined to withdraw ahead of a Wednesday filing deadline, and has compared his own situation to the manufacturer’s 2016 campaign — a comparison that, read carefully, amounts to an admission that surviving an allegation and being cleared of one have, in this particular marketplace, become interchangeable so long as you outlast the news cycle. We cannot recommend this as a general consumer strategy. We note only that, on the evidence of this week, it appears to be working.

The Umbrella Model, meanwhile, is currently sitting in a drawer somewhere in the West Wing, its job status described by multiple outlets as safe “for now” — a phrase that, in our testing, has never once meant safe.

EDITOR’S NOTE

We want to be careful here, because a comparison chart is a blunt instrument and this is not, actually, a close call. One of these products is a pool. The other is a documented pattern of alleged violence against a spouse and a child, corroborated across two separate relationships five years apart, that a sitting Republican senator felt compelled to describe publicly, at real personal cost, because nobody else with the standing to say something was going to. We built this review around the format of a product comparison because the actual comparison — a prosecutor’s accuracy versus a congressman’s alleged violence — deserves to be seen for what it is: not two roughly equivalent scandals requiring separate but comparable levels of concern, but one real scandal and one manufactured one, and a White House that has, so far, reserved its only outward emotion for the manufactured one. That is the whole review. Everything above it is just the packaging.
