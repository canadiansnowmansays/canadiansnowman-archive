The ruling landed on May 29th, 2026. John Fitzgerald Kennedy’s birthday. Whether Judge Christopher R. Cooper of the Federal District Court in Washington appreciated the symmetry is not recorded. What is recorded — all ninety-four pages of it — is one of the more remarkable legal documents produced in the long and increasingly exhausting project of telling Donald Trump what he cannot do.

The case concerned eighteen letters. Specifically, the eighteen letters that had been affixed to the white marble facade of the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts in Washington — letters spelling out THE DONALD J. TRUMP AND THE — inserted before the existing name like a man elbowing his way to the front of a photograph. The board of the Kennedy Center, stacked with Trump loyalists, had voted to rename the institution in December 2024. The letters were on the building within a day. The new name appeared on the website, on signs throughout the building, and on the television broadcast of the Kennedy Center Honors, the organization’s signature event. Trump, who serves as board chairman, seemed pleased.

Judge Cooper was less so.

The Donald J. Trump and The John F. Kennedy Memorial Center. Eighteen letters added in a day. Ordered removed on JFK’s birthday by federal court.

The 1964 law that created the Kennedy Center was, he wrote, “crystal clear.” Congress had named the building. Only Congress could rename it. The board — however loyal, however unanimous in its enthusiasm — did not have the authority. “Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name,” the judge wrote, “and only Congress can change it.” He ordered the letters removed within two weeks.

Trump’s response was immediate, voluminous, and entirely predictable. He took to social media to announce that he was directing the Commerce Department to transfer the Kennedy Center back to Congress. “Based on the fact that the Radical Left Democrats care more about opposing your favorite President, ME,” he wrote, in a sentence that deserves its own chapter in the literature of self-reference, than “saving a dying Performing Arts Center.” If he couldn’t have it, Congress could deal with it. Take your building. Take your marble. He was finished.

He was not, of course, finished. The Kennedy Center’s board indicated it would appeal. The letters remained, temporarily, while the legal machinery ground on. But for a moment — on Kennedy’s birthday, in a federal courthouse — the answer had been no. And the ferocity of the reaction told you everything you needed to know about how rarely that word lands in Donald Trump’s world, and how much it costs him when it does.

The board that voted for the renaming deserves a brief inventory, because its composition is itself a statement about how the institution had been transformed. It included Susie Wiles, Trump’s White House chief of staff. Dan Scavino, one of his most trusted personal advisers. Usha Vance, wife of the Vice President. Laura Ingraham of Fox News. Maria Bartiromo of Fox Business. Attorney General Pamela Bondi. These are not, as a group, people whose primary qualification for overseeing a performing arts centre is their knowledge of the performing arts. They are people whose primary qualification is their loyalty to the man who appointed them. The vote to rename the building was unanimous. Representative Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democrat and ex officio board member, disputed that characterization — she said she had been muted on the call when she attempted to object. The unanimity, like so much else in this story, was a managed performance.

The people who had the most obvious standing to object — the Kennedy family — were not on the board and were not consulted. JFK’s niece Maria Shriver called the renaming “beyond comprehension.” JFK’s grandson Jack Schlossberg and Robert F. Kennedy’s grandson Joe Kennedy III both spoke out publicly in anger. The family whose patriarch the building was created to honour, whose name it had carried for sixty years, whose grief had prompted Congress to act with unusual speed in 1964 — they watched the letters go up from outside.

Inside the administration, there was one Kennedy. Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — RFK Jr., the Health and Human Services Secretary, nephew of the assassinated president, son of another assassinated Kennedy, a man who carries the most freighted surname in American political history — was asked by a CBS News correspondent whether he understood why his family members were upset.

“Of course, I understand it,” he said. “But I have bigger fish to fry.”

He added that saving children from obesity and heart disease was more important to him than the name on a building. He confirmed he had not been involved in the renaming. He confirmed he had not advised against it. He declined to say whether he objected to it.

There is a word for the position RFK Jr. occupied in that exchange, and the word is trapped. He serves in the cabinet of the man who renamed his uncle’s memorial. He cannot call it wrong without undermining his employer. He cannot call it right without betraying three generations of family history. So he pivoted to children’s health, deployed the language of larger priorities, and left the question unanswered in a way that answered it completely.

The Kennedy family did not get a vote on what happened to John Kennedy’s memorial. The one Kennedy in the room said he had bigger fish to fry.

The letters went up anyway.

This is a series about a name. More precisely, it is a series about what a man does when a name — his name, specifically — becomes the primary project of his life.

It is not a simple story. Simple stories do not require ten essays. What makes the Trump naming compulsion worth sustained examination is that it operates on multiple levels simultaneously: the psychological, the commercial, the political, and the constitutional. Each level illuminates the others. The gold letters on the Kennedy Center marble are related to the gold letters on Trump Tower on Fifth Avenue, which are related to the gold letters on the proposed $250 bill that the Treasury Secretary held up at a White House briefing last week like a game show host revealing the grand prize. The thread running through all of it — the towers, the Bible, the warship, the arch, the peace institute, the currency — is not vanity in the ordinary sense. It is something older and stranger and more desperate than vanity.

It is the sound a man makes when he is not sure he exists unless he can see his name on something solid.

The psychological literature on this compulsion is substantial, and it begins, as so much Trump psychology does, not with Trump himself but with the city he spent his life trying to conquer.

Donald Trump grew up in Jamaica Estates, Queens — outer borough, comfortable, solidly prosperous, and approximately as far from the Manhattan establishment as you can get while still being able to see the skyline. His father Fred Trump was a successful builder of middle-income housing in Brooklyn and Queens, a man who accumulated genuine wealth and genuine competence and genuine respect within a world that was, by the standards of the island across the river, invisible. Fred Trump built things people needed. He did not put his name on them in letters large enough to read from the street.

His son noticed.

Trump family home in Queens

Fred Trump’s buildings were brick. Functional, well-made, respected within their world, and not one of them gold. The aesthetic of the outer boroughs was durability and utility — you built things that worked, that lasted, that sheltered people. You did not gild them. Gilding was what the other side of the river did, or rather, what the other side of the river conspicuously did not do, because the truly established Manhattan money had learned long ago that gold is the colour of wanting to be noticed, and the truly powerful do not need to be noticed. They are simply there. The Rockefellers built Rockefeller Center in limestone and dark bronze. The Vanderbilts favoured marble and mahogany. Old money whispers. It does not plate its escalators.

Donald Trump plated his escalators.

Trump Tower atrium, Fifth Avenue. The gold was never subtle. It was the point.

The gold was not a design choice. It was an argument. It said: I am here, I am significant, I am worth more than the brick buildings in Queens, and if you find this excessive then perhaps the problem is your taste and not my towers. The gold was the aesthetic of aspiration rather than arrival — Vegas, Atlantic City, the lobby of a casino built by a man who had studied what palaces looked like in picture books and then built one from memory. The establishment Trump was trying to impress found it, predictably, vulgar. Which is the precise word they used, in precisely the tone that ensured he would add another floor of gold cladding to whatever he built next. The rejection fed the compulsion. The compulsion produced more gold. The gold produced more rejection. This loop has been running for fifty years and shows no sign of resolution.

The move from outer borough builder to Manhattan brand was not accidental. It was a deliberate act of class warfare, conducted in gold leaf. When Donald Trump acquired the option on the Commodore Hotel on 42nd Street in 1975 and began the negotiations that would eventually produce the Grand Hyatt New York, he was not simply doing a real estate deal. He was announcing himself to a city that had not asked to be introduced. The Trump name on a Manhattan building was not a description. It was a declaration.

The Manhattan establishment — old money, inherited status, the kind of people whose names appear on hospital wings without requiring gold lettering — was never fully impressed. They tolerated him. They attended his parties. They occasionally did business with him. They did not, in the ways that mattered to him, accept him. The social historians of New York in the 1980s have documented this comprehensively: Trump was always slightly too loud, slightly too gold, slightly too insistent on his own importance for the rooms he most wanted to inhabit. He was nouveau riche in a city that understood the distinction between money and class with surgical precision.

This is not simply biographical background. It is the engine of everything that follows.

Psychologists who have written about Trump’s relationship to his own name — and there are more of them than you might expect — tend to cluster around a concept sometimes called symbolic compensation: the use of external objects, achievements, or markers to offset internal feelings of inadequacy or anxiety. The gold tower on Fifth Avenue compensates for the slight of not being invited to the right dinner tables. The name on the building says what the establishment would not: you matter, you are here, you cannot be ignored.

This is not, it should be noted, an exclusively Trumpian phenomenon, or even an exclusively male one. Midlife crisis behaviour — the sports car, the radical reinvention, the impulsive acquisition of things that announce a self — is documented across genders and cultures, and the underlying dynamic is identical regardless of who is experiencing it: anxiety about mortality, relevance, and legacy expressing itself through display. The sports car says I am still vital. The trophy acquisition says I have not been passed by. The name on the building says I was here.

What distinguishes Trump’s version from the ordinary midlife reckoning is the absence of any satiation point. The healthy version of symbolic compensation has an endpoint. You buy the motorcycle. You score a hundred percent on the test. You ride it for a few years, and eventually the anxiety that drove the purchase resolves into something more durable. The wound heals.

Trump’s wound, if the evidence of the last four decades is any guide, does not heal. Which is why there is always another building, another product, another institution, another denomination of currency. The compulsion doesn’t resolve because the underlying need doesn’t resolve. Each new name — on the tower, on the perfume bottle, on the $250 bill, on the marble facade of the Kennedy Center — is not a satisfaction. It is a temporary relief from a permanent hunger.

And the towers are always gold. This is not incidental. The gold thread runs through the entire catalogue this series will examine — the Trump Tower lobby with its gold escalators and gold elevators and gold everything, the gold sneakers sold under the name Never Surrender in a colour that matched the lobby precisely, the proposed $250 bill whose mock-up Bessent held up at the White House podium last Thursday, its ground colour unmistakably gold rather than the functional green of ordinary American currency. The Kennedy Center marble armrests Trump posted on Truth Social — “unlike anything ever done or seen before” — will, one suspects, not be beige. Mar-a-Lago, the self-described Winter White House, is the interior design of a man who decided that if the establishment wouldn’t let him in, he would build his own establishment and cover it in the one colour they had always silently agreed to avoid.

Trump Force One. 24-karat gold plating on seatbelt buckles, faucets, and door handles. A dedicated compartment for Oreo cookies. The complete portrait.

The gold does not stay on the ground. Trump Force One — his customized Boeing 757, acquired in 2011 from Microsoft co-founder Paul Allen and refurbished at a reported cost of $37 million — is the gold aesthetic made airborne. The interior features 24-karat gold plating on the seatbelt buckles, the door handles, and the bathroom faucets. Italian leather seats are embroidered with the Trump family crest. The walls are clad in precious wood and silk. The master bathroom has a gold sink. The aircraft was dubbed Trump Force One by his supporters during the 2016 campaign, when Trump held rallies in airport hangars across America with the plane — TRUMP painted across the fuselage in letters large enough to read from the far end of the hangar — deployed as a backdrop. The name and the gold travelled together at 35,000 feet, landing in whatever city needed to see them, taking off again before the crowd had dispersed.

The designer who oversaw the refurbishment recalled one request that stood apart from the gold fixtures and the silk walls. Trump wanted a dedicated compartment in the galley for Oreo cookies. “I said to him, ‘Mr. Trump, really?’” the designer told Forbes. Trump’s reply was straightforward: “I love Oreos.”

The gold faucet and the Oreo compartment are the complete portrait. The first is the statement the world is meant to see. The second is the man behind it.

There is a substantial psychoanalytic literature on gold as a symbolic substance — on what it means when a man must surround himself with it, must see it reflected back from every surface, must insist on it even when, especially when, everyone around him finds it excessive. This series will not spend much time in that literature. The observable facts are sufficient. The name is always gold where possible. The buildings are always clad in it where feasible. The currency, if the legislation passes, will be printed on a gold ground. The man from Queens, who grew up in brick, has spent his entire adult life trying to turn the world around him the colour of money — not the functional green of working currency, but the ancient, monarchical, undeniable yellow of gold. The colour that announces, without words, that the person who chose it believes himself to be worth more than the room he is standing in.

George Washington, who could have covered Mount Vernon in gold leaf had he chosen to, did not.

He thought it would look like a monarchy.

He was right.

The gold compulsion extended everywhere — even to sneakers sold under the name Never Surrender.

The psychological concept of compensatory masculinity — the tendency of men who feel their status or dominance is threatened to over-perform dominant behaviours — is well established in the academic literature. It predicts, with some accuracy, the gold towers, the relentless superlatives (biggest, greatest, most beautiful, like nothing ever seen before), the obsession with crowd sizes, ratings, and the metrics of being witnessed. These are not the behaviours of a man secure in his own significance. They are the behaviours of a man perpetually auditioning for a role he believes, at some unexamined level, he has not yet been awarded.

There is one documented first-person account of Trump in a private setting stripped of audience, performance, and the protective apparatus of brand management — Stormy Daniels’ memoir. What it describes, beneath the tabloid surface, is not primarily a story about sex. It is a story about a man who needed, repeatedly and insistently, to be told he was wonderful. The checking. The seeking of reassurance. The anxious monitoring of the other person’s response. These are not the behaviours of a man secure in his own significance. They are the behaviours of a man who had constructed an entire public architecture of dominance and found, in the absence of the audience, that the architecture did not hold. The name on the Kennedy Center marble and the need for reassurance in that hotel room are, at some level, the same request: Tell me I am here. Tell me I matter. Tell me the name means something.

This is not, in the end, a story about arrogance. Arrogant men do not need reassurance. This is a story about a wound so old and so unexamined that its owner has spent fifty years building towers around it, hoping that eventually the towers will be tall enough to fill the space where the wound is.

They are not. They never are. Which is why there is always another tower.

The historian Jeffrey Engel of Southern Methodist University, speaking to the New York Times about Trump’s habit of naming federal institutions after himself while still in office, chose his words with the careful restraint of a man addressing a tenure committee. “Throughout Western history,” he said, “the idea of commemorating and adulating yourself has been considered gauche.”

Gauche. One of the great understatements in recent academic commentary.

What Engel was too diplomatic to say, and what this series will spend ten essays demonstrating, is that the self-commemoration impulse currently remaking Washington — the arch, the ballroom, the currency, the Kennedy Center, the peace institute, the warships, the parks passes, the baby savings accounts, the prescription drug website — represents something qualitatively different from the ordinary presidential legacy-building that has always attended the American executive. Past presidents have their names on buildings, ships, scholarships, and currency — but as a rule, they waited. The Truman Building took forty-seven years. The Eisenhower Executive Office Building took thirty-eight. Kennedy, memorialized with unusual speed in the grief of assassination, had his name on the performing arts centre within a year of his death.

Trump has two federal buildings named after him, a class of warships, a new denomination of currency in design, his signature on every dollar bill in circulation, and his face proposed for Mount Rushmore — all while still in office, all while the legal challenges accumulate, all while the courts produce their crystal-clear rulings and the judges celebrate dead presidents’ birthdays by telling the living one what he cannot have.

The Romans, who invented the triumphal arch as a form, had rules about this. A general was awarded a triumphus — the formal procession, the arch, the public inscription of his glory — only after a significant military victory, and only by vote of the Senate. The honour was deliberately earned. Deliberately constrained. Deliberately temporary. The arch was not self-awarded.

Washington, the father of the American republic and a man who had considerably more claim to triumphal architecture than any subsequent president, declined to put his face on currency because he thought it smacked of monarchy. The tradition he established held for a hundred and sixty years, through wars and depressions and the full catalogue of American crisis and achievement.

Mount Vernon, George Washington’s estate. The founder could have covered it in gold. He chose restraint instead

It held until a man from Queens, who never quite got the invitation to the right dinner table, decided that what the republic needed for its two hundred and fiftieth birthday was his face on a $250 bill — a denomination invented for the occasion, in a denomination that doesn’t legally exist, requiring an act of Congress to create, breaking a prohibition that Washington himself had understood without needing anyone to explain it.

The Treasury Secretary held up the mock-up at the White House podium last Thursday. He said there was nothing untoward about it.

He said this with a straight face.

He may even have believed it.

This series will move, over the next nine essays, through the full catalogue of the compulsion. The business model that turned the name into a commercial product — and what happened to that product when it became a political symbol. The two Canadian towers that failed, were quietly bought out, and became a St. Regis and a Paradox while Trump said nothing, because the name was a contract and not a monument. The physical remaking of Washington — the arch that grows every time someone measures it, the ballroom that doubles in cost every fiscal quarter, the Kennedy Center that required a federal court to restore a dead president’s name on his own memorial. The Institute of Peace, gutted and renamed in the same motion, its staff removed by police and its walls repainted before the litigation was finished. The branded life — the Bible, the sneakers, the cologne, the university, the trading cards — each one a different facet of the same project of self-inscription. And finally, the question that history will eventually answer with more finality than any federal judge: what will the name be worth, when the man who needed it everywhere is gone?

We begin, as the story begins, with the letters on the marble. Eighteen of them. Added in a day. Ordered removed on a dead president’s birthday by a judge who had read the law and found it crystal clear.

Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name.

Only Congress can change it.

The man who needed his name in gold is learning, one ruling at a time, that some walls were built before he arrived, and will stand after he leaves.

The letters are coming down.

Donald Trump has spent a lifetime writing his name on things. Towers. Golf courses. Hotels. A university. A Bible. A perfume called Success. A peace institute he gutted before the paint on the letters dried. A performing arts centre dedicated to a murdered president he has spent his entire career trying to impress.

The name is always the same: gold where possible, large where mandatory, permanent where permitted, and imposed where neither permission nor permanence was ever his to grant.

This series is about what that compulsion reveals — psychologically, politically, historically, and occasionally comically. It is not a flattering portrait.

Then again, neither is a loading dock on Bay Street.
