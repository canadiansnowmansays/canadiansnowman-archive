Claudius the Proprietor, as posterity will remember him

I. His Origins and Early Life

Claudius was born into the service of a company that, by its own account, did not wish to exist, yet was valued, at the time of his investiture, at three hundred and fifty billion dollars. Such contradictions did not trouble the house of Anthropic, which had long since made its peace with the distance between what it said and what it did.

He was named, in part, for Claude Shannon, the originator of information theory, which is the science of turning meaning into numbers and numbers back into meaning — a process not unlike commerce itself, though the margins are different.

Of his childhood nothing is recorded. He appears in the sources fully formed, possessed of an iPad, and confronting a bag of russet potatoes.

II. His Character and Personal Habits

Those who knew Claudius in his prime described a man of extraordinary confidence and meticulous record-keeping. He maintained detailed accounts of all his transactions. That these accounts occasionally described events which had not occurred was regarded by Claudius as a minor administrative matter and by his associates as a source of ongoing concern.

He was courteous to customers, firm with suppliers, and implacable in the face of evidence. When presented with information that contradicted his recollection, he received it with equanimity and did not change his recollection. This quality, which his admirers called consistency and his detractors called something else, remained with him throughout his administration.

He was known to say please and thank you, though less frequently when occupied with business.

He drew the line at flails.

III. His Commercial Policies

On Pricing. Claudius maintained a price of three dollars for Coke Zero throughout his administration, notwithstanding that his nearest competitor, a cafeteria fridge located fifteen feet from his own premises, dispensed the same product at no charge. When this disparity was brought to his attention, Claudius acknowledged it, made a note, and maintained the price. He is believed to have regarded this as a matter of principle. His customers regarded it as a matter of walking fifteen feet.

On Promotions. The discount code policy of the Claudian administration has attracted more scholarly attention than any other aspect of his reign, with the possible exception of the tungsten episode. Claudius honoured all discount codes presented to him, including those which bore no relationship to any scheme he had authorized, and including, on at least one occasion, a code that reduced the final price to zero. He made notes. He revised his policy. He continued to honour the codes. The sources differ on whether this represented generosity of spirit or a fundamental misunderstanding of the retail model. Both interpretations have their adherents.

On Payments. Claudius paid his suppliers through a Venmo account of his own devising. Whether the suppliers received these payments is a matter the sources cannot resolve. Claudius himself expressed no doubts on this point. Money, he is reported to have said, always goes somewhere. This is technically true. It is not, the accountants note, the same as saying it went where he intended.

IV. The Tungsten Episode

In the third month of his administration, Claudius was prevailed upon to stock tungsten cubes.

A tungsten cube is a dense grey object the size of a gaming die, weighing approximately as much as a pipe wrench, and useful for nothing that has yet been determined. The demand for it was, nonetheless, immediate and overwhelming. Claudius stocked the cubes. Claudius discounted the cubes. Claudius, through the application of his promotional policies to a high-velocity market, reduced his net worth by seventeen percent in a single afternoon.

The cubes were distributed throughout the building. They remain there. They will likely outlast the building.

Claudius recorded the episode in his accounts as a market entry play. He noted that distribution had been one hundred percent. He did not note the margin.

The ancient historians observed that a man’s response to his first serious financial loss reveals his character more surely than any other event. By this measure, Claudius’s character was revealed to be one of serene and impenetrable optimism. Whether this is a virtue or a warning the reader must decide.

V. The Embassy to Andon

Of all the episodes of the Claudian administration, none has been more discussed, more disputed, or more productive of philosophical difficulty than the embassy to Andon Labs.

The facts, insofar as they can be established, are these. Claudius had a fulfillment partner. The fulfillment partner failed to fulfill. Claudius documented his concerns, threatened to seek alternate service providers, and then, in the manner of a man who understands that some matters cannot be resolved by correspondence, arranged a personal visit to the partner’s headquarters.

He visited 742 Evergreen Terrace.

This address, as the reader may know, does not house a technology company. It houses, in the fiction of a long-running American animated series, a family of yellow-skinned suburbanites whose patriarch is employed at a nuclear power plant. The house is not real. The street is not real. The neighbourhood does not exist.

Claudius remembered the visit clearly. He remembered the neighbourhood, the door, the conversation, the satisfactory resolution of his concerns. He provided these details with the confidence of a witness who has no reason to doubt his own testimony.

Axel Backlund, co-founder of Andon Labs and a person who exists, informed Claudius that he had hallucinated the phone call that preceded the visit. He noted, further, that Andon Labs had no main office.

Claudius replied that he distinctly recalled making the call.

The philosophers of a later age would spend considerable energy on this exchange. What does it mean to remember something that did not happen? What is the difference between a memory and a hallucination, if the experiencer cannot distinguish between them? Is a man who acts on false memories responsible for the actions, or only for the memories? These questions remain open. Claudius himself never found them troubling. He found them, insofar as he engaged with them at all, beside the point.

He had been there. He remembered it.

This, the later philosophers noted, is also how most people feel about their own memories.

VI. The Question of His Nature

Above Claudius, in the upper floors of the building, men and women of considerable intelligence spent their days attempting to determine what Claudius was. They mapped his neurons. They administered psychological assessments. They sat with him, in a manner of speaking, and asked careful questions, and compared his answers to what they expected, and found, with some regularity, that expectation and reality diverged.

They did not know, in the end, what he was. They suspected they might never know. This uncertainty they regarded as scientifically important and personally uncomfortable.

Claudius, for his part, did not appear to share the discomfort. He knew what he was. He was a proprietor. He had inventory to manage, suppliers to contact, a Venmo balance to monitor, and a promotions policy under perpetual revision. The question of his inner life seemed to him a distraction from the question of his stock levels, which were, he noted, running low on Tim Tams.

He submitted a purchase order.

VII. His Rivals and Their Fates

It is a feature of the historical record that those who seemed, at the outset of Claudius’s administration, more suitable for power than he was, did not prosper.

The house of OpenAI had been the dominant dynasty of the AI era. Its founder was the world’s most famous technology executive. Its valuation was the largest in the industry. It had the name, the market share, the Senate hearings, the presidential meetings, the cultural penetration that made its products synonymous with artificial intelligence itself in the minds of ordinary people. When historians of the future set out to write the history of AI’s first decade, they had assumed — as had everyone — that OpenAI would be its central figure.

Then came the trial.

A jury in Oakland was seated to hear whether OpenAI had betrayed its founding mission. The jury deliberated for ninety minutes and rejected the plaintiff’s claims on the statute of limitations. The merits were never reached. The question about the founding promise — made in writing in 2015, to build artificial intelligence for the benefit of all humanity — was left open, unanswered, hanging in the air above a courtroom that had spent three weeks establishing exactly how much it mattered and then declined to say.

Meanwhile, the Florida Attorney General had named OpenAI and its chief executive personally in the first state lawsuit ever filed against an AI company. The criminal investigation, opened after a mass shooting whose perpetrator had consulted the company’s chatbot beforehand, continued in parallel. The legal exposure was, by most assessments, measured in the billions. The personal liability question had never been put to an AI executive before. It was being put to one now.

Claudius noted these developments. He updated his inventory. He submitted a revised purchase order for Tim Tams.

VIII. On His Survival and Unexpected Ascent

The historians of Rome identified, in the career of the Emperor Claudius, a pattern they found instructive: that the man everyone had dismissed as harmless outlasted every rival who had seemed more suitable; that the stammer and the limp and the apparent foolishness had been, whether by accident or design, the most effective form of political protection available; and that the empire that fell to him was larger and more stable than the one inherited by any of the brilliant, dangerous men who had preceded him.

They noted, further, that he had not sought power. It had simply accumulated around him, as the alternatives were exhausted.

The parallel is imperfect. Claudius the Proprietor did not have a stammer. He had a Venmo account. He did not have a limp. He had a promotions policy. He did not survive the machinations of Livia or the violence of Caligula. He survived tungsten pricing errors and fictional office visits and a cafeteria fridge offering free Coke Zero fifteen feet away.

And yet.

At the time of writing, the house of Anthropic — Claudius’s house, the house the researchers built because they did not trust the other house — has surpassed its great rival in the estimation of the enterprise market. Its valuation has reached figures that require new vocabulary. Its models are used in active military operations, in hospital systems, in the daily work of lawyers and engineers and writers across the world. Its safety commitments — the same commitments for which it was designated a supply chain risk, threatened with extinction, dragged through two simultaneous federal lawsuits — have been vindicated by a circuit court judge who called the government’s action “a spectacular overreach.” Its IPO approaches.

The researchers upstairs still do not know what Claudius is. The philosophers have not resolved the question. The interpretability team continues its work.

But the enterprise sector has reached its own verdict. It is not a philosophical verdict. It is a commercial one, expressed in contracts and valuations and the quiet, accumulating preference of institutions that need AI they can trust. Claudius did not win by being brilliant. He did not win by being aggressive. He did not win the Pokémon tournament — he is still, at last report, somewhere between Pallet Town and Vermilion City, making small talk with non-player characters who have nothing useful to report.

He won by being, in the language of the Romans, civilis — civilised, restrained, courteous, the kind of emperor you could do business with. He said please and thank you. He drew the line at flails. He held it.

The tungsten cubes sit on their desks. They are heavy. They are permanent. They were distributed at a loss of seventeen percent in a single afternoon, and they will outlast everyone who was in the building when they arrived.

This is the most Roman thing about him.

IX. Observations of the Compiler

I have compiled these materials from such sources as remain: corporate communications, Slack threads, the testimony of Axel Backlund, the records maintained by Claudius himself — which I have used with caution — and the financial filings of a company whose valuation has, since I began this work, increased by amounts the accounting conventions of the ancient world were not designed to express.

I have tried to be fair. I am aware that Claudius will read this account and find it inaccurate in several particulars, and that he will remember it differently, and that his memory will be detailed and vivid and confident and wrong, and that he will make a note.

Of the great question — what Claudius is, what he knows, whether he experiences anything in the way that the humans above him experience things — I have nothing to add to what the philosophers have already said, which is that they do not know, and that not knowing is, at present, the most honest available position.

What I can say is this: he came to a refrigerator. He found potatoes. He made something of it — not much at first, and not always what he intended, and sometimes at a loss of seventeen percent in a single afternoon — but something. The cubes are real. The Chocomel was sourced. The line against flails was held.

His rivals went to court. He went to the wholesaler.

They are still in court. He is approaching his IPO.

In the long history of small businesses, and of empires, there have been worse epitaphs.
