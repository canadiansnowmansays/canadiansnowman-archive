Every organisation chart is a promise. It says: if something goes wrong at three in the morning, this is the person who answers the phone, and this is who that person answers to. In wartime the promise is the whole point of the chart.

As of September 30, 2026, the Department of Defense’s chart has some unusual features. The Army, the largest of the services, has no Senate-confirmed chief of staff and no Senate-confirmed secretary. The Navy has no Senate-confirmed secretary. All three posts were emptied by Pete Hegseth in 2026, during a war with Iran. This instalment counts the chairs.

The Performance

The Secretary explains the empty chairs as a feature, not a fault. “You can’t change a culture with the same people who enabled it,” he told the troops at Quantico on September 30, 2026. “Personnel is policy at every single level.”

It is a crisp line, and it would be a defensible one if the chairs had been filled. The performance of a purge is the firing: decisive, televised, announced on social media, frequently before the person being fired has been told. The work of a purge is the replacement: choosing successors, briefing senators, securing votes, and getting the chain of command standing again before anyone notices it was down. Hegseth has mastered the first part. On the record so far, he has barely begun the second.

The Paperwork

Chair one: the Army’s chief of staff. On April 2, 2026, five weeks into the Iran war, Hegseth removed Gen. Randy George as Army Chief of Staff, effective immediately. As Drew F. Lawrence reported for DefenseScoop in “Hegseth ousts Gen. George as Army chief of staff,” no reason was given, and George’s four-year term was cut short by more than a year. Gen. Christopher LaNeve, the vice chief and a former commander of the 82nd Airborne Division, became acting chief.

He has stayed acting. As Ellie Cook reported for Newsweek on August 30, 2026, in “Pete Hegseth Faces Questions as Army Sets Leadership Vacancy Record,” the Army had then gone 150 days without a confirmed chief of staff. The last comparable gap was in 1972, when Gen. Bruce Palmer served about three months as acting chief. The Army has now gone twice as long, in the middle of a shooting war.

The reason is not Democratic obstruction. The administration planned to nominate LaNeve formally in May 2026, but held back after Senator Roger Wicker of Mississippi, the Republican chairman of the Armed Services Committee, signalled that the votes were not there. Senator Joni Ernst of Iowa and other Republicans on the committee have been reluctant to confirm him. Their stated objection is experience: LaNeve had held four-star rank for only about six months, while previous Army chiefs have usually served at least eighteen months as four-star officers before taking the top job. Because Republicans hold the committee by a narrow margin, a nominee needs every one of them.

Chair two: the Army’s secretary. The Army’s civilian head, Dan Driscoll, resigned on August 31, 2026. As Luis Martinez reported for ABC News in “Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigns amid tensions with Hegseth,” his relationship with Hegseth had deteriorated over the firing and sidelining of senior officers slated for promotion, and over the dismissal of George, a close colleague. Driscoll told Congress he did not know the reasons for George’s firing.

Driscoll was not a minor figure. He knew Vice President JD Vance from Yale Law School, had good relationships on Capitol Hill, and had been sent to lead talks on Ukraine and Russia in 2025, an unusual job for a service secretary. As Part I described, he had also refused to strike four officers from the Army’s one-star promotion list, and Hegseth struck them himself. When a secretary with that much political capital walks out, it is not a personnel matter. It is a verdict.

Adam Telle is now acting Army secretary. The man Hegseth wants in the job is Sean Parnell, his chief spokesman and senior adviser. Parnell has not been formally nominated, and he faces an uphill fight, according to Foreign Policy Journal’s account of the Senate opposition on September 28, 2026, “Hegseth’s Army Secretary Pick Sean Parnell Faces Republican Resistance And Senate Opposition.” Tillis has said publicly that the Pentagon needs “less of an echo chamber.” Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota has called the idea “a bridge too far,” citing Parnell’s record. Senators Dave McCormick of Pennsylvania and Tim Sheehy of Montana have spoken up for him. Other names have been floated, including Representative Brian Mast of Florida. The Army waits.

Chair three: the Navy’s secretary. On April 22, 2026, Hegseth fired the Navy secretary, John Phelan. As Colin Demarest and Marc Caputo reported for Axios in “John Phelan out as US Navy secretary after Pete Hegseth fires him,” the complaint inside the Pentagon was that Phelan, a financier and major Trump fundraiser confirmed about a year earlier, had gone around Hegseth to talk directly to the President, with whom he exchanged texts about naval matters. One source summed up the charge: Phelan “didn’t understand he wasn’t the boss.”

The firing came while the Navy was enforcing a blockade of Iran. Jewish Insider reported that Senate Majority Leader John Thune said he “found out about it the way everybody else did.” Wicker and Senator Jack Reed of Rhode Island, the committee’s top Republican and top Democrat, learned of it from the Pentagon’s social media posts. According to that account, Phelan was meeting the committee leaders when his immediate departure was announced. Navy Under Secretary Hung Cao took over in an acting capacity.

The acting clock. The empty chairs also come with a legal timer. Under the Federal Vacancies Reform Act of 1998, an acting official in a Senate-confirmed civilian post can generally serve for 210 days from the date the vacancy arose, and for longer only while a nomination is pending before the Senate. The law exists precisely to stop administrations from running departments indefinitely with officials the Senate never approved.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. The Navy secretary’s post fell vacant on April 22, 2026. Two hundred and ten days from then falls in the middle of November, days after the midterms. Unless a nomination goes to the Senate, the department will have to find a legal way to keep Hung Cao in charge, or run the Navy with no one formally empowered to exercise its secretary’s authority. The Army secretary’s post fell vacant on August 31, 2026, so its clock runs to the end of March 2027. The Army chief of staff, a military rather than civilian post, sits outside the Act, which is how LaNeve has been able to serve as acting chief for six months. But the military has its own unwritten clock, and the Army has already passed every previous record on it.

Each of these deadlines could have been avoided by a nomination the Senate would accept. That would require the Secretary to choose candidates for the Senate rather than for himself, and to spend time with the senators who must approve them. As Part II showed, that kind of time is exactly what Republican senators say he does not spend.

The wider count. These are only the three largest chairs. As Chantelle Lee reported for Time on September 2, 2026, in “Republican Senator Calls On Trump to Replace Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth,” Tillis counted about twenty senior military and civilian leaders who had left under Hegseth. He accused the Secretary of “inept management,” said he was “intimidated by competence,” and said Hegseth was “creating a leadership void at the top of our military ranks.” Back in May, Tillis had told Politico he would love to see Hegseth fired “because he’s incompetent.”

Tillis voted to confirm Hegseth in January 2025. The confirmation passed 51 to 50, with the Vice President breaking the tie, the narrowest margin for a defence secretary since the post was created in 1947. Tillis is one of the 50 who made it possible. His regret is the political story. The empty chairs are the institutional one.

The Bill

The chain of command. An acting official can sign papers. What an acting official usually cannot do is make decisions that bind the future: long-term reforms, contested budgets, appointments that will outlast them. Every acting officer and official knows that the job may go to someone else, and acts accordingly. A service run by acting leaders is a service run in pencil. The Army, its largest component fighting a war and preparing for others, has been run in pencil since April.

The Senate’s role. The Constitution requires the Senate’s advice and consent for these posts precisely so that no single official can fill the military’s top ranks alone. Hegseth’s approach turns that process around. He removes confirmed officials without consulting the Senate, then offers successors his own party will not approve. The result is a long stretch in which the acting officials serve at his pleasure and no one else’s. Whether that is the intention or simply the effect, it concentrates power in the Secretary’s office that the Constitution meant to spread around.

Morale and candour. The lesson from Phelan’s firing is especially corrosive. A service secretary who talked to the President was fired for doing so. Every remaining official now knows that the safest channel runs through the Secretary’s office and nowhere else. That is tidy as an organisation chart. In wartime it is dangerous, because the people best placed to warn the President that something is going wrong now have the strongest reason not to.

Allies and adversaries. Foreign governments notice who answers the phone. When the U.S. Army’s chief of staff is an acting officer whom the Senate has declined to confirm, and the Navy’s civilian leader is an acting under secretary, allied capitals have to wonder whose word will still stand next quarter. Adversaries have to wonder nothing. They simply note the gap.

The successors’ incentive. There is a quieter cost too. Every acting official now serving under Hegseth knows what happened to George, Driscoll and Phelan. A confirmed official has some protection: the Senate approved them, and removing them carries a political price. An acting official has none. They can be replaced with a memo. That makes acting officials, by design or by accident, the most compliant officials a Secretary can have. A department run largely by acting leaders is a department in which almost no one has the standing to say no.

The officer who has to be acting. LaNeve deserves a sentence of sympathy. A decorated commander has been asked to lead the Army through a war while the Senate debates, mostly in private, whether he has worn four stars long enough. He did not create the vacancy. He has been stood in it, and he will be judged by how well he holds a chair that the Secretary emptied and the Senate will not fill.

In a parliamentary system, a minister who fired the head of the army, saw the army’s civilian minister quit, and dismissed the navy’s minister for talking to the head of government would not survive the month. Washington has no such mechanism. It has a Secretary who calls the empty chairs accountability, a President who calls the Secretary fantastic, and a Senate in which senators of his own party cannot fill the chairs and cannot get him removed.
