The Ed Sullivan Theater on West 53rd Street, home to The Late Show under David Letterman and later Stephen Colbert, before its rapid demolition following Colbert’s final broadcast

There is a building on West 53rd Street in Manhattan — the Ed Sullivan Theater — that has housed more American history than most museums. Ed Sullivan introduced the Beatles there in 1964. David Letterman broadcast from that stage for twenty-two years. Stephen Colbert inherited the desk, the marquee, and the mandate in 2015, and held them until Paramount’s new owners decided, eleven months ago, that the franchise was no longer worth defending.

The demolition crews arrived within days of Colbert’s final broadcast.

That detail — the speed, the indecency of it — tells you something about who is running CBS now and what they think the place is for. But to understand what was lost, you have to go back to the beginning. You have to go back to a man named Murrow, standing on a London rooftop in the Blitz, microphone in hand, reporting the war into American living rooms while bombs fell around him.

The Boys

Edward R. Murrow broadcasted from a London rooftop during the Blitz, 1940. His live dispatches brought the war into American living rooms and helped define CBS’s reputation for serious journalism.

Edward R. Murrow joined CBS in 1935. He did not invent broadcast journalism so much as he became it — the standard against which everyone who followed would be measured and, with few exceptions, found somewhat wanting.

His wartime dispatches from London were not merely reporting. They were a kind of moral instruction: this is what is happening, this is what it means, this is what it demands of you. The sound of his voice — measured, grave, unsentimental — became the sound of integrity itself.

After the war, Murrow built what history has come to call the Murrow Boys, a corps of CBS foreign correspondents who redefined what serious journalism could look like on radio and, later, on television. In 1951, working with producer Fred Friendly, he launched See It Now — the first television newsmagazine, the direct ancestor of 60 Minutes — and proceeded to use it as a vehicle not for comfort, but for confrontation.

The confrontation that mattered most came on March 9, 1954.

Senator Joseph McCarthy had spent four years terrorizing American public life — destroying careers, trafficking in innuendo, weaponizing the fear of communism to settle political scores and silence dissent. Reporters and editors had covered him, some had criticized him, but few had taken him on directly and at length. Murrow did. Using McCarthy’s own words — his own footage, his own Senate hearings — Murrow documented the senator’s abusive conduct and concluded the broadcast with one of the most quoted sentences in the history of American journalism:

“The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, but in ourselves.”

By December 1954, McCarthy’s Senate colleagues had voted to censure him.

Murrow did not topple McCarthy alone. But he demonstrated that broadcast journalism could do something more than describe events — it could illuminate them, could bring to bear the full moral weight of evidence and reason in the service of democratic accountability. That is what CBS stood for, at its best. That is the standard the institution set for itself, in its founding decade.

The Most Trusted Man in America

Walter Cronkite at the anchor desk of the CBS Evening News, the “most trusted man in America,” whose 1968 Vietnam editorial helped shift national opinion and influence a presidency

In 1950, it was Murrow himself who recruited Walter Cronkite to CBS News. Cronkite would spend the next three decades building something that had not previously existed: a national anchor, a trusted public voice, a figure whose word the country accepted not because he was infallible but because he had demonstrably earned that acceptance night after night, crisis after crisis.

He was present for all of it. He told the nation that John F. Kennedy was dead, his voice briefly breaking before he composed himself and returned to the work. He anchored the moon landing in 1969, his customary journalistic restraint briefly overwhelmed by wonder — “Oh boy!” — and the country forgave him, because it shared the feeling. He covered Watergate, the Iranian hostage crisis, Vietnam.

It was Vietnam that produced his most consequential moment as a journalist. In February 1968, following the Tet Offensive, Cronkite travelled to Vietnam and returned to deliver something unusual for network news: a personal editorial conclusion. He called the war a stalemate. He said, plainly, that negotiation was the only reasonable path forward. President Lyndon Johnson, watching in the White House, reportedly turned to an aide and said: “If I’ve lost Cronkite, I’ve lost Middle America.”

Johnson announced weeks later that he would not seek re-election.

Consider the weight of that. A television journalist, by telling the truth as he saw it, altered the calculus of a presidency. Not through partisan advocacy, not through ideological alignment, but through the accumulated credibility of twenty years of honest reporting. Cronkite had not set out to be the most trusted man in America. He had simply refused to be anything less than trustworthy, for long enough that the country came to understand this about him.

He anchored the CBS Evening News until March 6, 1981. His farewell broadcast drew 43 million viewers. His sign-off — “And that’s the way it is” — was not a slogan. It was a statement of professional philosophy.

The Stopwatch

The iconic 60 Minutes stopwatch, which opened the groundbreaking newsmagazine created by Don Hewitt and featuring legends like Mike Wallace, Morley Safer, and Ed Bradley

While Cronkite was building the Evening News into a national institution, his colleague Don Hewitt was cooking up something different in the documentary division, where he had been exiled after a falling-out with CBS News leadership.

Hewitt had a theory: that television journalism could be both serious and popular. That you didn’t have to choose between rigour and ratings. That investigative reporting, if told with the same narrative craft as fiction — characters, stakes, revelation, consequence — could hold an audience. His four-word formula, which he repeated throughout his career, was: “Tell me a story.”

On September 24, 1968, 60 Minutes premiered on CBS. It featured two correspondents — Harry Reasoner and Mike Wallace — presenting multiple stories in a single hour. The stopwatch ticked at the top of the broadcast, and it has been ticking ever since.

The early seasons were a struggle. The ratings were poor, the show’s future uncertain. But CBS was willing to keep the experiment going, partly because a newsmagazine cost far less to produce than a drama or a sitcom, and partly because Hewitt kept proving that the journalism was worth something. 60 Minutes broke stories. It held powerful people accountable. It produced interviews that mattered.

By the mid-1970s, it had broken into the Nielsen top ten. By the 1980s, it had become the most-watched programme in American television — not the most-watched news programme, the most-watched programme, full stop. It held that position, on and off, for decades. It made the top-ten list for an unprecedented twenty-three consecutive seasons, five of them as the number-one broadcast on all of television.

Mike Wallace. Morley Safer. Dan Rather. Diane Sawyer. Ed Bradley. Lesley Stahl. Andy Rooney, delivering his wry personal essays at the end of each broadcast, having known Murrow since they were both covering the Eighth Air Force in London in 1944. The correspondents came and went, aged and retired, and the show continued, because the idea at its centre was sound: that serious journalism, presented with craft and rigour and respect for the audience’s intelligence, was something Americans would choose to watch.

Hewitt remained executive producer until 2004 — thirty-six years. He died in 2009. 60 Minutes outlasted him.

The House That Dave Built

The Ed Sullivan Theater marquee during the David Letterman and Stephen Colbert eras — the final chapter of CBS’s late-night legacy before the demolition crews arrived

The third great CBS institution of the post-war era was built on laughter rather than gravity, but it was no less serious in its cultural purpose.

Late-night television was not a CBS invention. Steve Allen created it at NBC in 1954, Jack Paar made it unpredictable and emotional in the late 1950s, and Johnny Carson spent thirty years at The Tonight Show turning it into the closest thing American popular culture had to a nightly civic ritual. When Carson retired in 1992, the succession became a drama that consumed the television industry. NBC, passing over Carson’s own preferred choice, gave the job to Jay Leno.

David Letterman took his wounded dignity and his genius for comedy and walked across the street to CBS.

He arrived at the Ed Sullivan Theater in August 1993 — the very stage where the Beatles had played, where Sullivan had hosted thirty years of American popular culture — and began doing something that no one else in late night was quite doing: treating the format as a laboratory for his own sensibility, which was sardonic, skeptical, midwestern, and profoundly suspicious of the entertainment apparatus he was working within. The bit that involved dropping watermelons off a building. The inexplicable recurring appearances by Larry “Bud” Melman. The interviews in which Letterman’s restless intelligence could not quite conceal his impatience with the promotional machinery of Hollywood.

Letterman was, at his best, a kind of institutionalised dissent — a comedian who used the conventions of the talk show to quietly subvert them. He was also, at his best, capable of gravity. His first broadcast after the September 11 attacks was not a comedy broadcast. It was a meditation on loss and resilience that remains one of the most watched and most cited moments in late-night history.

He held the chair for twenty-two years. His final broadcast, on May 20, 2015, drew 13.7 million viewers. He passed the desk to Stephen Colbert, whom he trusted.

Colbert proved worthy of the trust. He spent a decade at the Ed Sullivan Theater doing what Letterman had done and what Murrow had done before him, in a different register: holding power accountable, bringing evidence and intelligence and moral seriousness to bear on the news of the day, and doing it in a way that millions of Americans found not merely tolerable but essential. He won Emmy nominations. He won a Peabody. He was, by any measurable standard, a success — consistently winning his timeslot, gaining viewers even as the broader late-night audience contracted.

None of that would save him.

The Standard

What Murrow, Cronkite, Hewitt, Letterman, and Colbert had in common — what made CBS, at its peak, something more than a television network — was a shared understanding that the institution existed in service of something beyond its own commercial interests. Murrow understood this. Cronkite demonstrated it every night for twenty years. Hewitt built a franchise around it. Letterman treated it as an aesthetic philosophy. Colbert spoke about it openly, in the language of his own Catholic moral formation, until the moment he was silenced.

The network that produced all of them was known in the industry as the Tiffany Network. The name was meant as a compliment. It implied quality, craft, a certain elevated standard — the sense that CBS did not merely produce programming, it produced journalism, and that journalism meant something beyond the next quarter’s earnings report.

Walter Cronkite asked himself, at every editorial decision, what the story required of him. His successors at 60 Minutes asked themselves, for decades, the same question.

Scott Pelley — who joined 60 Minutes in 2004, who won half of all the major awards the programme collected during his tenure, who once said that “what would Walter do?” was shorthand at CBS for “how can we make this story right?” — was still asking that question last week when he stood up in a staff meeting and said that the new management was murdering 60 Minutes.

He was fired the next day.
