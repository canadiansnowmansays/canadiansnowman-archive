Europe is sweltering, this is how Parisians cool down

The thing to understand about the British house is that it was designed by people who were cold.

Not occasionally cold. Constitutionally, ancestrally, four-centuries-and-counting cold. The thick brick, the small windows, the low ceilings that trap a fire’s warmth, the carpet running wall to wall even in the bathroom — every feature of the housing stock on that island is a small monument to the proposition that the enemy is the chill and the goal is to keep what heat you can. It is excellent design. It is the accumulated wisdom of a damp northern people who knew exactly what climate they lived in.

They no longer live in it.

Last week, as the New York Times reported, a heat wave settled over western and central Europe and turned that accumulated wisdom into a liability. The houses built to hold heat held it. A red warning for extreme heat — the highest level Britain issues — came into force for London and much of central and southern England, with forecasts reaching 38 to 40 degrees Celsius, which is to say around 100 to 104 on the Fahrenheit scale. The Met Office noted that the country’s June record, 35.6 Celsius, had stood since 1976. By midweek it was in serious danger. The last time Britain reached 40 degrees at all was July 2022, in Lincolnshire, and that was treated as a generational event. It is becoming a regular one.

Across the Channel the story was worse and the numbers larger. France recorded its hottest day ever — not its hottest June day, its hottest day — with Météo-France reporting a national average across its thirty stations of 29.8 Celsius. An average. The peak, in the small town of Pissos, was 44.3 Celsius, about 112 Fahrenheit. Paris was forecast to touch 40, which would be unprecedented for June in a city that has stood, taking the weather as it came, since before there was a France. Spain’s weather service reported highs of 40 or more across great swaths of the country, with a peak of 45.1 Celsius in Andújar, in the south. Andorra, Austria, Belgium, Britain, France, Germany, Luxembourg, Slovenia, Spain, and Switzerland were all, simultaneously, under their respective top-level heat warnings.

Now, a single heat wave is weather, and I am not going to commit the error I criticized in the introduction by pretending one hot week proves a thesis. Attributing any individual event to climate change requires careful study, and the honest scientists say so. But the honest scientists also say, without hedging, that heat waves around the world are getting hotter, more frequent, and longer, and that Europe is warming faster than any other continent on Earth. The single week is weather. The trend the single week sits inside is not. In 2025, nearly the entire continent ran hotter than normal, and researchers estimate that recent years have brought Europe tens of thousands of heat-related deaths annually. That is not a forecast. That is a body count, already tallied, in the past tense.

Classic British brick terrace houses. Built over centuries to hoard every scrap of warmth against damp northern winters, these homes now turn into heat traps during extreme summer temperatures.

I will leave the question of why the heat keeps settling on this particular corner of the map — why France and Spain and Britain, why this stretch of western Europe again and again — to the next essay, because the answer is a small marvel of physics and geography that deserves its own space. For now it is enough to know that the heat came, that it broke records that had stood for half a century, and that it found a continent built, almost everywhere, to make the suffering worse. Which brings us back to the houses, because the houses are where the abstraction becomes a verdict.

Consider what a house actually is, stripped to its purpose: it is a machine for managing the difference between the temperature outside and the temperature a human body can tolerate. For most of architectural history, in most of the inhabited world, that meant a machine for keeping warmth in — because for most of human history, in most temperate places, cold was the thing that killed you. The English terrace, the Scottish croft, the timbered farmhouse of northern France: these are all, fundamentally, devices for hoarding heat, and they are very good devices. Thick masonry walls store the warmth of a fire and release it slowly through the night. Small windows minimize the surface through which precious heat can escape. Low ceilings keep the warm air close to the people. Every choice points the same direction: trap it, hold it, do not let the cold win. A house like that, in January, is a kind of triumph.

The same house, in a 40-degree July, is an oven. Every virtue inverts. The thick walls that stored winter’s fire now store summer’s heat and radiate it inward all night, so the house never cools. The small windows that kept the warmth in now refuse to let it out, and offer too little opening for a cross-breeze even when the night air finally drops. The whole brilliant apparatus runs in reverse, and the people inside — who did nothing wrong, who simply inherited a sensible house — cook inside the cleverness of their ancestors. This is not a failure of the building. It is the building doing precisely what it was designed to do, in a world that has changed the sign on the problem from minus to plus.

Air conditioning, you will say, is the obvious answer, and so it is, and so it isn’t — a knot I will spend an entire later essay untangling, because the politics of it turn out to be a story in themselves. For now, note only the shape of the difficulty. In France, installing the things has become a genuine political flashpoint — a fight about energy, about emissions, about who gets to be comfortable and at what cost to everyone’s shared future. In Britain, where the housing stock fights cooling at every turn, high energy prices do the rest of the discouraging. And even in the Mediterranean, where the old architecture knew the trick — the courtyards that pull cool air through a building, the heavy shutters closed against the midday sun, the thick white stone that throws the light back at the sky and stores the night’s coolness for the day — the newer buildings have abandoned that inheritance, thrown up fast and cheap with glass and concrete and techniques that trap heat as efficiently as any British terrace. So the continent that most needs to shed heat is, in large measure, built to keep it, and the retrofit is slow, expensive, contested, and in some cases architecturally close to impossible.

A dog day afternoon in Paris, cooling off in a mist fountain

There is a particular cruelty in that Mediterranean detail, and it is worth pausing on, because it foreshadows a theme I will return to at the very end of this series. The people of southern Europe once knew how to build for heat. The whitewashed villages of Andalusia, the shuttered townhouses of Provence, the deep-shaded courtyards of Italy and Greece and North Africa — these were sophisticated passive-cooling technologies, refined over millennia by people who had no electricity and could not afford to get the answer wrong. And then, in the postwar rush to build fast and modern and cheap, much of that knowledge was simply thrown away, replaced by buildings that assumed air conditioning would handle what the architecture used to handle. The old wisdom was not disproven. It was merely forgotten, in the confidence that a machine would do the work. We will see, before this series is over, that some of the future lies in remembering what the Romans and the Andalusians knew. But that is for later. For now, the forgetting is just one more way the houses have been mismatched to the weather.

This is the gap I promised to write about, in its purest form. The bricks are not stupid. The shutters were not stupid. The wall-to-wall carpet in the loo was, for three hundred years, a sensible response to the world as it was. The buildings are doing exactly what they were designed to do. It is the world that changed, and the buildings, being buildings, cannot change with it on any timescale that helps the people sweltering inside them this week. A civilization can revise an opinion overnight. It cannot re-window ten million houses overnight, or re-roof them, or rebuild the dense old city centres that hold the day’s heat like a stone holds the sun. The mismatch between how fast the climate moves and how slowly the built world can follow is, in a sense, the central practical tragedy of this whole crisis, and the houses are merely its first and most intimate illustration.

I think about this in Toronto, where Dior — my husky, bred by her ancestors for a cold that Toronto used to reliably supply — spends the deep summer pressed against the kitchen tile in an attitude of total defeat, a magnificent animal engineered for the Yukon and stranded in a city that increasingly is not one. She is, in her way, the British terrace house: superb design for a climate that is leaving. She cannot grow a thinner coat any more than London can grow new windows. She just lies on the cool floor and waits for a winter that comes later and leaves earlier than the one she was built for. There is something instructive in watching an animal so perfectly adapted to the wrong conditions. She is not defective. She is, by every measure of her breed, magnificent. She is simply built for a world that is withdrawing from underneath her, and there is nothing she or I can do about the coat. We can only find her the coolest tile and feel, obscurely, that we owe her an apology.

Yes this is Dior, after a warm walk in the park

The Times piece, sensibly, ends with advice for staying cool, and it is good advice — block the afternoon windows, mist your skin, seek out the museums and the cathedrals and the subterranean places, of which Europe has many, built by people who understood thermal mass better than we do. Barcelona has opened climate shelters. Paris has its drinking fountains. London now publishes a “Cool Spaces” map, a phrase that would have baffled a Londoner of 1976 and will, I suspect, sound perfectly ordinary to a Londoner of 2046. These are sensible measures, and I do not mock them; the people compiling those maps are doing serious and humane work.

But sit with what a “Cool Spaces” map actually is. It is a municipal document instructing residents on where to flee when their own homes become dangerous. It is the civic equivalent of the cool kitchen tile — a place to lie down and endure until the heat passes. That is what adaptation looks like at street level: not a triumphant mastery of the new conditions, but a managed retreat, a list of refuges, a quiet civic admission that the ordinary infrastructure of life — the home, above all — can no longer be relied upon to keep you safe in summer. It is not nothing. It is, in fact, a quietly heroic response to an emergency the municipalities did not create and cannot solve alone. But it is also a confession. When a city has to publish directions to safety from the heat indoors, the houses have already lost the argument.

We built for winter. Summer is collecting.

Dispatch — June 25, 2026: The Collecting

I wrote the essay above as the heat wave was building. In the days since, it has gone from a warning to a reckoning, and the news has caught up with — and surpassed — every grim thing I set down. A series about the gap between what we built and what is coming should not let the coming go unrecorded. So here, before the rest of the series turns to causes and accountability, is what the summer has collected in a single week.

Paris reached 40.3 degrees Celsius last Wednesday — about 104.5 on the Fahrenheit scale — only the fourth time in a hundred and fifty years of record-keeping that the city has crossed forty degrees at all, and the first time it has done so in June. The “unprecedented for June” that forecasters were bracing for when I wrote the essay above is no longer a forecast. It happened. Wednesday was, by France’s national thermal index, the hottest day ever recorded in the country since the measurements began in 1947, day and night temperatures averaged across thirty stations. Britain broke its own June record, set in 1976 and mentioned above as if it were a museum piece; it is now a former record. Spain endured 45-degree heat in the north, in regions that are supposed to be its cool refuge.

But the temperatures, terrible as they are, are not the part that will stay with me. It is the dying. Spain’s health ministry estimated this week that as many as two hundred and twelve people may already have died in this heat wave. And in France, in the detail I cannot stop turning over, at least forty people — most of them young, some of them children — have drowned. They drowned because they did exactly what a sweltering human body begs to do: they went to the water. They jumped into canals and rivers and quarries to escape houses that had become ovens, and the water, colder beneath its warm surface than it looked, killed them. The Prime Minister called it “a tragic scourge.” It is the cruelest possible illustration of the thesis of this entire series — people fleeing the failure of one piece of infrastructure, the un-air-conditioned home, straight into the danger of an improvised substitute, because the society around them had built no safe place to be cool. The houses failed, so the children went to the canal.

The infrastructure failed in ways more literal still. Near Toulouse, authorities had to throttle back a nuclear plant because the water of the Garonne, the river drawn on to cool the reactor, had itself grown too hot to do the cooling — a detail I will return to several essays from now, when I write about air conditioning and the comfortable assumption that France’s nuclear grid can simply power its way through the heat. The grid can do no such thing if the heat shuts the reactors down. Across France nearly a hundred and twenty thousand homes lost power as demand for cooling overwhelmed the network. The Louvre and the Eiffel Tower closed early. A hospital server in Paris overheated and took down the patient records of a hospital in Belgium, cancelling surgeries in another country. In London — and I promise I am not inventing this — an event titled “Extreme Heat: Improving governance and strengthening action around the world” was cancelled because of extreme heat. In an English county, a council meeting at which a Reform UK councillor had planned to move the scrapping of the local climate-emergency declaration was itself cancelled by the heat; the councillor, undeterred, emailed reporters to say the cancellation only proved his point about the need for “resilience.” One does not know whether to laugh.

I will give the last word of this dispatch to the scientists, because they have done the work that turns a hot week into a verdict. An analysis by the Climameter group found that human-caused climate change made this heat wave as much as four degrees Celsius hotter than such an event would otherwise have been. And the World Health Organization’s European office reported this month that more than two hundred thousand people across Europe have died of heat-related causes in just the last four years — most of those deaths, it stressed, preventable. Two hundred thousand. That is not a projection or a model or a worst case. It is a count, of the recent dead, on a single continent, and it is the number against which every argument in the rest of this series should be weighed.

The essay above ended with a phrase I chose before I knew how literally it would be borne out. Summer is collecting. Last week, in Paris and Madrid and the canals where the children went looking for relief, it collected.

London children flocked to the city’s waterways on Tuesday to cool off from the punishing heat

Degrees

45.1 — the temperature in Celsius recorded Monday in Andújar, in southern Spain: about 113 Fahrenheit. Andújar is known for its olive oil, a thirteenth-century church, and a mountain sanctuary. It is now also known, briefly, for being one of the hottest places in a continent full of hot places, which is the kind of fame no town asks for.

And one more, added with this dispatch: 40 — the number of people, most of them young, who had drowned in France by the end of this single week, having fled the heat of their homes for water that proved colder and crueler than it looked. Of all the numbers this series will record, that is the one I would most like to have been wrong about.
