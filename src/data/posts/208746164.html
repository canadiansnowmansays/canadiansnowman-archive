By the sixth century CE, the Roman Empire that had once bankrupted its treasury and scandalized its moralists over silk had split, and its eastern half — what historians now call the Byzantine Empire, though its own citizens simply called it Rome — inherited the same basic problem its western predecessor had never solved: an enormous, seemingly permanent appetite for silk, and no domestic capacity whatsoever to produce it. What had changed since Pliny’s era was the immediate supplier. Where classical Rome’s silk had passed through the Parthian Empire on its overland journey from China, Parthia had been replaced in the third century CE by the Sassanid Persian Empire, a more centralized and more commercially aggressive successor state that inherited Parthia’s geographic chokehold on the overland silk routes and, crucially, showed no more interest than the Parthians had in sharing that chokehold’s profits with Constantinople.

The Sassanid-Byzantine relationship across the fifth and sixth centuries was defined by a genuinely unusual economic arrangement: two empires that fought repeated, expensive wars against each other along their shared frontier in Mesopotamia and the Caucasus, while remaining bound together by an almost total Byzantine dependence on Sassanid middlemen for a luxury good that had become, by this point, not merely a fashion preference among the Byzantine elite but a genuine instrument of state ceremony and diplomacy. Byzantine emperors used silk robes, woven with elaborate and often religiously symbolic imagery, as a central tool of imperial self-presentation and as diplomatic gifts to foreign rulers whose loyalty or neutrality Constantinople wished to secure — which meant that Sassanid control over the raw silk supply chain was not merely a commercial irritant to Byzantium but a genuine strategic vulnerability, since an empire that could not reliably obtain silk could not reliably conduct the specific forms of diplomacy and imperial theater that silk had become essential to.

The Sassanids exploited this vulnerability with considerable commercial discipline. Byzantine merchants were, for long stretches of this period, effectively barred from making direct contact with Central Asian or Sogdian silk suppliers, forced instead to purchase silk that had already passed through Sassanid hands at whatever markup Persian merchants and officials chose to impose, a structural arrangement that mirrored, almost exactly, the position Parthia had occupied with respect to classical Rome three centuries earlier. The Byzantine emperor Justinian, who ruled from 527 to 565 CE and pursued an extraordinarily ambitious program of military reconquest and legal codification across the Mediterranean world, found the arrangement intolerable enough to pursue a series of alternative strategies aimed at circumventing Sassanid control entirely, none of which succeeded for long. Justinian attempted, at various points, to cultivate alternative trade partnerships with the Christian kingdom of Aksum in what is now Ethiopia, hoping Aksumite merchants might be induced to acquire silk through Indian Ocean routes that bypassed Sassanid Persia altogether — a plan the historian Procopius records explicitly, describing Justinian’s direct instructions to the Aksumite king to buy silk from Indian merchants and resell it to Byzantium at a price competitive with the Persian monopoly. The plan failed, according to Procopius, essentially because Persian merchants had already secured their own advantageous position at the Indian ports in question and could simply outbid or otherwise exclude Aksumite competitors before Byzantine buyers ever got access to the silk.

The Aksum strategy’s failure was not simply a matter of Persian merchants outmaneuvering their Ethiopian counterparts in the marketplace; it played out against a genuinely violent proxy conflict in the Arabian peninsula that reveals how seriously both empires took the stakes of this indirect trade competition. Around 525 CE, the Christian kingdom of Aksum, with Byzantine encouragement, invaded the kingdom of Himyar in what is now Yemen, then ruled by a Jewish king, Dhu Nuwas, who had been persecuting Christian communities within his territory and who Byzantine sources link, whether fairly or as convenient propaganda, to Sassanid Persian sympathies. The Aksumite conquest of Himyar installed a Christian client ruler favorable to Byzantine interests and gave Constantinople a friendlier foothold near the mouth of the Red Sea, precisely the strategic position needed to support alternative, Persia-bypassing maritime trade routes toward India. That the arrangement still failed to break the Sassanid silk monopoly says less about any flaw in Byzantine strategic thinking and more about how deeply entrenched Persian commercial relationships with Indian and Central Asian silk suppliers had become by the sixth century — entrenched enough to survive not just ordinary commercial competition but a Byzantine-backed war fought explicitly to dislodge them.

There was one further attempt, and it is the most revealing of the lot because it came from the middlemen themselves. In the 560s, Sogdian merchants — the Central Asian traders Part III of this series described operating out of Samarkand — approached their Sassanid overlords with a proposal to sell silk directly into Persian markets, and were refused; Persian authorities, according to the Byzantine historian Menander Protector, went so far as to publicly burn a consignment of Sogdian silk to make the point. The Sogdians, now working under the Turkic Khaganate that had recently taken control of their region, responded by going over Persia’s head entirely and sending an embassy to Constantinople under a merchant named Maniakh, proposing a direct Turkic-Byzantine commercial and military alliance against the Sassanids. Justin II received them in 568 CE and sent a return embassy under a general named Zemarchus, who travelled to the Turkic khagan’s camp in the Tian Shan and left an account of the journey that survives in fragments. The alliance produced some diplomatic contact and no lasting commercial arrangement, but the episode is worth recording because it shows every party in the system behaving exactly as its position dictated: the middlemen trying to cut out the middleman above them, the consumer empire willing to talk to anyone who might break the monopoly, and Persia burning silk in public rather than permit the smallest crack in an arrangement it had spent three centuries building.

It was against this backdrop of repeated failed attempts to route around Sassanid Persia commercially that the most consequential single act of industrial espionage in the ancient world’s history reportedly took place. Procopius, writing in the mid-sixth century, describes a group of monks arriving at Justinian’s court sometime around 552 CE with a proposal: they claimed to have lived for an extended period in a country identified as “Serindia,” generally understood as a region within or near China, where they had observed the process of silk production firsthand and had learned that the fabric came not from tree bark or any of the other theories Roman writers had entertained for centuries, but from the cocoons of a specific caterpillar reared on mulberry leaves under carefully controlled conditions. Given Justinian’s backing and encouragement, these monks reportedly returned to the source region and smuggled silkworm eggs back to Constantinople concealed inside hollow bamboo canes, along with sufficient knowledge of mulberry cultivation and sericulture technique to actually establish a functioning domestic silk industry on Byzantine soil for the first time in the empire’s history.

It’s worth treating this famous story with a degree of appropriate historical caution, since Procopius is our primary source and later writers, including the near-contemporary historian Theophanes of Byzantium, offer variant and not entirely consistent accounts of exactly who carried out the smuggling and under precisely what circumstances — some versions describe a single Persian monk rather than a pair, and the details of the bamboo cane concealment specifically appear more prominently in some retellings than in the earliest sources. What is not seriously disputed, regardless of the exact mechanism, is the outcome: sometime in the middle of the sixth century, Byzantium acquired both live silkworm eggs and workable sericulture knowledge, breaking China’s effective monopoly on silk production for the first time in the roughly seven centuries since silk had first begun moving west along Zhang Qian’s original routes.

Byzantine silk workshops, established initially under close imperial control and treated as a state monopoly in their own right, began producing silk domestically within the empire, most notably in workshops in Constantinople and later in Byzantine-controlled territories in Greece and southern Italy — a production capacity that would persist and eventually spread further into the broader Mediterranean world over the following centuries.

The strategic consequences of this technology transfer were significant, though they unfolded gradually rather than immediately transforming the silk trade overnight. Byzantium no longer needed to purchase all of its silk through Sassanid intermediaries, which removed one significant point of Persian economic leverage over Constantinople and allowed the Byzantine state to develop its own silk industry as both a domestic luxury good and, eventually, an export commodity and diplomatic tool in its own right — Byzantine silk textiles, produced under imperial workshop control and often restricted from export except as ambassadorial gifts, became prized items in courts across medieval Europe for centuries, showing up today in cathedral treasuries and royal burial sites as far away as France and England, wrapped around relics or used to line the tombs of kings, valued precisely because Byzantine silk carried both genuine material luxury and the prestige of coming directly from the court of Constantinople rather than through some anonymous chain of Central Asian and Persian middlemen. China, for its part, never fully lost its silk trade even after losing its monopoly on production — Chinese silk retained a reputation for particular qualities of weave and pattern that Mediterranean production, even once established, did not entirely replicate, and the overland and maritime routes carrying Chinese silk westward continued operating for centuries after Justinian’s monks completed their mission. But the specific commercial and strategic leverage that Persia, and Parthia before it, had derived from being the indispensable gatekeeper of an otherwise unobtainable good was gone, permanently, the moment those eggs survived the journey in their bamboo canes.

The domestic silk industry Byzantium built on the back of the smuggled eggs was, notably, not left to develop as an unregulated private trade — Constantinople treated silk production and sale with the same instinct for state control that had characterized the empire’s approach to the good for centuries, even after it no longer depended on foreign suppliers. The Book of the Eparch, a tenth-century regulatory manual governing the trade guilds of Constantinople, devotes extensive and remarkably specific attention to the silk trade specifically, laying out strict rules on which categories of silk garment could be sold to foreign merchants at all, reserving the very finest imperial-purple silks, dyed with the famous and extraordinarily expensive murex-derived pigment, exclusively for imperial use and explicitly forbidding their export under any circumstances — a prohibition serious enough that a Western European bishop visiting Constantinople in the tenth century, Liutprand of Cremona, recorded his considerable frustration at having a load of purple silk garments confiscated by Byzantine customs officials on his way home, on the grounds that the garments’ quality and color exceeded what foreigners were legally permitted to possess. The designs themselves are worth a word, because the surviving Byzantine silks reveal how thoroughly the fabric had absorbed Persian influence even as Byzantium congratulated itself on independence from Persian supply. The characteristic Byzantine pattern — a repeating grid of roundels, each enclosing a symmetrical pair of confronted animals, often flanking a stylised tree — is directly descended from Sassanid textile design, and the motifs themselves, elephants, lions, griffins, mounted hunters, are drawn from the same Persian royal iconographic vocabulary. A number of these textiles survive today precisely because they were used to wrap relics in western European churches, where they were kept in reliquaries and treasuries rather than worn out: the so-called Elephant Silk from the tomb of Charlemagne at Aachen is among the best known. There is a certain irony in a Christian emperor of the Franks being buried wrapped in a Byzantine fabric decorated with a Persian imperial motif, woven from a technology stolen from Buddhist and Confucian China, and preserved for a millennium because it happened to be holding a saint’s bones. Very little on the Silk Road ever arrives anywhere with its origins intact.

This regulatory instinct reveals something durable about how Byzantium had come to think about silk even after breaking the original Persian monopoly: the fabric had become so thoroughly entangled with imperial identity and diplomatic signaling that Constantinople was unwilling to treat it as an ordinary commodity even once it no longer needed to, choosing instead to construct its own tightly controlled domestic monopoly in place of the foreign one the smuggled eggs had helped it escape.

The Sassanid-Byzantine rivalry itself, meanwhile, continued for decades after the silkworm smuggling episode, culminating in a catastrophically destructive final war between 602 and 628 CE that left both empires exhausted, territorially diminished, and financially depleted just in time for an entirely new power to emerge from the Arabian peninsula and conquer the Sassanid Empire outright within a decade of that war’s end, while stripping Byzantium of Egypt, Syria, and Palestine in the same sweeping wave of conquest. The Islamic caliphates that emerged from this conquest would inherit both the Sassanid Empire’s former silk routes and, in time, direct control over some of the very Central Asian territories — including Samarkand, discussed in the previous installment of this series — that had once supplied silk to Sassanid middlemen in the first place. The two empires that had spent a century locked in a silk monopoly struggle neither of them ultimately won would both, within a single generation, cease to exist in anything like their previous form — a reminder, recurring throughout this series, that control over a trade route’s profits has never been a reliable predictor of an empire’s long-term survival, and that the silk itself, indifferent to whichever human institution currently claimed to control its passage, simply kept moving.

There’s a specific lesson in this for anyone tempted to read Silk Road history as a story primarily about which empire “won” control of the trade at any given moment. Parthia lost its gatekeeper position to the Sassanids. The Sassanids lost their silk monopoly to a pair of monks and some mulberry leaves, and then lost their entire empire to the Arab conquests a few decades later. Byzantium, having finally achieved silk self-sufficiency after a century of strategic effort, spent that hard-won advantage largely on tightening its own internal monopoly rather than on any lasting geopolitical transformation. None of these outcomes tracked neatly with military strength, and none of them proved durable across more than a few generations. The fabric itself is really the only constant across two hundred years of this particular chapter — desired in Constantinople, produced first in China and then, eventually, almost anywhere the climate would support a mulberry tree, and entirely unconcerned with which empire’s treasury happened to be collecting the tax on it that century.
