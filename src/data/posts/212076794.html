by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,609 is about the most consequential product demonstration in history, and about the man who gave it — a forty-five-year-old professor of mathematics, chronically underpaid, supporting a mother, a brother, a mistress, and three children on a salary that the Republic of Venice reviewed with the enthusiasm republics reserve for professors’ salaries. His name was Galileo Galilei, and I want to introduce him to you first as what he was in the summer of 1609: not yet a martyr, not yet a symbol, but a man with money problems and a very good ear for rumours.

The rumour, that summer, came out of the Netherlands. The year before, a spectacle-maker named Hans Lippershey had applied for a patent on a tube with two lenses that made distant things appear near. The patent was refused — partly, the records suggest, because the device seemed too easy to copy, which is the seventeenth century’s way of telling you it was about to be copied everywhere — and descriptions of the instrument travelled faster than the instruments did. Galileo, in Padua, heard of it and did something that separates him from every other man who heard the same rumour: he treated the description as sufficient. He worked out the optics from first principles, ground his own lenses, and within weeks had a tube that magnified three times; then eight; eventually twenty and more — while the Dutch originals were toys of three or four. He had never seen the thing he improved. He had only heard its number, and reverse-engineered his way past it. The Numbers Game salutes the method: given the count, the rest is workmanship.

Now the demonstration, which I have loved since I first read of it, because I spent eighteen years in the corporate world and I know a perfect pitch when I see one. In August 1609, Galileo led the Doge and the senators of Venice up the campanile of San Marco and let them look through the tube — not at the heavens; he was far too good a salesman to open with the heavens. He pointed it at the sea. Ships, the senators found, could be identified through the glass a full two hours before the sharpest lookout could name them with the naked eye. Two hours. Venice was a maritime republic whose fortunes turned on knowing what was coming — which sails, whose flag, laden how — and Galileo had just sold them two hours of the future. The Senate doubled his salary on the spot and made the professorship a life appointment. I ask you to notice what was actually purchased. Not optics. Not astronomy. Lead time — the earliest possible sight of the approaching fact. Every intelligence agency, every trading desk, every wire service I have ever filed to is in the business Galileo invented on that bell tower: the arbitrage of seeing the number before the other fellow does.

And then, with the salary banked — the sequence matters, and I record it in his defence and ours — he pointed the instrument up.

What happened over the following months, into the winter and the January of 1610, is the part of the story where I stop being wry, because it is one of the handful of moments in the record when the inventory of the universe was formally enlarged. He looked at the Moon and saw mountains — measured them, in fact, from the lengths of their shadows, a surveyor’s trick performed on another world — where all authority since Aristotle had required a perfect, polished sphere. He looked at the Milky Way and found it resolved into stars beyond counting: the great smear across the sky was not a vapour but a census, unconducted. And on the night of January 7, 1610, he looked at Jupiter and noted three small bright points near it, in a line. He took them for fixed stars. The next night they had moved — and moved the wrong way. Within days there were four, and they were not wandering off; they were circling. Circling Jupiter. Four moons attending another planet, in plain violation of the tidy doctrine that everything in the heavens turned around us — four bodies that had been up there performing their orbits through every century of confident philosophy, waiting for someone to raise a tube.

Here is the idea at the centre of this essay: an observation is an entry that cannot be reversed. Everything else in this series’ long museum of numbers can be walked back — estimates revised, ledgers restated, adjectives retired at sea. But a count, once genuinely made, is out of anyone’s jurisdiction, including the counter’s. Galileo could have smashed the telescope on the morning of January 8 and the moons would still have been there, and worse — from authority’s point of view — they would still have been seeable, by anyone, with thirty florins’ worth of ground glass. That is what changed in the winter of 1609. Not the sky; the sky was as it had always been. What changed was that the sky had become checkable, and a checkable claim belongs to a different category of thing than a taught one. Doctrine says: it is so because it is written. The tube says: look. There has never been a ruder instrument.

And Galileo — this is where the threads of this series braid, and I have been waiting three essays to braid them — published. Fast. Sidereus Nuncius, the Starry Messenger, was written, printed, and on sale in Venice by March 1610, weeks after the observations, rushed through the press because Galileo understood with total clarity that a discovery unposted is a discovery unowned. Somewhere, the ghost of essay 1,519 stirs: Leonardo sat on the anatomy of the heart for decades and lost the credit to the centuries; Galileo sat on the moons of Jupiter for approximately eight weeks and has owned them for four hundred years. He even managed the credit itself like an accountant, naming the four moons the Medicean Stars after the Grand Duke of Tuscany’s family — a dedication that converted four points of light into a court appointment in Florence within the year. Posterity quietly repossessed the name; the moons are called Galilean now, which proves that flattery buys the appointment but the count buys the eponym. Post the number, sign the ledger, invoice the prince separately. It is the complete manual of early modern science in one sentence, and the man wrote it in a season.

The Medici dedication did its work, and here the essay must record a transaction that no one on the bell tower could have priced. Within the year, Galileo resigned the Venetian professorship — the life appointment, the doubled salary — and moved to Florence as philosopher and mathematician to the Grand Duke. The Venetians were not pleased; there were murmurs that the raise had been extracted for an instrument the whole world could now grind for itself, the two hours of lead time having depreciated, as lead time does, the moment everyone owns a tube. On the numbers, Florence was the better offer: no teaching, higher standing, a court’s resources. But mark the column that does not appear on any offer sheet: jurisdiction. Venice was a republic with a long, proud record of telling Rome to mind its own business; the historians’ consensus is that the Serenissima would never have surrendered her mathematician to an Inquisition summons, and Venetian friends told him as much at the time. Tuscany was a duchy that could not and would not refuse. The best career move of Galileo’s life was the removal of his own legal shelter, freely chosen, handsomely compensated, and invisible in the accounting for twenty-three years — the longest-fused entry in this series until the ledger finally called it in. Keep that transaction in mind. We will need it three essays from now.

I want to close the joyful half of this story on the campanile, though, because the two hours haunt me. The Senate bought early sight of oncoming ships. Galileo, turning the same instrument upward, bought early sight of an oncoming century — the checkable sky, the demoted Earth, essay 1,543’s disinterested ledger now equipped with its own audit instrument. And the institutions of his world, which had managed the drifting equinox for three hundred years by filing the memos, essay 1,582, were about to receive a finding they could not file. Lead time is only a gift if you use it to change course. It can also be nothing more than a longer view of the thing that is coming for you, and no one on the bell tower that August asked which kind they were buying.

Dior operates a modest observatory of her own — the bay window, southern exposure — from which she maintains continuous watch on the approaches to the property, and I have concluded she understands the campanile transaction perfectly: her entire posture is lead time. She sees the mail carrier four houses off; the alert is issued; the household is granted its two hours, compressed to ninety seconds. But she has never once looked up. I held her to my chest one clear February night and aimed her at Jupiter itself, and she looked at my ear. The sky, her posture said, has never approached the house. She may be the only strict empiricist in this essay, and I include the Senate.

The count moves on, as it does — and here I must do something the series has done only once before, at essay 1,337, and the veterans will know what it means. The next landmark on the map is essay 1,633. I will not make it funny now and I will not be able to make it funny then. The man on the bell tower is sixty-nine in that essay, and he is kneeling, and he is reading aloud from a document that other men have written for him, and the document says the count was wrong. You have three essays’ distance. I mention it now because this series does not ambush its readers with the grave entries; it walks you toward them in the light.

Four new entries in the universal ledger, posted in a Venetian spring. The audit of the auditor comes later.
