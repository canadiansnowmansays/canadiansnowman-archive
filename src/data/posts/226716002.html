On the night of Sunday, November 22, 2026, seven small boats with no one aboard crossed the dark water outside Benghazi, on the coast of eastern Libya. They were Ukrainian naval drones: unmanned, explosive-laden and steered by operators a very long way from home. When they found their targets, a Russian corvette went to the bottom with a combat helicopter still on its deck, and a Russian cargo ship that Kyiv says was carrying weapons was hit as well.

The next day President Volodymyr Zelensky said that Ukraine had done it. It was the first time Kyiv had openly claimed an attack on Russian ships in the Mediterranean.

Odesa is nearly 2,000 kilometres from Benghazi. Four years ago, Ukraine had almost no navy. The Black Sea Fleet that once bottled up its ports has lost about a third of its ships to Ukrainian drones, and the rest no longer feel safe anywhere.

This series has spent eleven parts on what Washington wanted from Ukraine and what Moscow demanded of it. This part is about what Ukraine did in the same week with no one’s help: what it built, where it reached, what it absorbed, and who stepped forward to carry the weight.

Two thousand kilometres from home

According to The New York Times, the Security Service of Ukraine, the SBU, took part in the operation and said the drones were launched from a carrier ship at sea. It did not say where they were loaded. Suspicion fell on western Libya, where Ukrainian operators have been working for years. Libya has two rival governments. The one in the west is backed by Western nations. The one in the east, which controls Benghazi through General Khalifa Hifter, has grown close to Moscow since Russia lost its Syrian bases after Bashar al-Assad fell in 2024.

The SBU is the same service that, a week earlier, helped send a large section of the Kerch Strait Bridge into the sea, as Part XI described. During the war it has changed from a domestic counter-intelligence agency into something closer to a deep-strike force. It now operates, in practice, wherever Russia does.

Analysts told The Times that Benghazi is a newer port of call for Russian warships, which used to prefer Tobruk. Hitting ships there on short notice suggests very good intelligence and fast planning. An energy analyst pointed out what the warships are for: they escort the tankers carrying Russian oil and gas from the Arctic and the Black Sea to North Africa. That is the money that pays for the war. The signal to anyone else thinking of hosting Russian ships in eastern Libya was plain enough: it is no longer safe to dock there.

The strike off Libya was not an isolated stunt. The day before, a Russian cargo ship, the Sparta II, was hit in the Gulf of Finland. Ukraine has not claimed that one, but Finland’s prime minister said his country supported Kyiv’s right to carry out such strikes. In March, a Russian shadow-fleet tanker carrying liquefied natural gas to Egypt was disabled in the central Mediterranean.

None of this is free of risk, and an honest account should say so. The UN mission in Libya condemned the attack and warned that the country must not become a battleground for other people’s wars. Russia accused Kyiv of dragging Libya into the fighting. The Mediterranean is crowded with civilian shipping, and earlier this year an armed Ukrainian drone washed up on a Greek shore, for which Kyiv apologised. A country fighting for survival has every right to strike its invader’s warships. It also has every reason to be careful where it does so, because its friends are watching.

But the point of Sunday night was not lost on anyone in Moscow. Zelensky said Ukraine was responding to Russian aggression, and then added the sentence that sums up this whole essay: “We are developing Ukrainian weapons.”

The friend in Washington

Ukraine is developing its own weapons because it has to. The country that armed it in 2022 has largely stopped.

The week before Benghazi, President Trump met Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen. The New York Times reported that Trump called Putin “my friend,” and that Putin left declaring his years of isolation over, having ruled out any truce at sea or on Ukraine’s energy grid.

On the flight home, according to Politico, Trump phoned a meeting of European leaders gathered in Berlin to support Ukraine. Five European officials said he told them Putin was serious about peace and that they could save money, as well as lives, by bringing the war to an end. The White House denies that account. On PBS’s Washington Week on November 20, Susan B. Glasser of The New Yorker pointed out a fact that many Americans may not know: the United States no longer gives Ukraine anything directly. What American weapons still reach Kyiv are bought and paid for by Europeans.

I went through that week in full in I Can’t Speak to America, and I will not repeat it here. From Kyiv, the summary is short. The American president calls the man bombing Ukrainian cities a friend. The American aid has stopped. And, by five officials’ account, the American president has asked the Europeans to stop theirs as well.

A country in that position has two choices. It can do as it is told, or it can do it itself.

Moscow’s reply

Putin made clear in Shenzhen what he thought of the Kerch bridge strikes. According to The New York Times, he said: “There will be no negotiations, only retaliation.”

The retaliation was already under way. On Thursday, November 19, swarms of jet-powered drones hit Kyiv for hours. Residents ran for the shelters as anti-aircraft fire echoed across the city. At least one person was killed. The Dutch and Austrian embassies were damaged, and drone debris landed on the grounds of the Brazilian Embassy. The Kyiv Independent reported that St Volodymyr’s Cathedral was damaged too. The European Union’s ambassador, Katarina Mathernova, counted 34 air-raid alarms by nightfall. As she stepped from her armoured car into her hotel, a Russian drone exploded directly overhead. She said afterwards that she needed a moment to recover.

Whether the embassies were targeted is unclear. The timing is suggestive. A day earlier, Russia’s embassy in The Hague had condemned the Dutch defence firm Destinus, whose Ruta cruise missiles helped bring down part of the Kerch bridge. Debris also fell on the grounds of Ukraine’s own Foreign Ministry. The foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, said, according to United24, that the ministry would add the fragments to its museum.

That is the Ukrainian tone, and it has been earned. The United Nations counted at least 372 civilians killed in October, nearly double the figure for October 2025. Odesa’s historic centre was hit repeatedly the same day. And on the afternoon of Monday, November 23, a few hours after Zelensky announced the Libya strike, Russian drones struck central Kyiv again, sending smoke up from a site a few metres from the presidential office.

The arithmetic

What has all this cost Russia? The Kremlin hides the answer from its own people, but the answer is leaking out.

On November 23, the exiled Russian outlets Meduza and Mediazona published a new estimate, drawing on data that two groups of pro-Ukraine hackers took from Rosstat, Russia’s state statistics service. According to The New York Times, the records show at least 490,000 soldier deaths registered by the Russian state as of September. Russian death records run far behind, however, and most of the deaths from 2026 and many from 2025 have not yet been entered. Projected forward, and counting the missing who are almost certainly dead, the toll comes to more than 700,000 Russian soldiers killed. The Times could not confirm the figures independently, and war casualty counts are disputed for years afterwards. Even so, the number is far higher than earlier estimates.

Michael Kofman of the Carnegie Endowment told The Times that Russia is losing soldiers faster than it can recruit them, though it is still on track to sign up about 409,000 this year with large cash bonuses. That pace lets Russia keep fighting, but probably not gain much. Unless it changes how it fights, he said, it faces “diminishing returns, with no prospect for improvement.”

Honesty requires the other column too. The Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated 150,000 Ukrainian combatants killed through June. Sites that track public records put Ukraine’s dead and missing at well over 200,000. Attacking armies almost always lose more than defenders, and Russia’s tactics have made its losses worse. But no one in Kyiv thinks of these numbers as a victory. They are the price of not being traded.

They are also the answer to the argument heard most often in Washington: that Ukraine should give in because Russia is winning. An army that loses more men than it can replace, to gain a few villages a month, is not winning. It is enduring, and so is Ukraine.

Made at home

So Ukraine builds. The Flamingo cruise missiles that buckled the Kerch bridge, as Part XI described, were made in Ukraine. The sea drones that sank a corvette off Benghazi were made in Ukraine. Where foreign help still comes, it increasingly comes as partnership rather than charity: the Dutch Ruta missiles were built by a European firm and fired by Ukrainian crews.

Some of the most effective defences are not weapons at all. The New York Times reported on a Ukrainian electronic-warfare system that fools Russian drones and their navigation into thinking they are in Lima, Peru, on the other side of the world. Ordinary phones near the front get caught up in it too, so that fitness apps and ride-share receipts show their owners wandering South America. Soldiers now talk about being sent to Peru as if it were a posting. One operator summed it up: “We’re in Lima every day.”

That is the character of this war’s fifth winter. Ukraine has learned that promises from Washington run out, and that a weapon you build yourself cannot be switched off by someone else’s phone call.

The Steppe Wolves

The last weapon Ukraine has is its people, and some of them are older than anyone expected.

When Russia invaded in February 2022, Ukraine’s army took only soldiers aged 60 and under. A man from the Zaporizhzhia region whose call sign is Grandpa was 65, and the recruiters turned him away. So, as The New York Times reported, he started his own unit. They call themselves the Steppe Wolves, and they would rather be known as pioneers than pensioners. About a dozen of them now work out of a plain building in the Zaporizhzhia region. Units from across the front bring them faulty rockets, and the Wolves repair them in exchange for keeping some to fire. Soldiers send them coordinates. A special operations commander who has worked with them since 2022 said they are fast, mobile and effective, and that he would gladly take them into his unit. Judging people by age is wrong, he said; what counts is motivation.

Dnipro, 64, needs glasses to drive and repairs detonators in a workshop that smells of gunpowder. Petro, 68, is a former competitive weightlifter who still lifts at a nearby gym because the rockets are heavy. He and his wife run an animal shelter on the side. Until recently none of them was paid, and Petro knew that if he were killed his family would receive nothing. He said it without bitterness. The point, he said, was to drive the enemy out.

Then there is Arsen, 66, a welder from Kharkiv who went to a recruiting office in 2022 and was told he was not needed. His son joined the army in January 2024. Six months later his son was dead. Arsen spent half a year in grief, avoiding people and life, until he decided he had to do something. His wife was against it at first. Then she told him to go, that she understood.

“My son fought for me,” Arsen said. “Now I fight for him.”

With the army short of troops, Zelensky signed a decree opening one-year military contracts to Ukrainians over 60. It took time to reach the men who had been fighting all along, but it did. The Steppe Wolves are official now.

Russia pays large bonuses to fill the holes left by 700,000 dead. Ukraine has men in their sixties who fought unpaid for years because nobody would let them enlist. It is the clearest difference between the two armies.

Washington calls Putin a friend and has stopped sending help. Europe pays for most of what still arrives. Ukraine reaches Benghazi with its own drones, takes the drones on its own capital, and sends its grandfathers to fix the rockets. It is, for the moment, largely on its own. It has not been traded.
