The urgency of sudden censorship: Protests erupted immediately after Jimmy Kimmel’s suspension.

Jimmy Kimmel answered the question before anyone could properly ask it.

It was May 2026, the penultimate week of The Late Show, and Kimmel was one of a parade of late-night colleagues who had come to the Ed Sullivan Theater to pay their respects. He sat across from Colbert and made his case — passionately, correctly — that late-night television was not dying of natural causes. “We’re being poisoned,” he would tell Vulture days later.

Then he turned to Colbert’s live studio audience and said: “When I got knocked off the air for a few days, people cancelled Disney+. Why aren’t you people cancelling Paramount+?”

He paused for the laugh, then delivered the answer: “Because you didn’t have it in the first place.”

It got the laugh. It also contained, in compressed form, one of the more significant analytical observations about media power and public accountability in the current moment. But it only partially explains why the outcry over Colbert’s cancellation — and the broader CBS demolition — never reached the pitch of what happened when Jimmy Kimmel was suspended for five days in September 2025. Understanding the full gap between the two cases tells you something important about how institutional destruction proceeds in an era of attention fragmentation and outrage fatigue.

What Happened to Kimmel

To understand the contrast, you need the Kimmel story in full.

In September 2025, conservative activist Charlie Kirk was assassinated. It was a shattering political moment, and the immediate aftermath was ugly on all sides. Kimmel, on air the following night, accused the right of trying to pin a left-wing ideology on the suspect despite evidence to the contrary. FCC chairman Brendan Carr — the same Brendan Carr who had been slow-walking the Paramount-Skydance merger approval — appeared on a podcast the next day and said Kimmel’s remarks were “some of the sickest conduct” he had seen, adding what amounted to a corporate threat: “We can do this the easy way or the hard way.”

Nexstar Media Group, which owned numerous ABC affiliate stations and was itself seeking FCC approval for its own merger with Tegna, promptly pulled Jimmy Kimmel Live! from the air. Disney followed. The statement was opaque — the show would be “preempted indefinitely” — and within hours it read to most people as a firing.

Disney felt the heat — 1.7 million streaming cancellations in one week forced a reversal.

What followed was one of the more remarkable corporate reversals in recent American media history. Within forty-eight hours, hundreds of protesters had assembled outside Disney’s headquarters in Burbank and outside Kimmel’s Hollywood Boulevard studio, chanting “ABC bent the knee.” Disney stars began publicly urging fans to cancel their subscriptions. Marvel actor Tatiana Maslany called for a Disney+ boycott. New York City mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani refused an appearance on a local ABC affiliate in protest. In seven days, Disney reportedly lost 1.7 million streaming subscribers across Disney+, Hulu, and ESPN — approximately 243,000 per day. The cancellation rate on Disney+ reportedly doubled, from 4 percent to 8 percent.

On September 22, five days after the suspension, ABC reinstated Kimmel. He signed a new contract extension through 2027. The FCC threat evaporated.

Why Colbert Got No Equivalent

A graceful exit ten months out — no emergency, no sustained fire.

The differences between the two situations are structural, psychological, and — perhaps most importantly — temporal.

The Kimmel suspension looked like an emergency. The Colbert cancellation looked like a decision.

When Kimmel was pulled from the air with no explanation, the story read as a sudden, alarming act of political censorship — a man disappeared from television overnight with the FCC’s fingerprints on the door. The ambiguity made it feel urgent. Nobody was entirely sure what had happened or whether he was coming back. The protest impulse was fed by genuine uncertainty and the psychological urgency of an open question.

When Colbert’s cancellation was announced, the story had already been pre-processed through the corporate euphemism machine. It was a “purely financial decision.” It would take effect in May 2026 — ten months away. Colbert himself received the news with characteristic composure and said, in essence, that he agreed it was complicated. The show had a graceful, extended farewell. The emergency signal never fired. You cannot sustain mass outrage for ten months about a decision that has been framed as regrettable but understandable. Human attention simply doesn’t work that way.

Paramount+ was not Disney+.

Kimmel’s audience had something to cancel that cost them real money and that they were actively using. Disney reportedly lost 1.7 million subscribers in seven days between the announcement of Kimmel’s suspension and his return. That is economic leverage, and Disney felt it immediately and unmistakably. Paramount+ is a substantially smaller streaming service with a narrower subscriber base. The Colbert audience overlapped with it only partially. As Kimmel noted, many of them didn’t have it at all. You cannot boycott something you’ve never bought. The economic pressure point simply wasn’t there.

The Colbert cancellation was a symptom of something bigger and more diffuse.

When Kimmel was suspended, the cause was clear — one host, one joke, one FCC threat, one corporate capitulation. Reverse the capitulation, host returns. The problem had a solution.

The CBS situation had no equivalent lever. The damage was not one decision that could be reversed but a long series of institutional dismantlements: a settlement here, an editorial interference there, an executive producer gone, a news president gone, a radio service shut down, correspondents fired one by one across weeks. Each individual decision was contestable. The pattern was damning. But patterns are harder to protest than incidents. There was no single moment when the audience could say here is the thing, and here is the person who can reverse it. Bari Weiss couldn’t give Owens his job back. David Ellison couldn’t unsettle the settlement. The merger had already been approved. The Late Show was already over.

Outrage fatigue is real, and it compounds.

The Colbert cancellation was announced in July 2025 — two months before Kimmel. By September, when the Kimmel suspension triggered the street protests, people had already processed and filed away the Colbert story. The CBS situation had been unfolding for months, each development arriving in a media environment already saturated with political emergency. The 60 Minutes firings, the Pelley termination, the radio shutdown — all of these arrived in the same weeks as decisions about Gaza, about the federal budget, about election integrity, about a dozen other acute crises competing for the same reservoir of public attention.

Kimmel’s suspension cut through the noise because it was sudden, specific, and apparently reversible. The CBS demolition did not cut through because it was gradual, diffuse, and accomplished before anyone quite registered the full scope of what was happening.

What the Contrast Reveals

Kimmel himself drew the correct moral, even if he framed it as a joke. The institutions that were dismantled at CBS — 60 Minutes, the radio service, the Late Show franchise, the editorial independence of the news division — were not owned by their audience in the way that Disney+ is owned by its subscribers. You can cancel a streaming subscription in ninety seconds. You cannot cancel a news institution’s destruction.

That asymmetry is at the heart of the problem. The people who built CBS News into the Tiffany Network — Murrow, Cronkite, Hewitt, Letterman — were building something that existed in public trust, not in private accounts. The audience for Walter Cronkite did not pay a monthly fee to receive the benefit of his integrity. They simply received it, nightly, as a feature of the civic commons. It was a public good in the most literal sense: non-excludable, available to all, sustained by institutional commitment rather than consumer choice.

When a public good is dismantled, the people who benefited from it have no obvious mechanism for defence. They can protest — and some did, for Colbert. They can write letters. They can cancel what subscriptions they have to whatever streaming services happen to be adjacent to the problem. But they cannot recreate the thing itself by the force of their subscription decisions, because the thing was never theirs in that sense.

Bill Owens understood this when he wrote his resignation letter. Wendy McMahon understood it when she wrote hers. Scott Pelley understood it when he stood up in that staff meeting and said what he said, knowing it would cost him his job.

“This show is too important to the country,” Owens wrote.

Not too important to its subscribers. To the country.

That distinction — between an audience and a public — is precisely what the Ellison era at CBS has erased. And it explains, more than any individual programming decision, why the demolition proceeded with so little organised resistance.

You can protest an injustice you can see. What happened at CBS was accomplished largely by the time most people were paying attention. By the time Pelley was fired, the institutional work was done. What remained was the clearing away.

Kimmel told Vulture that late-night television was not dying, just “being poisoned.”

He was talking about late-night. But he could as easily have been talking about CBS News, about 60 Minutes, about the World News Roundup, about the standard that Walter Cronkite held himself to for twenty years and that the institution upheld, imperfectly but genuinely, for the seventy years after him.

Good night, and good luck.

The words that closed CBS News Radio’s final broadcast were Murrow’s. They were not ironic. They were not nostalgic.

They were a warning that had already come true.
