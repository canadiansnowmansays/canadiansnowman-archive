Most people know the North American Aerospace Defense Command for one thing: the Santa Tracker. Every Christmas Eve, the binational command in Colorado Springs follows a sleigh around the planet for millions of children, and the joke works because everyone understands that NORAD’s real business is invisible. It watches the sky over the pole, where the shortest flight path from Russia to the United States runs straight across Canada. It shot down a Chinese spy balloon in 2023. It still scrambles fighters when Russian bombers drift towards Alaska and the Canadian Arctic. Nobody notices any of this, which is how you know it is working.

Isaac Stanley-Becker ends his October 21, 2026 report for The Atlantic on that point. NORAD’s work is invisible, he writes, until it stops working.

It has not stopped working. But something has started to go wrong inside it, and it is the one part of the machine that no procurement budget can replace.

Part VI of this series looked at the plumbing: seventy years of American networks, equipment and intelligence that the Canadian Armed Forces run on, and Carney’s slow, expensive effort to build an alternative. It ended on a split. The officers, according to Philippe Lagassé, expected the relationship to recover once Trump was gone. Their prime minister was preparing for the possibility that it would not.

Two days later, Stanley-Becker reported that the officers are losing faith too.

I. The Postings Nobody Wants

For decades, a tour at NORAD headquarters at Peterson Space Force Base, or at one of the other American commands, was a plum job for an ambitious Canadian officer. It meant working on intelligence, air and missile defence and joint operations at the centre of the most capable military on earth. Canadian generals have also served as deputy commanders of U.S. Army corps in North Carolina, Texas and Washington State. These were the postings you put on a résumé.

Now, U.S. and Canadian officials told Stanley-Becker, Canada is struggling to find senior officers willing to take them. The problem is serious enough that Canadian officers have raised it with counterparts in Colorado Springs, Ottawa and Washington. The reasons come in three parts.

The first is career risk. An officer who spends years in the United States while relations spiral may find that time counts against them at promotion boards back home.

The second is access. Officers expect to be treated as something less than equals, with doors closed to them as a side effect of the politics. One Canadian officer put the arithmetic plainly: if you are a two-star general with one or two important jobs left in your career, why spend one of them in a post where you might be underemployed, with little access and little meaningful work to show for it?

The third is family. Spouses and children worry that the anti-Canadian mood Trump has stirred up will make American communities unwelcoming. Officers serving in the United States told colleagues that their families did not want to visit over the holidays because they were afraid of crossing the border.

NORAD’s official answer was that its senior Canadian roles “remain fully staffed and highly regarded as career-enhancing opportunities.” That may be true on the organisation chart. The officials who spoke to Stanley-Becker, anonymously, were describing who is applying for the next round.

II. How the Jokes Stopped

This did not happen overnight. Stanley-Becker traces it back to Trump’s first day in office in January 2025, when an executive order gave U.S. Northern Command, which shares NORAD’s headquarters, the mission to “seal the borders and maintain the sovereignty, territorial integrity, and security of the United States.” The order did not say which borders. Canadian officers in Colorado eventually asked their American colleagues directly: was there a plan to militarise the northern one, along the world’s longest undefended frontier? They were told that anything done would be cooperative.

That reassurance got harder to believe. In February 2025, defending new tariffs, Trump wrote that “Canada ceases to exist as a viable Country” without American support and should become “our Cherished 51st State.” In April he told Time, “I’m really not trolling.”

At first, Stanley-Becker reports, 51st-state references got laughs in NORAD staff meetings. By the end of 2025 they were met with silence or a curt reply. Outside the command, the mood had moved further. An Angus Reid Institute poll found that almost half of Canadians thought their government should treat the United States as an “enemy” or “potential threat.” Only 27 percent called it a friend and ally.

That is the country the officers go home to on leave, and the country their families live in while they are posted south.

III. From the Top Down

If the insults had come only from the president, they might have been written off as bargaining theatre. They did not.

In May 2026, Under Secretary of Defense Elbridge Colby used X to pause American participation in the Permanent Joint Board on Defence, the body Roosevelt and Mackenzie King had created in 1940, and to accuse Canada of failing to make credible progress on its defence commitments. Part VI covered the substance. Stanley-Becker adds the reaction inside NORAD, which was less about the board than about the man: who is this guy, and how much power does he have?

In August, Pete Hegseth posted a photo of two young female Canadian cadets with the caption “this is real,” a sneer at their appearance aimed at an allied military. Wayne Eyre, Canada’s chief of the defence staff from 2021 to 2024, called it “highly insulting.” In September, Trump circulated a cartoon on Truth Social of himself beating Carney with a hockey stick. A Canadian official described it as unimaginable conduct “even in our most dystopian imagination.”

There is a pattern here that readers of He Loves War will recognise. Contempt for an institution is rarely announced as policy. It shows up as a series of small humiliations, each deniable on its own, until the people inside the institution draw their own conclusions. Canadian officers have drawn theirs.

IV. What Part VI Got Half Right

Lagassé’s point still stands, as far as it goes. Intelligence sharing, exchanges and NORAD operations continue. Some of Stanley-Becker’s sources stressed the same thing: nobody has taken their eyes off the radar scopes or the infrared cameras. The pipes are still carrying water.

But Lagassé’s officers were describing operations, and Stanley-Becker’s are describing careers. The two can diverge for a long time before anyone outside notices. A command can stay fully staffed while the best candidates quietly decline. Systems can keep running while the people who know each other well enough to improvise in a crisis are replaced by people who arrived reluctantly and plan to leave on time.

“The day-to-day relationship has deteriorated,” David Perry, an Ottawa defence analyst, told Stanley-Becker. “There’s been a tangible change in the way the two countries interact on defense and security.” Stanley-Becker notes that senior officers he speaks with across allied countries, even Denmark, whose forces prepared earlier this year for a possible American move on Greenland, still insist that military-to-military ties are holding. Canada, he suggests, may be the first place where they are cracking.

So the honest correction to Part VI is this. The plumbing is intact. The plumbers are starting to look for other work.

V. The Dog That Bites Back

Why should Washington care whether a Canadian two-star general prefers Ottawa to Colorado Springs?

Stanley-Becker answered that question seven months earlier, in a different setting. On March 16, 2026, writing for The Atlantic as the war with Iran drove up oil prices, he reported Trump demanding that allies send warships to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and warning that a refusal would be “very bad for the future of NATO.” The allies shrugged.

Germany’s defence minister, Boris Pistorius, said flatly: “This is not our war; we did not start it.” Denmark, which had sent a frigate to the Red Sea in 2024 to protect shipping from Houthi attacks, was suddenly guarded. And Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, asked in a televised debate whether she could still call the United States her country’s most important ally, answered: “No, I can’t do that anymore.” She named Europe, the Nordic countries and Canada instead.

Rasmus Sinding Søndergaard of the Danish Institute for International Studies called it “the theory in practice.” For years, experts had wondered whether treating allies badly would have consequences when the United States needed something. Hormuz answered the question. Stanley-Becker closed that piece with a proverb: you can only kick a dog so many times before it bites back.

Now apply the same logic to the north. Several of Stanley-Becker’s October sources raised a war with China, which would quickly threaten Alaska. Moving American forces to Alaska depends on Canada: its airspace, its roads, its ports and its cooperation. In that moment, Washington would want Canadian commanders who had served in the United States and knew their American counterparts personally, so that nobody was building trust from scratch in the middle of a crisis. Generals who turned down American postings because of the friction, Stanley-Becker writes, are “much less likely to be sympathetic.”

That is the cost, and it is the kind that never shows up in a budget. It shows up the day someone in Washington picks up the phone and the person on the other end is a stranger.

VI. Self-Harm

The irony, as Stanley-Becker notes, is that Canada is finally becoming the kind of ally Washington has spent decades asking for. It reached NATO’s 2 percent target. It has pledged 5 percent by 2035. It is spending billions on radars and sensors and is still in talks about Golden Dome. When David McGuinty, Canada’s defence minister, released the defence industrial strategy in February 2026, he described the plan as “obviously continuing to work with the U.S., but now much more expansively looking to diversify.”

The diversification keeps moving. This month, Ottawa paused its purchase of 16 U.S.-made Black Hawk helicopters for its special forces because of trade and diplomatic friction, as EurAsian Times reported on October 14, 2026, citing CBC News and Radio-Canada. It joins the F-35 review and the Swedish early-warning aircraft on the list.

Christopher Sands, who directs the Center for U.S.-Canada Studies at Johns Hopkins, told Stanley-Becker that past trade fights between the two countries were combative but conducted “like a courtroom drama,” managed out of sight by diplomats. This one has been fought in public, and it has “poisoned people-to-people relations.” The armed forces were never going to stay immune. “Trust is easy to lose and hard to build,” Sands said.

Eyre’s verdict was the bleakest. “It’s sad, disappointing, and disturbing to see that deep relationship come under attack,” he said. “In my view, it’s a case of the U.S. undergoing self-harm by discarding and calling into question its competitive advantage, which is this system of alliances.”

VII. The Empty Chair

Here is where the series has arrived after seven parts.

Carney has the rhetoric of rupture and the habits of an alliance manager. He spends to NATO targets, talks about Golden Dome, and keeps NORAD running while shifting procurement to Canada and Europe, joining SAFE and weighing an associate membership in an EU that has not yet invented it. Part VI called that two policies running at once. It still looks like the only sensible course.

What Part VII adds is that the decision is no longer only his. Canada’s officer corps, one career choice at a time, is making its own judgement about whether the United States is still a place to build a future. Nobody ordered this. No minister announced it. It is happening in conversations between officers and their spouses and in applications that never get submitted.

That is how alliances actually wear out. Not with a treaty torn up in public, but with a chair in Colorado Springs that the best candidate decided not to fill. The radar keeps sweeping, the Santa Tracker will run again this Christmas Eve, and the command will issue its statement about full staffing. The difference will only show on the day someone needs a Canadian general who trusts Washington, and finds that the one who might have been there went home.
