Every tenor knows the rule about entrances: the orchestra waits for you, and the audience forgives a late arrival only if the voice is worth it. On Tuesday, November 17, the president of the United States stepped off Air Force One in Manila to a band of Filipino performers playing “Y.M.C.A.” on drums and trumpets. The summit he had come for was already over.

The leaders of Southeast Asia had been meeting for days. By the time Donald Trump landed, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. had handed the chairmanship of the bloc to Singapore and was holding his closing news conference. He cut it short. “I cannot keep the president of the United States waiting,” he told reporters, as The New York Times reported in its live coverage, and went off to collect his guest. Trump never left his resort that evening. The meeting with the Quad, a trade signing with Thailand and the dinner were all brought to him.

The trade signing did not happen either, at least not as billed. The White House had called it a deal; Bangkok had warned it expected only a statement of progress, and that is roughly what it got: a memorandum, with the real agreement promised in the coming weeks. He signed something anyway; a signing is still a photograph. Later, at the meeting with the region’s leaders, he told Marcos, the son of the dictator ousted in 1986, that he had known his parents well and that he had very good genetics.

The questions from home followed him across the Pacific, and he met each one with a shrug. Asked about the air-travel meltdown and the bank outages that had rattled Americans in the days before he left, he told reporters that this was life, and that such things happen when you are big and powerful and have all the money. “It’s fine,” he said, as Katie Rogers and David E. Sanger of The Times reported.

It was a characteristic opening night, and Wednesday was a reprise. At Malacañang Palace, Trump announced what he called “the biggest trade deal we’ve ever made, by far” with the Philippines, without saying what was in it, ABC News reported. The palace’s own account spoke only of an understanding on economic cooperation, according to Rappler. Marcos, who had sent Jollibee fried chicken to his guest’s hotel the night before, mentioned Trump’s brief stop at least six times in a three-minute speech, The New York Times reported. Trump greeted Imelda Marcos, 97, the dictator’s widow, and called her the most beautiful; she blew him a kiss from her wheelchair. Then he flew on to Shenzhen, where a larger and better-rehearsed production was waiting, and where the director is not him.

A voice that has lost its range

The man who arrives in Shenzhen is not the man who arrived in Beijing in May. On November 3, American voters handed his party a sharp defeat in the midterm elections, and in January a Democratic Congress will take its seats with subpoenas, budgets and confirmation votes in hand. The Nvidia scandal, which brought down the most celebrated chief executive in America a week ago, has reached into his cabinet. The tenor still has the lungs. What he has lost is range.

There is a long presidential tradition of flying abroad after a beating at home. Bill Clinton left for the Philippines days after Republicans took Congress in 1994, Barack Obama toured Asia after the 2010 rout, and Trump himself flew to France after losing the House in 2018. This trip was planned before November 3, when few Republicans foresaw what Rogers and Sanger called a near-complete wipeout. He kept the itinerary anyway.

Asia has noticed. Lily Kuo of The New York Times described the trip as a pivot to foreign affairs after the rebuke at home, and the region is reading it that way. Southeast Asian leaders were watching, the Times reported, for any sign that defeat had humbled him. A former American ambassador to Vietnam, Ted Osius, says the region’s hedging now runs away from Washington. Outside, in central Manila, thousands marched against him and against Pax Silica, the American plan to build artificial intelligence supply chains that bypass China, which officials like to call the successor to Pax Americana.

Michael Schuman, writing in The Atlantic, describes a diminished president and allies unsure of American support, yet finds that Beijing’s heavy hand keeps the door open for Washington. Gordon Flake of the Perth USAsia Centre told him Australia still has no clear Plan B. The region wants the old America back.

None of this weakens him in Beijing’s eyes so much as it changes the calculation. Ali Wyne of the International Crisis Group told Kuo that a Democratic Congress may push Trump to tighten export controls or speed up arms sales to Taiwan, and that “Xi will have to work much harder to keep Trump on side.” Xi does not need to court a lame duck. He needs to keep one from being pushed around by everybody else.

The chorus changes the score

Two days before the tenor reached the stage, his own chorus rewrote the music.

On Monday morning, November 16, the Commerce Department issued a rule that will require data centres outside the United States to obtain a government licence before supplying significant volumes of computing power to companies headquartered in China, Russia, Iran and a handful of other countries. The rule takes effect in 90 days. It closes the loophole that let Chinese firms rent Nvidia chips they cannot buy, and Ana Swanson and Tony Romm of The New York Times reported that it could open a new front in a fragile trade truce. Chip stocks fell that day, and on Tuesday the selling moved to Hong Kong.

The rule also landed on the very governments Trump was meeting in Manila. Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Singapore have become offshore computing hubs for Chinese companies such as Alibaba and ByteDance, and the 132-page policy threatens billions of dollars of data centre investment, Adam Satariano and Alexandra Stevenson of The Times reported. Singapore said it was investigating Megaspeed. A consultant in the region told them that “the ‘don’t make us choose’ era is coming to an end.”

The rule itself is less remarkable than its credits. The Commerce Department’s own release said it had been written by, and at the direction of, the Treasury Department and the National Security Council. Christopher Padilla, who served at Commerce under George W. Bush, told Alan Rappeport and Ana Swanson of The Times that he had never seen the department publicly disown a rule it was publishing and would have to enforce. It was, he said, “like open mutiny,” and on the eve of a summit at that.

The divisions behind the release are now public. According to Jonathan Swan, Maggie Haberman, Ana Swanson and Shawn McCreesh of The Times, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick stood almost alone in pressing for a softer rule, while Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, the trade representative Jamieson Greer and much of the National Security Council staff pushed for months for broad limits. Trump approved the rule in August. Commerce did not publish it for three months. Late on Monday, apparently to answer criticism that Lutnick had been slow, the Justice and Commerce Departments announced a criminal investigation of Megaspeed. In private, his advisers say, the president’s sympathies move from one camp to the other from one day to the next.

The most surprising singer in this chorus is Bessent. Last year he was the administration’s voice of de-escalation, the man who brokered the truce that calmed the tariff war. This year he has turned hawk, frustrated that China has not delivered the farm purchases and critical minerals it promised. He has publicly blamed Commerce for failing to sanction Megaspeed, the Singapore data centre company at the centre of the Nvidia scandal, and asked on Fox News whether Nvidia pushed illicit sales to inflate its earnings.

Two singers, one plane

Bessent and Lutnick are both travelling with the president, which led Rappeport and Swanson to suggest that the tensest meeting of the trip might take place aboard Air Force One. The tenor, for his part, declined to choose between them. On the South Lawn before he left, Trump described one view as not wanting to insult China and the other as going down and dirty to do what has to be done. “You can’t say either is wrong or right,” he said. He added that you did not want to insult China just before you got there.

On paper, the president sided with his Treasury secretary. In his words, he sided with nobody, and China hawks inside and outside the administration now worry that he may reverse himself or treat the new rule as a chip to be traded with Xi. The same contradiction runs through his treatment of Nvidia. The day the administration tightened the rules, the DealBook newsletter of The Times noted, the Wall Street Journal reported that it still planned to name Nvidia a national champion in artificial intelligence, under Lutnick’s department. Trump also said he would not mind seeing Jensen Huang back in charge, because Huang had learned a big lesson.

He went further. Asked about Alice Huang, the Megaspeed founder whom American officials have identified as an asset of China’s Ministry of State Security, Trump said he did not know that she was a spy. Told that Rubio had warned him, he said he had not heard that. H.R. McMaster, his national security adviser in 2017 and 2018, offered the Times a diagnosis that fits the whole week: Trump is often hampered by “his belief that he can get the deal nobody else can get,” and so he vacillates and weakens his own hand.

Where’s Jensen?

In Beijing in May, Jensen Huang was the man at Trump’s elbow. In September he sat at the head table of the state dinner for Xi. In Shenzhen there will be an empty seat where he used to be. His absence was the question Katie Rogers of The New York Times put at the centre of her preview of the trip.

Trump will not raise the scandal with Xi. He told reporters he always thought of Huang as “a fine man” and said he did not recall being briefed about Alice Huang, although Rubio briefed him in person on Air Force One in May. Craig Singleton of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies told Rogers that Beijing may have helped build the bipartisan case for cutting off its own access to chips. Huang and Lutnick have both been asked to testify before the Senate Banking Committee in December.

On Tuesday Nvidia’s board set up a committee to oversee national security policy, export control compliance and trade risk, while it searches for a permanent successor. That morning Huang said he would be honoured to return if the board asked; that evening its new chair, Harvey Jones, told analysts it had no such plan. The company reported revenue of $115.8 billion for the quarter, more than double a year earlier, and forecast $130 billion for the next, according to its quarterly results.

The news that matters in Shenzhen came on the call. Nvidia said it would stop shipping H200 chips to China, The New York Times reported. The H200 is the only advanced chip it is allowed to sell there, the one Huang lobbied for for months and Trump cleared last December in exchange for a 25 percent cut for the government. Chinese customers bought about $2.4 billion worth last quarter, a sliver of the total, and the company’s forecast assumes no Chinese data centre revenue at all. So the company Trump regards as central to the stock market arrives at a summit about chips having written China out of its plans. It comes without a chief executive, without a seat at the table and under a rule its most powerful defender in the cabinet fought and lost.

Smiles on stage, walls behind the set

Beijing answered the rule on Tuesday. A government spokesman called it a “textbook case of economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction,” according to Alexandra Stevenson and Murphy Zhao of The Times, and the commerce ministry warned that China would take countermeasures if Washington persisted. Stevenson and Zhao describe a pattern that predates this week. In front of the cameras, Trump and Xi have shared laughs and pats on the arm. Behind the set, each of their meetings this year has been preceded by a fresh economic offensive. Then, on Wednesday morning in Shenzhen, hours before Trump’s arrival, Xi opened the APEC business summit with the other half of the act. China would cut tariffs and widen market access, he said, and would go further, rewriting its rules to give foreign companies “full manufacturing access,” according to CNBC.

The list runs in both directions. Washington has proposed barring American investment in Chinese biotechnology, hypersonic weapons and artificial intelligence. Beijing now requires approval before dozens of its own technologies can be shared abroad, lets Chinese firms sue foreign banks for obeying American restrictions, and has used a new security review to block Meta’s $2 billion purchase of Manus and SoftBank’s $5.4 billion takeover of ABB Robotics.

The truce that is supposed to hold all this together is shorter than it looks. Neither side confirmed its extension until hours before it expired, and it runs only until January, partly because China is still rationing rare earths. Yun Sun of the Stimson Center expects Xi to press Trump to ease chip controls.

Officials have set expectations accordingly: carry out the existing trade deal, cut tariffs and keep rare earths flowing. A Beijing trade lawyer, Zhou Yong, told the Times that tariffs are now the minor issue. The real disputes over chips, AI and rare earths are the ones nobody knows how to bargain over.

The hawks’ stage

Part I of this series ended its account of Huang’s fall with a line that has aged well: his exit leaves the stage to the hawks. A week later the hawks have the score as well. They wrote the rule, put their names on it and flew in on the plane. The man who used to argue loudest for selling China American chips is out of a job, and the chip he fought for has stopped shipping.

What they do not have is the tenor. Trump still believes, as McMaster put it, in the deal that only he can make, and Shenzhen is the stage where he hopes to make it. He arrived on Wednesday as the first American president to visit China twice in one calendar year, with his commerce secretary disowning his own rule and his Treasury secretary demanding answers from America’s most valuable company. Xi, who plans every bow, was waiting at the foot of the stairs.

Nobody expected it. In more than a decade as China’s leader, Xi had never personally met a foreign visitor at the airport, the Times reported; in May he sent his vice president. This time he rode to the tarmac in a Red Flag limousine, walked a red carpet to the foot of the stairs, shook Trump’s hand and embraced him, while a crowd waving both flags chanted a welcome in Mandarin. China, Xi explained, is “a nation of etiquette with a tradition of reciprocity”: Trump had met him on the tarmac in Maryland in September. Putin, who landed in the middle of the night, got Xi’s deputy, Cai Qi. Wu Xinbo of Fudan University read the gesture as a message to a president bruised by the midterms that China would still take him seriously. It was also a lesson in staging. When a director comes down from his box to greet the star at the stage door, the star feels the production is all about him. That is usually when the director is surest it is not.

Xi built this stage in a city Deng made, and he has rehearsed for months. The tenor has a fine voice and a short attention span, and he arrived a day late with the score still changing in his hands. On Thursday he sings with two other tenors, one of them from Moscow. That is Part IV.
