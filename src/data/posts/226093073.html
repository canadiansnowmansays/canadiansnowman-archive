On Tuesday, November 17, Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire reported in The Atlantic that the President had decided to pursue neither strikes nor talks. The Iran war, they wrote, had slipped into a dangerous limbo. “There just aren’t many good options right now,” one outside adviser told them, and the midterms had “made it worse.”

On Wednesday, November 18, five reporters at the New York Times reported that the President was weighing another round of extensive strikes.

Both stories were true. That is the condition this diary has been describing for months: a war run on options kept open, in which waiting is a policy until it is not. What changed in the week was who was pushing. Israel wants the waiting to end. Tehran thinks it is winning by waiting. And the American public, whose verdict on the war arrived on November 3, still cannot see much of what the war has done.

The Window Reopens

The pressure is coming from Jerusalem. Benjamin Netanyahu, who has led a caretaker government since Israel’s election in October, is pressing for the United States to join a four- to seven-day campaign of airstrikes, according to those reporters, Julian E. Barnes, Eric Schmitt, Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan and Ronen Bergman. The director of Mossad, Roman Gofman, held meetings in Washington, including with the C.I.A. director, John Ratcliffe. Israel’s military intelligence chief, Shlomi Binder, made the same case to his American counterparts. A senior Israeli official disputed that Netanyahu had pressured anyone.

The Times is plain about the motive. While the opposition tries to form a government, renewed fighting could delay or derail it and create pressure for a unity government that would include Netanyahu. Part CVI recorded that he pressed for a strike before Israel’s vote and again before America’s. This is the third attempt, and the first that does not have an election to aim at, only a coalition to stop.

Israel’s case rests on intelligence that Iran has rebuilt its missile and drone arsenals and advanced its chemical and biological programmes, and that it could fit such weapons to drones. American analysts see some of the same evidence and read it differently. Their prevailing view is that Iran is building these capabilities to deter a nuclear or large-scale attack, not to use them first. The Israelis have also shared intelligence that Iranians plotted to use chemical agents against senior American officials, which one person briefed on it linked to the decision to move Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent onto a military base.

The machinery is in place. B-1 and B-2 bombers in the United States have been on 96-hour notice for weeks. Pete Hegseth and Gen. Dan Caine have given the President a menu of options drawn up by Adm. Brad Cooper of Central Command. American personnel have been withdrawn from bases in the region as a precaution, and another Patriot battery has gone to Israel. The targets would be Iran’s rebuilt missile and drone stocks, its energy facilities and Revolutionary Guards command posts. Israel wants more: its nuclear sites, and strikes on the power grid that would cut electricity to industry and civilians. The President has not agreed to that.

One correction to this diary’s arithmetic. The arrival of the U.S.S. Theodore Roosevelt made three American carriers in the region, only the second time since 2003. The U.S.S. George Washington then left for the Pacific. The count is back to two.

What may decide it is not intelligence at all. The President, the Times reports, is “highly sensitive” to the criticism that the United States has in effect lost the war. He is entering the lame-duck years of his second term, and he has often answered pushback with a show of force. An American official offered the other reading: the economic campaign has “devastated” Iran, so major combat is no longer needed, though “all options” remain.

The Limbo

The Atlantic’s account of the same week explains why waiting has looked attractive. The President believes the blockade and sanctions will squeeze the regime into submission and that time is on his side. Marco Rubio put it bluntly: “Eventually you have to eat. Eventually the economic realities penetrate the skull of even the most radical lunatic.” The President told reporters that military action did not seem “very necessary” for now, “but we’re locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason.”

Tehran holds the mirror-image view. It believes the midterms proved it has the leverage and that its position will only strengthen. The head of its Supreme National Security Council said Iran would not allow the removal of “even a single particle of enriched nuclear material.” Both sides, in other words, are betting on the clock.

The limbo is not cheap. About 17 American warships are in the region. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz remains far below normal, Brent crude sits just under $100, and Danny Citrinowicz, a former Israeli intelligence officer, says it will be very hard to get back to the $70 of February. Iran, meanwhile, keeps up the pressure in ways that cost it little: France says a Revolutionary Guards-linked cyber unit got into the refrigeration systems of about 700 French businesses in October, and Iranian-linked hackers were suspected of targeting more than 30 Minnesota water systems in July.

Trita Parsi, whose toll-booth thesis this diary has leaned on since the summer, offers the most plausible way out. He calls it the “Swiss cheese” scenario. Washington quietly loosens enforcement of the blockade and sanctions while formally keeping both. More tankers slip through. The Gulf states make their own arrangements with Tehran. The result could be “a new equilibrium” in which the United States can claim to be enforcing a blockade, but weakly enough that “Iranians don’t have an incentive to escalate.” It would buy time, and leave the President in purgatory: unable to claim victory, never having to admit defeat. Youssef and Lemire end with a warning that suits this whole week. Both sides think time is on their side, and “that may be the most dangerous assumption of all.”

Strategically Empowered

What does Tehran think it has won? Iranian officials told Julian E. Barnes, Dustin Volz and Farnaz Fassihi of the New York Times that they see the midterm results as an Iranian victory over Washington. Their strategy of bringing economic pain to American voters by closing the strait, they said, had paid off. Iranian state television and newspapers have made the result a running story. “The beginning of the fall,” read one headline. “A historic warning,” said another.

Representative Jim Himes of Connecticut, who will chair the House Intelligence Committee from January, agrees with them more than an American might like. “The message of the election will not be lost on the regime,” he said. “They feel more strategically empowered against the Trump administration than they have in a long time.” He also offered the image of the week: Iran as “a cornered animal” that “might seek to negotiate,” but is likely to “lash out in some pretty wild ways, maybe in the service of negotiation.”

Himes settled one of this diary’s longest-running questions. Since the opening weeks of the war we have recorded the public absence of Mojtaba Khamenei and the rumours that he was badly injured or dead. “The supreme leader is alive, and he is functional and operational,” Himes said. Western intelligence officials now judge that he is more than a figurehead, and that his views are close to the hardliners’.

Tehran reads the American side closely, and it has noticed the split. Vance’s comment after the vote, that the war cost Republicans their majority, was taken in Iran as evidence of deep divisions in the White House. The President has noticed it too. He has privately complained that his Vice-President should have said going to war was the right decision, Tyler Pager reported for the New York Times. In public he has answered the question himself: “If you had it to do again, would you do it again? The answer is yes.” Rubio says the President “deserves a tremendous amount of credit” for it. Vance’s allies say a return to major combat would badly damage his chances in 2028. “Iran thinks Trump is under pressure from his constituents and his party to end the war,” said Peiman Salehi, an analyst close to the Iranian government. “So, they are not going to stand down.”

Iran is also not standing alone. On Washington Week, the PBS programme made with The Atlantic and broadcast on November 20, Youssef described Russia as Iran’s main military supplier, providing missiles, explosives, intelligence and targeting help, and taking Iran’s Shahed drones, improving them and sending them back. China, she said, is its economic lifeline. The motive is to keep the United States tied down in the Middle East, with fewer munitions for Ukraine and fewer ships for the Pacific. She added that Hegseth has little incentive to tell the President any of this, since his job depends on the President’s opinion of him.

The obstacle to a deal is the one this diary has named since Part C: sequencing. Iran wants sanctions relief and payments from Arab states before ships move freely. Washington wants the attacks on shipping to stop before any money is unfrozen. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, was in Pakistan on Sunday, November 15, and Oman the next day. Iran is still talking. It believes it is talking to a weakened man.

What We Don’t Know

The President and his Defense Secretary have a ready answer to how the war has gone: we won. Missy Ryan of The Atlantic set out on Sunday, November 22, how much the administration has done to stop anyone checking.

Last month Senator Chris Murphy, who sits on the Appropriations Committee that must approve the money to repair bombed bases and the extra $67 billion the administration wants for the war, asked to visit Al Udeid in Qatar, Central Command’s regional headquarters, which Iran hit early in the war. He asked for a tour, a briefing, anything. The Pentagon said no. Senator Joni Ernst, a Republican and an Army veteran, was told on her own Gulf trip that she could not visit bases either. Behind both refusals sits a memo Hegseth signed on March 3: “No DoW support shall be made available for congressional travel to the USCENTCOM AOR without my approval.” That might have made sense in the war’s first weeks. It is still in force. Robert Wilkie, the Pentagon’s congressional liaison during the Iraq war, told Ryan that commanders used to welcome such visits, because “it’s ‘No bucks, no Buck Rogers.’”

Ryan’s list of unanswered questions reads like this diary’s own. Why did an American strike hit a school in Minab on the first day of the war, killing more than 170 civilians? A Central Command report exists; it has not been released. How badly were American bases damaged? What we know comes largely from witnesses’ photos, satellite images (many released by Iranian media) and outside estimates. How many times has Iran hit American forces? Strikes on the air command centre at Al Udeid and on an American ship in the strait were never disclosed by the Pentagon; the press found them. How did 23 American service members die, and how were more than 850 wounded? And is that count right?

There are reasons to wonder. In July four deaths and dozens of injuries disappeared from the Pentagon’s casualty totals for the war. Officials blamed a “data disruption.” Days later they reappeared under a new heading, “Overseas Operations,” on the theory, set out in a letter from the President to Congress, that a new conflict with Iran had begun on July 7. The Congressional Budget Office, refused information, had to estimate the value of lost equipment from news reports: at least $1.9 billion. Republican Senator Mike Rounds complained that Democrats are being left out of classified threat briefings while the Pentagon asks them for money. “Pretty tough to ask all of my colleagues to support that,” he said, “if they’re not even invited in to hear the information in the first place.”

Hegseth set the tone early. When a drone killed six American troops at Port Shuaiba in Kuwait in the war’s first days, he told reporters on March 4: “When a few drones get through or tragic things happen, it’s front-page news. The press only wants to make the president look bad.” He held no press conference between early May and November, and broke the silence only to argue against his own impeachment.

“How on earth could we make decisions that we are empowered by the Constitution to make about whether we fund war if we can’t see it?” Murphy asked. From January, his party will be the one making them. Representative Jason Crow, a former Army Ranger who sits on the Armed Services and Intelligence committees, told Anne Applebaum of The Atlantic that the most viable tool is the budget: carve the Iran operations out of the January appropriations and wind them down. He rejects the argument that “when you break it, you buy it.” Of the vote on November 3, he said: “We want out. Americans want out.” That is the backdrop to the strike plans on the President’s desk. He is weighing a second round of a war whose first round Congress has not been allowed to see, and which it may soon refuse to fund.

Eventually You Have to Eat

Rubio’s line has an answer, and it comes from Tehran’s grocery shops. Ehsan, a 44-year-old textile worker, and his wife Fariba now spend 30 to 40 per cent of his salary feeding themselves and their ten-year-old daughter, double the share of a year ago, Yeganeh Torbati, Adina Renner, Shirin Hakim and Sanam Mahoozi reported for the New York Times. Some staples cost 10 to 40 times what they did six years ago; cooking oil is 41 times its 2020 price. Official inflation was 88.7 per cent in October, food inflation 113 per cent. The rial has lost more than 60 per cent of its value against the dollar in a year.

The blockade has not cut the food supply. It has cut the dollars. “Fewer dollars come in, so every importer pays more for the ones that do,” said Javad Nazari, a Tehran market analyst. The central bank’s printing of money to cover deficits makes it worse, as a senior Iranian official admitted in August. Shoppers who used to buy 20 or 30 eggs now buy two or four. Billboards advertise bread and shampoo in instalments. Shahrzad, a 28-year-old teacher in Ahvaz, carries meat and fruit home in a black plastic bag, embarrassed to be seen with what have become luxuries. Hundreds of common medicines have roughly doubled in price; blood-pressure drugs are up 77 per cent since the war began. Zahra, a 23-year-old student, avoids clinics as long as she can. Reyhaneh, an unemployed teacher in Mashhad, rations the meat in the fridge. “It feels as if so many doors have been closed to us,” she said.

This is the squeeze working exactly as designed. The question the White House has not answered is who it is designed to break. The people eating less are not the people giving orders, and American intelligence says those people intend to hold on “at nearly any cost.”

A Military Dictatorship

Netanyahu’s case for more bombing has always included the promise that it could set off a popular uprising. Arash Azizi’s report in The Atlantic on November 20 is the best evidence yet of what the war has actually done inside Iran. The regime has executed 74 political prisoners since February, according to Iran Human Rights, and more than 100 more are on death row. Café owners in Tehran are being prosecuted for celebrating Halloween. At least 118 Baha’is have been arrested or summoned to court. On Tuesday, November 17, security forces raided the home of the women’s-rights activist Sepideh Qolian in Dezful. She was not there, so they beat and arrested her father and teenage niece, and arrested her brother.

“A lot of us were happy when the dictator was killed,” a Tehran teacher told Azizi. “But the war has turned a religious dictatorship into a military dictatorship.” A journalist in Karaj put it more simply: “The war was supposed to bring freedom, but it has suffocated it.”

The opposition has little to offer in reply. Reza Pahlavi called Iranians into the streets on January 8, promising more than 50,000 defectors from the security forces. Thousands were massacred and no defectors appeared. “I am upset at him because he lied to us,” a young woman who marched for him told Azizi. Pahlavi still has a following, and might win a free election, but his movement has turned exclusionary, his own mother has come out against the war, and not one prominent official of the Islamic Republic has joined him. The other opposition groups are in exile, unpopular or confined to Iran’s edges. The reformers who might lead a transition, Mir Hossein Mousavi and Mostafa Tajzadeh among them, are in prison. A war that was supposed to open Iran has closed it tighter.

The Cost Ledger

The Pentagon now puts the cost of operations and replacement munitions at about $48 billion as of mid-November, and has given senators only a classified estimate they cannot share. It wants an extra $67 billion. The CBO, working from news reports, values lost equipment at no less than $1.9 billion. The State Department, which saw no reason for secrecy, says repairing American diplomatic facilities in the region cost about $184 million through June. Damage to the military’s own bases has not been costed, because, the Pentagon says, it has not decided what to rebuild. Brent crude is just under $100. In Iran, food prices have more than doubled in a year. The human toll on the American side is 23 dead and more than 850 wounded, by a count that has already been reorganised once.

The Numbers

  • Four to seven: days of airstrikes Israel wants the United States to join.

  • 96: hours’ notice the B-1 and B-2 bombers have been on for weeks.

  • Two: American carriers in the region after the George Washington left. Briefly, there were three.

  • 17: American warships in the region.

  • “The beginning of the fall”: an Iranian headline on the midterm result.

  • March 3: the date of Hegseth’s memo barring Pentagon support for congressional visits to the war zone. Still in force.

  • More than 170: civilians killed at the Minab school on the first day. The investigation has not been released.

  • 23 dead and more than 850 wounded: the American toll, as the Pentagon counts it.

  • 41: times the 2020 price of cooking oil in Iran.

  • 74: political prisoners executed in Iran since the war began.

  • 50,000: defectors Reza Pahlavi promised. Zero arrived.

And Narges Mohammadi, for whom this section is always kept. Two arguments for more war were made to the White House this week. Israel says bombing will bring Iranians into the streets. Washington’s own policy says hunger will force their rulers to give way. Azizi’s reporting answers both: the war has made the regime harsher, the opposition weaker and the people poorer, and the rulers are still in charge. Narges has spent much of her life in prison for arguing that Iranians deserve better than the men who govern them. Last year, Azizi recalls, supporters of Reza Pahlavi threw stones at her while she spoke. She is jailed by the regime and stoned by part of its opposition, and neither the bombs nor the blockade have done anything for her. She is still under sentences totalling 44 years.
