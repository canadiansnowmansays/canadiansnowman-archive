Part of the series: Israel/Palestine: The Original Sin and Its Compound Interest

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

The United Nations Security Council chamber. Since 1972, the United States has cast at least 45 vetoes to block resolutions critical of Israel — more than once every 18 months for over half a century.

Since 1972, the United States has used its veto power in the United Nations Security Council to block resolutions critical of Israel on at least 45 occasions. The exact count varies depending on how resolutions are categorized, but the order of magnitude is not in dispute. Forty-five times — roughly once every eighteen months for half a century — the international community attempted to apply the framework of international law to Israeli conduct, and the United States prevented it.

This is not a minor diplomatic footnote. The United Nations Security Council is the only international body with the legal authority to impose binding obligations on member states, authorize the use of force, and enforce international law. American vetoes have not merely blocked individual resolutions. They have prevented the construction of any enforceable international legal framework governing the Israeli-Palestinian conflict for seventy-five years. The occupation has been allowed to deepen. The settlements have been allowed to expand. The blockade of Gaza has been allowed to continue. The civilian casualties of successive military campaigns have been allowed to accumulate. At every point where the international community might have imposed accountability, the United States has said no.

Understanding why requires understanding the specific constellation of American domestic and strategic interests that has produced this extraordinary record of diplomatic cover — a record that has cost the United States more in international credibility than almost any other single aspect of its foreign policy.

The Strategic Foundation: Why the US-Israel Alliance Was Built

American support for Israel was not always as unconditional as it has become. The Truman administration recognized Israel in 1948, but Truman’s motivations were a mixture of genuine sympathy for Jewish statehood, domestic political calculation, and humanitarian concern for Holocaust survivors — not a strategic assessment that Israel was a vital American interest.

The strategic relationship was forged in the crucible of the Cold War, and specifically in the aftermath of the 1967 Six Day War. Israel’s stunning military victory over Soviet-armed Arab states demonstrated that it was the most capable military force in the Middle East — more capable, more reliable, and more politically compatible with American interests than any alternative partner. The Nixon administration, guided by Henry Kissinger, formalized the strategic relationship after the 1973 Yom Kippur War, in which American military resupply was decisive in preventing Israeli military collapse. From 1973 onward, Israel became the cornerstone of American Middle East strategy — a reliable, democratic, militarily powerful ally in a region that was otherwise hostile, unstable, and aligned against American interests.

This strategic assessment was not unreasonable in its historical context. Israel provided military intelligence, shared technology, combat experience from its multiple wars, and a democratic example in a region of authoritarian states. The US-Israel defence relationship has produced genuine strategic benefits for both parties, including joint weapons development, intelligence sharing, and the maintenance of an American military presence in the region that has served American interests in multiple contingencies.

President Richard Nixon with Israeli Prime Minister Golda Meir and Henry Kissinger at the White House in 1973, during the Yom Kippur War period. This era marked the formalization of the modern U.S.-Israel strategic alliance, with massive American military resupply proving decisive.

What has changed — what the current catastrophe in Gaza has made unmistakably clear — is that the strategic calculus of 1973 no longer accurately describes the costs and benefits of unconditional American support. The cost of enabling the occupation, the settlements, and the military campaigns in Gaza has now exceeded the strategic benefit of the alliance in ways that most serious American foreign policy analysts — including many who are deeply supportive of Israel — acknowledge privately even when they will not say so publicly.

The Domestic Political Architecture

The strategic rationale for the US-Israel relationship has been reinforced, and in some periods determined, by a domestic political architecture that makes meaningful pressure on Israel domestically catastrophic for any American politician who attempts it.

The architecture has three primary pillars.

The first is the organized American Jewish community and, specifically, the pro-Israel lobby — most prominently AIPAC, the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, but extending to a broader network of organizations, political donors, and media relationships that have made support for Israel a de facto prerequisite for significant political careers in both parties. The political scientists John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt published a controversial but carefully documented analysis of this lobby in 2007 — “The Israel Lobby and US Foreign Policy” — which argued that the lobby’s influence on American policy toward Israel exceeded what American strategic interests alone would dictate. The argument was condemned by some, disputed by others, and acknowledged as substantially accurate by a significant portion of the foreign policy community that discussed it privately while refusing to engage it publicly.

AIPAC conference stage. The organized pro-Israel lobby remains one of the three key domestic pillars sustaining unconditional U.S. support.

The second pillar is evangelical Christian Zionism — the approximately 30 to 40 million American evangelical Christians who support Israel on explicitly theological grounds, believing that the ingathering of the Jewish people in the Holy Land is a biblical prophecy whose fulfillment is a prerequisite for the Second Coming of Jesus Christ. This constituency is not primarily motivated by Jewish welfare or Israeli security. It is motivated by a theological commitment that makes Israeli territorial maximalism — including the settlements, including Israeli sovereignty over Jerusalem — a matter of religious obligation. It is a constituency that votes with high reliability, donates with high generosity, and has been a core component of the Republican coalition since the Reagan years. It has no equivalent interest in Palestinian welfare and no theological reason to support Palestinian statehood.

The third pillar is the defence industry. The $3.8 billion in annual American military aid to Israel is not primarily humanitarian assistance. It is a defence procurement program that requires Israel to spend a significant portion of the aid on American weapons systems — F-35 fighter aircraft, ammunition, missile defence components. The companies that manufacture these systems — Lockheed Martin, Boeing, Raytheon, General Dynamics — have their own political relationships, their own lobbying operations, and their own congressional allies who benefit from the defence contracts that the US-Israel military relationship generates. These interests have no intrinsic relationship to Israeli security or Palestinian welfare. They are interests in the continuation of the defence procurement relationship, which the continuation of the conflict helps sustain.

These three pillars together create a domestic political environment in which any American politician who attempts to condition military aid to Israel, support UN resolutions critical of Israeli conduct, or advocate publicly for genuine pressure on the settlement enterprise faces a coalition of opposition — organized, well-funded, and electorally consequential — that most political careers cannot survive.

Fourteen Presidents and the Pattern They Made

Truman recognized Israel in 1948. Eisenhower forced Israel out of the Sinai in 1957 — the last time an American president successfully used direct pressure to reverse an Israeli military action — by threatening to cut off American aid. Kennedy was the first president to make significant weapons sales to Israel. Johnson provided the diplomatic cover for the 1967 war. Nixon provided the military resupply that saved Israel in 1973 and formalized the strategic partnership.

Carter achieved the only successful American-brokered peace agreement in the conflict’s history — the Camp David Accords between Israel and Egypt in 1978 — through the application of sustained, direct, personal pressure on both Menachem Begin and Anwar Sadat. It remains the model of what American diplomatic engagement can achieve when a president is willing to spend political capital and apply genuine pressure. Carter was defeated in 1980 and is widely regarded among Washington’s pro-Israel establishment as the most hostile president to Israel in American history. He spent the subsequent four decades documenting the occupation and calling it apartheid in a book that cost him considerable establishment support and was, on the evidence, accurate.

Reagan increased military aid and deepened the strategic partnership. He also sold arms to Saudi Arabia and other Arab states in ways that the Israeli government opposed, demonstrating that the relationship had limits even then. Bush 41 withheld loan guarantees from Israel to protest settlement construction — the last time any American president used financial leverage to resist settlements — and subsequently lost the Jewish American vote in his re-election campaign in proportions that have been analyzed ever since as a cautionary tale for anyone considering similar pressure.

Israeli F-35 fighter jets, largely funded by annual U.S. military aid. The third pillar — the American defense industry benefits directly from the continuation of this relationship.

Clinton brokered Oslo, hosted Camp David, and ultimately blamed Arafat for the collapse in ways that were politically convenient and analytically inadequate. Bush 43 allowed the settlements to expand throughout the Second Intifada and the post-Intifada period, endorsed Israeli military operations in Lebanon in 2006, and described Sharon’s unilateral withdrawal from Gaza in 2005 as an act of courage — a characterization that ignored the continuing blockade that made the withdrawal less than it appeared.

Obama declared settlements illegitimate and an obstacle to peace in every public statement he made on the subject, and continued delivering $3.8 billion per year in military aid to the government building them. He achieved the Iran nuclear deal — which Israel opposed ferociously and which was the most significant constraint on Iranian nuclear ambitions in the conflict’s history — only to have it dismantled by his successor. His administration abstained on UN Security Council Resolution 2334, which declared settlements a “flagrant violation” of international law, in the final weeks of his second term — a decision that required waiting until he no longer faced domestic political consequences.

Trump moved the US Embassy to Jerusalem, recognized Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights, endorsed the annexation of the Jordan Valley as an Israeli objective, and produced the Abraham Accords — normalization agreements between Israel and four Arab states that carefully excluded any Palestinian voice or any resolution of Palestinian claims. He appointed his son-in-law Jared Kushner, whose family foundation had donated to Israeli settlements, as his Middle East peace envoy, and produced a “peace plan” that the Palestinian leadership was not consulted about and that required the Palestinians to accept permanent Israeli control of settlements and Jerusalem in exchange for limited autonomy over disconnected territory.

Biden called himself a Zionist, declared unconditional support for Israel after October 7th, approved military aid transfers throughout a campaign that the ICJ found posed a plausible risk of genocide, used the Security Council veto three times in the first months of the war, and attempted late in his term to condition some weapons transfers in ways that were too limited and too late to affect the campaign’s conduct meaningfully. He was a man of genuine personal conviction about Israel and genuine moral anguish about Gaza, who spent eighteen months demonstrating that personal conviction and moral anguish are not policy instruments.

The Cost: What the Veto Has Purchased

Seventy-five years of American diplomatic cover for Israeli conduct has purchased specific outcomes in the conflict and specific costs for American interests.

The outcomes: an occupation now in its 58th year, a settlement enterprise that has made the two-state solution physically impossible, a Gaza Strip that has been blockaded for seventeen years and is now rubble, a regional war involving Hamas, Hezbollah, and Iran that has destabilized Lebanon and brought the Middle East closer to wider conflict than at any point since 1973.

The costs: the collapse of American moral authority across the Global South, demonstrated most precisely in the contrast between American condemnation of Russia’s violation of Ukrainian sovereignty — a war in which international law was invoked with full American force — and American cover for Israeli violations of Palestinian sovereignty — a conflict in which the same legal framework was vetoed out of existence at the Security Council. The Global South has noticed this contrast and drawn conclusions from it that will shape the international order for decades.

Additional costs: the erosion of American credibility as a mediator in any future Middle East negotiation — no Palestinian leadership and no Arab government can trust an American-brokered process that has historically delivered settlements rather than a state. The strengthening of Iran’s regional position — every Israeli military campaign that kills Palestinian and Lebanese civilians is an Iranian propaganda victory. The radicalization of Muslim populations globally. The collapse of public support for Israel among younger generations in Western democracies, including among younger American Jews, who have grown up watching the occupation and cannot reconcile it with the liberal democratic values they were raised on.

There is also a direct military cost that receives almost no attention in American political discourse. The Iran war and the Gaza campaign have depleted American munitions stockpiles to the point where the United States has told Japan it cannot deliver Tomahawk missiles on schedule because they are needed now in the Middle East. American THAAD missile interceptors — each costing approximately $12.5 million — have been expended in numbers that have strained even American resupply capacity. The world’s most powerful military, fighting alongside and on behalf of a country of ten million people against Iran’s ninety million, found itself running short of munitions in ways that have affected commitments to Ukraine and to Pacific allies. The strategic cost of the American-Israeli relationship is no longer abstract. It is being measured in depleted stockpiles and delayed deliveries to allies who are watching and drawing their own conclusions about American reliability.

None of these costs appear on the American political balance sheet in a way that domestic political actors can price. They are externalities — paid by others, distant from the electoral calculations that drive American policy. They are the compound interest on the original sin, accruing in foreign capitals and street protests and international legal proceedings, while Washington calculates the domestic political cost of changing course and concludes, administration after administration, that the cost is too high.

It is not too high. It is simply distributed differently than the benefit — the benefit accrues to specific, organized, well-funded political constituencies in America, and the cost accrues to Palestinians, Lebanese, Israelis living under perpetual insecurity, and the international order that American power built and is now undermining.

Canada, too, bears an accounting here. Canadian foreign policy has historically positioned itself as more balanced than American policy on the Israel-Palestine conflict — a tradition rooted in the Pearson-era multilateralism and middle-power diplomacy that defines Canada’s self-image in the world. That tradition has been progressively abandoned over the past two decades, as successive Canadian governments have aligned more closely with the American position for a combination of strategic, domestic political, and alliance-management reasons. The Carney government faces a choice about whether to restore that tradition — to use Canada’s voice in international forums, its trade relationships, and its credibility as a country without colonial guilt in the region to advocate for accountability and a political solution. That choice is still being made as this series is completed.

What Would Change It

Pew Research, March 2026: Sharp generational decline in support for Israel, especially among younger Republicans and Democrats. The domestic architecture sustaining 45 vetoes is visibly fracturing.

The American veto will continue as long as the domestic political architecture that produces it remains intact. That architecture will change when its components change — when the organized pro-Israel lobby loses the electoral influence it currently holds, when evangelical Christian Zionism declines as a political force with the demographic shift in American Christianity, when the defence industry calculus shifts, when the cost of the current policy becomes visible enough in American strategic interests to override the domestic political calculation.

Some of these changes are already underway — and accelerating faster than most analysts anticipated.

A Pew Research Center survey in March 2026 found that 57 percent of Republicans under 50 have an unfavorable view of Israel, up from 50 percent the previous year and 35 percent in 2022. That is a 22-point swing in four years inside the party that has been Israel’s most reliable institutional defender. Among Republicans over 50, support remains at 75 percent — but that cohort is aging out of electoral dominance. The Iran war has deepened and accelerated a generational fracture that was already visible before October 7th. Tucker Carlson, the most influential voice in the right-wing media ecosystem that delivered Trump’s 2024 coalition, has broken with Trump over Iran, accusing Israel of pushing the president into war and making him a “slave to foreign interests.” Republican candidates in Florida are selling out of T-shirts that say “No American should die for Israel.” The shift is no longer theoretical. It is happening at campaign stops in farm country.

Perhaps the most telling admission came from Laura Loomer — the far-right media figure who has positioned herself as Trump’s most aggressive pro-Israel enforcer. In an interview with the New York Times in May 2026, she acknowledged telling Trump directly: “You’re probably going to be the last pro-Israel president we ever have.” According to Loomer, Trump responded: “You’re right.” A White House spokeswoman did not confirm the exchange — but did not deny it either. When the most committed pro-Israel voices inside the MAGA movement are privately conceding the political trajectory, the domestic architecture that has sustained the American veto for seventy-five years is not merely shifting. It is cracking.

The Palestinian people do not have decades to wait for that crack to widen into policy change. Gaza does not have decades. The question of whether any solution exists must be asked now, even if the honest answer is uncomfortable.

That question is the subject of the series’ final instalment.
