There’s a statue in nearly every major Vietnamese city — two women, often depicted riding elephants, swords raised, sometimes shown one carrying a child on her back mid-battle. They are Hai Bà Trưng, the Trung Sisters, and their rebellion against Chinese rule in 40 CE predates Ngo Quyen’s victory at Bach Dang, which I covered in Part II, by nearly nine hundred years. It also sits at the very front of the thousand years of Bắc thuộc I introduced in Part I — because the Trung Sisters rose up while Chinese administrative control was still relatively new, barely a century and a half old, making their revolt the earliest attested large-scale rejection of it.

I want to tell their story with the same caveat I raised in Part I about historiography, because the Trung Sisters sit at an even more contested intersection of myth and record than Ngo Quyen does. But I also want to be honest that the caveat doesn’t diminish what’s genuinely remarkable here: two women led an uprising that, for roughly two to three years, drove Han Chinese administration out of a substantial stretch of what is now northern Vietnam — a rebellion large enough and threatening enough that the Han court dispatched one of its most decorated generals to crush it.

The World Before the Revolt

By 40 CE, Han China had controlled the Red River Delta for about 150 years, since the absorption of Nam Viet in 111 BCE that I described in Part I. That century and a half of administrative rule had brought the layered structure I discussed previously — Chinese-appointed governors, Confucian bureaucratic norms, taxation, and a gradual pressure toward cultural and administrative assimilation, all sitting atop a Vietnamese social base — the làng village structure, ancestor worship, wet-rice cultivation patterns — that had persisted underneath.

One feature of pre-Han and early Han-period Vietnamese society is worth pausing on specifically, because it’s essential to understanding why a rebellion led by two women wasn’t an anomaly the way it would have been in a strictly Confucian social order. Pre-Sinicized Vietnamese society, along with several other cultures in the broader region at this period, appears to have been considerably less patriarchal in its inheritance and leadership norms than the Confucian Chinese administrative system being imposed on top of it. Vietnamese women in this era could and did inherit property, hold positions of local authority, and participate in leadership in ways that grew more restricted as Confucian norms deepened their hold over subsequent centuries. The Trung Sisters, in other words, weren’t rebels against their own society’s gender norms rising up in some singular, exceptional defiance — they were, by several scholarly accounts, working within a still-intact framework of Vietnamese female authority that Chinese administrative and cultural pressure was in the process of eroding. Their revolt can be read as being partly about that erosion, not just about tribute and taxation.

This matters for how we should read the timing of the revolt as well. It came at a moment when the first wave of intensive Sinicization — the deliberate imposition of Chinese family law, inheritance customs, and administrative practice on the Lac aristocracy — was reaching a kind of critical mass, roughly five generations into direct Han rule, long enough for local elites to feel its accumulating weight but not so long that memory of a different, less restrictive social order had entirely faded. That combination — enough time for grievance to build, not so much time that resignation had fully set in — recurs across several of Vietnam’s later resistance movements as well, and it’s worth keeping in mind as this series moves forward toward the French and American periods.

Trung Trac, Trung Nhi, and the Spark

The sisters — Trung Trac and Trung Nhi — were daughters of a local Lac lord (the Lac being the Vietnamese aristocratic lineage system that predated and persisted alongside Han administrative structures) in the Me Linh region, near modern Hanoi. Trung Trac was married to Thi Sach, himself a member of another prominent Lac lord family, and the marriage represented exactly the kind of local aristocratic alliance-building that persisted underneath Chinese administrative rule.

The traditional account holds that the immediate spark for rebellion was the execution of Thi Sach by the Chinese governor of the commandery, To Dinh — variously explained in different tellings as punishment for anti-Han agitation, a response to Thi Sach’s own resistance activities, or, in some later retellings, simple governor’s cruelty and overreach. Whatever the precise trigger, the effect was to turn Trung Trac from an aggrieved aristocratic wife into the leader of an uprising, joined immediately by her sister Trung Nhi, and rapidly gathering support from other local Lac lords and their followers who had their own grievances against Chinese taxation, forced labour demands, and the steady erosion of local Vietnamese aristocratic privilege under direct Han administration.

It’s worth being honest, as with Ngo Quyen, about how much of this account rests on later chronicle traditions written centuries after the events they describe, often compiled under later Vietnamese dynasties with their own interest in constructing a heroic, continuous narrative of resistance. The core facts — that a rebellion occurred in 40 CE, that it was led by two aristocratic Vietnamese women, that it achieved real if temporary success, and that it was eventually crushed by a major Han military expedition — are corroborated across Chinese and Vietnamese sources alike, which gives this rebellion a firmer evidentiary footing than some of the more legendary embellishments that later attached to it (the elephants, in particular, are plausible but not independently verified by contemporary sources). Han chroniclers, writing from the perspective of the empire being resisted rather than the empire that would later mythologize the resistance, had no obvious incentive to invent or inflate the scale of a rebellion led by women against Han administration — if anything, an empire concerned with projecting an image of civilizational and administrative superiority might have preferred to minimize or omit an episode in which a large, well-organized force led by two women temporarily expelled its officials from a significant province. That the episode appears clearly in Chinese sources anyway lends it real credibility.

The Uprising’s Reach

What makes the Trung Sisters’ rebellion remarkable isn’t just that it happened, but how far it spread and how long it held. Contemporary and near-contemporary Chinese records indicate the uprising didn’t remain a localized affair around Me Linh — it spread across a significant portion of the Red River Delta and, by some accounts, into areas further south, drawing in numerous other Lac aristocratic families and their followers who saw an opportunity to throw off Han administrative control entirely. Sixty-five towns or fortified settlements are said, in some traditional accounts, to have joined or fallen to the rebellion — a figure that should be treated with appropriate skepticism given the centuries of retelling between event and record, but that at minimum signals a rebellion of real scale rather than a minor local disturbance.

For a period most sources place at roughly two to three years, the rebels succeeded in expelling Han administrators from a substantial stretch of territory. Trung Trac was proclaimed queen — some accounts render this literally as ruler of an independent state — establishing a court and, by tradition, attempting to reduce the tax and labour burdens that Han administration had imposed. This is, again, a genuinely significant achievement regardless of how the later mythology embellished it: a rebellion led by women succeeded, for a real span of years, in ending direct Chinese administrative control over a substantial territory that had been under that control for roughly a century and a half.

It’s worth pausing on just how unusual this is by the standards of the ancient world more broadly — women leading a successful, multi-year military and administrative expulsion of an imperial power is a genuinely rare episode in any ancient historical record, not merely in the specific context of East and Southeast Asian history, and it’s part of why the Trung Sisters have attracted comparative scholarly attention alongside figures like Boudica in Roman Britain, another woman who led a large-scale, if ultimately unsuccessful, rebellion against imperial administration at almost exactly the same historical moment — Boudica’s revolt against Roman rule in Britain came in 60-61 CE, only two decades after the Trung Sisters, a coincidence of timing across opposite ends of the ancient world that historians have occasionally remarked on without being able to draw any direct causal connection between the two.

The Han Response

The scale of the uprising was serious enough that the Han court in 42 CE dispatched one of its most respected and experienced generals, Ma Yuan — a figure already decorated for campaigns elsewhere in the empire — at the head of a substantial expeditionary force to reassert control. Ma Yuan’s campaign is well documented in Chinese historical sources, including his own reported communications back to the imperial court, which gives historians a genuinely useful primary-source counterweight to the later, more mythologized Vietnamese chronicle tradition.

The campaign was hard-fought and took real time — Ma Yuan’s forces had to contend with difficult terrain, supply challenges moving a large army into the Delta, and determined resistance from the rebel forces — but by 43 CE, the rebellion had been decisively crushed. The traditional Vietnamese account holds that Trung Trac and Trung Nhi, facing defeat, chose suicide by drowning themselves in the Hat Giang river rather than face capture — a detail that, again, sits more in the register of heroic legend than verified contemporary record, but one that has become inseparable from how the sisters are remembered and commemorated in Vietnam today. Ma Yuan’s own subsequent actions — reportedly including efforts to more thoroughly impose Han administrative and cultural norms on the Delta in the aftermath, precisely to prevent a recurrence — are better documented, and represent a hardening of Chinese rule that, if anything, intensified administrative and cultural pressure on Vietnamese society for the following centuries rather than relaxing it. Some accounts describe Ma Yuan erecting bronze pillars to mark the southern boundary of Han territory and to serve as a monument to Han authority, though the precise location and even the historicity of these pillars remains debated among historians, another instance of a detail that has taken on outsized symbolic weight in later retellings regardless of its underlying evidentiary basis.

Why the Trung Sisters Endured as Symbol

It would be easy to read the Trung Sisters’ story as, ultimately, a story of failure — a rebellion that achieved a few years of independence before being crushed by superior imperial resources, followed by an intensification of exactly the administrative and cultural pressures the rebellion had risen up against. And in the narrowest military and political sense, that’s accurate. But the Trung Sisters became, over the following two millennia, one of the most durable and frequently invoked symbols in all of Vietnamese national and cultural memory — arguably more central to Vietnamese self-conception than Ngo Quyen’s actual, successful victory at Bach Dang nine centuries later.

Part of why is straightforward: an uprising that succeeded, even temporarily, this early in the thousand years of Bắc thuộc, offered later generations proof that Chinese administrative control was not permanent, natural, or unbreakable — a psychologically important data point for anyone contemplating resistance in later centuries, long before Ngo Quyen’s ultimately successful campaign gave Vietnam its permanent independence. But part of why is also specifically about the fact that the leaders were women, and what that has meant to how the story gets retold and invoked across very different eras of Vietnamese history.

Vietnamese anti-colonial nationalists in the early twentieth century invoked the Trung Sisters explicitly and often, holding them up as evidence of an ancient, indigenous Vietnamese tradition of resistance to foreign rule that colonial French administration was simply the latest chapter of. Ho Chi Minh’s movement continued this invocation, and the Vietnamese Communist Party after reunification built the Trung Sisters into state-sponsored commemoration — temples, streets, and the annual holiday still observed in their honour — partly as continuous nationalist symbolism and partly, more specifically, as evidence for a claimed tradition of Vietnamese women’s equality and leadership that the postwar communist state wanted to position itself as the inheritor and champion of, even as the actual, lived record of Vietnamese women’s political representation in the decades since reunification has been considerably more complicated and, by many measures, has lagged the founding mythology considerably. I’ll return to that tension directly in this series’ addendum on Vietnamese women, later in the series, rather than resolving it prematurely here.

A Note on Lady Trieu

The Trung Sisters were not, in the traditional chronicle record, the only instance of women’s military leadership against Chinese rule during the Bắc thuộc period. Roughly two centuries later, in the third century CE, another aristocratic Vietnamese woman, Trieu Thi Trinh — commonly known as Lady Trieu — is recorded as having led a further uprising against a subsequent Chinese dynasty’s administration, again achieving temporary success before ultimately being defeated. Her story carries an even heavier overlay of legend than the Trung Sisters’ — she’s frequently described in later tradition riding an elephant into battle, delivering a famously defiant quote about refusing to bend to any man’s will that most historians treat as later invention rather than contemporary record — but her presence in the tradition matters for the same reason the Trung Sisters’ does: it establishes women’s military and political leadership against foreign administrative rule as a recurring, rather than singular, feature of Vietnamese historical memory, appearing again roughly two centuries after the Trung Sisters and reinforcing the same pattern each time it recurs.

Lady Trieu’s uprising, like the Trung Sisters’ before it, was eventually crushed, and like the Trung Sisters, the manner of her death in the traditional account (again by suicide rather than capture) has become as central to her commemoration as the military campaign itself — a recurring narrative structure across multiple centuries of Vietnamese resistance mythology that’s worth noting on its own terms: heroic female leadership, temporary success, ultimate defeat, and death by the leader’s own hand rather than submission to a foreign captor. Whether this pattern reflects a genuine recurring historical reality or a narrative template later chroniclers applied to multiple distinct historical women is a question historians continue to debate, and one I don’t think this series can definitively resolve. What’s not in dispute is that the pattern, real or partly constructed, became foundational to how Vietnam tells its own history to itself.

What This Means Going Forward

I want to close this installment by returning to the caveat I opened with, because I think it’s important for how this series proceeds. The Trung Sisters’ rebellion is, by the best available evidence, a real historical event of real scale and consequence — corroborated across both Chinese and Vietnamese source traditions, not merely a later invention. But the specific details that have made the story so enduring and so useful across two thousand years of retelling — the elephants, the defiant final stand, the precise numbers of towns that rose up — sit closer to legend than to verified record, layered on by centuries of Vietnamese chroniclers and, later, nationalist movements that needed exactly this kind of founding story of women’s leadership in resistance to foreign rule.

That layering doesn’t make the Trung Sisters less important to Vietnamese history. If anything, it makes them more important, in a different register than pure military history — they’re important as a case study in how a society builds and sustains a national mythology out of a real but partial historical record, and then uses that mythology across two millennia of subsequent history to make sense of new threats as they arise. That’s a pattern this series will keep encountering as it moves forward — into the long centuries of independent Vietnamese dynasties, into the French conquest, and eventually into the wars of the twentieth century, where Ho Chi Minh and Vo Nguyen Giap will draw on precisely this well of memory — Trung Trac and Trung Nhi riding into battle against an empire that outnumbered them — to frame their own resistance as the latest chapter in a story Vietnam has been telling about itself for two thousand years.
