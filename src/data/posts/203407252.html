Satellite-style temperature map of a heat dome over western Europe, with extreme highs concentrated across France, Spain, and beyond. Such patterns, driven by stalled jet stream blocking, have become more frequent and intense.

There is a question I kept circling while writing the opening of this series, and it is a fair one — fair enough that a reader who asks it is owed a real answer rather than a wave of the hand. The question is: why here? Why does western Europe, and France above all, seem to draw the worst of these summers, year after year, as though the continent had been singled out for special punishment? It is one thing to say the planet is warming. It is another to explain why the warming arrives as a furnace over Paris and Andújar and the southwest of France while other places, no less guilty of emissions, are spared the headlines. The honest answer is that this is partly physics and partly geography, and that the two have lately entered into a conspiracy that no one designed and everyone now suffers.

Let me begin with the machinery in the sky, because once you see it you cannot unsee it, and it explains a great deal more than this one heat wave.

High above us, circling the planet, runs the jet stream — a river of fast-moving air at the altitude where airliners cruise, generated by the temperature difference between the cold pole and the warm tropics. For most of the modern era this river ran reasonably straight and reasonably fast, sweeping weather systems from west to east at a brisk clip. Rain came and went. Hot spells arrived and were promptly shoved along. The briskness was the point: a fast jet stream is a tidy housekeeper, never letting any one weather system overstay its welcome.

But a warming Arctic — and recall from the introduction that the Arctic is warming far faster than the rest of the planet — narrows the temperature difference between pole and tropics that drives the whole system. And a weaker temperature difference makes for a lazier, loopier jet stream, one that meanders in great northward and southward bulges rather than running straight. When the jet stream bulges north over a region, it can get stuck — wedged in place by areas of low pressure on either side, in a configuration meteorologists call a “blocking pattern” or, when it takes a particular shape, an “omega block,” after the Greek letter the loop resembles. A scientist at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts described it, during this very heat wave, as “the equivalent of a traffic jam in the atmosphere which locks in heat.” The housekeeper has gone on strike. The weather system that would once have been swept along now sits, and sits, and sits.

What sits, in these cases, is a dome of high pressure — the famous “heat dome” you will have read about. Under high pressure, air sinks, and sinking air is compressed, and compressed air heats. So the dome does two cruel things at once: it presses down a lid of hot, sinking air, and it clears the sky of the clouds that might have offered shade, so the sun hammers the ground unobstructed all day long. The heat builds, the night offers little relief because the lid holds the day’s warmth in, and the next day it builds again from a higher starting point. As one climate scientist put it with admirable economy, “the heat dome is really what the jet stream is doing; the heat wave is what we feel at the surface.” The dome is the cause; our suffering is the symptom.

Now add the geography, because this is where France draws its unlucky hand.

When one of these blocking patterns sets up over western Europe, it does not merely trap the local air — it acts, in the words of a forecaster at France’s weather service, like “a vacuum cleaner, drawing in heat and masses of hot air rising from North Africa” and blasting it northward. Look at a map and the problem becomes obvious. Just across a narrow sea from France and Spain lies the Sahara, the largest hot desert on Earth, an essentially inexhaustible reservoir of superheated air. When the atmospheric plumbing aligns the wrong way, that Saharan air gets pumped directly north into western Europe, and France — sitting at the receiving end of the pump, stretched between the Mediterranean and the continental interior — takes the full blast. This heat wave, like several before it, was driven by exactly this: a high-pressure ridge drawing Saharan air north and parking it over the continent. The Europeans have taken to giving these anticyclones names, as they once named only storms; a recent one was christened Cerberus, after the three-headed dog that guards the gates of the underworld, which tells you something about the mood.

And then there is the sea, which has turned traitor. The Mediterranean and the waters of the Bay of Biscay have themselves been running through marine heat waves — the sea surface several degrees warmer than it should be. In a normal summer, the relatively cool sea provides a nighttime mercy: as the land cools after sunset, breezes flow in off the water and bring the temperature down enough to sleep, to recover, to survive. But when the sea itself is feverish, that mercy is withdrawn. The cooling breeze does not come, or comes warm. The nights stay hot. And it is the hot nights, far more than the hot days, that kill — because the human body can endure a brutal afternoon if it is granted a cool night to recover, but deny it that recovery for days on end and the old, the ill, and the alone begin to die. France’s coastline, far from cooling the country, has lately been helping to cook it.

Meteorologists have a term for the phenomenon that is doing so much of the killing: the “tropical night,” a night in which the temperature never falls below twenty degrees Celsius. It sounds almost pleasant, a phrase from a holiday brochure. It is in fact one of the deadliest signatures of the new climate. The human body cools itself at night; that is when the cardiovascular system, strained by the day’s heat, is supposed to get its respite. A single tropical night is survivable. A week of them, stacked one atop another with no cool trough between, is what turns a heat wave from an ordeal into a mass-casualty event, because the body never gets to reset, and the accumulated strain falls hardest on exactly the people with the least reserve — the very old, the very ill, the very alone. During the June 2026 heat wave, Météo-France warned of “exceptionally high temperatures, both day and night,” and Portugal recorded nearly nation-wide tropical nights across its southern half. The daytime records make the headlines because a number like 40 degrees is dramatic. But the quieter statistic — the night that would not cool — is the one that fills the morgues.

How much of this is climate change, as opposed to the ordinary bad luck of weather? Here the science has grown precise enough to answer, and quickly. Within days of the June 2026 heat wave, a rapid-attribution analysis by the Climameter group concluded that human-caused climate change had made the event as much as four degrees Celsius hotter than a comparable heat wave would have been in a pre-industrial climate — with Paris running roughly two and a half degrees warmer, and cities like Zaragoza in Spain about four degrees warmer, than the same weather pattern would have produced in the past. Four degrees is not a rounding error. Four degrees is very often the entire difference between a heat wave that is merely uncomfortable and one that kills, between a night that dips to a survivable nineteen and one that hangs at a lethal twenty-three. The heat dome and the double jet and the Saharan pump are the machinery; the warming is the thumb on the scale that turns each event a few degrees more extreme than it would otherwise have been, pushing what used to be rare into what is now routine, and what used to be survivable into what is not.

So that is the answer to “why here”: a lazier jet stream that stalls more often, a high-pressure dome that compresses and bakes the trapped air, a Saharan furnace next door whose heat gets pumped north on demand, and a feverish sea that has stopped providing the nighttime relief it once did. Four factors, each amplified by warming, converging on one unlucky stretch of the map.

But I promised physics and geography, and I have saved the most striking piece of science for last, because it is the part that even well-read people tend not to know, and it is the part that most directly indicts the comfortable assumption that all warming is created equal. Western Europe is not merely warming. It is warming faster than almost anywhere else in the temperate world — and not only in the obvious sense that Europe as a continent outpaces the global average, but in a sharper, stranger sense that has recently come into focus. Researchers studying the trend have found that western European heat waves have been intensifying at a rate three to four times faster than the rest of the northern mid-latitudes over the past four decades. That is a remarkable figure. It means the heat is not just bad here; it is getting worse here faster than the simple arithmetic of global warming would predict.

Sea surface temperature anomalies showing marine heat waves in the Mediterranean and Atlantic waters around France — warmer seas reduce the cooling effect that once provided nighttime relief.

Why? The leading explanation points to a change in the jet stream itself — specifically, an increase in the frequency and persistence of what the scientists call “double jet” states, in which the single river of high-altitude air splits into two, with a zone of stagnant air between them that sits, conveniently and lethally, right over Europe. One body of research found that the rising persistence of these double-jet events explains almost the entire accelerated heat-wave trend in western Europe. The catastrophic heat wave of 2003, which killed as many as seventy thousand people across the continent, was anchored by a double jet that lingered for twenty-nine days — nearly a month of stagnant, baking air locked in place over a population with no defenses against it. In plainer terms: the atmospheric machinery is not merely delivering a hotter version of the old weather. It is delivering a new kind of weather, one in which the hot spells lock in place over Europe more often and for longer than they used to, for reasons bound up with the same Arctic warming that is melting Canada’s North. The thing that is unfreezing my country’s permafrost is, by the same disordered physics, helping to anchor the furnace over France. It is all one machine.

This matters for the series beyond mere explanation, and here is the connection I want you to carry forward. Recall the central image: the house built for winter, the infrastructure calibrated to a climate that has departed. France did not build its cities, its housing, its daily rhythms, or its expectations for a Saharan furnace pumped north under a stalled jet stream over a feverish sea. It built them for a temperate maritime climate of warm-but-bearable summers — the climate that actually obtained for the centuries during which Paris and Lyon and Toulouse took their present shape. The geography did not change. The Sahara was always next door; the Mediterranean was always there. What changed is the physics that governs how often, and how violently, that geography gets weaponized against the people living in it. The map stayed the same. The machine that reads the map turned hostile.

Parisians cooling off in fountains near the Eiffel Tower during extreme summer heat. France’s heat-emergency systems, built after the 2003 catastrophe, now help mitigate such conditions.

I will say, in fairness and as a kind of grim consolation, that the French have learned. After the catastrophe of 2003 — to which the next essays will return, because it haunts every European conversation about heat — France built what is now regarded as one of the most sophisticated heat-emergency systems in the world: graduated national alerts, the power to shut down schools and public events, the postponement of even the sacred national school exams when a red warning falls. There is evidence the system saves thousands of lives in each subsequent heat wave that would otherwise have been lost. So the story is not only one of helplessness. It is also a story of a country that took a horror seriously and adapted, intelligently, to a threat it had finally understood. That, too, is part of what this series is about — the difference between the societies that renovate the house and the ones that insist the weather will surely return to normal any summer now.

It will not. The anvil is over Paris, and the physics that put it there is not going home.
