Two days before Americans vote, here is the week’s writing from Substack, sorted the usual way: politics first, then war, the courts, history and technology, a generous helping of satire, and one quiet farewell from a small Ontario city that doesn’t exist.

If you’re arriving for the first time, I write from Toronto, a step outside the American room but close enough to hear the furniture being moved. Most of what I cover is the United States, because what happens there lands here, and in most other places too. The rest ranges over AI and science, Canada’s own quarrels, and enough history to keep the news in proportion. The satire, as always, is labelled. Start anywhere; the voice doesn’t change.

This weekend brings a small milestone of my own: the essay count passes 2,026, a number I’ve been quietly aiming for since The Numbers Game began matching essays to years. The series’ main run ends with it, in Essays 1999 and 2000 and a capstone for 2026 itself. Two closing pieces are saved for the holidays: a special edition on Year 0, a year that never existed, on Christmas Day, and on Boxing Day a 2,000-year-old man who has counted it all, with thanks to Mel Brooks and Carl Reiner, who met him first.

The US midterms are on Tuesday, and a whole shelf of series has been building toward them. He Loves War: The Hegseth Files finished its planned run this week, and will be back with a coda once the votes are counted. Let’s Talk Texas has followed Talarico and Paxton all autumn to a Senate race that wasn’t supposed to be close, and on Tuesday Texans settle it. The Household Ledger has added up what two years have cost American families, one line item at a time. When Democracy Fights Back has watched the maps, the courts and the polling places. Loyal Opposition has asked whether the Democrats are fit to fight, The Pendulum and the Ratchet whether anything undone can be put back, and Super PACman and The Grievance Machine have each closed with a coda, on the money and on the excuses being written in advance.

This year Trump built a fighting cage on the White House lawn. The cage came down, but the fight only got bigger. On Tuesday morning, election day, look for Fight Night, the official programme for a bout that now stretches from sea to shining sea, complete with the tale of the tape, the cornermen, a referee's chair that is still empty, and the scorecards both corners printed in advance. It caps a week in which the satire outran the calendar: nine pieces in Today's Satires, more than one a day, plus two Numbers Game essays that slipped into costume. The Office of Retroactive Justification alone filed four of them. When the news writes the first draft, all I have to do is fill in the forms.

Once the midterms are in the rear-view mirror, a fresh slate of series is waiting in the wings, to roll out over the coming weeks and months: FIFA Fraud; The Third Rail, on Social Security’s 2032 shortfall, ending in a draft bill; The Conditional Women; and one on the rise and fall of Trumpism. Also on the way is The Endless Frontier, a new series on the American science that federal money built, from ENIAC onward, what it gave the world, and what is now at risk as researchers head north to Canada and overseas. Alongside it is The Seed Corn Ledger, an interactive ledger you can open from within the series, setting what American science built against what is now under strain.

In Fiction, This Rough Magic brings the first season of Sketches from Elsinore to a close. The title borrows the Stratford Festival’s theme for 2026, taken from Prospero’s farewell to his art, and as the real festival town dims its stages for the winter, its fictional cousin down the road must do the same. Stephen Leacock closed Sunshine Sketches with a night train back to Mariposa, a town his readers could reach only in memory. Elsinore is luckier. Its lights stay on, and it will be back next season.

A reminder on Your Phone Bill and Mine: the CRTC hearing is November 30 in Gatineau, four weeks from tomorrow, and I’ll provide a summary in December.
