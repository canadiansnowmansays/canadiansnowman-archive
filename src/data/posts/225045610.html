Australians have a word for it. When a parliamentary party decides its leader has to go, someone calls a spill: every leadership post is declared vacant, and the party room votes in secret, usually within hours. Kevin Rudd went to bed as prime minister on 23 June 2010 and was a backbencher by the next morning, deposed by his own deputy, Julia Gillard. Three years later, on 26 June 2013, he spilled her in return. Tony Abbott lost the job to Malcolm Turnbull in September 2015, and Turnbull lost it to Scott Morrison in August 2018. For most of a decade, Canberra changed prime ministers more often than some countries change governments.

Canada has its own version, slower and more polite. On 2 February 2022, Conservative MPs used the procedure in the Reform Act to remove Erin O’Toole as leader, by 73 votes to 45, five months after he had lost a general election. In both countries the logic is the same. The leader serves at the pleasure of the caucus, and the caucus can withdraw that pleasure in an afternoon.

What both systems seldom do is spill a winner. That is what makes the challenge now under way in the United States Senate so unusual.

The challenge

On the night of Thursday, 12 November 2026, nine days after his party won the Senate, Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced that he would run against Chuck Schumer for majority leader. The next day, at a Democratic gathering in Denver on the future of the party, he went further and ruled out a run for president in 2028, as Carl Hulse reported for The New York Times. Some Democrats had suspected that the leadership bid was a way to raise his profile and court progressive voters ahead of 2028. Giving up the bigger race was his answer.

“I believe that the size and scale of these majorities provides an opportunity for the United States Senate to answer the call of the American people,” he said. “We can’t go back to business as usual.”

He called himself the underdog, and the arithmetic agrees. Schumer has led Senate Democrats since 2016, and he was a chief architect and funder of the campaign that won them the majority. His aides say he had collected enough commitments for another two-year term before he even had an opponent. Van Hollen claims “more than 10” supporters. The vote is by secret ballot, scheduled for 1 December.

Two of those supporters have gone public. Jeff Merkley of Oregon, in line to chair the Environment and Public Works Committee, praised Van Hollen for rallying support “for the people against the powerful.” Peggy Flanagan, the senator-elect from Minnesota, called him “the leader and fighter we need,” and she is one of several newcomers who campaigned on a change of leadership. Progressive activists and some labour groups are campaigning for him. Most senators, sensibly, are saying nothing. A secret ballot exists precisely so that they do not have to.

Nobody asked Chuck, again

Readers of this series have met this argument before. Part IV, “Nobody Asked Chuck,” catalogued Schumer’s troubles with his own party’s voters in Michigan, Maine and Florida, where primary electorates kept rejecting the candidates he preferred. In Maine, the eventual nominee, Troy Jackson, said openly that he would not support Schumer for leader. Part XVII noted that all four of Bernie Sanders’s marquee Senate candidates, Jackson, Abdul El-Sayed, Peggy Flanagan and Angie Nixon, had called for replacing him.

Part XVI, “The Audit Comes Back,” added the other column of the ledger. Schumer recruited Mary Peltola, Roy Cooper and Sherrod Brown. He backed James Talarico and Josh Turek early. He pitched Scott Colom on the Mississippi race over lunch at his apartment in Brooklyn, a state he privately called his “white whale,” and Colom won it. Late on 17 November, a final batch of votes carried Peltola past Dan Sullivan in Alaska by more than 12,000 votes and gave Democrats their 56th Senate seat, as Kellen Browning and Emily Schwing reported for The New York Times. Whatever the base thinks of his speeches, he built the majority that Van Hollen now proposes to lead.

His allies put it more briefly. Schumer can “point to the scoreboard,” one of them told Jonathan Lemire of The Atlantic, and most Democrats still expect him to win. The case against him is less about results than about age and timing. At 75, he is too old for those who want a new generation. He angered colleagues with his 2025 deal to avoid a government shutdown, and progressives think he was too slow to turn on Benjamin Netanyahu. Some allies have floated a way through. If Schumer announced that he would not seek re-election in 2028, sceptics could vote for him as a caretaker, and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez would have an open path to his New York seat, should she choose the Senate over the White House. Over in the House, the question has already been settled.

On 18 November, House Democrats held their own secret ballot and nominated Hakeem Jeffries for speaker by 251 votes to 11, with two abstentions, as Annie Karni reported for The New York Times. He still needs a majority of the full House in January, but the margin leaves little doubt that he will become the first Black speaker in American history. Nobody ran against him, and even some of his loudest critics did not want to be seen opposing him in public. Seven newly elected democratic socialists, among them Claire Valdez of New York and Donavan McKinney of Michigan, said they had voted no. Their voters, they said, had sent them with a mandate on Medicare for All, immigration enforcement, money in politics and Gaza.

Jeffries had done his counting first. In the days before the vote he met almost every member and member-elect, and less than 24 hours before it he endorsed a broad expansion of Medicare, a step towards the plan the left has long championed. That is how a leader heads off a spill instead of surviving one: he moves towards his critics before they can organise. Schumer’s newcomers want the money for the war in Iran cut off. So far he has offered them a temporary extension with restrictions.

So this is not a spill of the Australian kind, where a party room panics over bad polls. It is something closer to the reverse. The leader won, and the challenger says the win itself demands a different leader.

The real dividing line

The difference between the two men is not mainly about style, age or how well they perform on television. It is about the war in Iran, and a date.

Federal spending authority runs out on 11 December. Van Hollen wants Democrats to refuse any money for the military operation in Iran, even if that means shutting down the government a fortnight before Christmas. “Right now, under our current leadership, we have a situation where they’re not willing to use the most important power we have to end a war, which is to defund it,” he said in Denver.

Schumer has not embraced that tactic. He has said he intends to force an end to the war, and in an interview with Michael Barbaro for The Daily from The New York Times on 6 November he named the power of the purse as the way to do it. But he and other senior Democrats have been weighing a temporary extension, with some restrictions on the military money, that would run into early 2027. By then Democrats will hold both chambers and, presumably, far more leverage than they have in a lame-duck Congress still run by Republicans. Many Democratic senators share his caution. They worry that a shutdown over the holidays would be hard to end once it began, and that it would wreck their plans for an aggressive start to the new majority in January.

Both positions deserve a fair hearing, because both are serious.

The case for waiting is about leverage. Today, Democrats are a minority with a filibuster. In January they will control every appropriations bill that reaches the floor. A president can veto spending; he cannot force Congress to pass it. Six weeks from now, the party will be able to write the restrictions itself instead of bargaining for them. A shutdown fought now, from the minority, with Republicans still holding the gavels, could end in a retreat that teaches the White House the opposite lesson.

The case for acting now is about time. Six weeks is not nothing in a war. The war was, by many accounts, including the vice president’s, the single largest reason the Democrats won. Gasoline prices were at a record high for an election day, according to AAA, and voters tied those prices to the fighting. A party that ran against the war and then funded it, even temporarily, would be inviting the charge that it says one thing in October and does another in December. Van Hollen’s Denver line put that worry in its most quotable form: “Voters fired Trump and his policies of corruption, the war and high costs, but they haven’t fully hired the Democrats.”

The rush to vote

Some Democrats would like the whole contest over sooner. They have privately suggested moving the leadership election up from 1 December, so that the party does not spend the next three weeks arguing with itself. Brian Schatz of Hawaii, who is running unopposed to be the No. 2 Democrat, endorsed Schumer with a statement that doubled as an argument for speed: “We have no time to waste. We must move quickly to vote, unify, and get to work.” Now that the field seems settled, the caucus could simply vote to bring the date forward.

There is a Westminster lesson in that impulse, and it cuts both ways. Spills are fast because a party that is visibly fighting itself bleeds support every day the fight goes on. But the Australian experience also shows the cost of making leadership contests too easy. After a decade of revolving prime ministers, both major parties changed their rules. Labor now requires a large majority of its caucus to remove a sitting Labor prime minister. In December 2018 the Liberals adopted a rule that a leader who has won an election can be removed only by a two-thirds vote of the party room. Voters, it turned out, did not much like being governed by people who spent their evenings counting numbers against each other.

The Senate Democrats are not about to adopt Canberra’s habits. But rushing the vote would carry a smaller version of the same risk. A contest settled before anyone has had to defend a position in public settles nothing about the question that matters.

What both sides agree on

Strip away the leadership fight, and something more interesting appears. On one point the caucus seems close to unanimous.

Many senators, Hulse reported, now favour a shift in how the party communicates, putting its new members at the front. That is a real change in an institution where first-term senators have traditionally been neither seen nor heard. “We have enormous energy because of the success of the new members,” said Peter Welch of Vermont. “We should put them front and center.”

That is the Van Hollen argument, adopted by Schumer’s side without the change of leader. It concedes that the voters chose a particular kind of Democrat this year, the pastor from Kansas, the seminarian from Texas, the pediatrician from South Carolina, the logger from Maine and the district attorney from Mississippi, and that the party’s established voices are not the ones the country just voted for. Whether that concession is enough is what the newcomers themselves will decide when they cast their first secret ballot.

The vote that counts

There are two votes ahead, and only one of them is secret.

On 1 December, or earlier if the caucus moves the date, Senate Democrats will choose their leader behind closed doors. Schumer will very probably win. A senator can tell an activist one thing and the ballot box another, and no one will ever know. That is the purpose of a secret ballot, and in a party caucus it is a reasonable one.

On 11 December, the Senate will vote in public on whether to keep the government open and on what terms, including whatever it decides to do about the money for Iran. Every senator’s name will be on the record. If Van Hollen loses the leadership but persuades enough colleagues to vote against funding the war without restrictions, he will have won the argument he actually cares about. If Schumer wins the leadership and the party then funds the war without conditions into the new year, Van Hollen will have lost the vote and won the point, and the voters who were promised an end to the war will notice.

This series has spent eighteen instalments asking whether the Democrats could function as a loyal opposition. Part XVII found them in a position Westminster has no name for: a majority in Congress that is still the opposition to the government. A spill, in Canberra or Ottawa, is how a party answers the question of who leads it. The Senate Democrats will answer that on 1 December. Ten days later they will answer the harder question: what they intend to do with the power the voters gave them.
