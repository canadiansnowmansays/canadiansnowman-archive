I finished the epilogue to this series on April 27, 2026, with a university in Kunar still smoking and a family hiding in a dark room in Islamabad. I did not expect a coda. But on August 12, 2026, The Atlantic published a piece by Tricia Bacon and Daniel Byman titled “A Silver Lining in Afghanistan,” and it deserves one, because it tells a true story in a way that manages to obscure almost everything the epilogue documented.

The Atlantic’s argument is straightforward and, on its own narrow terms, correct. Five years after the American withdrawal, al-Qaeda has not rebuilt itself into the force it was in 2001. It has not struck the United States or Europe from Afghan soil since the last Americans left Kabul airport. Its leader was killed by an American drone in 2022. Its would-be successor is reportedly hiding in Iran, and the Taliban — for reasons of its own survival, not sentiment — appears reluctant to let him back into the country. The White House’s own counterterrorism chief has called the Taliban a “moderately cooperative” partner against the Islamic State’s regional franchise, ISKP, which has grown into the more serious threat in Afghanistan’s mountains. None of this was supposed to happen. The men who ran Afghanistan into the ground for five years before 9/11, sheltered the plotters, and outlasted twenty years of American occupation are now, by Washington’s own quiet accounting, useful.

That is a genuinely interesting finding, and I don’t dispute it. What I dispute is the frame around it — because the word “silver lining” is doing a specific piece of work, and that word is a metric. The metric is: did anyone attack the United States or Europe. By that metric, Afghanistan is a qualified success story. By every other metric — whether Afghans are alive, fed, in school, or being shelled by their neighbours — the same five years look like the epilogue I wrote in April: a country with 21.9 million people needing humanitarian aid, girls locked out of secondary school, malnutrition wards running out of supplies, and, since February, an actual shooting war with Pakistan that has killed civilians in Kabul, Kandahar, and a university in Asadabad. The Atlantic piece does not mention that war. Not once. It measures Afghanistan’s recovery by whether the phone stays quiet in Washington and London, and by that measure alone, declares a “silver lining.” It is, without quite meaning to be, a perfect specimen of the “consequential indifference” I named in the epilogue: an American institution grading Afghanistan’s condition entirely by what it costs us, not what it costs them.

But there is a second irony sitting underneath the first one, and it is the one that actually belongs in this series, because it is the pattern I have been tracing since Part 1: the terrorist becomes the partner, and the ally becomes the threat.

Consider what the Atlantic piece is actually describing when it praises the Taliban’s cooperation against ISKP. It is describing Washington leaning, quietly and pragmatically, on the government the United States spent twenty years and two trillion dollars trying to destroy — because that government now controls the whole country, has intelligence networks the collapsed republic never had, and shares an enemy. Fine. I said in the epilogue that curbing terrorism requires working with old enemies, and the Atlantic’s reporting is a decent case study of exactly that.

Now consider what Pakistan is doing in the same five years, to the same government, over the same border. Pakistan is a “major non-NATO ally” of the United States, a designation it has held since 2004, granted for its cooperation — however compromised — in the post-9/11 war on terror. It is the country that sheltered Osama bin Laden a few hundred metres from a military academy. It is the country whose Inter-Services Intelligence directorate created, armed, and sustained the Afghan Taliban for decades, gave its leadership sanctuary in Quetta throughout the American occupation, and only turned against its former client once the Pakistani Taliban — a separate but related organization that Pakistan’s own patronage helped midwife — began killing Pakistani soldiers on Pakistani soil. Since February 26, 2026, Pakistan has been in what its own defence minister called “open war” with Afghanistan. It has bombed Kabul and Kandahar. It struck a drug rehabilitation centre named Omid — Hope — and killed roughly 400 patients who were, by every account from the ground, unarmed men receiving treatment for an addiction crisis that Pakistan’s own decades of proxy war helped create. It broke an Eid ceasefire on the day it was declared. It shelled a university in Kunar in April and called the reporting of it “a blatant lie.” And the United States response, as I documented in the epilogue, was to say that Pakistan has “a right to defend itself” — a statement Pakistani officials reportedly read as a green light to keep going.

Put the two facts next to each other. The government Washington fought for twenty years is now praised in The Atlantic as a moderately cooperative counterterrorism partner. The government Washington calls an ally is bombing hospitals and universities in the country next door, and gets told it has a right to do so. If you were designing a case study in how little “ally” and “enemy” actually mean in American foreign policy — how entirely the labels are functions of whose interest is being served this week rather than any fixed judgment about conduct — you could not improve on this pairing. It is the same insight the epilogue reached with the mujahideen, with the Soviets, with Pakistan’s own decades-long relationship with the Taliban it now bombs. Support today becomes hostility tomorrow. But the Atlantic piece shows the mirror version: hostility yesterday becomes quiet cooperation today, and nobody in Washington seems bothered that the state doing the actual bombing this year is the one wearing the ally badge.

It is worth sitting with how Pakistan earned the “ally” label in the first place, because the history makes the current war stranger, not less strange. The major non-NATO ally designation dates to 2004, granted for cooperation in the immediate post-9/11 years — the same years in which Pakistan’s intelligence service was, by the assessment of American officials and journalists alike, simultaneously sheltering the Afghan Taliban leadership across the border in Quetta. Pakistan spent the better part of two decades hedging: cooperating with Washington in public while preserving the Taliban as a strategic asset in private, on the theory that whoever eventually ruled Kabul would need to answer to Islamabad. That bet paid off in August 2021. It has not paid off since. The Pakistani Taliban that now kill Pakistani soldiers and prompted this year’s war are not some unrelated group; they are, by most serious accounts, an offshoot of the same jihadist ecosystem Pakistan spent decades cultivating next door, now turned partly against its architect. Islamabad built the fire and is now bombing its neighbour’s house for failing to put it out.

Layer onto that the detail I flagged in the epilogue about India: in October 2025, India reopened its full embassy in Kabul and began courting Taliban officials over mining rights, prompting Pakistan’s defence minister to accuse Afghanistan of acting as an Indian proxy. Here is a government that funded, housed, and legitimized the Taliban for over twenty years, now treating a rival power’s diplomatic outreach to that same Taliban as a hostile act deserving of war footing. The through-line from the epilogue holds without needing to strain for it: relationships in this region are never fixed by conduct, only by whichever patron currently needs the Taliban to be a client rather than a threat. Washington calling the Taliban “moderately cooperative” and Pakistan calling them an Indian proxy are not contradictory readings of two different governments. They are two patrons applying the same self-interested test to the same government within months of each other, and reaching opposite verdicts.

There’s a further wrinkle the Atlantic doesn’t touch, and it is worth pulling out because it undercuts its own thesis. The piece credits the Taliban’s cooperation against ISKP as a sign of stabilizing counterterrorism relations. But the epilogue already showed what that “stabilization” costs Afghans directly: to keep its own fighters from defecting to ISKP, the Taliban has become more repressive, and the crackdown on ISKP’s domestic operations appears to have pushed the group toward more international plotting rather than less. The security gain the Atlantic celebrates was purchased, in part, with more internal Taliban repression — the same repression that keeps Afghan girls out of school and fills Kabul’s prisons. A “silver lining” measured only by attacks that didn’t happen in New York or Paris says nothing about what happened to buy that outcome inside Afghanistan’s own borders. This is the through-line of the whole series: every actor who claims a limited, defensible objective in Afghanistan — Persia’s satraps, Alexander’s garrisons, the British, the Soviets, the Americans, and now Pakistan and, more quietly, Washington’s own counterterrorism apparatus — ends up imposing costs on ordinary Afghans that never make it into the ledger of whoever is doing the counting.

The Atlantic piece closes with a real and defensible lesson: that curbing terrorism sometimes means working with old enemies, and that the job of policymakers is to weigh the moral costs of those partnerships against the security gains. I don’t disagree with that as a principle. I disagree that you can apply it honestly to Afghanistan in August 2026 while leaving out that the same five years produced a war with a supposed American ally that has, by the UN’s own count, killed and displaced tens of thousands of Afghan civilians, destroyed a rehabilitation centre, and damaged a university — and that Washington’s only public comment on any of it was to endorse the bomber’s right to keep bombing. A silver lining that only covers the cloud facing Washington is not a silver lining. It’s a weather report for one city, filed as if it described the whole sky.

What would an honest ledger for Afghanistan actually require, five years on, if it wasn’t just counting attacks that didn’t reach Western capitals? It would need to sit the al-Qaeda numbers next to the war numbers: yes, no attack on the US or Europe since 2021, but also 21.9 million Afghans needing humanitarian assistance before Pakistan fired a shot, and tens of thousands displaced since it did. It would need to note that the same Taliban government being praised as a counterterrorism partner is, by the UN’s own reporting, still supplying weapons to the Pakistani Taliban even as it cooperates against ISKP — cooperation and complicity running in the same government at the same time, because that is what surviving in Afghanistan’s neighbourhood actually requires. It would need to weigh a dead al-Qaeda leader in 2022 against 400 dead patients in a rehabilitation centre in 2026, and ask whether either number tells you anything true about the other. The Atlantic’s ledger does none of this. It opens the books to one column and calls the balance a silver lining.

Pakistan bombed a building called Hope in March. The Atlantic found a different kind of hope in August, in a different capital, using a different scale. Both are true. Only one of them was written for the people actually living under the mountains.
