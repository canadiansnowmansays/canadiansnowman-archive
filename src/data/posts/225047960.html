Every confidence trick needs three sets of people. There is the man running it. There are the respectable people who vouch for him. And there are the marks, who hand over their money and their loyalty because the vouchers made the man look safe.

When this series last checked in, on July 20, 2026, the first set was in a federal detention centre in Miami. Andrew and Tristan Tate had been arrested on July 18 on their way into a bare-knuckle boxing event, at Britain’s request, to face rape and sex-trafficking charges. The question that addendum ended on was whether the second set, the friends in Washington who had already pried them loose from Romania once, would do it again.

Four months on, that question has an answer. And the third set, the marks, have started giving one of their own.

The phone that didn’t ring

Hours after the arrest, on the morning of Sunday, July 19, a representative of the brothers contacted Alina Habba. As Megan Twohey, Isabella Kwai, Matei Barbulescu and Alan Feuer reported for The New York Times on July 23, the choice of number was no accident. In January 2025, shortly after being named a counsellor to President Trump, Habba had gone on a podcast and assured Andrew Tate that he had backing in the new administration. Now the representative wanted to know whether she was free to talk.

She did not reply. People familiar with her thinking told the Times she wanted nothing to do with the brothers.

The rest of the guest list emptied the same way. Paul Ingrassia, acting general counsel of the General Services Administration and a former member of the Tates’ legal team, posted a complaint about politically driven prosecutions, then told the Times he no longer had any connection to them. Donald Trump Jr., who once called Andrew a friend, said nothing. Neither did Barron Trump, nor Candace Owens, who had championed both brothers on her show. Roger Stone explained that his retweet of Tristan had gone out before the news broke. Paolo Zampolli, a special envoy for the president, had hosted the brothers at a Washington party two days before the arrest; he said he had not understood how serious the allegations were.

The White House press secretary, Karoline Leavitt, said the president had no plans to intervene. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who will sign the final order if a judge approves the extradition, said from Manila that there was no role for him to play at that point, and possibly never would be.

A few voices stayed loyal. Representative Abe Hamadeh of Arizona called it lawfare. Tucker Carlson, who once gave the brothers the gentlest interview of their careers, told the Times, without evidence, that the arrests were punishment for criticising Israel. The traditional right went the other way: Ben Shapiro said the movement should have cast them aside years ago.

The best summary came from Lauren Southern, the former right-wing influencer who has accused Andrew Tate of raping her in Romania. She told the Times that now there was a reckoning, “suddenly everyone wants to pretend they had nothing to do with building this beast.”

Readers of this series will remember how that building was done: Andrew Tate turned his huge audience of young men into a political asset, offered it to Mr. Trump’s advisers and family, and got a travel ban lifted in Bucharest in February 2025. The protection was always conditional. It was worth giving as long as it cost nothing. A federal arrest warrant, executed by the government’s own marshals and argued in court by the government’s own prosecutors, made it expensive. The vouchers did what vouchers do when the man they vouched for is in handcuffs. They remembered other engagements.

The court holds

The brothers’ lawyer, Joseph McBride, told the Times they were focused on winning in court rather than calling in favours from the president’s circle. So far, the court has not obliged.

On Thursday, August 27, the Tates came back before a federal judge in Miami to ask for release while they fight extradition. The Miami New Times counted roughly 30 young men outside the courthouse, Gen Z and millennial supporters who had travelled from Orlando, Austin, New Orleans, Los Angeles and London. Some tried to get into the hearing and were turned away.

Thirty. The Associated Press describes the brothers as dual U.S.-British citizens with a social media empire built on wealth, male dominance and misogyny, and Andrew alone as having more than 10 million followers on X, most of them boys and young men. The movement that was supposed to be a voting bloc could not fill a city bus outside a federal courthouse.

Inside, the brothers denied the accusations, attacked their critics, and told the court that their brash online personas were characters rather than their real selves, as Courthouse News Service reported. It is an interesting defence for two men whose brand was that they were the only honest men left, telling young men the hard truths about women that everyone else was too cowardly to say. The judge was not persuaded either way. In September, she ordered both brothers held for the duration of the extradition fight.

Then, on Monday, October 5, the case file opened. Court documents made public in the United States set out how British police say the operation worked. According to Agence France-Presse, investigators describe a “lover boy” model: women were drawn in with the promise of a romance, then coerced into making online pornography. McBride’s response was that no American court should hand a person over to a foreign country on the word of secret accusers.

That is the £21 million machine this series described in December 2025, now set out in a prosecutor’s language rather than a columnist’s. The personas were the marketing. The romance was the recruitment. The masculinity, as this series argued from the start, was the fake news.

The Times noted one more figure worth keeping in mind. In recent years the United States has refused a British extradition request only once.

The author changes the subject

Every movement has a scripture. The manosphere has several, but the most widely read predates all of them. Robert Greene’s The 48 Laws of Power, published in 1998, is a manual of manipulation, deceit and sabotage, drawn from the careers of Napoleon, Bismarck, Kissinger and Ivan the Terrible. It has sold nearly 10 million copies in the United States alone. It found a second life during the pandemic, carried by TikTok and Instagram to an audience of young men and to the masculinity influencers who sell to them.

As Alexandra Alter reported for The New York Times on November 11, 2026, Greene’s new book is about something else entirely. In August 2018, a burst blood clot damaged the right side of his brain. He woke up in hospital partly paralysed and, to his own surprise, ecstatic. He began planning his next book in the medical transport home. The Law of the Sublime, published this month, runs to 496 pages on awe, self-transcendence and letting go of the ego. The man who told readers to crush their enemies completely now tells them they are part of a boundless universe.

He does not renounce the old book, and says he has no regrets. But he told the Times he worries his followers will be disappointed: “They’re always wanting to go back to power.”

Two of his other admissions matter more for this story. Greene says the original book came out of deep anger at being pushed around by arrogant bosses, written by a man in his late thirties who had never held any power himself and thought of himself as a bit of a loser. And when asked why the book took off again among the young, he pointed to fear: young people frightened about their careers, the job market and what artificial intelligence will do to both, reaching for anything that promises them control over their own lives.

That is the market the Tates were working. Not strong men looking for a creed, but frightened ones looking for a lever. The founding text of the movement was written by someone who felt powerless, for readers who felt powerless, and it sold the feeling of control. The Tates took that feeling, attached a webcam business and an online pimping “degree” to it, and charged admission.

Greene, at 67, walking with a cane, has moved on to the sublime. His readers, he admits, keep asking for the old stuff. The question for the midterms was whether the people the Tates were selling to would keep asking too.

The ballot

In 2024, young men were the great prize of American politics. Mr. Trump went to UFC fights and sat for podcasts with comedians who railed against cancel culture. The Tates were in the crowd with him at UFC 327 in Miami as recently as April 11, 2026. CNN’s exit polling, as Newsweek noted, had him winning men under 30 by a single point, a remarkable result in a group that traditionally leans Democratic.

On November 3, 2026, that result was undone. Axios reported on November 5 that Democratic support among men under 30 rose from 51 per cent in 2024 to 59 per cent, and among young women from 61 to 70 per cent. The AP VoteCast survey of more than 100,000 midterm voters found that about three-quarters of voters under 30 backed their local Democrat. The Associated Press reported on November 7 that many of the young men Mr. Trump won in 2024 either sat this election out or switched sides.

Honesty requires a caveat here, and it is a large one. Young voters did not walk away because of two brothers in a Miami jail. The young people the AP interviewed talked about working several jobs to get by, about prices, about artificial intelligence data centres arriving in their towns. There is a war in Iran that is not going well. Pollsters on both sides of the aisle say the young are still up for grabs.

But the wager behind the masculinity brand was specific. It said that if you offered young men a performance of dominance, toughness and grievance, they would give you their loyalty in return, whatever else was happening in their lives. The brothers built a business on that wager, and the movement around them built a political strategy on it. In November 2026, the young men were offered the performance again, and a clear majority turned it down.

What the real thing looks like

The most useful thing written about American manhood this month did not mention the Tates at all.

On November 15, 2026, The Atlantic published David Brooks’s conversation with Phil Klay on its podcast The Permanent Questions (also on YouTube). Klay served as a Marine public affairs officer in Anbar province during the 2007–2008 surge, won the National Book Award in 2014 for his story collection Redeployment, and has recently been chosen to lead PEN America. Brooks set the scene with the state of young men: out of work in large numbers, depressed, dying by suicide at far higher rates than young women, and living with the aftermath of what he counts as three failed wars this century, Iraq, Afghanistan and now Iran.

Into that, Brooks said, came an administration that presents itself as an advertisement for a particular kind of masculinity. Klay’s verdict on the version Pete Hegseth is selling was that it is not traditional at all, but a cartoon assembled from bits of tradition and built for the camera. He pointed to the video the defence secretary found time to produce in the middle of a faltering war, promising to raise testosterone in the ranks. Compare that, Klay said, with Socrates in Plato’s Symposium: a good soldier, yes, but also a lover of wisdom and a whole human being.

Klay’s idea of honour is less glamorous and much harder. It is living up to the duties of the role you have taken on, whether that role is Marine, general, public official or parent. He told a story he had heard from his friend Elliot Ackerman, a decorated veteran of the Second Battle of Fallujah. A Marine crossing an open highway is shot. A second Marine runs out to drag him back and is shot too. Everyone can now see what waits for the next man. A third Marine goes anyway and brings them both in. Ackerman says he has never actually felt courage as an emotion. What moves men across that highway, in his view, is love for one another, and it is the opposite of fear.

Brooks, near the end, asked Klay to name his core virtue. Brooks’s own was humility. Klay’s answer was one word: “Love.” Everything decent, he said, flows from it. Brooks offered a piece of evidence from the Grant Study, which followed Harvard men through their lives. Among those who served in the Second World War, the trait that best predicted promotion was not intelligence, strength or measured courage. It was the warmth of the home they grew up in, and their love for their mothers. The men who had learned love at home knew how to give it to the men under their command.

There was rage in the conversation too. When Brooks asked how he felt walking past the graves of America’s recent wars, Klay struggled to answer, and the word he finally found was waste. Brooks closed the episode by observing that what moved him most was the mixture: a man still angry at a country that had not guarded the lives entrusted to it, who answered that anger not with bitterness but with love.

Set that beside the product the Tates were selling. Their masculinity measured a man by what he could take: money, women, obedience, attention. Klay’s measures him by what he will cross an open highway for. Theirs was built on grievance and sold as strength. His begins with the admission that you are part of something larger than yourself, which is roughly where Robert Greene ended up after his stroke, by a much harder road.

The con, four months on

None of this means the con is over. Thirty young men travelled to Miami for two accused sex traffickers. Millions still follow the brothers. Greene’s readers still want the laws of power, and somebody will always be willing to sell them a sharper version. The extradition could take months, and the Romanian case is still unresolved.

But look again at the three sets of people every con requires. The man running it is in a federal cell, having told a judge that his online persona was only a character. The vouchers have found other engagements and stopped answering the phone. And the marks, the frightened young men the whole machine was built to recruit, were offered the performance once more in November and, in large numbers, declined it.

Stephen Leacock once observed that advertising is the science of arresting the human intelligence long enough to get money from it. The Tates’ advertising worked for a long time. This autumn, for the first time, it ran into competition: a war-weary novelist on a podcast, a power guru humbled by his own body, and several million young voters who had apparently stopped listening.

The con was always that tough men take. The answer turns out to be the one Phil Klay gave in a single word.
