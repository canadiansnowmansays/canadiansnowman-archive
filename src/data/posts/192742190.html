On January 20, 2017, Donald Trump stood on the steps of the United States Capitol and delivered an inaugural address unlike any in American history.

“For too long, a small group in our nation’s capital has reaped the rewards of government while the people have borne the cost,” he said. “Washington flourished — but the people did not share in its wealth. Politicians prospered — but the jobs left, and the factories closed. The establishment protected itself, but not the citizens of our country.”

He called it “American carnage.” He promised a different kind of America — one that would put its own interests first, that would no longer be “the policeman of the world,” that would judge its international relationships purely on the basis of whether they benefited Americans, and that would withdraw from the multilateral commitments that the post-war generation had built as the architecture of a stable world.

The speech was not, in its diagnosis, entirely wrong. The post-war order had indeed produced benefits that were unequally distributed. The trade liberalization that had enriched American consumers and global corporations had hollowed out American manufacturing communities. The alliances that guaranteed European and Asian security had been financed disproportionately by American taxpayers. The international institutions that American diplomacy had built were often captured by bureaucratic interests that served their own continuation rather than their stated purposes.

These were legitimate grievances. They deserved serious policy responses. The question was whether the response Trump offered — withdrawal, unilateralism, the subordination of every international commitment to immediate transactional calculation — was a serious policy response or a demolition job masquerading as reform.

The first Trump term answered that question.

The Architecture Under Stress

The post-war institutional framework had survived significant stress before 2017. Vietnam had damaged American credibility. Iraq had broken it. The financial crisis of 2008 had demonstrated the order’s economic vulnerabilities. Russian revanchism and Chinese rise had created structural challenges that the order’s institutions were not well designed to manage.

But the order had survived all of these stresses for a fundamental reason: the United States, however wayward its specific actions, had remained committed to the order as an institutional framework. Even in the depths of the Iraq disaster, even when American credibility was at its lowest, American officials maintained that the rules-based international order was valuable, that multilateral institutions served American interests, and that alliances were assets rather than liabilities.

Trump’s first term was different. It was not a stress test of the order’s resilience against external challenges. It was a stress test of the order’s resilience against its most important member’s deliberate and sustained assault from within.

The assault was methodical. Trump withdrew the United States from the Trans-Pacific Partnership on his third day in office. He withdrew from the Paris Climate Agreement. He withdrew from the Iran nuclear deal — the JCPOA — that the Obama administration had negotiated with Iran, Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and China after years of painstaking diplomacy. He withdrew from the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty. He withdrew from the Open Skies Treaty. He withdrew from the UN Human Rights Council. He threatened to withdraw from the World Trade Organization, NATO, and the UN itself.

Each individual withdrawal could be argued on its specific merits. The TPP was genuinely imperfect. The Iran deal had genuine weaknesses. NATO burden-sharing was genuinely unequal. But the cumulative effect of relentless withdrawal — the pattern of treating every multilateral commitment as a bad deal to be renegotiated or abandoned — was not policy reform. It was institutional demolition.

The message to America’s allies was consistent: your security depends on your utility to us, and your utility will be reassessed on a case-by-case basis. Germany was told it needed to spend more on defense or lose American protection. South Korea was told it needed to pay more for the American troops protecting it. Japan was told its trade surplus with the United States was unacceptable. Canada and the EU were hit with tariffs that Trump justified using the same national security provision designed to restrict imports from adversaries.

The message to America’s adversaries was equally consistent: the alliances and institutions that had constrained American power for seventy years no longer reliably constrained it, and the adversaries’ calculation of American resolve needed to be updated accordingly.

The Iran Deal and Its Consequences

The withdrawal from the JCPOA in May 2018 deserves particular attention because it is the most direct precursor to the Iran war of 2026.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action — negotiated over two years by Secretary of State John Kerry, coordinated with Britain, France, Germany, Russia, and China, and implemented in 2016 — had frozen Iran’s nuclear program in exchange for sanctions relief. Iran’s uranium enrichment was capped at 3.67 percent — far below weapons-grade. Its stockpile of enriched uranium was reduced by 98 percent. International inspectors had unprecedented access to Iranian nuclear facilities.

The deal was not perfect. It had a fifteen-year sunset clause — after which Iran could theoretically resume enrichment without restriction. It did not address Iran’s missile program or its regional proxy network. And it had been negotiated under duress — Iran accepted constraints it would not have accepted from a position of strength, and the constraints’ durability depended on the deal’s benefits being real and lasting.

Trump’s withdrawal ended those benefits without reversing the constraints — for a time. Iran initially continued to comply with the deal’s terms even after the US withdrawal, hoping that European efforts to maintain the sanctions relief would succeed. When it became clear they would not, Iran began systematically exceeding the deal’s limits — increasing its enrichment levels, expanding its centrifuge capacity, reducing inspector access.

By the time the Biden administration attempted to renegotiate the deal in 2021-2022, Iran’s nuclear program had advanced to the point where the terms available were significantly worse than the original agreement. By 2025, Iran’s stockpile of highly enriched uranium — enriched to 60 percent, close to weapons-grade — was large enough to produce multiple nuclear weapons if further enriched. The nuclear threat that the JCPOA had frozen was now more advanced than at any point in the deal’s history.

This was the direct consequence of the Trump withdrawal. The administration that launched the Iran war in 2026 was, in effect, dealing with a nuclear program that its predecessor had allowed to advance by withdrawing from the agreement that had frozen it.

The irony is precise. The Iran war’s stated primary objective is to prevent Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons. The nuclear program that makes that objective urgent was allowed to advance by the same political movement that launched the war to eliminate it. The arsonist is now the firefighter.

The NATO Challenge

Trump’s relationship with NATO was the most consequential of his first-term institutional challenges, and the one whose consequences are most visible in the Iran war of 2026.

Trump’s critique of NATO was not entirely without merit. The alliance’s formal commitment that members should spend 2 percent of GDP on defense was widely ignored — only a handful of member states met the target, and the free-rider problem was real. An alliance in which one member provided the overwhelming majority of the military capability was structurally unbalanced in ways that were genuinely unsustainable.

But Trump’s approach to this legitimate problem — public humiliation, private threats to withdraw, the systematic undermining of allied confidence in American commitments — did not produce the defense spending increases he claimed credit for. Those increases, where they occurred, were driven primarily by Russian aggression rather than American pressure. What Trump’s approach did produce was a fundamental recalculation by European allies of the reliability of American security guarantees.

German Chancellor Angela Merkel, speaking in Munich in May 2017, said that Europe could no longer fully rely on its traditional partners and had to take its destiny into its own hands. It was the most significant statement by a European leader about the trans-Atlantic relationship since NATO’s founding. French President Emmanuel Macron declared NATO “brain dead” in 2019.

The damage to alliance confidence was not abstract. When the Biden administration took office in January 2021 and attempted to restore alliance relationships, it found that the four years of Trump had produced structural changes that good rhetoric could not quickly reverse. European governments had begun the process of building strategic autonomy — investing in their own defense industrial base, coordinating military planning without American participation, developing independent intelligence and logistics capabilities — that, once started, was difficult to stop even when American reassurances were forthcoming.

The Europe that is watching the Iran war of 2026 from the sidelines — refusing to join, developing postwar Hormuz escort plans without American participation, building alternative security architecture at accelerated pace — is the Europe that Trump’s first term created. The second term is completing what the first term began.

The Domestic Fracture Deepens

The first Trump term also accelerated the domestic fractures that Vietnam had opened and Iraq had widened — the divisions about American power, American values, and American identity that are the substrate on which foreign policy is made.

Trump’s 2016 victory was, among other things, a verdict on the foreign policy establishment that had produced Vietnam, Iraq, and the financial crisis — a verdict delivered by the communities that had paid the disproportionate costs of those failures. The working-class men whose sons fought in Vietnam and Iraq, whose jobs were displaced by the trade liberalization that the establishment had championed, whose communities had been hollowed out by the economic consequences of a globalization that benefited those at the top — these communities found in Trump a vehicle for their accumulated grievances.

This is not, primarily, a story about racism or authoritarianism, though those elements were present. It is, primarily, a story about the legitimacy deficit of an establishment that had governed in its own interests while claiming to govern in everyone’s. The post-war order’s benefits were real, but they were distributed unequally. The costs were borne disproportionately by those who had the least political voice. The credibility gap — the distance between what the establishment said and what it did — was the same gap that Vietnam had opened and that had been widening ever since.

Trump did not create this deficit. He exploited it. The difference matters, because the deficit will outlast Trump. Even if American politics produces a successor who is more committed to multilateral institutions and alliance relationships, the domestic political conditions that made Trump possible — the inequality, the cultural displacement, the institutional distrust — will persist and will constrain any successor’s foreign policy options.

Canada experienced this dynamic with particular clarity. The renegotiation of NAFTA into the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement — CUSMA — in 2018 and 2019 subjected America’s closest ally and largest trading partner to the same tariff threats and public humiliations that Trump was applying to adversaries. Canadian steel and aluminum were declared a threat to American national security under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act — a provision designed for use against hostile powers, not against a country that had sent troops to Afghanistan alongside Americans and whose border had been undefended for two centuries. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau called the designation “insulting.” Trump called Trudeau “very dishonest and weak.” Keenleyside’s careful architecture of Canadian-American partnership — the Permanent Joint Board on Defence, the continental economic integration of the post-war decades, the mutual embedding of the two countries’ institutions — was being subjected to the same transactional dismantling as every other American international commitment. From Toronto, it was clarifying. The order didn’t just apply to distant allies. It applied next door.

The post-war settlement’s domestic foundation — the broad political consensus that American global leadership served everyone’s interests, not just the establishment’s — had eroded to the point where it could no longer sustain the commitments the order required. That consensus cannot be restored simply by changing the occupant of the Oval Office. It requires rebuilding something more fundamental: the sense that the order’s benefits are broadly shared, that the costs are fairly distributed, that the institutions of American democracy are capable of governing in the common interest rather than in the interest of the connected and the credentialed.

That is a project of decades, not of one election cycle.

What the First Term Established

The first Trump term established four things that will shape American power for a generation.

First, that the post-war order’s institutional architecture is not self-sustaining. It requires active maintenance by the United States, and when that maintenance is withdrawn, the architecture begins to decay at a pace that is faster and more consequential than its builders anticipated.

Second, that American alliances are more fragile than they appeared. The trans-Atlantic relationship that had survived Suez and Vietnam and Iraq and the financial crisis did not survive four years of deliberate American contempt without structural damage. The allies are still there. They are not as reliably there as they were.

Third, that the domestic political conditions that sustained American global leadership no longer reliably exist. The bipartisan foreign policy consensus that provided the domestic foundation for the post-war order has been replaced by a fractured political landscape in which international commitments are contested across party lines in ways they were not before.

President Biden addresses the Munich Security Conference, February 19, 2021. “America is back. The trans-Atlantic alliance is back.”

And fourth, that the transition from one administration to another does not reset the damage done. The Biden administration’s four years of alliance repair, multilateral re-engagement, and institutional restoration — genuine in intention, significant in some of its achievements — did not fully undo what the first Trump term had done. The allies had learned to hedge. The adversaries had learned to probe. The institutions had learned to function without reliable American leadership.

When the second Trump term began in January 2025, it inherited an order that had been significantly weakened by the first — and proceeded to complete the demolition that the first had begun.
