Some time after one o’clock on the afternoon of September 24, 2026, with the most consequential bilateral meeting of the year concluded behind closed doors, the President of the United States took the General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party to see the building site.

There was the new helipad, officially opened that week. There was the hole where the East Wing used to be, soon to hold the ballroom. Reporters, readmitted to the grounds after a morning spent outside the gate, shouted questions about Iran, about Taiwan, about Jimmy Lai. The president answered none of them. He offered instead a piece of information nobody had asked for.

“He is an expert on stone, among many other things, and loves good granite.”

According to the pool, Xi Jinping stood beside him and said nothing. His expression did not change.

I cannot recall a more complete description of a relationship than that sentence and the silence that followed it. One man was showing the house. The other was deciding whether he would ever need to buy it.

The Hall He Means to Top

The granite was not idle flattery. As Chris Buckley reported for The New York Times on September 24, Trump has cited the Great Hall of the People in Beijing as both inspiration and competition for his ballroom on at least six occasions in recent months. At the Republican midterm convention in Dallas this month, he promised, of the Chinese building, that “we’re going to top it, you know?”

The arithmetic is unkind. The Great Hall covers more than 1.8 million square feet; its auditorium seats about 10,000. The ballroom will cover 90,000. Buckley supplies the history the president seems not to have been told. Mao demanded a hall for 10,000 people in 1945, while he was still commanding from caves. It went up in under a year beginning in late 1958, during the Great Leap Forward, the industrial fever dream whose famine killed, by many scholars’ estimates, 20 million people or more.

So the American president has chosen as his architectural rival a monument to one autocrat’s vanity, raised at the height of that autocrat’s deadliest error. On the day it mattered, he walked the heir to that party around his own construction site asking, in effect, whether the stonework passed muster. Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions. Here the rider had dismounted, and was asking the other horseman to admire the saddle.

Two Speeches, Two Subjects

The morning had set the terms. In the Rose Garden, Trump spoke of “a truly great friendship” forged since his first election. He listed the topics as security, technology and “superintelligence,” his new word for artificial intelligence, which David E. Sanger of The New York Times noted almost nobody else has agreed to adopt.

Xi’s remarks, in Sanger’s description, were flatter and all business. He thanked his hosts on behalf of “1.4 billion people,” which is one way of reminding a room who governs the larger country. He said the two nations’ interests were “deeply intertwined.” He revived “strategic stability,” a phrase borrowed from nuclear doctrine and first floated at Busan in October 2025, meaning, roughly: here is where you may challenge us, and here is where you may not. He said artificial intelligence must be “always under human control,” a firmer statement than anything Trump has offered on a subject he has called a “hoax.” And he said the two countries must avoid the Thucydides Trap.

That last phrase deserves a paragraph of its own, because this was its second outing. The Thucydides Trap is the theory that a rising power and an established one tend to end up at war; in the analogy, China is Athens and America is Sparta. Xi posed the same question at the Beijing summit in May. The next day, as Michael Schuman recounted in The Atlantic on September 23, Trump explained on Truth Social that when Xi had “very elegantly referred to the United States as perhaps being a declining nation,” he meant the damage done under “Sleepy Joe Biden,” and “on that score, he was 100% correct.”

Xi said it again on the White House lawn. Nobody corrected him this time either.

Kurt Campbell, the former deputy secretary of state, told Schuman what Xi is doing with all this personal diplomacy: avoiding a great-power war while easing the United States into a more orderly decline. From Beijing, Wang Huiyao of the Center for China and Globalization put it more cheerfully. The two sides must coexist, compromise, accommodate each other. “That’s what Trump is doing.”

Readers of this series will recognise the third verb. It is the A in TACO.

Xi, for his part, knows how to flatter a brand. “Achieving the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation and making America great again can go hand in hand,” he said, borrowing the slogan whole. Trump had offered him friendship. Xi returned the favour with a marketing tie-in.

Two Readouts

The Oval Office meeting itself was closed to reporters. Its length depends on whom you ask: The Washington Times put the bilateral at a little over an hour, within a visit of roughly three. What was said inside reached the American public mainly from Beijing.

According to Xinhua’s official summary, as relayed in CNN’s coverage and parsed from Taipei by Chris Buckley of The New York Times, Xi urged Trump to “handle the Taiwan issue prudently” and to “adhere to the correct stance of opposing ‘Taiwan independence.’” On Iran, Xi said China would support a return by Washington and Tehran to the fourteen-point memorandum of understanding reached in June. On rare earths, the summary said nothing, though Xi did remind his host that “cooperation is a two-way street.”

The American account, in full: “We had a great meeting.” Trump declined, as CBS News reported, to say whether Taiwan, artificial intelligence or Jimmy Lai had come up.

A single verb carries a great deal of weight here. Washington’s long-standing formula is that it does not support Taiwan independence. Under the one China policy, it acknowledges Beijing’s position that Taiwan is part of China without ever accepting the Communist Party’s claim of sovereignty. Beijing has spent years trying to move the American verb from “not support” to “oppose.” Whether anything moved in the Oval Office on September 24, Americans have only Xinhua’s word to go on, set against silence from their own government.

This is not new. After the Beijing summit in May, as NBC News noted at the time, the White House readout made no mention of Taiwan at all, despite its prominence in the Chinese account. Two summits, one pattern: Beijing leads with Taiwan, and Washington writes as though the subject never arose. Meanwhile, Secretary of State Marco Rubio says the administration is still reviewing potential arms sales to the island. The $14 billion package described in Part IV remains frozen. The Chinese summary did not mention arms sales at all. With the package already on ice, it did not need to.

The Friendliest Press Corps

For the start of the Oval Office meeting, the White House admitted a limited number of photographers and no print reporters. It also let in Chinese photographers, and as it did so, according to The Washington Times, the president joked that China has the friendliest press corps.

The joke landed on a particular day. Around 1 a.m. Eastern, Judge Timothy J. Kelly of the U.S. District Court in Washington had ordered the White House to “immediately return, reinstate, and restore” the credentials of journalists from CNN, MS NOW and Politico, barred since September 18 over coverage the president called “fake.” Hours later, as Michael M. Grynbaum reported for The New York Times, CNN’s Betsy Klein was turned away at the gate three times. The administration’s own court filing said passes were ready for collection at 9:55 a.m. Nobody has explained why reporters could not get through until around noon.

The networks, which had suspended the television pool in protest, did not return. No American network aired live footage of the summit. The only video of the Rose Garden review came from the Associated Press, which sits outside the pool, and the pool did not plan to cover the state dinner. “Normally we’d be watching an elegant affair on the South Lawn,” Bill Hemmer told Fox News viewers, “and we don’t get access to that now.” Chinese state television, meanwhile, was on hand in Washington.

So the friendliest press corps was the one that answers to its government, standing in the Oval Office while American reporters stood on the pavement. That is not a metaphor I invented. It is the seating plan.

Vacuums fill. By late afternoon, an account styled after the president’s son was circulating a “BREAKING” clip of Xi stunning the room by declaring the two countries “partners, not rivals.” The footage carried a Beijing timestamp from May. The words were real but stale, lifted from a written arrival statement carried by Xinhua, as Reuters reported. Send the cameras home and this is what fills the screen.

What Stayed Off the Table

Some things did not need to be raised to be settled.

On Iran, the war this administration launched early in 2026 and has not managed to end, Trump was asked before the summit about a Wall Street Journal report that Chinese entities supplied Tehran with satellite imagery used in deadly July attacks on American troops in Jordan. As CNBC reported, his answer was that “they basically do what we do.” ABC News notes that the Treasury’s campaign against every country with financial ties to Iran, grandly titled Operation Economic Outcast, has somehow not reached Tehran’s largest oil customer. Xi, Trump said, has “behaved reasonably well.”

On detained Americans, the White House had assured Yufang Rong that her husband, the seismologist Youlin Chen, held since November 2024, would be raised. As Reuters reported, Trump raised Chen’s case in Beijing in May as well, with no result, and Beijing’s answer was already on record: China’s foreign ministry spokesman said “there is no such thing as wrongful detention.” Chen is not alone. The political scientist U Min Zin has been held since June, and NBC News lists Dawn Michelle Hunt and Nelson Wells Jr., both imprisoned since 2014. Whether any of their names were spoken in the Oval Office, neither government has said.

On trade, Alan Rappeport and Ana Swanson reported for The New York Times on September 24 that China is on pace to beat last year’s record $1.19 trillion trade surplus. American tariffs have diverted Chinese exports, not diminished them. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned Americans against being seduced by “cheap baubles,” yet the 12.5 per cent tariff announced on Chinese exports in August may be set high and then partly suspended to honour the truce. Meanwhile, threatened “excess capacity” tariffs would fall on some forty countries. Norway has had to file a submission explaining that its seafood surplus owes more to a long coastline than to state subsidy. Allies get the tariffs. The rival gets the truce.

On artificial intelligence, the one concrete proposal was a modest one. As NBC News reported, Bessent put forward a mechanism for the two governments to alert each other to AI incidents serious enough to threaten national security, a hotline in all but name. Xinhua’s summary says only that the two sides may “continue to engage in dialogue on A.I.” and “jointly prevent the misuse” of it. The hotline itself went unmentioned. Even if it comes, it is a modest step on from Lima in November 2024, where Joe Biden and Xi agreed that decisions on nuclear weapons should remain under human control. Xi’s phrase on the South Lawn echoed that Biden-era pledge.

What America Got

The pandas are coming.

Two of them, Ping Ping and Fu Shuang, are bound for Zoo Atlanta. Xi called them envoys of friendship, and Edward Wong of The New York Times observed, with admirable restraint, that they may be remembered as the most positive diplomatic outcome of the visit. The tradition dates to 1972, when Pat Nixon told Zhou Enlai she loved pandas and Ling-Ling and Hsing-Hsing arrived at the National Zoo within two months. The state dinner menu reached back to the same year, opening with a toast of Schramsberg, the California sparkling wine Nixon drank in Beijing.

The echo is telling, though not as intended. Nixon went to China to get something: a strategic realignment that pulled Beijing away from Moscow and reshaped the Cold War. The pandas were the souvenir. In Washington in September 2026, the souvenirs are what came home. America also received an invitation for 100,000 of its students to visit China over five years, and a two-month extension of the trade truce, to January 10, 2027, negotiated before the leaders sat down. The Chinese summary adds that each leader pledged to help the other host his next big meeting: APEC in Shenzhen in November, the G20 in Miami in December. The principal deliverable of the summit, in other words, was more summits.

In return, Xi received a tarmac greeting at Joint Base Andrews of a kind that, as Jonathan Lemire noted in The Atlantic on September 24, presidents have extended to a pair of popes and Nikita Khrushchev among very few others. He received a B-2 flyover, 479 service members in ceremonial dress, and a Rose Garden in which to lecture on Athens and Sparta. He received a closed room in which to say “oppose,” an American readout that would not repeat it, and a tour of the ballroom being built to outdo his party’s great hall.

The Scorecard

The TACO framework has four letters, and September 24 was not a day for the first of them. There were no threats. There was Accommodation at Andrews and in the Rose Garden, on Iran and on Taiwan arms. There was Obfuscation in a three-word readout and in questions waved away on the helipad. Capitulation requires a demand on the table to give way on, and so few American demands reached this table that it is hard to score at all.

Somewhere in Beijing stands a building of 1.8 million square feet that went up in a year, while the famine its builders chose not to see claimed some 20 million lives. It is a monument to a government that builds impressively, answers to no one and admits the photographers it likes. On September 24, the President of the United States showed its current custodian the foundations of a smaller copy and asked him to admire the stone.

Xi did not change his expression. He did not need to.
