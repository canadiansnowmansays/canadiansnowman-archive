The Returns

The polls closed at nine o’clock on a rainy Tuesday in Manhattan, and within the hour the longest-running open-seat soap opera in the country finally got its last act. Micah Lasher will be the Democratic nominee for New York’s 12th Congressional District, and — this being a district that has not sent a Republican to Washington in living memory — its next member of Congress.

The self-described nerd won the way Jerry Nadler always won: not with a viral moment or a celebrity turn, but with a résumé and a base. The Associated Press called it for Lasher, the Upper West Side assemblyman and former aide to half the officials in Albany, over Alex Bores, the East Side assemblyman whom the artificial-intelligence industry had spent some $27 million trying to elevate and then to bury.

The geography told the story the polls had promised. Lasher overperformed in the older precincts and ran up his margin on the Upper West Side, even making inroads into the East Side neighborhoods Bores represents; Bores did better in the younger districts south of Central Park — Chelsea, Kips Bay. In a district whose early-voting electorate had aged a full decade since last year, the candidate of experience beat the candidate of disruption by running where the votes actually were.

What the Money Bought

Whatever the final margin, the number that will outlive this race is the one with the dollar sign in front of it. By the tally of the ad-tracking firm AdImpact, NY-12 became the second most expensive House primary on record — a distinction earned almost entirely by the war over a single candidate. AIPAC, the bogeyman of nearly every other New York race this cycle, had almost nothing to do with it.

The money came instead from two sources that tell you everything about what this district has become. Groups linked to the Silicon Valley rivals OpenAI and Anthropic spent more than $12 million stuffing mailboxes and blanketing TV screens — including during the Knicks NBA Finals games — with other industry-tied groups chipping in roughly $4 million more. The target of all of it was Alex Bores, the East Side assemblyman who helped write a New York law to regulate advanced AI models and thereby made himself, in his own debate-stage phrasing, the industry’s “No. 1 enemy.” And former mayor Michael Bloomberg, never one to let an election pass without a contribution, put $10 million of his own behind Micah Lasher.

Here is the irony that gives the whole episode its shape. The proxy fight began when a super PAC tied to OpenAI and other deregulation-minded players singled Bores out for destruction. Rather than sink him, the attacks inadvertently elevated the little-known lawmaker into a front-runner — at which point entities aligned with Anthropic and other pro-regulation donors rushed in with millions of their own to defend him. The industry tried to make an example of Bores and instead made him a contender. The machine, aiming to crush the man, built him a pedestal.

The AI war ends with a verdict more ambiguous than either side wanted. By the final accounting it became a $27 million proxy fight between competing factions of the industry — and all of it, for and against, failed to put Bores over the top. The industry’s millions did not elect him; the counter-millions did not save him. In the end it was Bloomberg’s ten million and the endorsement infrastructure of the retiring incumbent that carried the institutionalist across the line. The technocrat who picked a fight with the most heavily capitalized industry in human history discovered that being the future is not the same as winning the West Side.

Bores, to his credit, named the thing plainly on his way out. The groups aligned with OpenAI and the other tech giants, he told his supporters, had set out “to make an example” of him — and they had, just not the example they wanted. “While we came up short tonight,” he said, “the example set here was very much not the one they intended.” He is right that it will be studied. A district can absorb $27 million in AI money and still elect the man who never had a viral moment. Whether that is a story about the limits of money or merely about the peculiarities of NY-12 is a question Palo Alto will be chewing on for a while.

The Dynasty and the Discipline

Jack Schlossberg, with his father, Edwin Schlossberg, right, saw his early polling lead evaporate.Credit...Dave Sanders for The New York Times

This series began with a question disguised as a name. The New Camelot — could the grandson of John F. Kennedy revive the family’s congressional tradition in the city where it was made? Jack Schlossberg entered with the three things the others would have killed for: a famous name, a famous face, and two million followers who would watch him do almost anything.

He worked hard to make the campaign about something bigger than himself — the family legacy, the prolific posting, the promise of activating a broad progressive base. But all of it, the New York Times suggested, seemed designed to shift attention away from a career path that does not tell much of a clear story about the candidate himself: a short stint as a political correspondent for Vogue, four months as a State Department assistant under John Kerry, and then a congressional bid. His culminating rally, at a Terminal 5 well past its own heyday, was sparsely attended — references to his grandfather threaded between dabs and Kesha songs for the younger set. The supporters who stopped to photograph him on the Upper East Side were, tellingly, either high school students or retirees: too young to vote, or old enough to remember the originals.

The single image that captures it comes from the Times: in one light, a campaign doused in 1960s sepia; look again, and it is bathed in the uncanny glow of a heavy Instagram filter. Camelot, filtered.

And when the polls turned against him, the heir simply refused to read them. “I think the hate we’ve been getting is evidence of how much support we’ve had,” he said in the closing days. “And I don’t believe any of the polls.” It is the most Kennedy thing he did all race — and the least. The dynasty was built on reading the room. The grandson, at the end, was reduced to denying the returns before they came in. He did not, when the night ended, formally concede; he told the room it had “been the honor of my life,” invoked his grandfather’s most famous line — ask what we can do to help our city — pumped his fist, and left the stage.

That is the whole arc in a paragraph. The name carried him in. Nothing carried him over. Schlossberg finished a distant third, with 11 percent — ahead, at least, of the vaccine expert and the never-Trumper, but never within reach of the two assemblymen who had done the unglamorous work of actually building a coalition. The Kennedy who was supposed to inherit Camelot was outpolled nearly four-to-one by a man whose chief enemies were OpenAI and a former mayor’s checkbook. The consultants, in the morning-after autopsy, were unsparing in the gentle way of people who do this for a living: entertaining but not serious, said one; Jack didn’t have a base, said another. State Senator Liz Krueger, who has represented the district in Albany for decades and fields constant questions about who’s worth a vote, offered the quietest epitaph of all — no one, she said, ever even started a conversation with her about Jack. He did not break through.

It would be easy, and wrong, to make sport of all this without acknowledging what the year actually cost him. Schlossberg launched this campaign in the shadow of genuine grief: his sister Tatiana, who wrote an unbearable New Yorker essay about her own terminal cancer, died in December at thirty-five, only weeks after he announced. He ran, his campaign manager said, amid a deeply personal tragedy. And he ran while the most famous current bearer of the family name, his uncle Robert F. Kennedy Jr., sat in the cabinet of the very president every other candidate in this race had pledged to impeach — the dynasty split clean down the middle, one nephew chasing a Democratic seat in Manhattan while the uncle appeared beside Mr. Trump at a White House cage match. The Kennedy brand Schlossberg tried to invoke was, by 2026, no longer a single inheritance. It was a contested estate.

There is one last grace note, and the gods of irony delivered it personally. For weeks the press had needled Schlossberg as the candidate who sporadically napped on the trail. On election night, it was Bores’s mother — wearing a “Mom” sash to her son’s watch party — who told a reporter she had spent the day campaigning, done a yoga class, and taken a two-hour nap. The nap, like the dynasty, turned out to belong to someone else’s family.

Sixty years after the original, Camelot turned out to be a place you can visit but not inherit.

The Recessional: A Man and His Corgi

And then there is George Conway.

He came to this race the way a man comes to a duel he is sure he will lose but is determined to fight anyway — the apostate Republican, the Lincoln Project co-founder, the cable-news fixture who switched parties for the privilege of running in a Democratic primary on a platform that began and ended with stopping Donald Trump. The animus that built against the president, his candidacy proposed to test, was as potent more than a year into the second term as it had been in the first.

Conway finished last — behind even Nina Schwalbe, the global vaccine expert who had struggled all race to get anyone to look at her. The animus against the President, which his entire candidacy was built to harness, turned out to be real but not transferable: the voters of NY-12 share his contempt for Trump almost unanimously — every candidate in the field backed impeachment and abolishing ICE — and precisely because that feeling was universal, it could not be Conway’s alone to run on. You cannot win a one-issue campaign when everyone agrees with you on the issue.

The image the New York Times left us with — and the one this series has been holding since the spring — is Conway walking his corgi, Clyde, through Central Park on a bright morning, the happy-hour crowd on the Upper West Side, the retired transit engineer gently pushing back on the impeachment talk, the half-marathon quip. It is the most photogenic campaign in the field and, very probably, the losing one.

Not everyone recognizes George Conway as he walks the streets of Manhattan, but some stop him anyway to admire his corgi, Clyde.Credit...Ryan Murphy for The New York Times

He came to put his money where his mouth was — he loaned his own campaign $2 million — and walked his dog through a city that mostly did not vote for him. That is not a tragedy. In a district this educated, this wealthy, this certain of itself, it may even be a kind of honour: the man who ran on principle alone, and finished last, in a race awash in tens of millions of everyone else’s money.

From North of the Border

From a considerable distance north of the 49th parallel, NY-12 always looked like the most American race on the board — too rich, too loud, too crowded with celebrity and consequence, the one place where the party’s argument with itself gets conducted in full view and at full volume. Manhattan spent tens of millions of dollars and the better part of a year to replace a man who held the seat for three decades by simply showing up, year after year, and being good at the work.

In the end it chose another man who is good at the work. Micah Lasher — the self-described nerd, the policy wonk in Nadler’s own mold, the one candidate who sighed at the thought of another direct-to-camera video — won the way Nadler always won: by being the most prepared person in the room and trusting that this district, of all districts, would still reward it. There is a detail the series has been saving since Part II, and it lands now with no embellishment required: before he was a governor’s policy director or a campaign strategist, Lasher was an accomplished childhood magician. The magician’s gambit, it turns out, was never the Kennedy glamour or the AI money or the cable-news fame. It was the boring trick of knowing the material cold. In a season when American politics rewarded the viral, the famous, and the heavily funded almost everywhere else, the nerd inherited the seat. Make of that what you will; I find it almost reassuring.

And up here, where we watch all of this with the fascinated horror of people who live next door to a very large and very complicated neighbour, we will do what we always do: turn off the returns around midnight, let the dog out one last time, and marvel that a country can spend so much, argue so loudly, and still, somehow, pick someone to go tell Washington what Manhattan thinks.

Somewhere on the Upper West Side, a corgi named Clyde has no idea any of this happened, and is the happier for it.

  • Alex Bores — 35,822 (35%)

  • Jack Schlossberg — 11,036 (11%)

  • Nina Schwalbe — fourth

  • George T. Conway III — fifth (last)

Final pre-primary polling (for reference)

  • Emerson College / PIX11 (May 16–17, 425 likely voters, ±4.8): Lasher 22%, Bores 20%, Schlossberg 11%

  • Of 10 public polls, only the Emerson survey was fielded independently; the four most recent all showed a Lasher–Bores race inside 5 points with 25%+ undecided

  • Schlossberg led four of six polls from January–April, almost entirely on name recognition, then faded to third as opponents ramped up

Outside spending

  • AI-industry proxy fight over Bores (OpenAI- and Anthropic-linked groups, for and against): ~$27 million all in

  • Bloomberg super PAC (for Lasher): $10 million

  • Self-funding: Conway loaned his campaign $2M; Schlossberg $1M

  • Among the most expensive House primaries in history

Turnout / demographics: Citywide early-vote median age rose a full decade to 57; NY-12 had the highest turnout and oldest median voter of any city district. Precinct results showed Lasher overperforming in older areas and the Upper West Side, Bores stronger in younger Midtown (Chelsea, Kips Bay).

Part VII concludes The New Camelot. The general election in November is, in NY-12, a formality: Micah Lasher is the overwhelming favorite to succeed Jerry Nadler.
