For months, the complaints from inside Delaney Hall — the ICE detention facility in Newark run by the private contractor GEO Group — were just that: complaints. Detainees said the food was moldy or unidentifiable. Families said the rooms were freezing or sweltering. A hunger strike started. Protests gathered outside and stayed for weeks. DHS called the allegations frivolous, said its state health inspection had found conditions overall satisfactory, and pointed to a statement promising three meals a day, clean water, and certified dietitians reviewing every menu.

Because federal officials had restricted access to the facility, none of it could be verified. That changed this week. Health inspection reports, court filings, and public records reviewed by the New York Times now provide the first documented evidence of what has actually been happening inside Delaney Hall, and the documents corroborate the detainees, not the department.

What the Inspectors Actually Found

A federal inspection conducted only three months after Delaney Hall began holding immigrants identified deficiencies in food refrigeration and found that food was not protected from overhead leakage, with condensation dripping directly into the freezers. That report came from ICE’s own Office of Professional Responsibility — this is not an outside advocacy group’s finding. It is the agency’s internal watchdog documenting the problem itself, months before the public controversy began.

New Jersey state health inspectors, granted limited access to the facility’s kitchen in late May, found potato salad sitting at 81 degrees — twice the temperature considered safe — alongside unsanitized food-prep surfaces and staff handling open garbage containers while preparing meals. Two covered trays of chicken stew were improperly cooled. Cooked meatballs in a hot-holding area registered nearly 50 degrees below the temperature needed to kill harmful bacteria. Staff were not washing their hands in a timely manner, including before starting work and after using the restroom. Cases of soda were stored in the same closet as chemicals. The dishwasher’s sanitizer concentration was below required levels.

Every one of those findings came from inspectors who were only permitted into the kitchen. They were denied entry to the housing and medical units entirely — the parts of the facility where the more serious allegations, about medical neglect and unsafe living conditions, actually concentrate. New Jersey’s deputy health commissioner, Dr. Novneet Sahu, has said the state cannot confirm or rule out whether Delaney Hall’s infection-control practices pose a serious risk to detainees or to the public outside the facility, precisely because the state has been repeatedly refused the access it would need to find out. The state attorney general is now suing to force that access. The suit is pending.

The Tuberculosis Case

The single most serious allegation in the documents concerns a detainee with an active case of tuberculosis. According to an affidavit filed as part of the state’s lawsuit, Dr. Sahu received a complaint from a physician at University Hospital in Newark reporting that a detainee had been brought in, treated, and returned to Delaney Hall with no follow-up plan to contain the infection. Tuberculosis is airborne and contagious; the entire premise of infection control for a case like this is exactly the follow-up plan the physician says never existed. DHS has denied that any detainee was diagnosed with tuberculosis at all. That is a direct factual dispute, made under oath in one direction and by press statement in the other, and it is precisely the kind of dispute that could be resolved by state inspectors being allowed into the medical unit — the access DHS has been fighting in court to deny.

I want to be careful about what this does and doesn’t establish. One physician’s account, relayed secondhand and now captured in an affidavit, is not itself a proven fact. But it is sworn testimony, offered in litigation where lying carries legal consequences, describing exactly the kind of infection-control failure that a facility holding a thousand people in close quarters cannot afford to have and that DHS has both denied outright and refused to let independent investigators verify. Those two responses — deny, and also block the people who could confirm or refute the denial — are not compatible with genuine confidence in one’s own account. They are compatible with an institution that does not want the account tested.

The Same Machinery, a Different Building

Readers of this series will recognize the shape of this immediately, because it is the same shape as Good’s car sitting in an FBI facility, the same shape as Pretti’s shooters going unnamed for six months, the same shape as DHS’s “standard investigation” framing of the Blanche-era Justice Department. An institution makes a claim. An institution then blocks the specific access that would let anyone outside the institution test the claim. When outside evidence eventually gets in anyway — bystander video in Good’s case, a medical examiner’s ruling in Araujo’s, a kitchen inspection in Delaney Hall’s — it does not vindicate the institution’s account. It contradicts it.

This is worth stating plainly because it is easy to read the shooting cases and the detention-conditions cases as two separate scandals, one about lethal force and one about facility management. They are not separate. They are the same institutional posture, applied to two different kinds of harm. Whether the question is “did an agent have a reasonable basis to fire a weapon” or “was a tuberculosis case properly contained,” the department’s answer has been to assert an outcome favorable to itself and then resist precisely the kind of independent verification that would let the public know whether the assertion is true. A government confident in its own conduct does not need to block state health inspectors from a kitchen, any more than it needs to keep a dead woman’s car in an evidence locker for six months. Confidence invites scrutiny. What we are watching, across both buildings and both kinds of harm, is something else.

What Fairness Requires Here, Too

In the interest of the same fairness this series tries to apply throughout: GEO Group disputes the substandard-conditions findings and says deficiencies are addressed through ICE’s own required corrective-action process when identified, and says the facility provides round-the-clock medical access and meals accommodating religious and dietary needs. DHS says the May kitchen inspection found conditions overall satisfactory and calls New Jersey’s lawsuit frivolous. Those are the positions on the record, and readers should weigh them against the specific findings above — the 81-degree potato salad, the unwashed hands, the sanitizer below required concentration, the denied access to housing and medical units — and decide for themselves how much weight “overall satisfactory” can bear against a documented record like this one.

It is also fair to note that detention facilities nationwide, under any administration, generate complaints, and that not every complaint reflects genuine crisis. What distinguishes Delaney Hall is not that complaints exist. It is that, for the first time, independent documentation — an internal ICE report, a state health inspection, a sworn medical affidavit — has actually corroborated them, in a period measured in months rather than the years it usually takes for this kind of institutional pattern to surface.

Where This Fits

This series has spent its first nine parts on what happens after an ICE or CBP agent kills someone: the investigations that don’t happen, the evidence that doesn’t move, the doctrine that might or might not let a state hold anyone accountable. Delaney Hall is the same administration’s machinery operating on people who are still alive — for now — inside facilities the public was not supposed to be able to see into. Cardinal Joseph Tobin, the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Newark, put it simply after leading a prayer outside the facility in June: people are cutting through the attempts to dehumanize, and seeing suffering human beings. The inspectors’ documents are the first evidence that suggests he is describing something real, not just a perception.

This series covered Blanche's confirmation hearing itself in Part III, and the resolution of that fight — the Senate's vote to confirm him as Attorney General — in Part VIII. What remains open is not his title, which is now settled. It is whether anything described across these ten parts changes as a result, or simply continues, unexamined, under an Attorney General instead of an Acting one.

What This Series Set Out to Do, and Where It Leaves Off

Part I opened with a file that was not closed — Renee Good’s shooting, and the investigation that never quite arrived at an answer anyone could verify. Ten parts later, the file is still not closed, and neither are several others: Good’s car has not moved from the FBI garage in any way the public can confirm. The agents who fired ten times on Alex Pretti in five seconds remain unnamed six months on. Julio Sosa-Celis’s case collapsed under its own agents’ sworn statements, and no one has yet said what happens to the officers who made them. Martinez’s death sat undisclosed for eleven months, and nothing in the record explains why it surfaced when it did rather than earlier. Delaney Hall’s medical unit remains, as of this writing, a place state inspectors are suing for the right to enter.

Between Part I and Part X, one thing did resolve: Todd Blanche went from acting attorney general to confirmed attorney general, a fight this series tracked in real time because it revealed, more clearly than any single shooting could, what the office had been built to protect and at what price a Senate majority was willing to let it be protected. That the confirmation succeeded while every open question about Good, Pretti, Araujo, Sosa-Celis, and Delaney Hall remains exactly as open as it was in Part I is not an irony this series imposed from outside. It is the finding. An institution can resolve the question of who runs it while leaving every question about what it actually did unresolved indefinitely — and calling that outcome closure requires nobody to have been paying attention to which questions actually got answered.

This series does not end because those questions are settled. It ends because a ten-part accounting of an administration’s first eighteen months at this scale is already an unusual thing to ask a reader to sit through, and because the honest thing to say, after ten parts, is not “here is how it turned out” but “here is what is now on the record, whether or not anyone follows up.” If Cornyn’s and Tillis’s written assurances hold, if New Jersey’s lawsuit gets the access it is asking for, if a single name on the ledger in Part IX gets an answer more solid than the template it arrived with — those will be stories this newsletter tells separately, as they happen, rather than promises this series can make on their behalf now.

A Note From the Canadian Side of the Border

We do not run detention facilities on this scale, and we do not have a private contractor holding a thousand people in a building that state health inspectors have to sue their own federal government to enter. What we have, watching from here, is the plain observation that a government’s confidence in its own institutions is supposed to be demonstrated by what it allows people to see, not by what it insists people believe without being shown.
