Seven days ago, in Part XXIX of this series, I imagined what it would take to get the current occupants of the White House to sit through two hours and forty minutes of a musical about a town that asked nothing of stranded Americans except the chance to help. I said I didn’t have much hope of it happening. I did not expect, a week later, to be writing about someone who did something close to the reverse — a Canadian standing in Gander itself, on the actual anniversary, saying aloud what I’d only been able to argue from a theatre seat in Toronto.

On September 11, 2026, in the arena at the centre of the town that took in nearly 7,000 stranded passengers twenty-five years ago, former Prime Minister Jean Chrétien — 92 years old, the man who held the office when those planes landed — stood up and told his “American friends” something that had been implicit in every ledger entry I catalogued last week and made it explicit. “Never mistake our generosity for weakness,” he said, according to the wire coverage that ran across CTV, CP24 and the Associated Press that afternoon. “We embrace our friends, and we help them in their moment of need. But we bow to no one.”

He was careful about what he was doing. He told the room he wasn’t there as a government representative — “just a former prime minister, 92 years old, and a very proud Canadian,” a line that drew applause on its own before he’d made his point. That distinction mattered, because what followed was not a diplomatic communiqué. It was closer to a warning delivered with the courtesy of a dinner guest who still expects to be invited back. “I say that not in anger, but as one friend to another,” he said. “Friends need to be honest with each other.”

The setting did the argument’s work before Chrétien opened his mouth. This was a solemn ceremony, built around the nearly 3,000 people killed in New York, at the Pentagon, and aboard Flight 93 — not a rally, not a press conference, not a venue built for a message about tariffs. Governor General Louise Arbour spoke. So did U.S. Ambassador Pete Hoekstra, whose invitation had briefly been contested by some residents given “everything the Trump administration has been saying and doing,” as Gander’s town council put it in a statement earlier that week, before the council itself defended asking him anyway: September 11, they said, is “bigger than politics, personalities or titles.” That is not a small thing for a town to insist on, twenty-five years into a friendship currently being tested by an American president who has floated making the country a “51st state” and renamed a Great Lake out of spite. Gander held the line on decency even while making room for the argument that decency has limits.

And then Hoekstra, by every account of the room, rose and hugged Chrétien after he sat down.

I want to sit with that image for a moment, because it is the whole thesis of this series compressed into a single gesture. An American ambassador, representing an administration that has spent 2026 calling Canadian negotiators “clowns,” threatening to embargo Canadian alcohol and dairy, and telling Doug Ford’s province it will be “treated like a State no longer,” stood up in a Newfoundland hockey arena and embraced the man who had just told him, in public, that his country’s generosity has a floor. That is not capitulation on either side. It is what it looks like when two people who actually remember what the relationship used to be — who were both alive and paying attention on September 11, 2001 — decline to let the current moment overwrite twenty-five years of memory in front of them.

Chrétien’s own memory of that day, which he spent several minutes recounting before he turned to the present, is worth restating plainly rather than summarising. Within days of the attacks, more than 100,000 Canadians gathered on Parliament Hill for a spontaneous vigil — a crowd size no other country produced. It was his government that moved the motion invoking Article 5 of the NATO charter, the only time in the alliance’s history it has ever been used, on the premise that an attack on the United States was an attack on all of NATO. Canadian troops went into Afghanistan within weeks. A hundred and fifty-eight of them died there. None of that is new information to readers of this series — I catalogued most of it in Part XXIX’s ledger — but there is a difference between a writer assembling a historical record and the actual prime minister of the day standing in the actual town, in front of the actual Americans who were there, telling that record back to them from memory. The ledger I built last week was argument. What happened in Gander was testimony.

It would be a mistake to read Chrétien’s “we bow to no one” as a rupture with the spirit of Part XXIX, or with Gander’s own instinct for hospitality over grievance. He did not go to Newfoundland to relitigate the tariff schedule. He went to mark a day when, in his words, “we saw the worst of humanity and the very best,” and he let the town’s own history make the political point so he barely had to state it directly. The bluntness came almost as an afterthought — “here is to truth, to respect, to friendship, and to good neighbours” — offered, notably, in the same breath as “long live the United States.” That pairing is the whole argument. You can tell a friend the truth and still mean the toast.

What strikes me most, rereading the coverage, is how little daylight there turned out to be between what I hoped Washington might someday hear in a theatre seat and what a 92-year-old former prime minister actually said out loud, unprompted, to the people who needed to hear it. I did not expect an answer this fast, or this clean, or delivered with this much grace by a man who has been out of office for over two decades and owed nobody a diplomatic performance. Gander gave the plane people shelter in 2001 without asking what they believed. Twenty-five years later, it gave an ambassador a hug and a former prime minister a stage to tell the truth on. Both, in their way, were acts of hospitality. Neither one was weakness.

I'll close, for once, without the usual distance a writer tries to keep from his subject. Mr. Chrétien, on behalf of what I suspect are a great many Canadians who watched that afternoon from home: thank you. The standing ovations you received in that arena were well deserved.
