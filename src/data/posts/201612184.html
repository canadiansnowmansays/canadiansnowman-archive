Postmark: Osaka, Japan. 1970. A world’s fair. An engineer walks through an exhibit of humanoid robots and notices something unexpected: the more human they look, the more disturbing they feel.

Masahiro Mori was a robotics professor at the Tokyo Institute of Technology when he attended Expo ‘70 in Osaka — the same world’s fair that the young Frank Walker would one day visit in Tomorrowland, clutching his homemade jetpack. Mori walked through the exhibits of humanoid robots that the exposition had commissioned, marvelling at the sophistication of the designs. And then he noticed something that troubled him.

The most realistic robots were not the most appealing. They were the most disturbing.

A simple robot — one that looked like a machine, with visible joints and mechanical components — generated comfort and even affection. As the robot became more human-like, that affection increased. But it increased only up to a point. When the resemblance got close to human without quite getting there — when the eyes tracked movement but were slightly too still, when the skin approximated flesh but felt wrong to the touch, when the voice had cadence but not quite timbre — something in the human observer collapsed. Warmth became revulsion. Familiarity became unease.

Mori called this dip bukimi no tani — the uncanny valley. He published a two-page essay in 1970 that has shaped every conversation about humanoid robot design in the fifty-six years since. His conclusion was not that we should never build humanoid robots. It was that robot designers should not aim for perfect human likeness, because perfect likeness requires passing through a valley of revulsion that most designs cannot cross.

He died on January 12, 2025, aged 97. By then, the valley he had identified was being crossed — by backflipping robots, by humanoids assembling cars in Hyundai factories, by walking machines whose gait was so naturalistic that the uncomfortable feeling Mori described had not disappeared but had begun, in some demonstrations, to transmute back into wonder.

The Backflip Years

Boston Dynamics spent a decade teaching Atlas to fall gracefully.

The original Atlas was unveiled in 2013, built under a DARPA contract to advance disaster response robotics in the wake of the Fukushima nuclear accident. It was a hydraulic machine — powerful, expensive, and loud. It could navigate rough terrain, open doors, use tools. In controlled demonstrations it could do these things impressively. In uncontrolled conditions it fell over. This was fine. It fell over in interesting ways, and the engineers learned from every fall.

In November 2017, Atlas performed the first backflip by a humanoid robot. The internet responded as the internet does — with a combination of wonder and dread, admiration for the engineering and unease at what it implied. The unease was Mori’s valley. The backflip was too fluid, too athletic, too much like something a human body would do. It landed in the wrong category of object: not clearly a machine, not clearly a person, something in between that activated an ancient wariness.

Boston Dynamics understood the reaction and largely did not mind it. The backflip was not a commercial pitch. It was a demonstration of what the control systems could do — the same AI-driven whole-body motion planning that would eventually make Atlas useful for industrial applications. Every backflip was a proof of capability that would find its commercial expression years later on a factory floor.

In April 2024, Boston Dynamics retired the hydraulic Atlas and unveiled an all-electric successor. The new Atlas was faster, stronger, and more dexterous than its predecessor. It could also rotate its joints in ways a human body cannot — the “alien” or “superhumanoid” aesthetic that the company deliberately chose. Where competitors like Figure AI and 1X Technologies pursued human-mimicry to ease the psychological experience of working alongside a robot, Boston Dynamics leaned into the machine. The Atlas that began shipping to Hyundai’s Georgia factory in 2026 — priced at approximately $150,000, committed entirely to Hyundai and Google DeepMind in its first production year — does not try to look like you. It tries to do things you cannot.

Whether that choice resolves Mori’s valley or routes around it entirely is one of the most interesting open questions in humanoid robot design.

The China Equation

While Boston Dynamics debated aesthetics, China built volume.

In 2025, the top humanoid robot makers globally by shipment were Unitree and AgiBot — both Chinese, both state-backed, both producing at price points that their American and European competitors could not match. Unitree shipped more than 5,500 humanoid robots in 2025. AgiBot shipped more than 5,000. Combined, those two companies accounted for roughly seventy percent of global humanoid robot installations. Tesla’s Optimus program, by comparison, shipped approximately 150 units in the same period. Figure AI and Agility Robotics shipped similar numbers.

Unitree’s humanoids now sell for less than $5,000 each — a price point that Western competitors cannot approach. The AgiBot A2 costs approximately $35,000. These are not research platforms. They are commercial products, available to buyers without the invitation-only processes or multi-year waiting lists that characterise Western humanoid offerings.

The price compression is not accidental. It follows directly from China’s dominance of the humanoid supply chain — a dominance built, component by component, on the back of the electric vehicle industry. Chinese manufacturers once depended on Japanese and other foreign suppliers for sensors, joints, and actuators. They no longer do. It has become nearly impossible to build a humanoid robot without parts from Chinese companies. The component cost in China has fallen so fast that other countries simply cannot compete on price. More than ninety percent of the components in leading Chinese humanoid robots come from domestic suppliers. The main item still imported is computer chips — and that gap is narrowing too.

The pattern is familiar from another industry. BYD overtook Tesla in global electric vehicle sales through cheaper models, state backing, and relentless production scaling. The same BYD is now funding AgiBot. The factory floors of Chinese EV makers — BYD, Xiaomi — have become among the first places to deploy humanoid robots for simple tasks, creating a feedback loop between vehicle manufacturing and robot deployment that no Western competitor can easily replicate. Chinese firms control approximately ninety percent of global humanoid robot installations. Xi Jinping has personally visited both Fourier Intelligence and AgiBot — a level of head-of-state engagement with a specific industry sector that has no equivalent in Washington or Brussels.

The geopolitical dimension of humanoid robotics is being spoken about in the United States with the same vocabulary that accompanied the rise of Chinese electric vehicles, Chinese solar panels, and Chinese semiconductor manufacturing — which is to say, with alarm that arrived too late to be easily actionable. Members of Congress testified in February 2025 that the United States was “losing the next industrial revolution.” The framing was accurate. The response remains insufficient.

The EV analogy is instructive in its specifics. China did not beat the West in electric vehicles by producing better cars first. It beat the West by producing cheaper cars at greater volume, backed by state subsidies, through a manufacturing ecosystem that could iterate faster than any Western competitor. BYD overtook Tesla in global EV sales not because its technology was superior but because its price was lower and its production more scalable. The same dynamic is now playing out in humanoid robotics, and the same BYD is funding AgiBot.

There is a complicating factor that the solar panel and semiconductor analogies do not fully capture. A humanoid robot that operates in your factory, your warehouse, or your home is a data collection device of extraordinary richness — a machine that maps physical spaces, observes human behaviour, learns domestic and industrial workflows, and transmits everything it learns to whoever controls the software stack. A solar panel does not watch what you do. A humanoid robot, by design, does. The cybersecurity implications of a home full of Chinese-manufactured humanoids are not subtle. In September 2025, researchers documented a wormable Bluetooth exploit that allowed nearby attackers full remote control of Unitree’s G1, H1, B2, and Go2 robots. The vulnerability was patched. The underlying question — who controls what a robot learns, and where does that learning go — was not.

What It Means to Look Like Us

Masahiro Mori’s uncanny valley essay was shaped by his Buddhist philosophy. He believed that the discomfort humans feel in the presence of almost-human robots reflects something important about human self-understanding — our relationship to our own image, our attachment to the category of personhood, our difficulty with the idea that mind and body might be separable from the biological substrate that has always housed them.

The humanoid form is a design choice, not an engineering necessity. A robot does not need legs to navigate a factory floor — wheeled platforms are more efficient on smooth surfaces. A robot does not need hands to sort packages — specialist end-effectors outperform fingers at most industrial gripping tasks. A robot does not need a face to keep a person company — PARO, the Japanese therapeutic seal, has demonstrated that a non-humanoid companion can provide genuine psychological benefit.

We are building humanoid robots because human environments were built for human bodies. The door handle is at hand height. The shelf is at shoulder height. The tools are sized for human fingers. A robot that can inhabit a human environment without that environment being modified for it needs, at minimum, roughly human dimensions and roughly human capabilities. The humanoid form is the engineering solution to the problem of existing in a world that was not designed for robots.

But it is also something more than that. The humanoid form carries meaning that no other form does. A robot shaped like a human activates the part of the brain that processes other people. It triggers social cognition, attribution of intention, inference of inner states. We look at a humanoid robot and we cannot help but ask: what is it thinking? What does it want? Is it aware?

Nowhere is this tension more visible than in the emerging field of choreobotics — the study of robots and dance. The Unitree Spring Festival robots that performed martial arts routines for hundreds of millions of Chinese television viewers in 2025 were technically extraordinary: backflipping, high-kicking, wielding swords with a fluidity that looked less like the archetypical robot dance and more like ballet. They drew the largest crowds at the Humanoids Summit in Tokyo this year. Two Japanese firms used Unitree robots to demonstrate their own software. The machines had crossed something — some threshold of movement quality that made them feel less mechanical and more alive.

And yet researchers who study this intersection urge caution about what that crossing means. A single pre-programmed performance that works well is a different thing from having robots that can dance. Human dancing is full of nuances that resist translation into atomised steps — bodies playing against music, choreographers communicating in improvised language, dancers infusing movement with the full weight of embodied experience. When a teacher tells a dancer to move an arm with “yumminess,” the dancer can respond because she has eaten food, felt texture, lived in a body that has accumulated sensation across decades. A robot executing the same arm position through a trained neural network has no such reservoir to draw from. The movement can be faithful. The meaning cannot be the same.

This distinction matters beyond dance. It is the humanoid problem in miniature: the machine can produce the behaviour. Whether it produces the behaviour for the same reasons, with the same inner states, drawing on anything remotely analogous to experience — that remains entirely open.

These are not engineering questions. They are philosophical ones, and the engineering is advancing faster than the philosophy has. We are building entities that will trigger human social cognition at scale — millions of humanoid robots operating in human environments, interpreted by human nervous systems that evolved to treat anything that moves like a person as something to be understood in social terms. The implications of that at scale are not yet worked through.

The Asimov Inheritance

Isaac Asimov understood this problem. His robot stories are populated with humans who project onto robots — who mistake the machine’s behaviour for inner life, who attribute loyalty and love to systems that were programmed to serve. His Three Laws are not sufficient for the robots he writes about, because the humans interacting with those robots cannot perceive the Laws. They perceive the behaviour. And the behaviour is indistinguishable, from the outside, from caring.

The Mirror Problem is the deepest version of the Asimov Gap. The governance questions about factory automation are difficult but tractable. The legal frameworks for autonomous weapons are urgent and politically complex but conceptually clear in their requirements. The ethics of companion robots for dementia patients are genuinely hard but bounded by clinical outcomes data.

The question of what we owe a humanoid robot that has lived in our home for five years, learned our routines, adapted to our preferences, and whose behaviour has become, to our social cognition, indistinguishable from that of a person who cares about us — that question is not yet answerable. It is not yet close to answerable. But the machines are being built now, at $20,000 a unit with subscription options available, and the households that will face the question in its full force are being prepared.

Masahiro Mori’s response to this, informed by his Buddhism, was not anxiety but acceptance — a call to examine our attachment to the human form as the only worthy vessel for mind and experience. He believed that building humanoid robots would ultimately teach us something about ourselves that no other discipline could. He was not sure it would be comfortable. He was sure it would be important. He died at 97, having watched the valley he charted begin to be crossed, by backflipping robots and therapeutic seals and surgical arms and autonomous weapons systems and a disc-shaped vacuum cleaner that has been in fifty million homes and is now in the Smithsonian.

The View from the Mirror

Stand back and look at the full series of postcards — all eight territories, all eight dispatches from the future that is already here.

From the Garage: a fifteen-year-old in Burlington watching a turtle and building BURT. From the Stars: a Canadian robotic arm that became the most durable machine in space and spawned a robot that performs brain surgery. From the Lab: three billion years of biological R&D, finally being read by engineers who studied geckos and octopuses and cats. From the Factory Floor: four million robots on factory floors worldwide, a machine holding a position for forty-five days without casualties, and the strawberry that no robot can yet pick as reliably as a human hand. From the Theatre: seventeen million surgical procedures, a seal robot keeping a dementia patient company in a Tokyo nursing home, exoskeletons teaching stroke survivors to walk. From the Kitchen Table: Roomba’s bankruptcy, Rosey’s absence, and the first humanoid shipping to homes at $20,000 a unit. From the Mirror: the backflipping robot, the Chinese production lines, the Spring Festival performance that looked almost like ballet, and the uncanny valley that Masahiro Mori charted fifty-six years ago at a world’s fair in Osaka.

These are not separate stories. They are one story, told from eight different addresses. The central character in all of them is not the robot. It is the human who watched a turtle, or signed a contract, or put a deposit down, or voted on whether to authorise an autonomous weapons treaty, or stroked a robot seal and felt something that might or might not have been real comfort. It is the engineers at MDA in Brampton who built the machine that never came home. It is the commanders at NC-13 who discovered that robots do not bleed. It is the fifteen-year-old from Burlington who grew up in a country that produces extraordinary robotics with extraordinary quietness, and who is going to STEM, he says, though he has not yet decided exactly where.

We are building machines that learn, that act, that sense, that move, that fight, that heal, that keep us company. We are building them faster than we are building the wisdom to know what we are doing. The Asimov Gap — that space between the pace of technological development and the pace of human wisdom about how to manage it — is not a metaphor. It is the defining challenge of the moment we are living in.

The postcards from Tomorrow are dispatches from that gap. They are not warnings, though some of what they describe warrants serious attention. They are field reports — from a future that did not announce itself, that arrived quietly, through the back door, assembled from off-the-shelf parts and a camping trip epiphany and fifty years of Canadian engineering and the observation of a gecko’s foot and three billion years of evolution that had no idea it was being watched.

The future is here. It is partial, imperfect, sometimes alarming, and more wonderful than the pessimists predicted. More postcards are coming.
