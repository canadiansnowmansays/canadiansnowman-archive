On April 29, 2026, the Supreme Court struck down Louisiana’s congressional map. On May 16, Louisiana held its Senate primary anyway — under conditions the ruling had transformed.

The connection between those two events — and the connections that have multiplied since — is the subject of this instalment.

The Single-Sentence Order

The Supreme Court rejected Virginia’s emergency application on May 15th. The order was one sentence. No vote count. No reasoning. No noted dissents.

Four Democratic-leaning House districts, a $70 million campaign, a constitutional amendment, a statewide referendum, and more than a million early votes — resolved in a single sentence.

Scott Surovell, the Democratic majority leader in the Virginia state senate, said he was not surprised that the Court “chose not to apply the U.S. Constitution or federal law when it does not benefit the interest of President Trump.” Ryan McDougle, the Republican who brought the suit, said the Court had “affirmed what we always knew: you cannot violate the Constitution to change the Constitution.”

Virginia had asked the Court to apply a principle already before it in a Mississippi case — that “Election Day” under federal law means the single day votes are counted, not the beginning of early voting. If the Court had agreed, the General Assembly’s October 31st vote was valid, the amendment stood, and the map was restored. Republicans are using that same principle in Mississippi to block late-arriving mail ballots. Virginia Democrats asked the conservative supermajority to be consistent.

The Court declined. Consistency, it turns out, is directional.

Virginia is now settled. The four seats are gone. The $70 million is spent. Beth Macy is still running in a district Trump carried by 25 points, armed with a spork. The woman in the Shenandoah Valley food pantry who believed, for the first time in her life, that she might have representation — she now knows, with finality, that she will not.

The question moves south.

The Same Project

In the eighteen days between May 9th and May 26th, the following happened in the United States.

On May 9th, Gary Chambers Jr. testified before the Louisiana Senate and Governmental Affairs Committee and called the legislature reconstructionists. The same day, all four Black members of Congress from Louisiana — the only four since Reconstruction — stood together in the State Capitol to testify against maps that would eliminate their districts.

On May 11th, the Supreme Court cleared the path for Alabama to eliminate the district that elected Shomari Figures in 2024.

On May 12th, South Carolina’s Senate voted to halt its redistricting effort, with five Republican defectors blocking the return to session. Shane Massey, the Republican Senate leader, voted no after Trump called him personally. “That’s a possibility, if not a likelihood,” Massey said of his own political future. “And I’m comfortable with that, because my conscience is clear.” The same day, the Missouri Supreme Court upheld the Republican congressional map.

On May 13th, Governor Brian Kemp called a special session of the Georgia legislature for June 17 to redraw congressional and legislative maps — not for 2026, but for 2028.

On May 15th, the Supreme Court rejected Virginia’s emergency application.

On May 16th, Senator Bill Cassidy finished third in his own Republican primary in Louisiana, ousted for voting to convict Trump after January 6th.

On May 19th, Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky lost his primary 55 to 45 to Ed Gallrein. Brad Raffensperger was eliminated from the Georgia governor’s race at 15 percent. And Trump endorsed Ken Paxton over John Cornyn in the Texas Senate runoff.

On May 21st, the Louisiana House and Governmental Affairs Committee advanced the amended redistricting map on a 10-7 party-line vote, after a ten-hour hearing in which dozens of voters testified in opposition and one testified in favour.

On May 26th, a federal court blocked Alabama’s new map. The South Carolina Senate adjourned without acting on its map. A Florida judge upheld the DeSantis map. And Texas voted in its Senate runoff.

That is eighteen days.

Massie vs Gallrein: From conscience to “Where do you need me, coach?”

The Massie defeat deserves its own accounting.

He was the most outspoken Republican critic of the war in Iran, the congressman who led the push to release the Epstein files, the man who voted against Trump’s tax bill. He lost to a former Navy SEAL recruited by Trump personally, in the most expensive House primary in recent history. Thirty-three million dollars. The same day, Trump endorsed Ken Paxton, the scandal-plagued Texas attorney general, over Senator John Cornyn in their Senate runoff — despite Cornyn’s 99 percent voting alignment with the president. Cornyn’s offence was being “not supportive of me when times were tough.” And in Georgia, Brad Raffensperger, the secretary of state who refused Trump’s demand to find 11,780 votes, was eliminated from the governor’s race at 15 percent.

Shane Goldmacher of the New York Times spoke with Massie weeks before the primary. Massie described his race as a signal to other Republicans of whether they could step out of line with the president on matters of policy. “For my colleagues, this election is about, can we deviate occasionally from the plan,” he said, “and still survive our primaries.”

The answer, as of May 19th, is no.

Gallrein, the man who replaced him, was asked what House committees he would seek. He said he would defer to the wishes of the president, the Speaker, and the Vice President: “I’m gonna say, ‘Where do you need me, coach?’”

This is the connection this series has been circling for twenty-nine instalments without naming it directly. The redistricting and the purge are not parallel stories. They are the same project.

You cannot resist the redistricting if resisting means losing your seat. The Indiana state legislators who refused Trump’s redistricting demands lost their primaries to Trump-backed challengers. Cassidy voted his conscience and was removed. Massie voted his conscience and was removed. Cornyn voted with the president 99 percent of the time and was passed over for a man who has been indicted, impeached, and accused of adultery — because loyalty is not measured in percentages. It is measured in submission.

Shane Massey, in South Carolina, named the cost of conscience and voted no anyway. He is, at this writing, the exception. The maps are enforced by the purge. The purge is enforced by the maps. Districts drawn to be safe make primaries the only election that matters, and primaries are where Trump’s endorsement is decisive. The system is closed.

Georgia: The Special Session

Gary Chambers told the Louisiana committee on May 9th that “Georgia beats you every year” because Georgia doesn’t “have a problem with Black people prospering.”

Four days later, Governor Kemp called the special session.

Sanford Bishop: The target of Georgia’s June 17 special session.

The session begins June 17. The target is the Second Congressional District, represented by Sanford Bishop since 1993 — the only Democratic-held seat outside Greater Atlanta. The current map gives Republicans nine seats to Democrats’ five. The next map is intended to make that arithmetic permanent, while the legislature still can.

Kemp’s calculus is different from Louisiana’s or Alabama’s. He is not racing to change maps for November — early voting for the May 19th primaries had already begun. He is racing to change them before Republicans potentially lose the power to draw them at all. This is the insurance policy, not the smash-and-grab.

On May 19th, Georgia voters clarified the landscape the special session will enter.

Burt Jones, the lieutenant governor and Trump’s endorsed candidate for governor, advanced to a June 16 runoff against Rick Jackson, a billionaire health care executive who spent $83 million of his own money. Brad Raffensperger — the man who told Trump he couldn’t find the votes — finished at 15 percent and is done in Georgia politics.

In the Republican Senate primary, Mike Collins led at 40.5 percent and will face Derek Dooley, the former football coach backed by Governor Kemp, in a June 16 runoff. Collins is the congressman the Wall Street Journal editorial board called “the village idiot” — the man who said a bishop critical of Trump “should be added to the deportation list.” He is the likely Republican nominee against Jon Ossoff, who ran unopposed, has $60 million, and is widely regarded as the most formidable Democratic incumbent on the ballot this November.

Keisha Lance Bottoms, the former mayor of Atlanta, won the Democratic gubernatorial primary outright at 56 percent. Biden’s endorsement worked. Democrats will have a nominee ready while Republicans fight through a June 16 runoff.

And in the races that matter most for the redistricting series: both Republican-appointed Georgia Supreme Court justices held their seats. Sarah Hawkins Warren defeated her Democratic-backed challenger 59 to 41. Charlie Bethel survived 51 to 49 — close, but enough. The court remains dominated by Republican appointees, eight to one.

This is the detail that connects to everything else. Virginia’s congressional map died on a 4-to-3 state supreme court ruling. Georgia’s 2028 maps, when they are drawn in the special session, will face a bench that has no such margin of vulnerability. The maps will almost certainly survive legal challenge. The insurance policy has its own insurance policy.

But there was something else in the Georgia returns that deserves attention. Not in the statewide races — in the Second District itself.

The New York Times interviewed voters in Bishop’s district on primary day. Martha Fennessy, a Republican in Plains, said she had voted for Bishop in the past and might do so again. “I think we’re fine like we are. I don’t see a need to go through the process of redistricting.” Edward Callaway, a 71-year-old Republican in Columbus: “I really hate gerrymandering. I think it’s one of the biggest threats to democracy we have.” James Mullenax, 75, a Republican from Columbus: “It just doesn’t seem right.”

Three Republican voters in the district that is about to be dismantled. All three opposed the dismantling.

Irene Smith, 67, a Democrat from Plains, was more direct about what would happen if they tried to eliminate Bishop’s seat: “Let ‘em try. He’s done good all these years, so he’s getting votes from both sides — Republicans and Democrats.”

And Teresa Bristol, 53, a Democrat from Alpharetta, named the redistricting as what brought her to the polls: “Our forefathers have fought for us to have the right to vote. For our opinions to matter. To have people who represent us.”

Mae Della Jones, 83, a retired juvenile case worker in Savannah, said what people who have seen this before always say: “I keep telling myself that hard times don’t last. And I keep singing, if I can hold out, the change is going to come.”

She is 83. She has been waiting for the change to come for longer than most of the legislators drawing these maps have been alive.

Louisiana: Before June 1

The Louisiana legislative session ends June 1. The maps under consideration eliminate one of two majority-Black districts. Senator Ed Price’s proposal to preserve both was rejected. Jay Morris said on the record that his map is designed to “maximize Republican advantage.” The Senate passed SB 121 on May 14, 27-10. The House committee advanced an amended version on May 21, 10-7. Both votes were party-line. The full House vote is expected this week.

The maps would eliminate Cleo Fields’s 6th District and force him into competition with Troy Carter in a single remaining majority-Black district stretching from New Orleans to Baton Rouge. Louisiana’s delegation would shift from 4R-2D to a projected 5R-1D. All four Black members of Congress from Louisiana — the only four since Reconstruction — testified against the maps on May 9. The Republican supermajority advanced them anyway.

Louisiana also reverted to a jungle primary for its House races — all candidates on a single ballot, regardless of party, on November 3. The closed-primary system that shaped Cassidy’s defeat will not apply to the House elections. The irony is precise: the same state that closed its Senate primary now opens its House primary, because the redistricting has made the primary format irrelevant. When the maps guarantee the outcome, the primary rules do not matter.

Meanwhile, the Letlow-Fleming runoff for Cassidy’s Senate seat is set for June 27. Julia Letlow, Trump’s endorsed candidate, led the primary with 45 percent and has secured endorsements from five Republican senators. Fleming is pushing for debates Letlow has declined. Cassidy’s concession speech remains the most direct rebuke of Trump’s retribution politics any departing Republican has delivered: “Our country is not about one individual. It is about the welfare of all Americans, and it is about our Constitution. And if someone doesn’t understand that and attempts to control others through using the levers of power, they are about serving themselves.”

Cassidy said this in Louisiana — the state where the maps are being redrawn, the ballots were discarded, the office was eliminated, and the senator who voted his conscience was removed. He said it and then he was gone.

But he is not entirely gone. After Trump endorsed Ken Paxton over John Cornyn in the Texas Senate race, Senate Republicans pushed back against two of the president’s legislative priorities — funding for his White House ballroom and a $1.8 billion fund to reward January 6 defendants. And Cassidy, freed by his defeat, for the first time voted with Democrats challenging the president’s power to wage war against Iran without congressional approval.

The honest vote that destroyed his career has, in the final months of his term, liberated him to cast more of them. The pattern this series has documented in Lamar Alexander’s memoir — the honest assessment arriving only after the senator is beyond the electorate’s reach — is playing out in real time. Except that Cassidy is not in retirement. He is still in the Senate, with seven months remaining, and the votes he is casting now are the votes he could not cast before. The purge, it turns out, has a side effect the president did not intend.

Three Answers on a Tuesday

On May 26 — the day this instalment was finalised — three states delivered three answers to the question Callais posed.

In Alabama, a panel of three federal judges — two of them Trump appointees — blocked the state’s attempt to use a new congressional map for November. The ruling was unanimous. The judges wrote that they “cannot see our way clear to requiring Alabamians to cast their votes in the 2026 elections under a districting plan tainted by intentional race-based discrimination.” They added: “We do not find the issue particularly complex or close.” Attorney General Steve Marshall immediately appealed to the Supreme Court, saying, “It is not a matter of whether we win this case, only when.”

In South Carolina, the State Senate adjourned without voting on a new map that would have eliminated James Clyburn’s majority-Black district. Early voting was already underway. Forty-five thousand ballots had been cast by 3 p.m. — the same number Louisiana discarded. Shane Massey’s stand held. State Senator Richard Cash, a Republican, said: “Neither my conscience nor my common sense will allow me to stop an election that is already underway.” State Senator Tom Davis, also a Republican, had written to his constituents the night before: “Two years of due diligence would have caught these mistakes. Two weeks did not, because two weeks is not due diligence. It is a rubber stamp.”

Democrats had flooded the polls on the first day of early voting to make the cost of cancellation visible. Brad Hutto, the Democratic minority leader, said: “The people in South Carolina was sending us a message that their vote mattered.” Clyburn spoke to multiple Republican senators during the week. “A critical number of Republicans in the South Carolina Senate just didn’t believe in putting a man over the law,” he said.

In Florida, a judge appointed by Governor DeSantis allowed the state’s aggressively redrawn map to stand — a map that cuts Democratic-held congressional seats from eight to four. The judge called partisan gerrymandering “the lesser of two evils” compared to maintaining a majority-Black district. The DeSantis administration argued that the Fair Districts amendments voters passed in 2010 should themselves be invalidated — that because one provision allowing race-conscious redistricting is now unconstitutional under Callais, the entire package, including the ban on partisan gerrymandering, should fall. The logic is circular and audacious: the Supreme Court strikes down race-conscious redistricting; therefore, the voter-approved ban on partisan redistricting is also unconstitutional; therefore, partisan redistricting is now unrestricted. The plaintiffs will appeal.

Three states. Three branches. Same ruling. Different answers.

And in Houston, two Black Democratic incumbents — Representative Al Green, 78, and Representative Christian Menefee, 38 — spent Tuesday zigzagging polling places in a race that should never have existed. Republican redistricting in Texas eliminated one of their districts and forced them into the same one. “This is the president’s heavy hand,” Green said. “He demanded this.” Adam Fontenette, a Houston resident canvassing for Green, said: “No matter who wins, we lose. They’re both too good. No matter who wins, we lose.”

That is the human arithmetic of gerrymandering. It does not appear in the legal briefs or the legislative debates. It appears in a district where two good public servants are being forced to destroy each other’s careers because the people who drew the lines decided that neither should exist.

That evening, the Associated Press called the Texas Senate runoff. Ken Paxton defeated John Cornyn 63 to 37 — a 26-point margin, in a race that had been 42-41 before Trump's endorsement one week earlier. Cornyn became the second sitting senator removed by a Trump endorsement this cycle, after Cassidy. A four-term incumbent who voted with the president 99 percent of the time was not loyal enough. The National Republican Senatorial Committee issued a statement that did not mention Paxton's name. The Cook Political Report shifted the seat from Likely Republican to Lean Republican. Texas is now on the competitive Senate map for the first time since 1988. The companion series Let's Talk Texas covers the full story.

Thirty-Seven Percent

All of this is happening at 37 percent.

A New York Times/Siena poll conducted May 11 to 15 — the same week as most of the events described above — found Trump’s approval rating at a second-term low of 37 percent. The war in Iran: 69 percent disapproval. The cost of living: 62 percent disapproval. The economy: 56 percent disapproval. Immigration: 64 percent disapproval.

Nearly two-thirds of voters said going to war in the Middle East was the wrong decision. Almost three-quarters of independents agreed.

This is the context in which the maps are being redrawn, the primaries are being purged, and the structural advantages are being locked in. You do not spend $33 million to defeat Thomas Massie, endorse Ken Paxton over John Cornyn, or call a special session to redraw maps for 2028 if you believe you can win on the merits. You do these things because you have concluded that you cannot.

In Part XXVII, Beth Macy said Democrats need to stop showing up to a knife fight with a spork. She is counting on a wave. The wave has real numbers behind it now — 37 percent approval, gas prices rising, Democratic enthusiasm in special elections running at levels that suggest a genuine realignment.

But as that essay concluded: a wave is not a map. A map is a guarantee. A wave has to break at exactly the right moment, in exactly the right districts, in exactly the right proportions.

The New York Times reported on May 17th that fewer than a tenth of the 435 House seats up in November are likely to be competitive — and that the number has been shrinking with every map redrawn. Florida went from fourteen competitive districts to one. Texas went from two to zero.

The wave has fewer places to break.

The View From Here

One year and eleven days. Twenty-nine instalments. Fourteen states. Two Supreme Court terms. One ongoing redistricting war that has now consumed the South, swallowed Virginia, reached into the 2028 cycle before the 2026 midterms have been held, and on a single Tuesday in May, produced a federal court blocking a map in Alabama, a state senate refusing to act in South Carolina, and a judge allowing a partisan gerrymander in Florida.

Two men with the same first five letters in their surnames made the same choice this month. Shane Massey, the Republican Senate leader in South Carolina, voted against Trump’s redistricting demands knowing he would likely face a primary challenge for doing so. Thomas Massie, the Republican congressman from Kentucky, voted against Trump’s war and Trump’s tax bill and Trump’s suppression of the Epstein files knowing the same.

Massey held. Massie fell.

The difference was not courage — both men had it. The difference was the map. South Carolina’s Senate districts are not gerrymandered to the point where a primary is the only election that matters. Kentucky’s Fourth Congressional District is. In a safe district, the primary is the general election, and in the primary, Trump’s endorsement is worth $33 million and a ten-point margin. The map made Massie vulnerable. The purge finished him off. The same project.

Massie said weeks before the vote: “For my colleagues, this election is about, can we deviate occasionally from the plan and still survive our primaries.”

Gallrein, his replacement, said: “Where do you need me, coach?”

That is the trajectory. From a congressman who asked whether independent thought could survive inside the Republican Party to a congressman who pre-emptively surrendered it. From a man who voted his conscience to a man who promised not to have one.

Democracy fights back. In Georgia, it fought back in the voice of Mae Della Jones, 83, who has been waiting for the change to come. It fought back in Martha Fennessy, a Republican in Plains who said she didn’t see a need to redistrict. It fought back in Irene Smith, who said “let ‘em try.” It fought back in Teresa Bristol, who named the Voting Rights Act decision as the reason she came to the polls.

On Tuesday, it fought back in Birmingham and Columbia. It lost in Tallahassee and Houston. In Louisiana, the maps advanced on party-line votes while the voters testified against them and the legislators declined to answer whether racism still exists in America.

The special session in Georgia begins June 17. The Louisiana maps are due before June 1. The Letlow-Fleming runoff is June 27. The Georgia governor and Senate runoffs are June 16. The Georgia redistricting special session is June 17.

The map is always the story. The purge is now part of the story. They are the same project, and the project is accelerating.
