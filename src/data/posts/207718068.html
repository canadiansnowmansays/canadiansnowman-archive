Winston Churchill entered Harrow School in April 1888, aged thirteen, and by his own repeated and remarkably unsentimental account, he was not a good student. He struggled with Latin and Greek to the point of near-total incomprehension, spent much of his time in the school’s lowest academic forms, and later wrote, with characteristic bluntness, that in all twelve years he attended school, “no one ever succeeded in making me write a Latin verse or learn any Greek except the alphabet.” This was not false modesty deployed after the fact by a famous man reflecting fondly on early struggles he had long since transcended. It was, at the time, a genuine and fairly serious problem for a boy whose entire family expected him to follow the conventional path of the aristocratic second or third son into a respectable career, ideally the Army, and who was visibly failing to demonstrate the baseline academic competence that path required.

What Harrow did give him — and this matters more for the rest of his life than any exam result — was a formidable memory and a genuine, self-directed love of the English language, cultivated almost entirely outside the formal curriculum that was failing to reach him. At thirteen, he won a school prize for reciting, from memory and without a single error, twelve hundred lines of Thomas Macaulay’s “Lays of Ancient Rome.” It is worth pausing on that detail, because it complicates the simple “poor student” narrative that gets attached to Churchill’s school years. He was not incapable of the kind of discipline and retention that produces academic success. He was, more precisely, uninterested in applying that discipline to subjects that did not engage him, and entirely willing to apply it, at considerable personal exertion, to subjects that did. It is a pattern that would recur throughout his adult career, for better and for occasionally alarming worse.

The family’s plan for Winston’s future, once it became clear that a conventional academic or legal career was not on offer, settled on the Army — specifically, entry to the Royal Military College at Sandhurst, which required passing a competitive entrance examination considerably beyond what Harrow’s academic track had prepared him for. Churchill failed the exam’s second, more demanding component — known as the “further” — not once but twice. He left Harrow in December 1892 to study privately in London under a professional exam coach, a man named Captain Walter James, whose entire profession was preparing underprepared young aristocrats for entrance examinations they had already failed at least once. It is difficult to overstate how ordinary and unglamorous this period actually was: no adventure, no charge, no escape story, just a nineteen-year-old failing an exam for the third time being genuinely possible, cramming under a private tutor because the institutional path had already rejected him twice. He finally passed the “further” examination in June 1893, placing 95th out of 389 candidates — a passing mark, certainly, but not a distinguished one, and one that qualified him only for a cavalry commission rather than the infantry commission his father had specifically wanted for him. Randolph’s response to the news, delivered in a letter Winston’s own son later described as one of the most severe rebukes on record in the family’s correspondence, is a useful corrective to any temptation to read this period as a gentle coming-of-age story. Randolph was, by every account, genuinely disappointed in his son at exactly the moment Winston most needed the opposite.

Churchill entered Sandhurst on September 1, 1893, at eighteen years and ten months old, and — in a pattern that should by now feel familiar — his academic performance improved dramatically once he was studying subjects of his own choosing rather than subjects imposed on him. He graduated in 1894 eighth in his class, a genuinely strong result, vindicating almost nothing about the school’s earlier assessment of him and a great deal about how selectively his intelligence actually operated. He was commissioned as a second lieutenant in the 4th Queen’s Own Hussars in February 1895, roughly a year before his father’s death, and immediately set about doing something that neither Harrow nor Sandhurst had specifically trained him for: turning himself, through sheer accumulated exposure to danger and a genuinely voracious appetite for both combat and the attention that combat could generate, into one of the most famous young soldiers in the British Empire.

The details of the next four years read less like a career and more like an itinerary assembled by someone actively trying to get shot at on every continent available to the British Army. In 1895, on leave from his regiment, Churchill traveled to Cuba to observe the Spanish colonial war against Cuban insurgents, where a bullet passed close enough to kill the horse standing beside him. In 1897, he joined the Malakand Field Force on India’s northwest frontier, in territory that is now part of Pakistan, embedding himself as both soldier and journalist after the commanding general initially declined to take him on as a combatant. In the summer of 1898, over Lord Kitchener’s explicit objections, he secured a place in the Sudan campaign and took part, on September 2, 1898, in the Battle of Omdurman — the last major cavalry charge in British military history, in which Churchill, riding with the 21st Lancers, shot at least three enemy combatants at point-blank range with a semi-automatic Mauser pistol he had armed himself with after injuring his sword hand shortly before the charge. He wrote about all of it, prolifically and profitably, publishing “The Story of the Malakand Field Force” in 1897 and beginning work on what would become “The River War,” his account of the Sudan campaign — building, in real time, the journalistic reputation and public profile that would fund his early political career before he had won a single election. “The River War,” published in two volumes in 1899, ran to well over a thousand pages in its original edition and included, alongside Churchill’s own vivid battlefield reporting, a sharply critical assessment of General Kitchener’s conduct of the Sudan campaign — criticism aimed at the very commander who had tried to keep the twenty-three-year-old Churchill off the campaign in the first place. It is worth noting the nerve this required from someone still, technically, a serving or recently-resigned army officer: publicly second-guessing the tactical judgment of one of the most decorated generals in the British Army, in a bestselling book, while still building a political career that would eventually require Kitchener’s cooperation as a wartime colleague two decades later. The book made Churchill genuinely wealthy by the standards of a young cavalry officer with no independent fortune of his own, and it demonstrated, for the first of many times across his career, a talent that his father conspicuously lacked: the ability to turn even a contentious, potentially career-damaging public stance into a commercially and reputationally profitable venture. Randolph’s public criticisms, when he made them, tended to alienate the colleagues he needed. Winston’s tended to sell.

It is worth pausing on the financial mechanics of this period, because they are usually treated as incidental to the adventure story when they were, in fact, central to it. Churchill’s army pay as a young cavalry officer was modest, and his mother’s own finances — dependent on an estate that Randolph’s political ambitions had never done much to enlarge, and further strained by Jennie’s own considerable spending habits — could not comfortably subsidize the lifestyle he wanted to lead. Writing was, from the very start, a deliberate income strategy as much as a creative outlet: “The Story of the Malakand Field Force” and the two-volume “River War” were both written with a keen, explicitly acknowledged eye toward the royalties and journalistic fees they would generate, in addition to whatever reputation-building they accomplished. This is the second inherited instinct worth flagging here, alongside the recovery-from-catastrophe pattern already noted in Part 2: a Jerome-family comfort with monetizing one’s own public profile that a more conventionally raised aristocratic son might have found undignified. Leonard Jerome had built and lost fortunes through direct market speculation. His grandson, lacking direct access to capital markets but possessing an increasingly marketable personal narrative, essentially speculated in his own celebrity instead — reporting from wars, publishing quickly, and converting each successive exploit into fees before the previous one had finished generating headlines. It is not a coincidence that the family’s later financial arrangements, well into Winston’s own political career, continued to depend heavily on book advances and journalism to cover expenses that his formal salary as a Member of Parliament, and later as a minister, never came close to matching.

He resigned his army commission in 1899, at twenty-four, having concluded — correctly, as it turned out — that he had already extracted more career value from the military than staying in uniform was likely to provide. His first attempt at Parliament that same year, standing for Oldham in a by-election held on July 6, 1899, ended in defeat — Oldham was, at the time, a two-member constituency, and Churchill and his fellow Conservative candidate both lost to the two Liberal contenders at a moment when the governing Conservative party’s popularity was already sliding nationally. It is a detail that tends to get compressed or omitted entirely in accounts eager to move straight to the escape story that made him internationally famous a few months later, and it is worth naming plainly here for a reason directly relevant to this series’ larger argument: Churchill’s campaign expenses in both this defeat and his eventual successful Oldham run the following year, during the 1900 “Khaki election,” were paid for by his cousin, the 9th Duke of Marlborough. The family that had extracted Leonard Jerome’s American dowry a quarter-century earlier to keep Blenheim’s roof intact was, by 1899, quietly underwriting the political career of the family’s most promising grandson — the inheritance flowing, this time, in the opposite direction, from English title back toward American-financed ambition. Winston did not need his own capital to enter politics. He needed his family’s continuing willingness to treat him as a worthwhile investment, and by every available account, they judged correctly.

When the Second Boer War broke out in October 1899, Churchill went to South Africa as a war correspondent for the Morning Post, and within a month of arriving was captured by Boer forces under Louis Botha after joining an armored train reconnaissance mission that came under ambush — a capture made considerably more embarrassing by the fact that Churchill, technically a civilian journalist, had been acting very much as a combatant when he was taken. His subsequent escape from the prisoner-of-war camp in Pretoria, and his journey through Portuguese East Africa back to British lines in Natal, made him a household name on both sides of the Atlantic almost overnight — the first genuine instance of the pattern that would define his entire public life: catastrophic-seeming setback, followed by a recovery so dramatic and so well-publicized that the setback itself was retroactively transformed into the origin story of his eventual triumph.

It is worth being precise about what this period actually established, because it is easy, in hindsight, to read Churchill’s youthful military exploits as simple derring-do on the way to greatness. What it actually established, before he had held a single political office, was the exact psychological template his grandfather Leonard Jerome had already demonstrated on Wall Street decades earlier: court catastrophic risk, absorb the loss when it comes, and recover in a manner conspicuous enough that the recovery becomes the story people remember, rather than the original failure. Harrow’s backward boy, twice-failed Sandhurst candidate, once-defeated parliamentary candidate, and once-captured prisoner of war had, by the age of twenty-five, converted every one of those setbacks into the raw material of a rapidly ascending public reputation. He had not yet held office. He had not yet made the single most consequential misjudgment of his early career — that comes in Part 6, at Gallipoli, sixteen years and one World War later. But the pattern was already fully formed, inherited in temperament from a grandfather he barely knew and demonstrated, repeatedly, at the one institution — the British Army — that had initially wanted almost nothing to do with him.

It is worth noting, finally, what this period did not yet include, because it will matter considerably by the time this series reaches its later movements. Churchill’s exploits between 1895 and 1900 were personally courageous by any reasonable standard, but they were also entirely his own risk to bear — his own body in the path of Boer bullets and Dervish spears, his own reputation on the line when the armored train was ambushed outside Ladysmith. The pattern would change character considerably once he held actual command authority over other men’s lives, first as a junior minister, then at the Admiralty, and it is the difference between those two kinds of risk-taking — personal daring versus institutional gambling with other people’s safety — that Part 6 will need to reckon with directly. A young officer risking his own neck for a byline is a very different moral proposition from a First Lord of the Admiralty risking forty-five thousand Allied lives on a strategic hunch about the Dardanelles. Both drew on the same inherited temperament. Only one of them left this series with an actual body count to account for, and I do not intend to let the charm of the earlier story soften the reckoning the later one requires.
