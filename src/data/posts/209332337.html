I have spent three essays now building an argument by analogy — that this administration operates something like Orwell’s Ministry of Truth, an office whose real business is forgetting. In this essay I can retire the “something like,” because in the matter of the national parks, two federal judges have made the comparison for me, from the bench, in writing. One said the government was rewriting the nation’s history “with a white-out pen.” The other cited 1984 by name. When the judiciary starts quoting the same novel you are, you are no longer straining for a metaphor. You are describing the thing.

The parks are, in some ways, the purest case this series will examine. A museum is a building full of curated argument; you can quarrel about tone and framing and what belongs on a wall. But a sign at the edge of a battlefield, or beside a house where enslaved people lived, or at an overlook where a glacier used to be — that is memory at its most modest and most physical. It is the nation leaning over and murmuring to a visitor, this happened here. To pull that sign down is not to reframe a debate. It is to unsay the sentence.

Across 2025 and into 2026, the sentences came down by the hundred.

The Order and the Order Beneath It

The instrument was the same executive order that reached the Smithsonian — “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History,” signed in March 2025, with its stated mission of purging “improper, divisive, or anti-American ideology” and its directive to the Interior Department to undo what the White House called a “false revision of history.” Beneath it sat a more operational document: Secretary’s Order 3431, issued by Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, which set the park service reviewing and removing exhibits its officials judged “concerning.”

Park staff, as reporting made clear, interpreted the mandate broadly — sweeping in material on slavery, abolition, civil rights, labor, immigration, women’s suffrage, the internment of Japanese Americans, the persecution of Indigenous peoples, gay rights, and climate change. By early 2026 the Interior Department had removed or flagged, by one accounting, hundreds of items. The euphemism traveled with the order, exactly as it did at the Smithsonian: the signs were not being censored, they were being brought into alignment; the history was not being erased, it was being restored to something truer. The white-out pen, the judge would later write, was being sold as a correction.

Let me give you three of the sentences that were unsaid, because the abstraction of “hundreds of items” hides what a removal actually is.

Three Erasures

The President’s House, Philadelphia. A few steps from Independence Hall, on the site where George Washington lived while Philadelphia was the nation’s capital, stood an exhibit nearly two decades in the making — a partnership between the city and the park service that told the stories of nine specific human beings enslaved by George and Martha Washington in that house. Not slavery in the abstract. Nine people, named, with biographies, standing in the exact spot where the man who would become the first president held them in bondage while the founders a hundred yards away wrote that all men were created equal. In January 2026, park service crews removed the panels — without public notice, without consulting the city that had co-created them. The paradox those panels existed to hold — liberty and bondage under one roof — was quietly taken off the wall, leaving only the liberty.

Grand Canyon, Acadia, and the vanishing science. At the Grand Canyon, signage describing the impact of settlers and the mistreatment of Native tribes came down. At Acadia in Maine, signs referencing climate change were removed. Across the system, the interpretive markers that told visitors a glacier had retreated, a sea level had risen, a people had been driven out, were flagged as “concerning” and pulled. A park sign about climate change is memory of a particular kind — it is the physical landscape testifying against a preferred narrative. Remove the sign and the glacier still melts, but the record that anyone once officially noticed has been rubbed off the overlook.

Stonewall. In February 2026, the park service removed a rainbow flag from Christopher Park, across from the Stonewall Inn — a national monument to the birthplace of the modern gay-rights movement — to comply with administration guidance. New Yorkers gathered and raised one again across the street, on a pole the government did not control. It is a small, almost tender illustration of what this series keeps finding: memory, when the official custodian abandons it, tends to be picked back up by the people who refuse to forget.

The Judges Reach for Orwell

Here is where the parks case becomes something more than a catalogue of removals, and where the metaphor of this series was, in effect, ratified by the federal bench.

In February 2026, on Presidents’ Day, U.S. District Judge Cynthia Rufe — an appointee of George W. Bush, which is worth noting for anyone inclined to file this as partisan reflex — ordered the President’s House slavery exhibit restored. And in doing so, she compared the administration’s claim of authority directly to Orwell’s Ministry of Truth. The government, she wrote, was asking the court to affirm its power “to dissemble and disassemble historical truths when it has some domain over historical facts.” Her answer was three words: “It does not.” She ordered the site returned to its physical condition as of January 21, 2026 — the day before the panels came down.

Then, in June 2026, in the broader case brought in Boston by a coalition of park rangers, historians, conservationists, and scientists, U.S. District Judge Angel Kelley issued a preliminary injunction against the whole enterprise. Her language is the reason this essay has the title it does. Under the guise of promoting American dignity, she wrote, the administration was ordering the removal of every sign that did not align with its preferred narrative, “thereby telling half-truths” — an effort, she said, “to rewrite the Nation’s history with a white-out pen.” Removing the signs, she warned, “sets a dangerous precedent of censorship and sanitization.” She gave the government twenty-one days to put them back, pointedly invoking the approaching 250th anniversary: restore the record, she said in effect, in time to honor the country honestly.

Two judges, two courtrooms, one conclusion, and both of them reaching independently for the vocabulary of erasure — white-out, sanitization, dissemble, disassemble, Orwell. This is not a Canadian pamphleteer straining to make a literary point. This is the federal judiciary looking at the evidence and naming the mechanism.

The Part Where It Doesn’t End

I would like to tell you the judges settled it, but this series does not get to end its essays that way, and neither does the record.

The administration did not accept Judge Kelley’s injunction. It appealed to the First Circuit, and a three-judge panel granted a stay — pausing her order, suspending the deadline to restore the signs, and permitting the park service to keep enforcing Secretary’s Order 3431 while the appeal proceeds. In plain terms: the injunction that would have put the history back was itself put on hold, and the removals were cleared to resume. As of this writing, the park service is once again legally free to review and permanently take down the displays it finds “concerning” — the hundreds of items touching slavery, abolition, suffrage, internment, and the rest.

So the state of the national memory in the parks, this summer, is genuinely in flux. A slavery exhibit at the President’s House, restored by one judge. A system-wide removal, blocked by another and then unblocked by a third. A rainbow flag taken down by the government and raised again by citizens. The record advancing and retreating sign by sign, courtroom by courtroom, in the year the country turns two hundred and fifty.

That timing is not incidental, and Judge Kelley was right to seize on it. The whole apparatus — the Smithsonian review, the parks purge, the disclaimers, the President’s House — is running against the clock of the semiquincentennial, the 250th birthday. The administration’s stated wish is to have the national memory tidied and flattering in time for the party. Which tells you what kind of anniversary is being planned: not a reckoning, not the hard and whole account a mature country can bear to give of itself, but a version restored — to borrow the Smithsonian’s word — to some cleaner year, with the difficult signs pulled from the trails and a warning label at the museum door.

What the Sign Said

Let me end with the smallest unit of all this, the thing actually at stake when a marker comes off a post.

A park sign is the least grand form of public memory there is. No marble, no rotunda, no oil portrait — just weatherproofed text on a low stand, read for thirty seconds by a family on the way to the parking lot. And that modesty is exactly its power. It does not argue. It does not editorialize. It says: nine people were enslaved in this house. This glacier was here in 1900 and is not here now. This is where they fought back. It is the nation, at its most unguarded, telling a passing stranger the truth about the ground they are standing on.

The white-out pen goes after those first — not the great monuments, which are hard to move and make headlines, but the low signs at the edges of the trails, which can be pulled on a Tuesday and noticed by no one until a historian happens by and asks where the panel went. That is how a memory is erased in a free country in 2026. Not with a bonfire. With a work order, a “concerning” flag, and a crew with a screwdriver, undoing one modest true sentence at a time, and calling it restoration.

Two judges called it what it is. The screwdrivers, for now, are back out.
