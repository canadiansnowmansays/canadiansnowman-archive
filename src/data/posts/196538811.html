On the night of June 25, 1975, Indira Gandhi — the daughter of Jawaharlal Nehru, the Prime Minister of the world’s largest democracy — advised President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed to declare a state of internal emergency under Article 352 of the Indian Constitution. Ahmed signed the proclamation. The electricity to every major newspaper in Delhi was cut before the presses could roll. Opposition leaders were arrested in their beds. Civil liberties were suspended. Censorship was imposed. The courts were muzzled. Indian democracy, built by her father’s generation at the cost of blood and decades, was switched off like a light.

It stayed off for twenty-one months.

The Emergency is the hinge of this series. Everything before it — the civilization, the Raj, Gandhi, Partition, Nehru’s gamble — leads to the question of whether Indian democracy could survive its most serious internal threat. Everything after it — liberalization, Hindu nationalism, Modi — is shaped by the answer. The answer is yes. But the yes came with scars, and the scars have not healed.

I. Indira

Indira Gandhi was not Mahatma Gandhi. They shared a surname by coincidence — Indira married Feroze Gandhi, a Parsi journalist and politician unrelated to Mohandas. The confusion has served the dynasty well. The name carries weight in India that no amount of clarification can fully dispel.

She became Prime Minister in 1966, chosen by the Congress old guard because they believed she could be controlled. They called her gungi gudiya — the dumb doll. They were catastrophically wrong. Within four years, she had split the Congress Party, outmaneuvered every rival, nationalized the banks, abolished the privy purses of the former princely states, and won a landslide election in 1971 on the slogan “Garibi Hatao” — Remove Poverty. Later that year, she intervened militarily in the Bangladesh Liberation War, dismembering Pakistan and creating a new nation. She was, at that moment, the most powerful leader India had ever had and among the most powerful women in the history of democratic politics.

The trajectory from there to the Emergency was a descent driven by a combination of political crisis, personal insecurity, and the institutional weakness of a system that concentrated too much power in a single office and a single personality.

II. The Road to June 25

By 1974, Indira’s government was in trouble. The economy was stagnating. Inflation was punishing. The oil crisis of 1973 had hammered Indian industry. Jayaprakash Narayan — known as JP, a Gandhian socialist and freedom fighter who had withdrawn from electoral politics decades earlier — emerged from retirement to lead a mass movement against corruption and authoritarianism. The JP Movement, centred in Bihar and Gujarat, drew students, workers, and opposition politicians into a broad coalition that demanded what JP called “total revolution.”

On June 12, 1975, the Allahabad High Court convicted Indira Gandhi of electoral malpractice in her 1971 election — specifically, the use of government officials and resources for campaign purposes. The court invalidated her election and barred her from holding elected office for six years. It was a legal earthquake. The Prime Minister of India had been found guilty of electoral fraud by her own judiciary.

Indira had two choices: accept the verdict and step down, or fight. She chose to fight. The Supreme Court granted a conditional stay, allowing her to remain in office while the appeal was heard but barring her from voting in Parliament. For Indira, this was not survival. It was humiliation. She was Prime Minister in name but stripped of parliamentary authority. The opposition smelled blood. JP called for a nationwide campaign of civil disobedience. He asked the police and military to disobey “immoral orders.”

That last demand — the call for the security forces to defy the elected government — gave Indira the justification she needed, or at least the justification she used. On the night of June 25, she declared the Emergency.

III. Twenty-One Months

The Emergency was not martial law. The military did not take power. The Parliament continued to sit — though it sat as a rubber stamp. The courts continued to function — though the Supreme Court, in a decision that remains its greatest shame, ruled in the habeas corpus case of ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla that during the Emergency, citizens had no right to approach the courts for the enforcement of fundamental rights. Justice H.R. Khanna was the sole dissenter. He argued that the right to life and liberty could not be suspended, even in an emergency. He was passed over for the Chief Justiceship in retaliation. His dissent is now regarded as one of the most important judicial opinions in Indian history.

What the Emergency looked like in practice was this: over 100,000 people were detained without trial. The press was censored — newspapers were required to submit copy for government approval before publication. Opposition leaders, including Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, Lal Krishna Advani, and Jayaprakash Narayan himself, were imprisoned. Trade unions were suppressed. Strikes were banned. The 42nd Amendment to the Constitution was passed, concentrating power in the central government and reducing the judiciary’s ability to review legislation.

And then there was the forced sterilization program.

Sanjay Gandhi — Indira’s younger son, unelected, holding no official position, but wielding enormous power as his mother’s enforcer — drove a campaign of mass sterilization as part of a population control agenda. Government workers were given sterilization quotas. Men were rounded up in slums and rural areas and subjected to vasectomies — often under coercion, sometimes by force. The official numbers claim approximately 8 million sterilizations during the Emergency. The real number was likely higher. The targets were overwhelmingly poor, lower-caste, and Muslim. The program was, by any reasonable definition, a campaign of state violence against the bodies of the most vulnerable citizens.

The forced sterilization campaign destroyed whatever remaining legitimacy the Emergency might have claimed. It turned a political crisis into a human rights catastrophe. And it embedded in Indian political memory a visceral distrust of state power over personal autonomy that persists to this day.

IV. The Miracle

In January 1977, Indira Gandhi did something that no dictator has to do and that most dictators never do. She called an election.

Why she did it remains debated. She may have believed her own propaganda — the censored press reported only good news, and her advisors, terrified of contradicting her, may have told her she would win easily. She may have wanted the legitimacy that only an election could provide. She may have calculated that a controlled election, with opposition leaders still finding their footing after months of imprisonment, was the safest path to a democratic mandate that would ratify the Emergency retroactively.

Whatever her reasoning, she miscalculated. Badly.

The opposition parties — a chaotic coalition spanning Hindu nationalists, socialists, former Congress members, and Gandhians — united under the banner of the Janata Party with a single message: democracy or dictatorship. Jayaprakash Narayan, gravely ill but unbowed, campaigned from his sickbed. The election was held on March 16, 1977. The result was a landslide. The Janata Party won 298 seats. Congress won 154 — its worst performance in history. Indira lost her own constituency. Sanjay lost his.

Morarji Desai became Prime Minister — the man Indira had imprisoned, now holding the office she had been forced to vacate. He was 81 years old. He had spent the Emergency in solitary confinement.

And this is where my story intersects with India’s. Three months later, on a June morning in London, a 25-year-old Canadian was standing on a sidewalk near St. Paul’s Cathedral, watching the Silver Jubilee procession of Queen Elizabeth II. The Commonwealth leaders passed in their limousines. Pierre Trudeau, the only one with his window down, acknowledged our yelling and our mad waving. And somewhere in that procession was Morarji Desai — the man who had been in a prison cell two years earlier, representing the world’s largest democracy at the court of its former colonizer.

I didn’t understand what I was seeing. I was a kid from London, Ontario, watching important people in expensive cars. I know now that I was watching proof — living, breathing, riding-in-a-limousine proof — that the Indian democratic experiment could break and then repair itself.

V. The Meaning

The Emergency lasted twenty-one months. Indian democracy has lasted seventy-nine years and counting. The ratio matters. The Emergency was a crisis, not a verdict. It was the exception that tested the rule and found the rule still standing.

But the scars are real, and they cut in multiple directions.

The first scar is institutional. The Emergency demonstrated that the Indian Constitution contained the tools for its own destruction. Article 352 allowed the government to suspend fundamental rights. The 42nd Amendment showed that a compliant Parliament could rewrite the constitutional order. The Supreme Court’s habeas corpus decision showed that the judiciary could abdicate its role as guardian of rights precisely when that role mattered most. The Janata government partially repaired the damage — the 44th Amendment, passed in 1978, restricted the government’s ability to declare an emergency and restored some judicial protections. But the lesson remained: Indian democracy’s safeguards were paper-thin against a determined leader with a parliamentary majority.

The second scar is dynastic. The Emergency did not end the Nehru-Gandhi dynasty. Indira returned to power in 1980, winning a massive majority. She was assassinated in 1984 by her own Sikh bodyguards in retaliation for Operation Blue Star — the Indian army’s assault on the Golden Temple in Amritsar, ordered by Indira to flush out Sikh separatists. Her son Rajiv succeeded her, won a historic majority on a sympathy wave, and was himself assassinated in 1991 by a Tamil Tiger suicide bomber. The dynasty continued through Sonia Gandhi and, nominally, Rahul Gandhi. India’s democracy survived the Emergency, but it did not break the dynastic pattern. The Nehru-Gandhi family has led the Congress Party for most of its post-independence existence. Whether this constitutes a democratic tradition or a democratic failure depends on your definition.

The third scar is the one that connects most directly to the present. The Emergency created the political conditions for the rise of Hindu nationalism. The Janata coalition that defeated Indira included the Jana Sangh — the predecessor of the BJP — and its cadres gained mainstream political legitimacy for the first time through their participation in the anti-Emergency movement. The RSS, which had been banned during the Emergency, emerged as martyrs for democracy. The men who would later build the Hindutva movement — L.K. Advani, Atal Bihari Vajpayee — served time in Indira’s prisons and came out with democratic credentials that insulated them from the charge that their ideology was itself a threat to democratic values.

The Emergency, in other words, did not just test Indian democracy. It rearranged Indian politics. The Congress Party’s monopoly was broken. The Hindu right was legitimized. And the stage was set for the transformation that would take another two decades to complete but that was, in retrospect, already visible in the wreckage of 1977.

VI. What the Emergency Teaches

I have written extensively in other series about democratic backsliding — about how democracies die not through military coups but through the slow erosion of norms by elected leaders who use democratic mechanisms to dismantle democratic protections. The Emergency is the textbook case, not because it was slow — it was sudden — but because it used the constitution against itself. Indira Gandhi did not stage a coup. She invoked Article 352. She passed amendments through a Parliament that was legally constituted, if morally bankrupt. She operated within the letter of the law while eviscerating its spirit.

Sound familiar?

The parallel to the present moment — in India and elsewhere — is not subtle. When a democratically elected leader with a parliamentary majority uses that majority to concentrate power, weaken judicial independence, suppress dissent, and redefine national identity in terms that exclude a significant minority, the mechanisms are different from 1975 but the trajectory rhymes. Whether the current Indian government is conducting a slow-motion Emergency — an Emergency without the formal declaration, an Emergency by a thousand administrative cuts — is a question this series will address in later essays.

For now, the lesson of 1975–1977 is simple and extraordinary: Indian democracy broke, and Indian voters fixed it. Not the courts — the courts capitulated. Not the military — the military stayed neutral. Not the international community — the international community barely noticed. The voters. Three hundred million people went to the polls and threw out the government that had suspended their rights. They did it peacefully. They did it decisively. They did it in numbers that no democratic election in history had matched.

Morarji Desai rode through London three months later. Pierre Trudeau had his window down. And a kid from Ontario had no idea what he was witnessing.
