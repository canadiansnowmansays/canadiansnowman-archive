by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,620, and I will declare the register at the top, as this series does: this is a serious one, with an edge to it, because it is about the difference between the two sets of books a nation keeps — the one it puts on the money, and the one it puts in the ground. In the autumn of 1620 a small ship crossed the North Atlantic carrying about a hundred and two passengers, and before it landed they signed a document, and in the winter that followed roughly half of them died, and the nation that grew from them chose, with the unerring instinct of nations, to remember the document and forget the dying. This essay is about both counts. You cannot understand the myth without the one it leaves out.

The facts first, kept honest, because the myth has had four centuries of unearned help and the facts have had to fend for themselves.

The ship was the Mayflower, a merchant vessel of no distinction, chartered to carry a mixed company across the sea. Not all of them were the pilgrims of the pageant. Slightly under half were religious separatists — English Protestants who had already fled to Holland seeking to worship outside the Church of England, and who now sought a place to do it permanently. The rest were what the separatists themselves called “Strangers”: ordinary colonists, craftsmen, servants, and adventurers recruited by the London merchants financing the voyage, who wanted returns, not righteousness. Bound for the mouth of the Hudson, within the territory of the Virginia Company where they held a legal patent, they made landfall instead far to the north, off Cape Cod, outside their patent, outside any authority, in a place where their permission to govern themselves simply did not run.

And that geographic accident produced the document, because the Strangers noticed the gap immediately. Some of them, the record suggests, pointed out that once ashore in a place where the patent was void, no one had any legal power over anyone — that they were, in the strict sense, a hundred-odd people on a beach with no government and no obligation to obey a soul. So on November 11, 1620, still aboard, before setting foot on the land, forty-one of the men signed a short agreement: they bound themselves into a “civil body politic,” and promised to make and obey “just and equal laws” for the general good of the colony. It has come down to us as the Mayflower Compact, and it is genuinely remarkable, and I want to honour it precisely for what it was before I complicate it.

Because in Numbers Game terms, the Compact is a beautiful thing: it is consent, counted and recorded, before the fact. Here is a group of people who understood, in a flash of clarity on a cold deck, that legitimate authority is not something you find lying on the shore — it is something a people count themselves into, by agreement, name by name. Forty-one signatures. Not a king’s grant, not a company’s patent, not the divine right of anyone — just a list of men agreeing to be bound by rules they would themselves make. It is one of the earliest instances in the European settlement of the Americas of government deriving its authority from the explicit, enumerated consent of the governed, and the line runs from that deck, however crookedly, to essay 1,776’s self-evident truths and essay 1,789’s tennis-court oath. The counted “yes,” recorded before landing, is a real ancestor of a real idea. I do not begrudge it a particle of its fame.

What I begrudge is the second count, the one the pageant drops.

They landed in November, at the beginning of a New England winter they were in no way prepared for, having arrived too late to plant and too weak to build. And through that first winter, in the damp and the cold and the scurvy and the disease aboard and ashore, they died — not dramatically, not in a single catastrophe, but steadily, a few every week, buried as they fell. Of the roughly one hundred and two who had crossed, only about half were alive when spring came. At the worst of it, the contemporary account tells us, there were only six or seven people well enough to care for the rest — to tend the sick, feed the fires, and, the record notes with terrible plainness, bury the dead. There were weeks that winter when a quarter of the entire surviving colony was upright. The founders of the American myth spent their first season on the continent primarily engaged in the work of digging graves for one another in frozen ground.

And here is the idea at the centre of this essay, the one that has kept me at the desk: a nation’s founding myth is an act of selective counting — it keeps the numbers that flatter the origin and quietly discards the numbers that were the origin’s actual price. The Compact is remembered because it is a credit entry: it makes the founders look like the authors of self-government, which they partly were. The winter is forgotten because it is a debit: fifty dead is not a heroic number, it is a catastrophic one, evidence of unpreparedness, misjudgement, and sheer brute suffering, and myths do not keep catastrophic numbers about their heroes. So the pageant hands the schoolchild the signed document and the harvest feast — the famous meal the following autumn, itself heavily edited — and files the mass grave under material not required. This series has watched the trick before, in other centuries. The Great Fire of London, essay 1,666, kept its official death toll at six because six was the number the record could see. The Bastille, essay 1,789, was stormed to free seven prisoners and remembered as the liberation of a people. The Mayflower kept its Compact and buried its winter. In every case the surviving number is not a lie, exactly — it is a selection, and the selection is the myth, and the myth is what a people agree to remember so that they can bear to have begun.

I should enter the darkest column too, briefly and plainly, because it is the one the myth buries deepest of all. The reason the surviving half of the colony did not simply starve in that second year is that they encountered the Wampanoag people, already living on that land in numbers vastly greater than the newcomers — and already, catastrophically, counted down, their coastal populations devastated in the years just before 1620 by epidemics carried by earlier European contact, whole villages emptied, the survivors weakened and wary. The English settled, in part, on ground that disease had cleared for them. The help the colony received, and the peace that held for a generation, and then the dispossession and the wars that followed as more ships came — that is the true and terrible arithmetic underneath the harvest pageant, and it is the count that four centuries of myth have worked hardest to keep off the page. The founding the nation celebrates was made survivable by a demographic collapse it did not cause but fully exploited, and made permanent by dispossessions it very much did. I file it here in its own column, unrounded, because the whole discipline of this series is that the buried numbers are still numbers, and the ledger is not honest until they are entered.

None of this is to sneer at the hundred and two, and I want to be careful about that, because contempt is cheap and these were human beings who suffered enormously. The separatists among them were, by their own lights, brave to the point of recklessness, and the ordinary Strangers no less so; they crossed a lethal ocean into a lethal winter for reasons that mattered to them, and half of them paid the whole price, and the Compact they signed was a genuine flicker of a genuinely good idea. The problem is never the founders. The problem is always the rounding — the later, comfortable, well-fed act of taking a story soaked in death and consent and disease and luck, and pressing it into a clean coin stamped with a buckled hat and a turkey. The founders lived the full ledger. The myth keeps the change.

There is one more entry I owe this essay, and it is the one that makes it a Numbers Game story rather than merely a corrective, because the full ledger survived at all only through an act of counting exactly like the one at essay 1,623. Nearly everything we know about that first winter — the steady dying, the six or seven left standing, the graves in the frozen ground — we know because one man wrote it down and kept it. William Bradford, who became the colony’s governor, spent decades composing a chronicle of what had actually happened, the deaths recorded plainly alongside the survivals, the hunger alongside the harvest. He kept the honest books. And here is the bitter irony this series cannot let pass: the myth that later discarded the graves was built on the very manuscript that had faithfully preserved them. The nation inherited Bradford’s count and then edited it — kept his Compact, kept his harvest feast, and quietly struck out his winter, cheerfully using the honest record as raw material for the dishonest legend. The keeper wrote down everything. Posterity, which is the least reliable accountant in these files, took what flattered and buried the rest — in a grave as unmarked, in the end, as the ones Bradford watched them dig.

Dior has no founding myth, which I have come to think is one of her great advantages over me. She did not arrive in this household by heroic crossing or signed compact; she arrived in a crate, unimpressed, and took the place over by simple occupation, and she has never once needed to tell herself a flattering story about how it began in order to feel entitled to the sofa. Her legitimacy rests on no document and requires no editing. She is, in this, more honest than every nation in these files, because she has never had to bury a single number to believe in her own right to be here. She just is here, fully and without narrative, which is a kind of freedom the rest of us traded away the first time we told a child the pretty half of where we came from.

The count moves on, as it does, and it moves from a founding on a beach to a founding on a map. The next landmark is essay 1,648, the year the exhausted powers of Europe ended thirty years of religious slaughter by inventing something that seems, now, as natural and eternal as weather, but which was in fact designed in a series of German towns by negotiators at the end of their tether: the sovereign state, the country as a bounded unit on a map, the box we have counted the world in ever since. From the myth a nation tells to the lines a continent drew. See you there.

A hundred and two aboard, forty-one signatures, half in the ground by spring, and a pageant that kept the signatures. In 1620 a small ship founded a nation and a mythology in the same voyage, and the mythology, as mythologies do, kept only the numbers it could bear to look at.

See you at 1,623, when two actors keep the count.
