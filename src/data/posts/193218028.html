Last Sunday I described the town — the river, the gardens, Ontario Street from the Tim Hortons to the Festival Theatre, the maples of Wellington Street and the oaks that have opinions about the sidewalk, the fields of Perth County spreading out in every direction to the horizon and beyond. If you haven’t been there yet, the geography helps. Elsinore makes more sense once you know where the Avon runs and how the land sits around it.

This Sunday: the people.

There are, of course, thirty-three thousand of them, and I cannot introduce all thirty-three thousand, though Gerald Fitch could, having attended enough public meetings over enough years that he has, at some point, been in a room with most of them. I will introduce the ones who appear most frequently in these sketches, so that when they appear you will already know them, and when they do something characteristic you will recognize it, and when they say their one true thing you will understand why it is true.

Leacock, who did this before me and did it better, began Sunshine Sketches with the people of Mariposa. I begin with the people of Elsinore. The method is the same. The province is the same. The gap between aspiration and reality that produces the comedy is, if anything, larger than it was in 1912, which is either progress or its opposite and possibly both.

Gerald Fitch is sixty-three years old, though he looks both older and younger than this depending on the light and the complexity of the document in front of him. He is of medium height and medium build, with the slight forward lean of a man who has spent decades inclined toward desks, and he wears, on all occasions that have been recorded, a variation of the same outfit: dark trousers of the sensible variety, a checked shirt purchased from a store that no longer exists, and a cardigan — grey in winter, a lighter grey in summer — with two pockets that are always full of something. What is in the pockets changes daily. The pockets do not.

He has been volunteering for the municipality of Elsinore since 2005, which is to say he has been filing, summarising, attending, distributing, and recording the life of this city for more than two decades without salary, without title beyond “volunteer coordinator,” and without any particular desire for either. He does it because it needs doing and because he is good at it and because, in Gerald’s view of the world, these two facts together constitute sufficient reason for most things.

His filing system is the institutional memory of Elsinore. The municipal records are the official memory, which is a different thing, being subject to freedom of information requests and the occasional intervention of people who would prefer certain things not to be officially remembered. Gerald’s system runs parallel to the official one, is more complete in certain respects, and is accessible to nobody but Gerald — an arrangement that has suited everyone, including the people it should perhaps concern.

He has a cat named Hansard — after the official record of parliamentary proceedings — who is nineteen years old and constitutionally opposed to being examined. This tells you everything you need to know about Gerald’s sense of humour, which exists, is dry, and surfaces approximately once every three weeks in a form that takes a moment to recognise as humour, which makes it considerably funnier when you do.

He moves at his own pace. He always has. Elsinore has organised itself around this pace the way water organises around a stone: not because the stone is immovable, but because going around is faster than going through, and the stone will be there regardless.

He reads the news because someone has to. He has copies of everything. In Elsinore, these two facts are considered equally important and possibly related.

Darlene Potts has been the Swan Warden of Elsinore since 2004, making her, by continuous tenure in a single civic role, the longest-serving public servant in Perth County — a distinction the county has not formally recognised because the position does not appear in its organisational chart, which Darlene has noted in writing on three separate occasions and which the county has thanked her for raising.

She is tall and lean in the way of people who spend considerable time outdoors in variable weather, with grey hair cut short and practical, and the particular quality of stillness that belongs to people who have learned to observe without disturbing. She wears clothing chosen for function in all seasons. She carries a notebook — dark green, hardcover, the same brand from the same office supply store in Stratford since 2004 — with a pen clipped to the cover.

She speaks rarely at public meetings. When she speaks, the room adjusts in a way that is difficult to describe and impossible to miss. She states what she has observed. She sits down. The observation lands afterward, like a stone dropped in the Avon: the entry is quiet, the rings spread wide.

She was on the river this morning at five forty-five with her notebook, as she is every morning. The record goes back twenty-two years. It is the most complete account in existence of what the Avon does in Elsinore across all seasons and conditions. She has not offered it to anyone. It is not for offering. It is for knowing.

She has a cat, which nobody in Elsinore knew until recently. The cat is named Hydrograph, which is a term from waterway measurement science. This tells you everything you need to know about Darlene Potts, including the parts she does not share at public meetings.

Mayor Doug — his full name is Douglas Arthur Prentice, though nobody has called him Douglas since his mother stopped doing so in 1987 — has been the Mayor of Elsinore for sixteen years, across four elections, on a platform that has remained consistent enough to be described as a philosophy and vague enough to survive contact with events.

He is sixty-one, broad-shouldered, with the comfortable solidity of a man who played rugby in his thirties and has maintained the shoulders without maintaining the training. He has grey hair, a reliable handshake, and a face that has learned, over sixteen years of public life, to produce the appropriate expression for any situation within three seconds of its arising. The expression is always warm. It is always correct. It conveys engagement, concern, appreciation, or resolve as required, in a register that is simultaneously genuine and non-committal, which is a considerable political achievement that Doug has never described as an achievement because he has never examined it as such.

He owns four suits. One of them he has called his good coat since his wife told him, in 2009, that it was the one that made him look like he meant it. It is navy. It fits well. It has attended every significant civic occasion in Elsinore for sixteen years. It is the best-known garment in Perth County.

He privately prefers the Elvis Festival in Collingwood. He has never said this out loud. He never will.

Alistair — one name, professionally, which in Elsinore is considered either an affectation or an appropriate reflection of his standing, depending on whether you are asking Wayne or the Swan Warden — is fifty-eight and in his final season as Artistic Director of the Elsinore Festival, a position he has held for nineteen years and will vacate in November with the complex emotions of a man who has given the best of himself to something and is not entirely sure who he is without it.

He is angular, with dark hair going silver at the temples and the posture of a man who has spent decades in rehearsal rooms: slightly forward, slightly intense, slightly more present than the situation always strictly requires. He dresses with precision — the jacket chosen, the shirt considered, the whole effect suggesting a man who believes that how you present yourself in the world is a form of argument. His shoes are excellent. He has opinions about shoes that he has expressed to very few people, recognising that shoe opinions in a city of thirty-three thousand in southwestern Ontario require careful audience selection.

He speaks in complete paragraphs. In rehearsal rooms, in offices, in the Festival Theatre parking lot, at the Tim Hortons, in the waiting room of the Small Animal Clinic — always complete paragraphs, always with the structure of a man who has thought before beginning and intends to finish. This is sometimes magnificent and occasionally inconvenient and always, underneath it, sincere.

He believes in theatre the way some people believe in God: not as an abstraction but as a daily practice with specific obligations and the particular conviction of someone who has seen it work. He has seen audiences leave the Festival Theatre changed in ways they could not have predicted when they arrived. This is what he wrote in Gerald’s eleven minutes when the grant application required measurable outcomes. He has believed it for thirty-one years.

This is his farewell season. He titled it This Rough Magic, which is what Prospero says before he breaks his staff. He chose it with care. He thinks about what it means every day. He is possibly not the only one who has noticed.

Wayne — Wayne Douglas Ellerby, though he goes by Wayne the way certain rivers go by their common names: completely, without remainder — is forty-four and the founder, operator, and sole full-time employee of Elsinore Heritage Tours, which offers the Bieber Heritage Walk, the Literary River Tour, the Gold Level Literary River Tour, and, as of this spring, the Presidential Package, the contents of which have not been fully disclosed to the Tourism and Culture Committee, though Gerald has noted this in a sub-folder.

He is compact and neat, with the particular neatness of a man who considers personal presentation a form of professional communication. His clothes are always pressed. His shoes are clean in all weather. He carries a slim black leather notebook that closes with a magnetic clasp. He moves through Elsinore with the smooth forward momentum of a man who has identified where he is going and has planned the route with sufficient care that obstacles are not obstacles so much as variables he has already accounted for.

He arrived eleven years ago from Owen Sound, having identified that Elsinore had an internationally significant cultural asset, a locally significant pop cultural asset, a river, and nobody connecting them for visitors. He has spent eleven years connecting them for visitors. He is currently learning Korean. His pronunciation has been described by his Korean-Canadian clients as sincere, which he has chosen to receive as a compliment and which, in their usage, was intended as one.

He has a ferret. He acquired it without adequate research, which is the only known instance of Wayne failing to research something adequately. The ferret is in good health. It is monitored quarterly.

His rivalry with Alistair is the central comic tension of Elsinore’s cultural life, so thoroughly established that people have stopped registering it as remarkable and simply navigate around it as a feature of the terrain. They are both right about different things. They will not resolve this. It is, in its way, the most honest relationship in Elsinore.

Reg Plimpton is fifty-four, Deputy Reeve of the Municipal Council, and the kind of bald that has been fully accomplished — no transitional phase, no hopeful remnant — with the clean decisiveness of a man who, having identified that hair was becoming a question, resolved it. His head is well-shaped. He wears suits of good quality and conservative cut, and shoes that are noticeably good, and a tie that signals seriousness without stating it, and the overall impression is of a man who has achieved approximately eighty-five percent of the effect he intended, the remaining fifteen percent being accounted for by the arm.

Reg’s right arm has a tendency to extend itself toward the person he is currently agreeing with, landing on the forearm or upper arm in a gesture of warm personal connection that he deploys with such frequency that it has become, among those who know him, a reliable indicator of the direction of the civic wind. When Reg’s arm is on your arm, Reg is with you. The question worth asking is always: who was Reg with six weeks ago.

His positions on all significant matters of public concern have evolved over twelve years with a consistency that is not of principle but of timing. He was against the Festival’s grant before he was for it. He was for the Swan Warden mandate review before he was against it. He wrote one letter about the tariffs being a wake-up call and a second letter about the tariffs being an attack on Canadian sovereignty. Both letters are in the Beacon archive. Gerald has copies of both in a folder he has labelled, with characteristic precision, Plimpton — Correspondence — Beacon.

He has recently developed an interest in Jim, the dog walker, whom he observes through his kitchen window on Tuesday mornings with the careful attention of a man who has identified something useful and has not yet determined how to acquire it. Jim has noticed. Jim has said nothing. Jim’s experience with the arm suggests it will take approximately six weeks to become clear what Reg wants — which is, in Jim’s assessment, about par.

Bradley Fenn is forty-three, bald by choice — he shaved his head in his mid-thirties with the deliberate energy of a man closing a negotiation — and in possession of the best shoes in Elsinore. They are Italian. They are resoled regularly. They arrived from Mississauga seven years ago along with a family, a career in municipal consulting, and a settled conviction that Elsinore, while charming, was not being managed to its potential.

He is organised and thorough and returns emails and has twelve slides for every occasion. He uses the phrase “unelected influence” the way other people use punctuation: frequently, precisely, to separate what he considers legitimate authority from what he considers its usurpation, where that line runs, with notable consistency, along the boundary between the things Bradley controls and the things he doesn’t.

He has begun preparing slides for the next election. This is either persistence or a warning, and in Elsinore these are beginning to be considered the same thing.

Norman Stalker is sixty-one and has edited the Elsinore Beacon for twenty-three years, having arrived from a larger paper in Kitchener in 2003 under circumstances he describes as a lifestyle choice and which were, in the fuller account available in the Kitchener Record archive from that period, somewhat more complicated. He has never confirmed or denied the fuller account. He has simply been in Elsinore for twenty-three years, which is its own kind of answer.

He is thin, with the angular thinness of a man who forgets to eat when something is interesting. He wears three sweaters on rotation — burgundy, dark green, and a blue that has faded to something beyond accurate colour description — all purchased from a store that no longer exists. He wears reading glasses on a chain that he puts on to read and removes to think, producing a constant motion that people who work with him have learned to read: glasses on means processing, glasses off means forming a judgment, glasses in hand means the judgment is not good.

He knows everything that has happened in Elsinore since 2003. He has published approximately sixty percent of it. The other forty percent lives in his head and in a locked filing cabinet in his office that Gerald has noticed and not commented on, because Gerald understands filing cabinets and what they are for.

He published the Moonbeam Column because I sent him a piece and he read it and said: “We’ll try it for a month.” He has not mentioned the original arrangement since. In Norman’s editorial language, this is a renewal.

He is the only person in Elsinore that Reg Plimpton is genuinely afraid of. Reg understands, at the level of instinct, that Norman has the archive and the memory and the forty percent he hasn’t published, and that restraint is a choice rather than a limitation, and that choices can change. Norman has no current plans to change this. He finds restraint more useful than disclosure. This is, in its way, a form of power that compounds with time.

Reverend Faye Drummond is fifty-five and has been the minister at St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon United Church for eleven years, having arrived from Hamilton with the combination of faith and practical intelligence that the United Church at its best produces: people who believe in the transformative possibility of community and have organised their lives around making it happen, while remaining fully aware that community is considerably more complicated than anyone suggests at the outset.

She preaches good sermons. Short ones. She has a gift for finding the parable in the local situation without naming the local situation, which means people leave St. Andrew’s feeling understood without quite knowing what was said. Alistair considers her the only other person in Elsinore who understands what a stage is for. They have never discussed this. They share a table at the Perth County Interfaith Breakfast and talk about other things. The understanding underneath is sufficient.

She has opinions about Bradley Fenn that she has expressed only to God and her journal, which amounts to equivalent privacy. She is preparing a sermon on artificial intelligence. It is taking longer than usual.

Constance Hay is seventy-eight and has lived in the house on Wellington Street that her grandfather built in 1891 for all but four years of her life, those four years having been spent at the University of Toronto studying history, after which she came home because Perth County was where the history was.

She is small — genuinely small, in a way that causes people to make incorrect assessments upon first meeting her, which she has found useful for seventy-eight years. She is upright. Her white hair is worn in a style established in approximately 1974, when she identified the correct arrangement and saw no reason to revisit the question. She states her opinion once. She does not repeat herself. She has found this sufficient.

She was on the original committee that brought the Festival to Elsinore. She has known every Artistic Director. She knew Alistair when he was twenty-six. She has watched him become what he became, and she is saving what she thinks of his farewell season for the last night of it, when it will mean the most.

She met Bradley Fenn once, at a Heritage committee function. She said it was nice to meet him. The way she said it caused Reg Plimpton, standing nearby, to quietly revise his assessment of Bradley’s electoral prospects by the end of the evening.

Maggie is forty-five and works as a veterinary assistant at the Perth County Small Animal Clinic, twelve minutes from Elsinore on the 19. She is Jim’s sister.

She has the physical calm of someone who works with animals and has learned that calm is not a feeling but a practice. She wears her dark hair back for work. Her hands are the hands of someone who uses them all day for work requiring both strength and gentleness in proportions that vary by the patient.

She listens to the presenting complaint. She examines the animal. She says what is actually wrong. This is either the simplest description of a job or the rarest quality in a person, and in Maggie’s case it is both.

Everyone in Elsinore comes through eventually. The farmer with his border collie. Wayne with his ferret. Mayor Doug with his golden retriever, named after a former premier that changes depending on the audience. Alistair’s colleague with the rescue greyhound, for whom Alistair delivered a seven-minute statement in the waiting room about the ethical obligations of animal stewardship in a civilized society. Maggie listened. Then she took the greyhound’s temperature. The greyhound was fine.

She and Jim see each other for Sunday dinners when schedules permit. She is good with his situation. He is good with hers. The boys eat their dinner.

Jim is thirty-six and walks eight dogs in Elsinore and the surrounding area on weekday mornings, beginning at six-fifteen. In the afternoons and evenings, three or four nights a week, he plays online poker.

He is of light build and easy physical presence, with a face that is genuinely at rest in a way that most faces are not — not concealing things, simply present. He dresses for weather. His clothes are unremarkable and chosen without apparent interest in how they appear, producing an unselfconsciousness that people find either reassuring or unsettling depending on what they have to hide.

He reads situations the way he reads dogs and cards: accumulating information, withholding judgment until it is sufficient, and then saying one thing, which is usually the right thing, and moving on. He has never articulated this as a method. He was not asked.

He knows which yards had which candidate signs in October and when they came down and whether they were replaced. He knows whose lights are on at six in the morning and whose cars are gone by seven. He knows, because the Swan Warden’s neighbour’s labrador walks past the Swan Warden’s house every Tuesday and Thursday, what her front garden looks like in every season. He does not share this information. He is not asked. He is the dog walker.

He told Maggie at Sunday dinner, in February, that “those words cannot bear such weight.” He was quoting the Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court without knowing he was quoting him, having arrived at the same conclusion from the ground up, over dinner, passing the bread.

Maggie said she thought she knew what he meant.

They ate their dinner.

Carly Meadows is twenty-four and has been the junior reporter at the Elsinore Beacon for eight months, having arrived from Toronto journalism school with a degree, a student debt, a genuine belief in local news, and a duffel bag that proved insufficient for an Ontario winter, which she corrected in November.

She moves quickly through the Beacon’s small office, learning its rhythms and its editor’s habits, covering what Elsinore produces: the council meetings, the Festival openings, the Farm Bureau, the paddle boat incident, the election. She is getting faster. She is getting better. She does not know this yet, which Norman considers the appropriate instinct and has not said so.

She reads the Moonbeam Chronicle every Sunday. She has not told me this. She cried at Waiting for Godot in July, alone in the dark, and wrote about it in her journal but not in the Beacon. Norman would have let her. She wasn’t ready.

She is paying attention. In Elsinore, this is where all the best things start.

And the Perth County farmer. He has no name here because he does not need one. He is everyone who has stood in a field in this county and understood, before anyone told them, that the ground does not wait. His family has worked Perth County since his great-great-grandfather broke this ground in 1851.

He drives into Elsinore for feed supplies. He reads the signs. He goes to the meeting when the meeting matters. He speaks when he has something to say and not before, which in Elsinore is either a virtue or a rebuke depending on what is being said by everyone else and how long they have been saying it.

He has one line per occasion. It is always the truest thing said.

This morning he was up before dawn and moving toward the barn, because the season is turning and the work does not pause for introductions, however well-intentioned.

This is Elsinore. Thirty-three thousand people. One river. One Festival. One Swan Warden who was on the bank at five forty-five this morning writing down what she saw.

The sketches begin next Sunday, with the Easter egg hunt and what the swans made of it.

Welcome, properly, to all of it.
