On August 4, 2026, Space Exploration Technologies Corp. filed its very first quarterly earnings report as a public company. Buried inside the usual first-earnings theatre — the subscriber counts, the capex numbers, the stock sliding eight percent anyway — was a sentence that ought to have made every wireless carrier on the planet sit up.

President and COO Gwynne Shotwell told analysts that SpaceX’s newly acquired EchoStar spectrum gives the company “a massive increase in capability,” and that the satellite fleet supporting it will likely grow tenfold. CFO Bret Johnsen laid out a roadmap: initial satellite data service by the third quarter of 2027, voice months after that, and a stated ambition of global direct-to-device coverage by 2028.

Read that timeline again. Two years, in a business that usually measures regulatory approval in decades.

I already have a small, personal stake in how this plays out, though not the one I first assumed. My own phone runs on Koodo, which is to say it runs on Telus — and Telus, as it turns out, has bet on the other satellite company. While Rogers signed with SpaceX back in 2023 and now offers Rogers Satellite nationwide, Telus has been quietly aligning with Bell behind AST SpaceMobile, a rival direct-to-cell operator with a grand total of seven satellites in orbit versus Starlink’s six hundred and fifty. My own household, without my ever choosing a side, has ended up in the camp betting on quality over quantity — or, if you prefer the less charitable reading, the camp still waiting for its network to actually exist.

That accidental allegiance is, I think, the whole story in miniature. Two Canadian satellite companies, two different sets of Canadian carriers, two entirely different bets on how fast a constellation can be built versus how good each satellite needs to be — playing out on a national stage while SpaceX simultaneously tries to replicate the winning half of that arrangement in dozens of other countries inside two years. It tells you exactly how SpaceX plans to pull off a rollout that would normally take a generation — and why the company’s approach inside the United States and its approach everywhere else are likely to look like two entirely different businesses wearing the same brand.

The problem money can’t shortcut

Start with the thing that makes telecommunications different from almost every other industry SpaceX has disrupted. Rockets are hard because physics is hard. Landing boosters is hard because nobody had done the engineering before. Spectrum is hard for a different reason entirely: it isn’t a technical problem at all. It’s a sovereignty problem.

Every country on Earth treats its slice of the radio spectrum the way it treats its airspace or its mineral rights — as a national asset, licensed out by a domestic regulator, coordinated internationally through the ITU so that signals don’t trample each other across borders. There is no global spectrum authority SpaceX can simply write a cheque to. There is Canada’s ISED, the American FCC, Ofcom in the UK, and a different regulator in every one of the roughly 190 other jurisdictions where a phone might want to talk to a satellite. Each one runs on its own timeline, its own auction calendar, its own political appetite for letting an American company muscle into a market its own carriers have spent decades building.

This is why the EchoStar deal, impressive as it is, solves exactly one country’s version of the problem. SpaceX now controls 65 megahertz of licensed American spectrum, which is a genuine and rare asset — most usable spectrum in mature markets was carved up years ago and is not for sale at any price. But it is American spectrum. It grants SpaceX precisely zero rights to put a phone call through in Manitoba, or Manchester, or Manila. If “Starlink Mobile” is going to exist as a real product in more than one country by 2028, the American playbook cannot be the global playbook. There isn’t time to run the EchoStar deal ninety more times.

Two ways to get on the air

Which leaves SpaceX with essentially two strategic paths in every market outside the one it has already conquered.

The first is ownership: acquire spectrum outright, either at a government auction or by buying it from an existing private holder, the way it bought EchoStar’s. The appeal is obvious — full control of pricing, branding, and network priorities, with no revenue-sharing and no dependency on a partner’s goodwill. Spectrum licenses are also durable, bankable assets that can be leveraged for financing long after the deal closes. But the costs run just as deep. Auctions are unpredictable and often eye-watering — Canadian 5G spectrum auctions have run into the billions of dollars. Private acquisitions like EchoStar’s require a willing seller, government sign-off, and months or years of negotiation, replicated country by country. And in most mature markets, there simply isn’t much spectrum sitting around unclaimed; you’re buying it from an incumbent, one deal at a time, forever.

The second path is partnership: strike a deal with a carrier that already holds the licensed spectrum in a given country, and ride its network as the satellite layer underneath. This is unglamorous compared to owning your own airwaves, but it is fast, cheap, and — crucially — it uses spectrum that has already cleared every regulatory hurdle a new entrant would otherwise have to climb. The carrier keeps its existing relationship with its regulator; SpaceX supplies the one thing it’s actually built for, which is the constellation overhead.

Given a stated two-year runway to go global, the math isn’t close. Partnership is the only structure fast enough to hit the deadline SpaceX itself just announced to investors.

It’s worth sitting with why ownership is so slow, because the friction isn’t bureaucratic laziness — it’s structural. A spectrum auction requires a regulator to first decide it wants to auction anything at all, then design the rules of that auction, then run a consultation on those rules, then hold the auction, then process the inevitable appeals from whoever lost. Canada’s own five-year spectrum outlook treats this as a multi-year planning exercise, not a quarterly initiative. A private acquisition like EchoStar’s is faster in theory, but only because a willing seller already existed — and even then, the deal still needed regulatory blessing before SpaceX could touch a single hertz of it. Multiply either process across the roughly thirty or forty countries where SpaceX might plausibly want a retail presence by 2028, and you are no longer describing a rollout. You are describing a full second career for SpaceX’s legal department.

Exhibit A: the thing Canada already built

Which brings me back to the carrier I’m not on.

Rogers signed its satellite-to-phone agreement with SpaceX in April 2023, and by December 2025 had launched it commercially as Rogers Satellite, now reaching more than 5.4 million square kilometres of the country — considerably more than any Canadian carrier’s terrestrial towers manage on their own. SpaceX supplied nothing but the satellites. Rogers supplied its own licensed wireless spectrum, its existing customer base, its billing relationship, and its brand. As of this spring, the arrangement had even grown reciprocal: Rogers customers can roam onto T-Mobile’s satellite network while travelling in the United States, and T-Mobile customers get the same courtesy in Canada, each carrier lending the other its home-field spectrum.

Notice what this is not. It is not SpaceX applying for a Canadian spectrum licence. It is not a Canadian auction. It is a bilateral commercial agreement between a satellite operator and a spectrum-holding carrier, stood up in roughly two and a half years from signature to national commercial service — almost exactly the runway SpaceX now says it needs to go from “initial data service” to “global direct-to-device” everywhere else.

Canada, as it happens, is also a country where the alternative path is structurally difficult even if SpaceX wanted it. Bell, Rogers, and Telus hold the overwhelming majority of usable Canadian wireless spectrum between them; a February 2026 residual auction saw 196 of 207 available licences awarded, and every single winner was one of the existing incumbents. There is no meaningful pool of unclaimed spectrum sitting in Ottawa’s vault waiting for an American satellite company to bid on it. The regulator, for its part, is still in the consultation stage on the specific question of satellite-to-device spectrum use, having opened a preliminary inquiry only in May 2026. If SpaceX wanted to own Canadian spectrum outright, it would be queueing behind a regulatory process that hasn’t finished writing its own rules yet.

Partnership, in other words, isn’t just the fast option in Canada. In the near term, it’s close to the only option.

There’s a second, quieter reason the partnership model works so well here, and it has nothing to do with speed. Rogers already carries the regulatory risk. It already has a relationship with the CRTC, existing licence conditions it operates under, and an established track record of compliance the regulator can point to. SpaceX, arriving cold as a new entrant seeking its own Canadian spectrum licence, would be asking a foreign regulator to trust an entirely unproven relationship. Riding on Rogers’ spectrum means SpaceX inherits none of that friction — it simply plugs its satellites into a regulatory relationship that already exists and already works. That’s not just faster. It’s a way of outsourcing trust itself.

What “Starlink Mobile, eh?” would actually look like

Here is where the shape of the thing gets interesting, and where the vocabulary matters. If SpaceX wanted to put its own retail brand on top of a Canadian carrier’s spectrum — Starlink-branded, Starlink-billed, but riding on, say, Telus’s licensed airwaves — the structure that already exists for exactly this purpose is the Mobile Virtual Network Operator, or MVNO. Canada has a whole ecosystem of them: companies like Loblaws’ PC Mobile riding on Bell, or Connex Telecommunications’ PhoneBox riding on Telus — genuinely separate brands and billing relationships layered atop someone else’s licensed network, a structure the CRTC formally opened the door to in 2022.

The Rogers Satellite arrangement isn’t quite an MVNO in the classical sense — Rogers, not SpaceX, is the retail face of it. But it is the load-bearing precedent for one. If SpaceX wants “Starlink Mobile” to exist as its own consumer brand in Canada rather than a feature bolted onto Rogers, the legal and commercial scaffolding to do exactly that already exists, tested, regulator-approved, and sitting right there waiting to be used. It would not require inventing a new category. It would require signing a wholesale agreement.

The asymmetry at the heart of the story

Which is really the shape of the whole global rollout, I suspect. Inside the United States, SpaceX is building something closer to vertical integration — buying its own spectrum, building its own capability, positioning itself to be a full carrier in its own right, not a tenant on someone else’s network. That fits a company that just told Wall Street it wants a bigger share of customer revenue, not a wholesale cut.

Everywhere else, the 2027–2028 timeline simply doesn’t leave room for that ambition. Outside the US, “Starlink Mobile” is far more likely to mean an MVNO-style arrangement, market by market — a Rogers here, a Bell-style AST-competitor there, whatever spectrum-holding incumbent is willing to sign — with SpaceX supplying the constellation and taking a wholesale cut, rather than owning the airwaves outright.

That’s not a lesser version of the ambition. It’s a faster one. And it means the real story of SpaceX’s global mobile push won’t be a single consistent strategy unfurling across 190 countries. It will be a patchwork — one flagship, vertically integrated home market, and a fast-moving trail of local partnerships everywhere else, each one negotiated on the particular terms of whatever carrier happens to already own the air in that country.
