by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,602 is about the invention of an idea so ordinary to us now that it takes real effort to see it was ever invented at all — the idea that you can own a piece of a thing you have never touched, run by people you will never meet, and sell that piece to a stranger before lunch. We call it a share. In the spring of 1602 it did not meaningfully exist, and by the end of that year it did, and almost everything this series covers about modern money runs downhill from the difference.

Let me build it up the way it was built up, because the cleverness is in the layering.

Before 1602, if you wanted to send a ship to the far side of the world for spices — and spices were the oil of the age, pepper and nutmeg and cloves worth staggering sums against their weight — you did it the old way: a syndicate. A handful of wealthy merchants pooled money for a single voyage. The ship sailed, and if it came back, the venture was wound up, the cargo sold, the profits divided, and the whole arrangement dissolved. Each voyage was its own little company, born at the dock and buried at the counting-house. Your money was locked in the hull until the hull came home, and if it did not come home — and a great many did not, as readers of essays 1,522 and 1,588 will remember — your money was at the bottom of the sea with it.

In 1602 the Dutch did something structurally new. Facing ruinous competition among their own merchants and a long war with Spain, the rival spice-trading companies of the Netherlands were merged, by the States-General, into one: the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, the United East India Company, the VOC. And instead of funding a single voyage, the new company raised capital that was permanent — locked in not for one sailing but for an initial term of twenty-one years, a standing pool of money that could build dockyards and fortresses and fleets and think in decades rather than tides. To raise it, the company sold participations to the public — not just to a closed syndicate of grandees, but to anyone in the Dutch Republic with money to put in. Merchants, yes, but also shopkeepers, widows, a maidservant, the city of Amsterdam’s ordinary burghers. Some eighteen hundred people bought in. The record survives of the very first entry in the company’s capital book, a modest sum from a merchant, and near the bottom of the early subscribers, in a detail this series treasures, the name of Neeltgen Cornelis, a servant, who put in a small amount and thereby became one of the first ordinary people in history to own a piece of a multinational corporation.

Now here is the turn that makes 1602 a Numbers Game year and not merely a business-history footnote. That permanent capital created a problem, and the solution to the problem was the actual invention.

The problem was this: if the money is locked in for twenty-one years, what does a subscriber do who needs their money back in year three? A daughter to be dowried, a debt to be paid, a simple loss of nerve? Under the old syndicate model there was no problem, because the voyage ended and paid out. Under the new permanent model, the capital never came back — so the claim on it had to become something you could hand to someone else. And so the VOC’s charter provided that a subscriber could transfer their participation to another party, through the company’s books, by a clerk making an entry. Your share of the company became detachable from you. It became a thing in its own right — a countable, nameable, transferable fraction of an enterprise, whose ownership was established not by holding any physical object but by your name being written in the right ledger on the right page.

And the instant a claim becomes transferable, a market appears to price it, because that is what markets are for. Almost immediately, in Amsterdam, people began buying and selling VOC participations among themselves — and to house the trade, the city built, within a few years, the Amsterdam Exchange, the first true stock exchange in the world, a permanent covered place whose entire purpose was the buying and selling of fractions of a company that most of the traders would never set foot in. Within a decade the Dutch had invented, atop the share, essentially every move the modern market still makes: they were trading shares they did not yet own and shares they were betting would fall; there was speculation, a short-selling scandal, an attempt to ban it, and the ban’s cheerful failure. The whole casino, assembled in one generation, on one foundation: the fraction you could sell.

And the appetite, once created, did not stay penned in shares. The same Dutch Republic that invented the tradeable fraction of a company would, within a single generation, stage the first great speculative mania in recorded history — the tulip frenzy of the 1630s, when contracts for flower bulbs that did not yet exist were traded at the price of houses, by people who never intended to plant a thing, until the whole structure of promises collapsed in an afternoon. I mention it here, thirty years early, because it is the direct child of 1602: once you have taught a society that a piece of paper can stand for a thing, and that the paper can be sold to a stranger for more than you paid, you have taught it everything it needs to talk itself into believing that the paper is the wealth. The share was the sober invention. Tulip mania was the invention drunk. Both ran on the identical discovery — that value could be detached from the object and made to live in the ledger — and this series will return, under later numbers, to the long line of the ledger getting drunk that runs from those bulbs straight through to the crashes of centuries the count has not yet reached.

Here is the idea at the centre of this essay, and it is the one I have been turning over since I started counting. The share is the moment ownership became a number rather than a thing. Before 1602, to own was to possess — a coin in your hand, a field you could walk, a bale of pepper you could smell. The share severed ownership from possession and reattached it to arithmetic: you owned three thousand guilders’ worth of a company, which was not a coin or a field or a bale but a position, an entry, a figure that could rise and fall while you slept and be transformed into any other thing by the writing of a line in a book. This series has watched numbers become weapons and shields and confessions. Here is the number becoming property itself — the most consequential abstraction in the history of money, because once ownership is a number, it can be divided without limit, sold without moving, accumulated without touching, and detached entirely from the doing of any actual work. Every stock ticker, every pension fund, every retirement account, every fortune made and lost without a single spice ship ever leaving harbour, is a descendant of the clerk in Amsterdam who wrote Neeltgen Cornelis’s name into a different column and thereby proved that ownership had become weightless.

I write, under other datelines on this masthead, about the strange far end of this invention — about concentrations of paper ownership so vast that a single man’s shares in companies he does not operate can be worth more than nations, about markets that move on sentiment untethered from any hull or harvest, about the peculiar modern condition in which the largest fortunes exist entirely as numbers in ledgers and have never been anything else. I do not moralise about it here; 1602 is where the road starts, not where it ends, and the men who built the VOC were not thinking about any of it. They were trying to solve a cash-flow problem for a spice company. But that is how the most world-altering numbers in this series almost always arrive — not announced as revolutions, but improvised as fixes, by practical people patching a leak, who did not notice that the patch was a new law of nature.

And I must not close the ledger of the VOC without the entry the company’s own account books kept in a different, darker column, because this series does not round the cost of things. The permanent capital that so brilliantly freed money to think in decades was put to work, over those decades, in conquest, monopoly enforced by cannon, and the trade in human beings; the same structural genius that let a maidservant own a sliver of the future also built one of the most ruthless commercial empires in history, and much of the dividend that flowed back to those eighteen hundred subscribers was drawn from ledgers that counted people as cargo. The invention was value-neutral; its deployment was not. The share made ownership weightless — and one of the first things the weightless new money learned to own, at a distance, through a clean entry in a clean book, was other human lives. Keep both halves of that on the page. The abstraction that built the modern retirement account was first proven on the account of an empire, and the empire, like all the empires in these files, kept its cruelty in the footnotes and its profits in the headline.

Dior owns nothing, holds nothing in the abstract, and would regard the entire concept of a share with the flat contempt she reserves for anything that cannot be smelled, eaten, or chased. Her theory of property is pre-1602 and absolute: a thing is hers if it is in her mouth or under her paw, and it ceases to be hers the moment it is not, and no ledger, no clerk, no exchange, no written claim will persuade her otherwise. I have watched her surrender a contested toy to physical possession without appeal, as though title simply followed the object — which, before the Dutch, it very largely did. She is the last pure holdout of the old economics, the world before ownership went weightless, and there are mornings on the lakeshore when I watch her guard a stick with her whole body and think she has the sounder system. You cannot short-sell a stick. You cannot leverage it. You can only hold it, and love it, and eventually let it go, and there is a version of the whole story of money that is just the long tragedy of forgetting that.

The count moves on, as it does, and it moves from a ship that carried cargo to a ship that carried a myth. The next landmark is essay 1,620, and it concerns a single small vessel crossing a cold ocean with a hundred-odd passengers, a document signed below decks before anyone touched land, and a first winter that would kill half of them — and the strange arithmetic by which a nation chose to remember the document and forget the graves. From the fraction you could sell to the founding you could sanctify. See you there.

Eighteen hundred subscribers, one permanent pool, one detachable claim, and a servant’s name in the book. In 1602 ownership stopped being a thing you held and became a number you owned, and the number, it turned out, could be sold to anyone, for anything, forever.

The count moves on... from the invention of ownership to two men at war with reality in the same year. The next landmark is essay 1,605 — a double, and the first this series has drawn: the year a cellar full of gunpowder came within a lit match of removing an English king... and the same year a Spaniard published the tale of a gentleman who mistook windmills for giants. Two men who could not make the count agree with the world.
