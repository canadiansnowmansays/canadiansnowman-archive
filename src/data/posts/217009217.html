Part I of this series left Mark Carney’s diplomatic case sitting, deliberately, on the table without a verdict: a Canadian prime minister who has spent eight months telling the world the old order is “over” and that Canada intends to help build whatever replaces it. Words are cheap in international relations, and critics on both sides of the border have been quick to point out that a country cannot declare its own sovereignty into economic existence. Fair enough. This essay sets the rhetoric aside and goes to the numbers — GDP, interest rates, jobs, inflation, trade exposure, and debt, measured against the United States over the same period Carney has spent making his case. Some of it supports him. Some of it does not. Both halves belong on the record.

A recession that didn’t quite happen

Canada’s economy spent the back half of 2025 looking genuinely fragile. GDP contracted 0.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025, and the opening months of 2026 brought no real relief — growth stalled near flat through the first quarter, well short of what forecasters had expected, as exports absorbed the impact of renewed American tariffs. The widely feared outcome, a formal recession, never quite arrived, but only barely; Canada’s own Department of Finance, in its Spring 2026 Economic Update, described a recession as “avoided” rather than as never having been a live risk.

What actually happened next is the more interesting part of the story. The second quarter of 2026 produced a genuine rebound: GDP grew 0.8% quarter over quarter, an annualised pace of 3.3% — the fastest since the third quarter of 2024, and ahead of what markets had pencilled in. The growth was broad rather than borrowed from a single sector: household spending rose 0.8%, driven by investment services, vehicle purchases, and rent; business investment strengthened notably, with spending on machinery and equipment reaching its highest level in two years and computer and peripheral spending surging nearly 17%, largely on the back of processing-unit imports for data centres. None of this erases the scare of the preceding two quarters, but it does mean Canada enters the autumn of 2026 with real momentum rather than the stagnation many analysts had priced in as recently as the spring. The International Monetary Fund’s own forecast now has Canada posting the second-fastest growth rate in the entire G7 across 2026 and 2027 — behind only the United States itself, which remains the more precise and more useful comparison than any blanket claim that Canada has “overtaken” its neighbour. It has not. It has, on the IMF’s own numbers, closed a meaningful part of the gap while absorbing a trade war the United States started.

Borrowing money is cheaper in Canada right now

The clearest, least ambiguous advantage in this comparison is monetary policy. The Bank of Canada has held its overnight rate at 2.25% since the summer, a level the bank itself has described as sitting at the lower end of what it considers a “neutral” range — not emergency-low, but comfortably below where it needs to be to slow the economy down. The US Federal Reserve, over the same period, has been operating with a target range closer to 3.75–4.00%, roughly a percentage and a half higher, after a series of cuts through late 2025 that markets expect to slow to at most one further reduction before the Fed holds steady into 2027. The practical difference shows up in exactly the places households and businesses feel it: mortgage renewal costs, small-business credit lines, and corporate borrowing all sit meaningfully cheaper north of the border than south of it, for the simple reason that Canadian inflation gave the Bank of Canada room to cut earlier and further during 2024–25 than the Fed had room to do, and Canada has not yet needed to reverse course the way some forecasters currently expect the BoC eventually will, likely in 2027.

Jobs: a genuine, measurable lead

Since the start of 2025, Canada has added jobs at a rate of roughly 3.4 per 1,000 people in the population; the United States, over the identical window, has managed close to 1.2 per 1,000 — Canada creating employment at nearly three times the American per-capita pace. This is not a cherry-picked or short-lived statistic; it reflects sustained hiring through a period in which Canada’s export sector was directly absorbing new American tariffs, which makes the job-creation record somewhat more notable than it would be in calmer trade conditions. Canada has also led the G7 in per-capita direct investment inflows over the same stretch, and Statistics Canada’s most recent survey of planned business capital spending shows firms intending to increase investment further through the remainder of 2026 — a leading indicator that tends to track future hiring more reliably than a single quarter’s job numbers do on their own.

Inflation: no clean win, and worth saying so plainly

Here the case for Canadian outperformance gets genuinely mixed, and an evidence-based accounting has to say so rather than skip past it. Canadian inflation had been tracking comfortably close to the Bank of Canada’s 2% target through most of 2025 — 2.1% for the year, ticking up to 2.4% in December on base-year effects from an expiring GST holiday, with core inflation easing to around 2.5%. Then, in May 2026, headline inflation spiked to 3.2%, breaching the top of the Bank’s 1–3% target range for the first time since December 2023 — driven directly by the same energy-price shock the war in the Middle East has inflicted on nearly every importing economy, Canada included. The Bank of Canada’s own September communication flagged continued upside risk to inflation from both the ongoing war and the reintroduction of aggressive American tariffs on Canadian exports, even while noting that underlying, core inflation has so far held closer to target than the headline number suggests. The honest summary: Canadian inflation is not clearly better than American inflation right now, and a piece built on evidence rather than boosterism should not pretend otherwise. What Canada does have, that the United States currently lacks, is a central bank with room to respond without immediately reigniting price pressures — a function of the rate gap already described above, not of inflation itself running lower.

Unemployment: the real counterweight

If there is one number in this entire comparison that argues against the “sovereign country, thriving” framing, it is the unemployment rate. Canada’s has run in the range of 6.5% to 6.9% through 2025 and into 2026, depending on the month measured, against a US unemployment rate that has held closer to 4.1%. That is not a marginal gap; it is two and a half percentage points, consistent and persistent rather than a one-off reading, and it reflects a structural slack in the Canadian labour market that has been present, in varying degrees, since the commodity-price adjustment of the mid-2010s. The job-creation figures cited above are genuinely encouraging, and they are also compatible with an unemployment rate that stays elevated, if population growth or labour-force participation are moving in the same direction fast enough — which, in Canada’s case, they largely have been. A fair reading of this series’ own thesis has to hold both facts simultaneously: Canada is creating jobs at nearly triple the American per-capita rate, and Canada’s headline unemployment rate is still substantially worse than the American one. Both are true. Neither cancels the other out.

Trade exposure: absorbing tariffs better than expected

Canada currently carries the lowest average tariff rate of any major United States trading partner, at 5.2% — the product of exemptions and carve-outs negotiated into the broader tariff regime Washington has applied more aggressively elsewhere. The practical consequence shows up in an unexpected place: despite declining in absolute terms as American importers shifted some purchasing toward other Asian suppliers, Canada actually moved up to become the United States’ second-largest source of merchandise imports, behind only Mexico, displacing China from that position for the first time since 2015. That is not entirely a story of Canadian strength — it partly reflects China’s own successful diversification away from the American market, reducing what the US buys from Beijing rather than Canada selling more — but it does mean Canadian exporters have, on the whole, weathered a hostile American trade environment considerably better than a casual reading of the tariff headlines from the past year would suggest.

None of this happened in a calm bilateral relationship, which is worth stating plainly for readers following this Substack’s ongoing “Great Canadian Trade War” coverage. These are the numbers from a period that included a recalled Canadian negotiating team, a collapsed round of trade talks last month, a 100% tariff threat triggered by the Davos speech covered in Part I, and Canada’s own retaliatory measures against American goods. A trading relationship functioning this well, on Canada’s side of the ledger, while simultaneously this dysfunctional at the political level, is itself a data point worth sitting with — it suggests the underlying economic integration between the two countries has proven more durable than the headline-level conflict between their governments.

Debt: the quiet, durable advantage

The least dramatic number in this comparison may be the most structurally important one. Canada’s general government gross debt sits at roughly 106% of GDP, against a US federal debt load closer to 121% — a gap that has held, in Canada’s favour, across every G7 debt comparison for most of the past decade, and one this Substack’s own “Thirty Seven Trillion” series has covered in detail from the American side of the ledger. It is not a figure that moves headlines the way a tariff threat or an annihilation quote does. It is, however, exactly the kind of fiscal headroom that lets a country like Canada absorb a recession scare, a trade war, and an oil-price shock in the same eighteen months without the debt-market consequences a similarly stressed United States would face carrying a debt load fifteen points of GDP higher.

So: sovereign country, or aspiration?

The honest answer is both, in proportions that shift depending on which number you’re looking at. On growth trajectory, job creation, borrowing costs, and fiscal headroom, Canada’s position relative to the United States over the past eight months is genuinely stronger than the trade-war headlines alone would suggest, and stronger than Canada’s own recent history through the mid-2010s productivity slowdown might have predicted. On unemployment and on the most recent inflation reading, the picture is either mixed or actively unfavourable, and pretending otherwise would turn this into exactly the kind of one-sided boosterism this Substack tries not to publish. Carney’s rhetorical claim, tested against eight months of data rather than eight months of speeches, holds up better than a sceptic would expect and worse than an unqualified victory lap would claim. That is, on balance, a more useful thing for a Canadian reader to know than either extreme would have been. Port of Vancouver. Second-largest source of U.S. merchandise imports is not the same thing as a calm trading relationship. It is what the exemptions and the integration look like from the water.
