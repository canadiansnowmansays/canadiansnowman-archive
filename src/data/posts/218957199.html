Ask most Canadians where separatism comes from and they will point east, but not far enough. They will say Quebec, and mean 1976 or 1980 or 1995. The correct answer is Halifax, and the correct year is 1867, roughly ten weeks after the country was born.

Canada’s first secession movement was not French and not small. It was English-speaking, loyal to the Crown to the point of tears, and it won nearly every seat it contested. It sent a delegation to London to get its province out of the union, failed, was offered money, and took it. That sequence, threat, refusal, cheque, is the template for this whole series.

The Botheration Scheme

Joseph Howe was the most famous man in Nova Scotia long before Confederation was a gleam in John A. Macdonald’s eye. A newspaper publisher who had fought for responsible government in the 1840s and later served as premier, he was a fervent imperialist who saw Nova Scotia as a seafaring province of the Empire, facing the Atlantic, not the hinterland of Canada West.

When Charles Tupper, Nova Scotia’s Conservative premier, brought home the Quebec Resolutions in 1864, Howe went to war. Between January 11 and March 2, 1865, he published a series of anonymous editorials in the Halifax Morning Chronicle mocking the proposal as the “Botheration Scheme,” as J. Murray Beck records in his Dictionary of Canadian Biography entry on Joseph Howe. His objection was not only sentiment. Nova Scotia had a thriving shipbuilding and carrying trade, a low tariff, and good customs revenue. Union with the Canadas meant a higher tariff set in Ottawa, a debt shared with a railway-mad partner, and representation as a small minority in a Parliament dominated by Ontario and Quebec.

Tupper never put Confederation to the Nova Scotia electorate. The British North America Act passed the Imperial Parliament without Nova Scotia’s people ever having been asked. Howe, and William Annand’s Morning Chronicle, made that the issue. When the people were finally asked, they answered with a ferocity that has never been matched in a Canadian election.

Thirty-Six of Thirty-Eight

Nova Scotia’s first elections as a province of Canada, federal and provincial, came in September 1867. The anti-Confederates took 36 of the 38 seats in the provincial House of Assembly and 18 of the province’s 19 seats in the new House of Commons, according to Colin D. Howell’s “Repeal Movement” article in The Canadian Encyclopedia. The single pro-Confederation survivor in Ottawa was Tupper himself, as Jon Tattrie’s “Nova Scotia and Confederation” entry in the same encyclopedia notes, the architect of union left standing alone in the wreckage like the last guest at a party nobody else had wanted to attend.

Pause on those numbers. In 1980, the Quebec sovereignty option lost with about 40 per cent of the vote. In 1995 it lost with 49.42 per cent. The Nova Scotia repealers of 1867 won 95 per cent of the provincial seats and 95 per cent of the federal ones. By the only measure available at the time, it was the most decisive popular repudiation of Confederation ever recorded, and it came from a province that had been in the country for less than three months.

The new provincial government under William Annand passed repeal resolutions in February 1868, and Howe sailed for London to ask the Imperial Parliament to undo what it had done. He spent from February to July 1868 there. Tupper followed.

The Colonial Office Says No

London’s answer was polite and absolute. Britain had wanted Confederation for its own reasons, chiefly a cheaper defence of North America against a large and recently battle-hardened United States, and it had no intention of unpicking the whole arrangement for one province. As Phillip Buckner puts it in his chapter “The Maritimes and the Debate Over Confederation,” in the University of Calgary Press collection Reconsidering Confederation, there was never any real chance the Imperial government would let Nova Scotia secede.

All Howe came home with, in Beck’s account, was a promise that the colonial secretary would ask Ottawa to review the effect of its tax, trade and fisheries policies on Nova Scotia. The referee declined to stop the match and suggested the players talk among themselves.

This is the first lesson of Canadian separatism, and it has never really changed. In 1868 the key was held in London. Since 1998, as Part IV will explain, it has been held jointly, through negotiation. In neither case is a vote, however lopsided, the same thing as an exit.

Better Terms

What happened next is the hinge on which this whole series turns. Macdonald, who understood leverage better than any Canadian politician before or since, did not try to crush the repealers. He went to Halifax, and he talked to Howe.

The deal was struck in January 1869. Ottawa raised the debt allowance on which Nova Scotia’s federal payments were calculated to $9,186,000 and increased the province’s annual subsidy by $82,698 a year for ten years, backdated to July 1, 1867, as Colin D. Howell sets out in “W. S. Fielding and the Repeal Elections of 1886 and 1887 in Nova Scotia,” published in Acadiensis. On January 30, 1869, Howe was sworn into Macdonald’s cabinet as President of the Privy Council. Later that year he became secretary of state for the provinces.

His followers never forgave him. Howe had to win a federal by-election in Hants County in 1869 against a repealer campaign that treated him as a turncoat, and Beck describes the contest as costing him a complete physical breakdown. He was named lieutenant-governor of Nova Scotia in May 1873 and died at Government House on June 1, 1873, less than three weeks into the job.

It is easy, and a little cheap, to call this a sell-out. Howe had run out of road, and “better terms” was what was left. But note what the episode established. A province had mounted an overwhelmingly popular campaign to leave. It had been refused the exit. And it had been compensated, in cash and in cabinet seats, for staying. The threat had not worked as a threat. It had worked beautifully as a bargaining chip.

Fielding Tries It Again

Nineteen years later, Nova Scotia did it again, and this time a sitting premier led the charge.

William Stevens Fielding, a former Morning Chronicle journalist who had become Liberal premier in 1884, inherited a province in real trouble. The age of wooden ships was ending, young Nova Scotians were leaving for New England, and Macdonald’s National Policy tariff of 1879 took the blame.

On May 5, 1886, Fielding introduced resolutions in the House of Assembly calling for the repeal of the British North America Act as it applied to Nova Scotia and the creation of an independent Maritime union, Howell writes. He then took the question to the people in a provincial election on June 15, 1886, and won 29 of the 38 seats.

On paper, Nova Scotia had now twice voted to leave Canada. In practice, Fielding’s platform was a two-way street: as the open-access Canadian history textbook Canadian History: Post-Confederation, in its section “Nova Scotia’s Second Thoughts” puts it, the Liberals combined a call for secession with a demand for better subsidies. Pay us more, or we go. The repeal movement was the stick; the subsidy was the destination.

It collapsed within a year. In the federal election of February 1887, Macdonald’s Conservatives took 14 of Nova Scotia’s 21 seats, and only three avowed repeal candidates were elected, according to Howell. On April 21, 1887, Fielding introduced a resolution suspending the repeal agitation indefinitely, and the Assembly passed it on April 27.

Fielding’s later career supplies the punchline. In 1896 he went to Ottawa as Wilfrid Laurier’s minister of finance, a post he held for fifteen years. The man who had moved Nova Scotia’s secession resolution ended up writing the country’s budgets. Canadian separatism has a way of ending with its leaders in the federal cabinet, which tells you something about how the country really works.

Maritime Rights

By the 1920s the grievance had changed shape but not substance. The Maritimes were losing population, losing seats in the Commons, and losing industry. The trigger this time was the railway.

The Intercolonial Railway, built under the terms of Confederation and long run with low freight rates to help Maritime goods reach central Canadian markets, was absorbed into the new national system after the First World War. In November 1918 the Borden government moved its headquarters out of Moncton to Toronto and put it under the Board of Railway Commissioners. Ernest R. Forbes, the historian who more or less wrote the book on the movement, calculated that basic freight rates in the Maritimes rose by between 140 and 216 per cent from 1916 to September 1920, against 111 per cent in Ontario and Quebec, in “The Origins of the Maritime Rights Movement,” published in Acadiensis.

Out of this came “Maritime Rights,” a slogan both parties fought to own, backed by a coalition of manufacturers, merchants, fishermen, farmers and labour. Forbes notes that a Nova Scotia legislator, Howard Corning, even proposed a secession resolution in 1923: the old repeal reflex, still twitching half a century on.

The real weapon this time was the ballot box at the federal level. In 1921 Mackenzie King’s Liberals won 25 of 31 Maritime seats; in 1925, with the region in depression, the Conservatives took 23 of 29, as Forbes summarizes in his Canadian Encyclopedia article “Maritime Rights”. King, who never let principle get between him and a seat count, appointed a royal commission under a British lawyer, Sir Andrew Rae Duncan, in 1926. Duncan recommended higher subsidies and an immediate 20 per cent cut in rail freight rates within and out of the region. The Maritime Freight Rates Act of 1927 delivered the rate cut. Forbes notes that Duncan’s more ambitious ideas, subsidies based on need and on regional development, were set aside.

Threat, refusal, cheque. Once again the region did not leave. Once again it was paid, partially, to stop asking.

The Last Colony In

Newfoundland is the strangest chapter in this story, because it is the only time a separate British North American country actually voted on whether to join Canada, and the answer was very nearly no.

The Dominion of Newfoundland had governed itself until the Depression bankrupted it. In 1934 it surrendered responsible government and accepted an unelected Commission of Government appointed from London. After the Second World War, Britain wanted out of the expense. An elected National Convention met from 1946 to debate the island’s future, and a former radio broadcaster, pig farmer and union organizer named Joseph R. Smallwood made himself the voice of union with Canada.

The Convention voted against putting Confederation on the ballot. London overruled it in March 1948 and put it on anyway. The historian Melvin Baker, in a paper titled “Falling into the Canadian Lap: The Confederation of Newfoundland and Canada, 1945–1949“, prepared in March 2003 for Newfoundland and Labrador’s Royal Commission on Renewing and Strengthening Our Place in Canada, documents how British and Canadian officials co-ordinated behind the scenes toward that outcome while insisting publicly that the choice was Newfoundlanders’ alone.

On June 3, 1948, Newfoundlanders voted. Responsible government, meaning restored independence as a dominion, came first with 44.6 per cent. Confederation took 41.1 per cent. Commission government took 14.3 per cent. With no majority, a run-off between the top two was held on July 22, 1948, and Confederation won 78,323 votes to 71,334, or 52.34 per cent to 47.66 per cent, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia‘s “Newfoundland and Labrador and Confederation” by Jon Tattrie and Andrew McIntosh. The Avalon Peninsula, including St. John’s, voted for independence both times. The outports carried Canada.

The Terms of Union were signed on December 11, 1948, and Newfoundland became Canada’s tenth province on March 31, 1949. Smallwood became its first premier and held the job for more than two decades. It is worth noting that 52.34 per cent is exactly the kind of narrow majority the Clarity Act debate would later fret over. If Ottawa doubts that a bare majority is “clear” enough to take a province out, it has some explaining to do about the bare majority that brought one in.

The Long Grievance

Newfoundland’s grievance did not end with the vote. It changed into a ledger, and the province has been keeping it ever since.

The most bitter entry is the Upper Churchill. Under a 1969 contract, Hydro-Québec has bought power from the Churchill Falls generating station in Labrador at roughly 0.2 cents a kilowatt hour, in a deal that runs to 2041, as The Canadian Press reported in “Quebec and Newfoundland and Labrador ready to sign Churchill Falls deal,” on December 11, 2024. Newfoundlanders have long watched Quebec earn billions on their river.

The most theatrical entry came on December 23, 2004, when Premier Danny Williams ordered the Maple Leaf taken down from provincial government buildings in a fight with Paul Martin over offshore oil revenue, as CBC News reported in “Maple Leaf flags removed in offshore feud”. The flags went back up on January 10, 2005. On January 28, 2005, Ottawa signed a new offshore arrangement that guaranteed the province at least $2.6 billion, including a $2 billion prepayment, according to the Heritage Newfoundland and Labrador article “The 2005 Offshore Arrangement”.

Eighteen days without a flag, $2 billion up front. Joseph Howe would have recognized the arithmetic. So would Fielding. Somewhere in a Tim Hortons in Corner Brook, a man in a ball cap presumably looked up from his double-double at the bare flagpole across the road and said what Canadians always say: “Again? Must be Tuesday.” He was not wrong. He was simply early.

What the East Taught Ottawa

Put the episodes side by side and the pattern is plain. Canadian separatism was born in a province that loved the Empire and resented the country, and at its peak it was more popular than any Quebec sovereignty vote has ever been. Thirty-six of thirty-eight is not a protest. It is a verdict. Yet the verdict produced no exit, and every one of these movements was paid. Better terms in 1869. Cabinet posts for Howe and later for Fielding. A 20 per cent freight-rate cut in 1927. A $2 billion offshore cheque in 2005. The threat to leave was rarely credible as a threat, yet it was consistently effective as leverage, because Ottawa always found it cheaper to pay than to test it.

That is the bargain the Maritimes struck first and Quebec perfected later. Whether it is wisdom, or simply a country that has been lucky with its accountants, is the question this series is asking.
