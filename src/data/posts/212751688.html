I saw it break on CP24 the way most Toronto mornings now begin: a red banner, a name I recognised, and a fresh application of a habit I have been cataloguing since June of last year. The post did not arrive out of nowhere. Trade talks between Ottawa and Washington had collapsed just after 10:30 p.m. the previous Friday, after Doug Ford told Prime Minister Mark Carney flatly not to take the deal on the table. Monday brought the escalation: Trump vowed 50 percent tariffs on all Canadian cars, trucks, parts, and steel effective January 1, 2027, dismissed Canadian negotiators as “clowns” from “among the worst Nations in the World to deal with,” and posted “WE DON’T NEED CANADA, THEY NEED US!” Ford answered in kind, telling the Associated Press that Ronald Reagan would be “throwing up” over the tariffs and that Washington wouldn’t get “a grain of sand out of Ontario,” before telling CBC that Trump could, in Ford’s phrasing, kiss his ass. On the morning of August 25, 2026, four days into that exchange, President Trump posted to Truth Social that the United States was giving “serious consideration” to renaming Lake Ontario “Lake America,” adding that this was because Washington did not “expect to doing much business with Ontario any longer.”

Thirteen parts into this series I have tracked a man who cannot walk past a building, a bill, a bridge, or a peace institute without wanting his name stencilled onto it. Lake Ontario is something new in the collection, and worth pausing on for exactly that reason: this is the first time the naming compulsion has been openly wielded as a tariff. Not vanity. Retaliation, wearing vanity’s clothes.

The pattern, briefly restated

Parts I through X of this series traced the psychological architecture of the habit — hotels, golf courses, a university, a peace institute, a music hall, a $250 bill that does not exist. Part XI covered the Kennedy Center’s workaround inscription after a judge ordered the name itself removed. Part XII catalogued the spring’s harvest of new federal-building signage. Part XIII turned to the gold statuary and the currency mock-ups. Throughout, the throughline was self-glorification: an itch to see the name fixed permanently to something that will outlast the man attached to it.

Lake Ontario breaks that pattern in one specific way. Trump is not proposing to call it Lake Trump. He is proposing to call it Lake America — the same move he made with the Gulf of Mexico in January 2025, and the same move he has floated, more loosely, for the Strait of Hormuz. The name isn’t the man this time. It’s the flag. And the flag, it turns out, is being used less as a monument than as a cudgel.

What “Gulf of America” actually bought him

Here the record is useful, because the Gulf of Mexico rename is now old enough to have a track record, and the track record is instructive. In February 2025, following Executive Order 14172, the U.S. Board on Geographic Names updated the Geographic Names Information System, and Google Maps complied — but only for users located inside the United States. As NPR reported at the time, Google’s own explanation was blunt: when official names differ between countries, users see their own country’s official name, and everyone else sees both. Mexican users kept seeing “Gulf of Mexico.” Users everywhere else got “Gulf of Mexico (Gulf of America)” in parentheses. Apple never made the change at all, a gap tipranks was still noting weeks later.

Eighteen months on, that is still the entirety of the “Gulf of America” achievement: one compliant platform, geofenced to one country, with a footnote everywhere else on Earth. Mexico’s government never recognised it. No other nation’s mapping authority adopted it. The rename did not travel an inch past the U.S. border it was drawn to celebrate. It became, in effect, a piece of domestic branding wearing the costume of a foreign-policy win — precisely the kind of gap between the announcement and the fact of the matter that this series exists to measure.

Hormuz: the same instinct, aimed at water that was never his to name

If the Gulf rename tested the limits of unilateral cartography over a body of water the U.S. actually borders, the Strait of Hormuz musings tested what happens when the same instinct is aimed at water it does not. Trump has floated “Strait of Trump” and “Strait of America” repeatedly since March 2026 — first as a stage aside at a Miami investor summit that he insisted was not a slip, then, according to reporting cited by the Daily Beast and CNBC, as a private option under discussion inside the administration if the U.S. succeeds in wresting control of the strait from Iran. As of this week, per UPI’s reporting from August 22, the administration’s position has hardened further: Trump now calls the strait “an American territory” outright, notwithstanding that it runs between Iran and Oman and touches no American shore at all.

No executive order. No Board on Geographic Names entry. No Google Maps update. Just a claim, restated often enough that repetition begins to function as a kind of squatter’s rights. It is worth noting what that claim is actually made of: not sovereignty, not a treaty, not an inch of coastline — only the conviction that saying a name enough times is the same thing as owning what it points to.

Lake Ontario, and the treaty nobody mentioned

Which brings the pattern home, literally. Lake Ontario is not a body of water the U.S. merely borders, the way it borders the Gulf; it is a boundary lake, governed since 1909 by the Boundary Waters Treaty and administered jointly through the International Joint Commission. Toronto sits on its northern shore. Its outflow becomes the St. Lawrence Seaway, itself a jointly managed waterway in which Canada holds the majority of the navigable length. Unlike the Gulf of Mexico — where the United States could at least point to its own coastline and its own domestic naming authority — a unilateral rename of Lake Ontario has no comparable foothold. There is no plausible reading of the 1909 treaty under which Washington gets to relabel a lake it shares fifty-fifty with a sovereign neighbour because that neighbour’s premier was rude at a press conference.

Which is, I think, the real tell. The Gulf of America rename was at least dressed up as heritage — “Restoring Names That Honor American Greatness” was the title of the actual executive order. Lake Ontario is not being offered that dignity. The Truth Social post did not claim history or heritage. It offered a straight transaction: no business with Ontario, no more Ontario on the map. Naming, here, is not a monument. It is a tariff schedule with better branding.

The company Lake Ontario now keeps

Naming disputes over shared water are not, in themselves, unusual. The Persian Gulf is called the Arabian Gulf by its Arab neighbours, and mapmakers have spent decades splitting the difference. The Sea of Japan is the East Sea in Seoul, and Google Maps handles the disagreement the same way it handles the Gulf of America: local label to local users, both names to everyone else. Crimea appears as Russian or Ukrainian territory depending on where the map is being viewed. These disputes share a structure Trump’s cartography does not: they are the product of two governments, each with a genuine claim, who have been arguing in good faith — or at least in mutual recognition — for generations. Nobody expects Seoul and Tokyo to resolve the East Sea question soon, but nobody doubts either side believes its own name.

What distinguishes the Gulf of America, the “Strait of Trump,” and now Lake America is that only one side is playing. There is no Canadian counter-claim to Lake Ontario, no long-simmering dispute this rename resolves. It manufactures a controversy rather than inheriting one, and it does so unilaterally, by presidential mood, with the full expectation that the rest of the mapped world will simply decline the invitation — exactly as Mexico declined it, and exactly as every non-American Google Maps user has been declining it in parenthetical form since February 2025. Trump is not entering the company of the Persian Gulf or the Sea of Japan. He is renaming things the way a man renames a file on his own desktop: confidently, and to nobody else’s effect.

The hypocrisy the pattern can’t quite hide

There is a second irony sitting underneath the first, and it belongs to this series specifically. The same administration renaming a lake, a gulf, and floating a strait rename has also spent considerable energy putting names back the way they were — provided the direction of travel serves the same end. Denali, the tallest peak in North America, carried that Koyukon Athabascan name officially since 2015, after decades of Alaskan advocacy to retire “Mount McKinley,” a label imposed on land the mountain’s original inhabitants had already named. On his first day back in office, Trump ordered it renamed McKinley again, over the objections of Alaska’s congressional delegation and without consulting the state that had spent thirty years working to change it in the first place.

Held up against Lake Ontario, the contradiction is almost too clean: erasing an Indigenous name to restore a nineteenth-century president is framed as “restoring names that honor American greatness”; renaming a binational lake because a Canadian premier gave a bad press conference is framed the same way, one sentence later, with the flag doing the work the history used to do. The throughline isn’t heritage. It’s discretion — the naming power flexes however it’s needed, restoring history when restoring history serves the moment and inventing a new name when inventing one serves it better, with no fixed principle connecting the two except that the President is the one deciding which direction “restoring” points this week.

What actually happens next: probably nothing, which is the point

If the Gulf of America precedent holds — and there is no reason yet to think it won’t — the practical effect of a Lake Ontario rename would be identical: an entry in the GNIS, an update to U.S.-located Google Maps, and a hard stop at the 49th parallel. Canada would not adopt it. The International Joint Commission has no mechanism for a unilateral relabelling by one treaty partner. Every atlas, weather service, and shipping chart outside the United States would carry on calling it what it has been called for four centuries. The insult would be real. The cartography would not.

That gap — between the size of the gesture and the size of what it actually moves — is worth dwelling on, because it is doing real work for the administration even as it accomplishes nothing on any map that matters. A renaming threat costs nothing to make, arrives instantly, and generates exactly the kind of outraged Canadian headline that lets the White House claim it is “winning” a trade fight without settling a single tariff line. It is escalation as content marketing. Four days of tariff threats and personal insults had already produced diminishing returns as spectacle by Tuesday morning; the lake gave Trump a fresh grievance to answer without lifting a tariff or signing an order — the low-cost, high-noise version of leverage, aimed at an audience that was never going to check whether the lake actually changed its name.

As of this writing, Queen’s Park had not yet issued a formal response to the Truth Social post, though it would be a mild surprise if Doug Ford — a man not known for letting a provocation pass unanswered, per his own performance at Monday’s news conference — stayed quiet for long. The likelier outcome is a fresh round of the same exchange: an American threat calibrated for domestic consumption, a Canadian rebuttal calibrated for the same audience on this side of the border, and a lake that keeps its name on every chart, forecast, and shipping manifest outside the United States regardless of what either side says this week.

That asymmetry is the actual story here, more than the insult itself. A renaming threat is nearly free to make and impossible to walk back gracefully, which is exactly why it keeps recurring across this series — the Gulf, the Kennedy Center, the peace institute, and now a lake he does not own the other half of. Each time, the mechanism is the same: declare the new name first, let the outrage generate its own news cycle, and let the actual geography quietly decline to follow along, the way it declined to follow along in February 2025 and has kept declining ever since. The rename doesn’t need to work. It only needs to be said loudly enough, in the right venue, at the right moment in a trade dispute, to read as strength to an audience that isn’t going to check the atlas.

Thirteen parts in, the constant has been a name looking for a surface. Part XIV is the first entry where the surface — an entire binational lake, governed by a century-old treaty, fed by a river two countries jointly manage — was never really the point. The point was making Ontario watch him reach for it, and calling that reach a foreign policy.
