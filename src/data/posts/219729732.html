In Part XCIX, this diary said the most important sentence of that week was one the President never said in public: that he expected to resume bombing Iran after the votes were counted on November 3. The Wall Street Journal had it from his aides. He had not said it himself.

On Thursday, October 8, he said it.

“I want to make it clear to everybody,” he wrote on Truth Social, that “while Iran is in very bad condition, both Economically and Militarily,” and “while the Blockade will remain in full force and effect,” the United States “will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections.” He added, as reported by Axios, that the two countries were “having productive discussions.”

Wars have been paused before for weather, for holidays, for prisoner exchanges and for summits. This one has now been paused, by public announcement, for a vote. The electoral calendar this diary has been describing for a month is no longer an inference. It is policy.

The war did not wait for it. On Saturday, October 10, a Houthi strike on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh killed at least 12 people and injured 309. One of the dead was an American citizen. As Vivian Nereim and Euan Ward reported for the New York Times, it was the deadliest attack on a Gulf Arab country since the war began in February. Leaving the White House for a rally in Tennessee, the President told reporters the United States “may” join the Saudi fight against the Houthis. “We’re going to look at it. We just found out about the recent attack. So we’ll make a decision. We move very quickly.” As Time noted, Thursday’s pledge covered Iran. It said nothing about Iran’s allies.

Wednesday’s Leak, Thursday’s Denial

The post did not come from nowhere. On Wednesday afternoon, October 7, Nancy A. Youssef and Jonathan Lemire reported for The Atlantic that the White House had asked the Pentagon for strike options that could be used before the midterms. The planning, two administration officials told them, showed how badly the President wanted to bring down gas prices and show progress before the vote.

The most striking part of their report is how little even the plan’s supporters expected it to achieve. Its advocates conceded that limited strikes would not bring Iran to the table, would not reopen the Strait of Hormuz and would not lower gas prices before election day. The case for it was presentation. “It would make the president look like he got a win,” a Republican familiar with the deliberations told them.

The Atlantic also reported what people close to the President believe: that he feels “privately humiliated” by his inability to end the war.

Less than a day later came the post ruling out any strike before the election. Brent crude, which had jumped above $105 on Thursday morning after missiles hit Riyadh, eased when the post appeared. That tells you who it was written for.

Five Vetoes

On Thursday evening, Julian E. Barnes and Eric Schmitt filled in the rest, reporting for the New York Times. In recent months, they wrote, the President’s national security team and the Pentagon have brought him five proposals for major operations against Iran or the Houthis. He vetoed every one.

That number should change how this diary describes him. Readers of Part XCVIII will remember the airstrikes on the Houthis that he ordered and then cancelled after a call from Mohammed bin Salman, with the bombs already loaded. It now looks like a pattern rather than an exception. The President who stood at the United Nations rostrum on September 22 and wondered aloud whether to “annihilate the Islamic Republic” has, in private, said no five times.

The plans keep coming. The latest, discussed at Camp David on Friday, October 2, in a meeting led by Vice-President Vance, is a short, intense campaign of about three days. It would target Iran’s missile and drone arsenal, much of which the Revolutionary Guards have rebuilt since the spring, along with energy facilities and Guards headquarters. The Theodore Roosevelt carrier group will join the George H.W. Bush and the George Washington as early as the end of October. That will be the first time since 2003 that three American carriers have been in the Middle East, and it will put more than 20,000 sailors and Marines afloat.

Fairness requires saying what the vetoes mean. Whatever his motives, the President has refused to escalate five times, and some of his own advisers now doubt that strikes would change Tehran’s calculus at all. Restraint for bad reasons is still restraint, and it has probably saved lives. But it is not a strategy. Since September, Iran has struck at least 13 ships by the British Navy’s count, and nearly 20 by intelligence estimates. None of those attacks drew a major American response. The pattern is a threat of annihilation in public, a veto in private, no answer to the attacks in the strait, and an announcement that presents all of it as a scheduling decision. That is less reckless than this diary has sometimes feared. It is also incoherent.

The Retreat

The same day, David E. Sanger and Eric Schmitt published a piece in the New York Times that reads as a ledger of where American forces have withdrawn. On the weekend of October 3 and 4, twelve B-1 bombers were rushed out of RAF Fairford in Gloucestershire. American and British officials cited specific, credible intelligence that Iran-backed operatives were planning drone strikes on the aircraft from just outside the fence, apparently modelled on Ukraine’s truck-launched drone raid on Russian airfields.

The withdrawals have been extensive. American personnel have been evacuated from bases in Bahrain, Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates and Qatar. Many of the region’s thirteen main bases have been rendered uninhabitable, and some are so badly damaged that the Pentagon has little interest in rebuilding them. Six service members were killed in the strike on Port Shuaiba in Kuwait. The Pentagon has refused to give Congress cost estimates for repairing the bases, which lawmakers think could run to tens of billions of dollars. Senator Chris Murphy, who travelled to the Gulf this week to see the damage, was refused entry to Al Udeid.

Even the count of the dead keeps moving. On the evening of Friday, October 9, the Pentagon quietly updated its casualty website, as John Ismay reported for the New York Times. It now lists 21 American service members killed, up from 18, and 865 wounded, 98 more than in August, with no explanation. When the Washington Post reported in September that more troops had died than the Pentagon admitted, Secretary Hegseth called the story “DISGUSTING and FAKE. A complete LIE.” The new figures suggest the Post was right. “There is no excuse for a mistake of this magnitude,” Representatives Adam Smith and Chrissy Houlahan said.

Dana Stroul, the Pentagon’s senior Middle East policy official under President Biden, put the strategic cost plainly: the Iranians “are likely to internalize a dangerous lesson — that by demonstrating the ability to get close to U.S. military bases far beyond the Middle East, Trump can be pushed around.”

The War Nobody Stopped

The war widened this week in Yemen, and it did so through inaction rather than any decision.

On October 6, Vivian Nereim, Saeed Al-Batati and Shuaib Almosawa of the New York Times published the inside story of how the Houthis took the Red Sea coast. In the weeks before the offensive, Lieutenant General Tareq Saleh, whose roughly 50,000 men held the coast near the Bab al-Mandab, warned both Riyadh and Washington what was coming. He asked the Saudis for air cover and the Americans for electronic jamming of Houthi communications. According to Yemeni officials, he got little from either. Washington offered planning and intelligence and ruled out airstrikes. Saudi Arabia flew only minimal sorties.

On the night of September 9, the government line outside Mokha broke. “Retreat! Retreat! Retreat!” one soldier remembered his comrades shouting. “The Houthis are already here!” Rather than regroup, government forces fell back all the way to Aden. They gave up about 1,800 square miles in a single night, including the island inside the strait. Saleh’s group says at least 500 of his fighters were killed and more than 1,500 wounded.

General Saleh then flew to Riyadh and settled into a five-star hotel, where, the Times reported, easy-listening music echoed through the lobby and tea was served with madeleines. Some of his soldiers, meanwhile, were stranded outside Aden, turned away by fighters from factions that are nominally their allies. Stephen Leacock could not have improved on the arrangement, and would not have tried.

“The Yemeni government is basically a fiction at this point,” Gregory Johnsen of the Arab Gulf States Institute told the Times. “They spend more time fighting one another than they do the Houthis.”

On Sunday, October 4, that government declared war to win back what it had lost. Saudi Arabia pledged “full operational support” until the Houthis are “eliminated.” The President has refused Saudi requests to carry out American airstrikes. But more than 200 American intelligence and military analysts are helping to select targets from command centres inside the kingdom, and American tankers are refuelling Saudi jets. The war Washington declined to prevent in September, it is now helping to fight in October, at greater cost and from further back.

Riyadh Under Fire

The Houthis answered the declaration by striking the Saudi capital. On Wednesday, October 7, Saudi authorities said strikes on airports in Riyadh and Abha had killed three people and wounded dozens. On Thursday the Houthis hit King Khalid International Airport again. As Euan Ward reported for the New York Times on October 9, that attack killed three more people, all Saudi nationals. One of them was a Saudia employee, Captain Hamoud Ali Alkalthami. A Saudia aircraft was damaged on the ground, and missile fragments fell on a children’s nursery and a medical clinic.

About one in four departures from Saudi airports was cancelled, according to Cirium figures cited by Vivian Nereim and Ismaeel Naar of the New York Times, and Lufthansa suspended Riyadh flights until October 16. The timing is bad for the Crown Prince. The WPC Energy Congress was due to open in Riyadh on Sunday, October 11, and the Future Investment Initiative, his annual gathering of world leaders and billionaires, follows later in the month. Saudi authorities have warned residents of legal consequences for sharing information about missile and drone attacks.

On Saturday, October 10, the Houthis went further. A cruise missile, according to a Gulf official and an American official, hit a passenger terminal at King Khalid airport. It killed at least 12 people, four of them Saudi nationals and one an American, and injured 309, some critically. On Sunday an injured Indian national died, raising the toll to 13. Secretary Rubio condemned attacks “that took numerous innocent lives, including an American citizen.” It was the deadliest attack inside Saudi Arabia since a mosque bombing in 2015. The airport suspended operations, Saudia flights were diverted, and the coalition reported “a projectile fell” near King Fahd International Airport in the east of the kingdom. Western embassies told their citizens to stay away. Mohammed al-Bukhaiti, a senior Houthi official, claimed the attack as retaliation for Saudi strikes on Yemen. He said the world should understand that “airports, seaports, wedding and mourning halls, hospitals, and markets in Yemen are civilian facilities,” and that “the same rules” must apply everywhere. The argument would carry more weight if the Houthis observed it. As Graeme Wood noted in The Atlantic on October 11, their leader, Abd al-Malik al-Houthi, spent much of a speech this week condemning Saudi strikes on a market in Taiz, saying “it was a market, not a military camp.” Days later his forces hit a passenger terminal. Their military spokesman promised more: “airport for airport, siege for siege, and escalation for escalation.” The Houthi-run health ministry says Saudi airstrikes have killed 91 people in Yemen since September 3, mostly women and children. That figure cannot be independently checked.

They struck again on Sunday, October 11. A projectile hit King Khalid airport and started a fire, killing a Pakistani resident and injuring 11, and the airport at Dammam in the east was also hit, as Euan Ward and Vivian Nereim reported for the New York Times. Riyadh’s schools moved lessons online for the week. The American embassy now requires its staff to get special authorisation to use the airports in Riyadh, Dammam and Jeddah. The World Petroleum Congress went ahead anyway, with diplomats in the city told to shelter in place. As Lisa Friedman reported for the New York Times on October 12, the chief executives of Exxon Mobil and BP stayed away, and the American energy secretary, Chris Wright, spoke by video link. The Yemeni researcher Nadwa Al-Dawsari, quoted by Wood, explained why talks keep failing: the Houthis are on “a divine mission” to “keep the war going,” and negotiation is tactical delay. “Why would they want a deal when they can get everything?”

The President now has to “make a decision” about a war he has already declined to join five times. The proposals he vetoed covered both Iran and the Houthis. The last time he bombed the Houthis, in 2025, the United States spent about $1 billion on munitions in the first month and declared a swift victory. The Houthis are still here. The Crown Prince reportedly urged him to act against the Houthis as long ago as September. Now an American is among the dead, and the President’s pledge to voters, by its own wording, leaves the Houthis outside it. Senator Murphy warned on Sunday against retaliating on Saudi Arabia’s behalf in a war he called “spiraling out of control.” F. Gregory Gause of the Middle East Institute told Vivian Nereim and Lisa Friedman of the New York Times the only exit he could see: an end to the Iranian-American war, “in which the U.S. gets the Iranians to commit the Houthis to an end of hostilities as part of the overall deal.” As of Sunday night, the President had not announced a decision. Wood’s conclusion is the bleakest of the week: the Houthis are holding Saudi Arabia’s transformation hostage “by doing the only thing they have ever done successfully: fighting. It is not obvious that anyone has enough of a corresponding appetite for war to stop them.”

The war is pulling others in. Pakistan and Turkey have activated last month’s Mecca defence pact, and a senior Pakistani official says Pakistani jets are already striking the Houthis. Britain is offering limited refuelling, and France is sending troops to guard Saudi energy sites on the Red Sea. On the ground, the Houthis have effectively encircled Taiz, and a government officer there says residents are running out of fuel and food. The United Nations counts nearly 200,000 Yemenis displaced since September.

The Toll Booth, Confirmed

In Thursday’s post the President also said that none of the oil passing through Hormuz was Iranian. Barnes and Schmitt reported that this is “debatable.” Iran lets through vessels that have paid fees or that may carry some of its oil, Chinese companies have paid those fees, and Iran has long blended its crude with Iraqi oil to disguise it. Treasury sanctioned 17 shadow-fleet vessels the same day.

This is the toll-booth thesis this diary has argued since the summer, now described in operation by American officials, and paid in part by the country whose president Trump hosted at a state dinner last month.

Tehran, meanwhile, looks strained rather than ready to give in. Hamid Rasaei, a hard-line lawmaker and a vocal opponent of a deal, was convicted and jailed this week, which may mean the faction favouring talks has gained ground.

The President’s “productive discussions” did not last the weekend. On Sunday, October 11, Mohsen Rezaei called Washington’s latest response “insufficient,” said Tehran was no longer responding to it, and threatened the region’s data centres and AI facilities if the fighting resumed, according to CBS News. And on Saturday evening, two days after the pledge, an American fighter jet fired a precision munition at a Panama-flagged vessel in the Gulf of Oman and disabled it. The pledge said nothing about Iran’s tankers either.

Then, on Sunday night, Tehran offered the exit Gause described. Abbas Araghchi called his Saudi counterpart, Faisal bin Farhan, and offered to mediate between the kingdom and the Houthis, warning that “insisting on a military approach and ignoring a political solution will further complicate the current situation.” The Saudis confirmed the call and said nothing about the offer, as Erika Solomon reported for the New York Times on October 12. Within a single day, Iran had told Washington it was no longer answering and told Riyadh it was ready to help.

The Cost Ledger

Brent crude passed $105 a barrel on Thursday, October 8, up 4 per cent on the Riyadh strikes, before easing on the President’s post. It was trading at about $104 on Monday, October 12, against just under $70 on the eve of the war. The yield on the 10-year Treasury touched 5.34 per cent again, around its highest level since 2002. American drivers are paying an average of $4.37 a gallon, according to AAA figures cited by The Atlantic, against just under $3 before February 28. At least 16 vessels have been attacked around Hormuz since October 1. Aramco’s chief executive, Amin Nasser, calls the world’s oil reserves “scarily thin,” and the President, under pressure over diesel, has allowed imports of Russian diesel, undercutting his own sanctions. “We are entering an age of prolonged insecurity,” David L. Goldwyn, a former State Department energy envoy, told the Times. As Lisa Friedman and Jenny Gross reported for the New York Times, Jorge Leon of Rystad Energy summed up how the market reads the President’s calendar: “there’s an expectation that there will be a ramp up in attacks after the midterm elections.”

The Numbers

  • Five: strike proposals the President has vetoed in recent months.

  • Three: days of bombing in the latest plan, and American carriers due in the region, the first time since 2003.

  • 13 to nearly 20: ships Iran has struck since September, none of which drew a major American response.

  • 1,800: square miles of Yemen’s Red Sea coast lost by the government in a single night.

  • 200+: American analysts helping to choose targets in a war Washington declined to join.

  • At least 20: people killed by Houthi strikes on Saudi airports between October 7 and 11, one of them American.

  • 309: people injured at King Khalid International Airport on October 10.

  • 21 and 865: American service members killed and wounded, by the Pentagon’s latest, quietly revised count.

  • Nearly 200,000: Yemenis displaced since September.

  • $4.37: a gallon of gasoline in the United States.

  • 22: days from Monday, October 12, to the midterm elections.
