Randolph Henry Spencer-Churchill was born on February 13, 1849, at Blenheim Palace itself, the third son of the 7th Duke of Marlborough, and entered the House of Commons in 1874 as the member for Woodstock at the age of twenty-five, in the same year he married Jennie Jerome. For the following twelve years, his career moved with a velocity that made him, by most contemporary assessments, one of the most talked-about politicians in Britain — and then, across a single fortnight in December 1886, he destroyed it so completely and so unnecessarily that his fall remains one of the standard cautionary tales taught to British political history students a century and a half later.

It is worth understanding what made Randolph’s early career so genuinely arresting to his contemporaries, because it is the template his son would later study, consciously or not, more closely than any biography of Winston usually credits. Randolph’s rhetorical style was audacious in a way that had little precedent among his own aristocratic peers: satirical, needling, willing to attack his own party’s leadership from within when he judged it useful, and delivered with a theatrical flair that packed the House of Commons’ public galleries whenever he was scheduled to speak. He spent the first six years of his parliamentary career, from 1874 to 1880, honing this style largely in opposition to Benjamin Disraeli’s Conservative government — an odd choice for an ambitious young Tory, attacking his own side’s leader, but one that established him as a figure willing to take genuine political risk for a larger payoff in public attention. It worked. By the time the Conservatives returned to office in 1885, Randolph was one of the most recognizable politicians in Britain, and the Fourth Party’s provocations had done real work in reshaping how the Conservative grassroots — the “National Union” of provincial associations he specifically courted — understood their own relationship to the party leadership.

The early trajectory was genuinely formidable. In the early 1880s, Randolph joined three other Conservative MPs — Sir Henry Drummond Wolff, John Eldon Gorst, and a young Arthur James Balfour, who would himself become Prime Minister two decades later — to form what became known as the Fourth Party, a small but disruptive faction that harried the Conservative leadership from within and championed what Randolph called “Tory Democracy,” a populist reworking of Conservative politics aimed at the party’s provincial associations rather than its traditional aristocratic base. It was audacious, tactically sharp, and it worked. Randolph served as Secretary of State for India from June 1885 to January 1886, where his tenure is most remembered for authorizing the annexation of Burma — a costly, controversial imperial land grab that fits, uncomfortably, into the ledger of empire this series intends to keep in view rather than politely set aside. The Third Anglo-Burmese War, launched under his authority in late 1885, was justified publicly on the grounds of Burmese misconduct toward British trading interests and a supposed strategic need to forestall French influence in the region, but its practical effect was the outright annexation of the entire Kingdom of Burma into British India within a matter of weeks, ending a monarchy that had ruled the territory for centuries and installing direct colonial administration in its place. It is the kind of decision that tends to appear as a single clause in summary biographies of Randolph — “he had Burma annexed,” as one standard reference puts it, with the flatness of a line item — when the actual human and political consequences ran considerably deeper and lasted considerably longer than a single sentence can convey. Randolph did not live to see most of those consequences play out; Burma remained under British colonial administration for another six decades, well into his grandson’s own wartime premiership, and the manner of its eventual departure from empire in 1948 was shaped, in no small part, by the resentments the 1885 annexation had planted. This is precisely the kind of imperial entry this series intends to keep on the books rather than treat as scene-setting for the more flattering material that follows, and it will not be the last one.

Between the India office and the Exchequer came the single episode that would haunt Anglo-Irish politics for the following century, and it is worth understanding in full because his own son would spend part of his career trying to undo it. When William Gladstone’s Liberal government committed to Irish Home Rule in early 1886, Randolph saw a tactical opening and moved on it with characteristic speed: “If Gladstone went for Home Rule, the Orange card would be the one to play,” he wrote privately, framing Ulster Protestant opposition to Irish self-government as a piece to be deployed rather than a cause he held with any personal conviction. He traveled to Belfast on February 22–23, 1886, to address Orange lodges directly, and followed it in May with a public letter declaring, in the phrase that would outlive every other line he ever wrote, “Ulster will fight, and Ulster will be right.” The letter was published in The Times on May 8, 1886, and the phrase became, in the words of one historian, the “ace of trumps” for every subsequent generation of Ulster Unionist resistance to Irish self-government, cited and re-cited through the Home Rule crises of 1893, 1912, and 1914. Randolph had, in effect, manufactured a sectarian rallying cry as a short-term parliamentary maneuver against Gladstone, with no apparent thought for how long the phrase might keep working once released into Irish politics. It is still, a century and a half later, one of the two or three phrases from the entire Home Rule controversy that any student of Irish history is expected to know by heart — which makes it, among Randolph’s various gambits, the one with by far the longest half-life, considerably outlasting the Chancellorship it was designed to help him hold.

The particular cruelty of this episode, for a series organized around inheritance, is what his son did with it twenty-six years later. On February 8, 1912, Winston Churchill — by then First Lord of the Admiralty and a committed supporter of Irish Home Rule — spoke in Belfast’s Ulster Hall in favor of the very policy his father had traveled to that same city to help kill. It was, by design or not, the same venue where Randolph had helped rouse Orange opposition in 1886; Unionist leaders treated Winston’s appearance there as a calculated insult to his father’s memory, and one warned openly of bloodshed if he went ahead. He went ahead anyway. Whatever else can be said about Randolph’s political gambling, his son’s willingness to walk into the same hall and argue the opposite case is as clear a piece of evidence as this series will find that Winston’s inheritance from his father was never a fixed ideology, only a fixed appetite for the theatrical, high-stakes political gesture — deployed, in this instance, in precisely the opposite direction his father had aimed it.

And in July 1886, at only thirty-seven years old, Lord Salisbury appointed him Chancellor of the Exchequer and Leader of the House of Commons simultaneously — the second-most powerful figure in the government, with a plausible future claim on the premiership itself.

He held the post for one hundred and forty-two days.

The mechanism of his downfall is almost absurd in its specificity, and worth walking through in exact terms, because the myth that has grown around it — Randolph as a brilliant man undone by hubris — tends to flatten what was actually a fairly narrow, avoidable miscalculation. Randolph drew up a budget built on cuts to military and naval spending, in keeping with the classical Gladstonian principle of “peace, retrenchment, and reform” that he had, somewhat opportunistically, adopted as his own. The Secretary of State for War, William Henry Smith, refused to accept the cuts. Rather than negotiate, Randolph attempted a gambit he had apparently used successfully before on a smaller scale: he submitted his resignation to Lord Salisbury on December 20, 1886, expecting the threat alone to force Salisbury to back him over Smith. It was, in effect, a bluff — the kind of high-stakes negotiating tactic a Wall Street speculator’s grandson might have found entirely familiar, though there is no evidence Randolph ever thought of it in those terms. Salisbury, who had grown wary of Randolph’s attempts to dominate the cabinet and had reportedly been waiting for exactly this kind of overreach, simply accepted the resignation. He published it in The Times on December 23, appointed George Goschen to the Exchequer in Randolph’s place, and that was that. Randolph had gambled his entire political position on the assumption that he was indispensable. He was not, and the miscalculation ended, in the assessment of nearly every historian who has examined it since, what had seemed a near-certain path to the premiership.

Randolph lived for another eight years after the resignation, remaining a member of the House of Commons but exercising steadily diminishing influence, his health and judgment both deteriorating in ways his contemporaries found increasingly difficult to watch. His final years are shadowed by a persistent claim — repeated in many popular accounts and treated more cautiously by serious biographers — that he suffered from neurosyphilis, contracted, according to the rumor, from an encounter shortly after his marriage. It is a claim that Churchill’s own official biographers, including Randolph’s grandson’s biographer Henry Pelling, have treated with real skepticism, noting that the specific medical diagnosis has never been conclusively established and that Randolph’s decline is equally consistent with other causes, including a possible brain tumor. I raise it here not to adjudicate a century-old medical mystery I have no ability to resolve, but because the rumor itself has done real work in how Randolph has been remembered: it offers a tidy, moralized explanation for his fall that the actual documented cause — a single overplayed hand at the bargaining table — does not provide. History, like markets, prefers a story with a clean villain to one with an ordinary, avoidable mistake.

His final major speech in the House, delivered in June 1894, was by every contemporary account a painful failure, his once-formidable oratorical skill visibly deteriorated. In the autumn of 1894, hoping a change of climate might restore his health, Randolph and Jennie set out on a journey around the world, making it as far as Cairo before his condition worsened enough that they turned back, arriving in England shortly before Christmas. He died in London on January 24, 1895, at forty-five years old — Winston was twenty at the time, a Sandhurst cadet who had passed his entrance exams only on the third attempt eighteen months earlier, and who had spent his entire adolescence, by his own repeated and unusually candid account, desperately seeking a father who was rarely present and, when he was, was rarely approving.

This is the inheritance Randolph actually left his son, and it is worth stating plainly rather than romantically: not political wisdom, not a network of useful alliances, not even much direct affection, by Winston’s own account of a relationship in which his father wrote him what Winston’s own son later called “one of the most formidable rebukes” on record, over a matter as minor as Winston’s own insouciant letter about his Sandhurst results. What Randolph left him instead was a cautionary case study, observed at the closest possible range, in exactly how fast a spectacular political rise could detonate over a single miscalculated gamble — and a lifelong, evidently unresolved need to prove, to a father who died before he could see any of it, that the Churchill name was not finished. Scaramucci’s essay speaks of Churchill’s “comeback story” as though it began with the 1929 crash and the wilderness years of the 1930s. It did not. It began considerably earlier, in a household where the family’s most talented political mind had already demonstrated, in humiliating and very public fashion, exactly how the story could end if you overplayed your hand. Winston spent the rest of his life, in one form or another, refusing to make his father’s specific mistake — while making a great many of his own, which is where Part 5 picks up.

There is an irony here worth sitting with, because it recurs throughout Winston’s own career in a form his father’s downfall should have made unmistakable. Randolph’s fatal error was mistaking his own indispensability — assuming that Salisbury could not govern without him, and gambling his entire position on that assumption being correct. Winston, across his own long career, would make strikingly similar bets on his own indispensability more than once, most catastrophically at the Admiralty in 1915, and would survive them only because his failures, unlike his father’s, arrived in wartime, where a nation’s appetite for second chances runs considerably higher than in the ordinary churn of peacetime cabinet politics. Randolph had the misfortune of overplaying his hand during a comparatively placid stretch of Victorian domestic politics, where his miscalculation had no larger crisis to hide inside. Winston had the considerably darker fortune of overplaying his own hand during the deadliest war in human history to that point, which meant his failure at Gallipoli, however catastrophic in its immediate human cost, was eventually absorbed into a longer war whose ultimate outcome gave him room to recover his reputation in a way his father’s political culture never afforded him. It is not a comfortable thing to say about a father’s death shaping a son’s later resilience, but the record supports it: Winston learned, watching Randolph, exactly what overreach looked like from the inside of a family that had lived through its consequences. He did not learn to avoid overreach entirely — he was, if anything, constitutionally incapable of that lesson — but he did, eventually, learn how to survive it, which his father never had the chance to do.
