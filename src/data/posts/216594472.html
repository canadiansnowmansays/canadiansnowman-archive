🖋 Wry and bright for most of its length — it is the happiest year in the second half of the century, and I mean to enjoy it — with a grave turn in the middle and a short, quiet coda at the end that belongs to my own country. I will tell you when each arrives. Dior attends the bright parts.

I count my essays, and every one of them, from 1,066 onward, has been lit in my mind by whatever light the century had: candles for a thousand years, then the filament Edison put in Lower Manhattan at 1,882. Essay 1,989 is where the light changes for the last time. It is the year the screen arrived — not the television, which had been in living rooms for a generation, but the screen as the place where the world would read itself. And it arrived, fittingly, in the same year the most famous wall of the century came down, by mistake, on live television.

Vague but exciting

Begin in March, in Switzerland, because the less famous wall is the more consequential one.

At CERN, the European particle physics laboratory outside Geneva, a thirty-three-year-old English computer scientist named Tim Berners-Lee had a problem that would be familiar to every keeper in this series. Thousands of scientists came and went; their documents lived on dozens of incompatible computers; information was constantly being lost, not because anyone destroyed it but because no one could find it. In March 1989 he wrote a short proposal to fix it. He called it, with magnificent modesty, Information Management: A Proposal. The idea was that documents on any computer could contain links to documents on any other, so that the whole of the laboratory’s knowledge would become a single web that anyone could move through.

His supervisor, Mike Sendall, read it and wrote three words across the top of the cover page: Vague but exciting. And then — this is the part that matters — he let Berners-Lee keep working on it.

Within two years the World Wide Web was running. In 1993 CERN put the technology into the public domain, free for anyone to use, forever. The wall between one computer and another, between one library and another, between the person who had the information and the person who needed it, came down. It is the single largest act of giving-away in the history of this series. Gutenberg, at essay 1,440, made a book cheap. Berners-Lee made every document on earth one click from every other, and charged nothing. I publish this series on a platform that sits on top of his proposal. So does nearly everything you read.

Vague but exciting deserves its place among the great marginal notes of history — beside the stopwatch at Highland Park and the pencilled “signed title page” in my relation’s memoir. It is the anti-Fisher. At essay 1,929 a professor declared, with total confidence, a permanently high plateau, and was wrong within days. Sendall declared nothing at all except that he could not yet see what this was, and that it was worth finding out. The most important managerial judgement of the century was an honest statement of uncertainty.

Immediately, without delay

Now Berlin, in November, and the other wall.

The Berlin Wall had stood since August 1961 — twenty-eight years of concrete and wire and watchtowers dividing a city, and with it the whole of the Cold War map that essay 1,945’s summer had set in place. By the autumn of 1989 the East German state was buckling: tens of thousands of its citizens were fleeing through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, and hundreds of thousands were marching in Leipzig. The government drafted new rules to let people apply for travel visas, meant to take effect the following day in an orderly fashion, and to release some of the pressure.

On the evening of the ninth of November, a Politburo spokesman named Günter Schabowski was handed a note about the new rules shortly before a live televised press conference. He had not been properly briefed. Near the end of the broadcast he read the note aloud, and a journalist asked the obvious question: when does this take effect? Schabowski shuffled his papers, looked at the note, and said — the words are on the tape — that as far as he knew, it took effect immediately, without delay.

It did not. The note said nothing of the kind. But East Germans had been watching, and West German television repeated it, and by late evening thousands of people were massed at the border crossings demanding to be let through immediately, as the man on television had said. At the Bornholmer Strasse checkpoint, the officer in charge, unable to get orders and facing a crowd he could not hold back, opened the gate a little before midnight. The other crossings followed. People climbed the Wall with hammers and champagne. The most heavily guarded border in Europe fell open because a spokesman misread the effective date on a memo.

I have written about counting errors for nineteen hundred essays: the miscounted fleet, the deleted days, the ticker running hours behind. This is the only one in the series that made the world better. One wrong word about when — a timing error in a press release — ended the division of Europe. Every bureaucrat who has ever dreaded a mistake on live television should keep a photograph of Schabowski on the wall. Sometimes the error is the history.

And the whole thing happened on screens. The East Germans learned the news from television. The world watched it live. The wall that had been built to keep people from seeing the West came down because people saw something on a screen and believed it. That is why 1989 is this series’ last change of light. From here on, the scenes are lit by screens.

The count of the Wall’s victims, by the way, was not kept by the state that built it. It was established afterwards, by historians working through the files: at least a hundred and forty people killed at the Berlin Wall in its twenty-eight years, most of them shot trying to cross. A border that counted every citizen who applied to leave did not count the ones it killed for leaving without asking.

Dior and the gate

The husky, it should be said, has her own views on walls, all of them negative. She regards every fence on our route as a provisional arrangement awaiting the correct sequence of persistence, and she has, on more than one occasion, stood at the gate of the dog park making exactly the case the crowd at Bornholmer Strasse made: that the gate should open immediately, without delay, on the authority of nothing but the size of the crowd that wanted it open — the crowd, in her case, being her. It works more often than I care to admit. The officer on duty, in our household, is me, and I have never once successfully held the line.

The other square

Now the grave turn, because the year of open gates was also a year of a square closed by force, and I will not round 1989 into a year of pure liberation.

Five months before the Wall fell, in the spring of 1989, students and workers had gathered by the hundreds of thousands in Tiananmen Square in Beijing, calling for political reform. On the night of the third and fourth of June, the Chinese government sent the army in. Troops and tanks cleared the square and the streets around it, and opened fire.

How many died? You know the answer this series always has to give, and this time there is a reason beyond chaos: the count was forbidden. The government gave a figure of around two to three hundred, including soldiers, and then made the subject itself unspeakable. Estimates from witnesses, diplomats and researchers run from several hundred to several thousand; one British diplomatic cable, released decades later, put it far higher, though that single source is disputed. The range is wide because the government that did the killing has spent more than thirty-five years making sure no one could count.

Some people tried anyway, that very night. Nicholas Kristof was Beijing bureau chief for The New York Times in 1989, and when the shooting on the Avenue of Eternal Peace drove him back, he ran to a nearby hospital to count corpses. In the days that followed, he and his wife and fellow correspondent, Sheryl WuDunn, cultivated sources in hospitals and morgues, slipping into one through a tunnel from the building next door. Their best estimate, which he still holds, is that between four and eight hundred people were killed and several thousand injured. Writing about it again this autumn, he added a line this series knows from the inside, having caught its own author misremembering at 1,963: “Victims lie or, more charitably, misremember.” The stories that spread afterwards, of machine guns mounted in the square and tanks rolling over tents with students inside, were not true. Perpetrators hide the dead; the grieving sometimes inflate them; an honest count has to check both. What happened was terrible enough without the embellishments. And the bravest people there that night, he wrote, were not the reporters, but the rickshaw drivers who pedalled into the gunfire between volleys to carry the wounded and the dead to hospital.

And so, once again, the work of counting has fallen to the families. A group of parents who lost children that night, who became known as the Tiananmen Mothers, spent years gathering the names of the dead, one by one, at real risk to themselves — a list of a couple of hundred names, painstakingly verified, assembled against the full weight of a state that wanted the number to be zero. It is the work I wrote about at essay 1,942: the honest count as the act of remembrance, the number turned back into names. In 1989 one wall fell because a crowd saw something on a screen. In Beijing, a government made sure that what happened in the square would never appear on any screen in the country at all.

I have stood in that square. It was a few years before the Beijing Olympics, and what I remember first is the size of it: a paved plain so vast that the people crossing it looked like punctuation. At the south end, Mao lies in his mausoleum, facing north towards the gate of the Forbidden City, where his portrait still hangs. Along one side stands the Great Hall of the People, and facing it across the square is the national museum, which I toured. Afterwards I walked through the Forbidden City. It was an eerie feeling, and for a long time I could not have told you why. It was not anything I heard. It was what I didn’t hear that was so loud. Nobody mentioned it. A square that size is built to hold history, and it holds a great deal of it, but there is no marker anywhere for the one night the rest of the world knows it by. No plaque, no date, nothing set into the paving. That is what a forbidden count looks like from the inside. It is not a lie you can catch. It is a blank you can walk across.

December 6

And a short coda, in a lower voice, because the last month of the year belongs to my own country, and I would not feel right closing 1989 without it.

On the sixth of December, 1989, at the École Polytechnique in Montreal, a gunman walked into an engineering classroom, separated the women from the men, and began shooting the women. Fourteen women were killed that afternoon, because they were women, and because they were studying to be engineers. I will not write his name. Theirs are read aloud every year, on the sixth of December, which Canada now marks as a national day of remembrance and action on violence against women. Fourteen names. It is the shortest list in this essay and the one my country knows by heart. The count is kept, every year, on purpose, so that the number never becomes only a number.

The closer

So 1989 is the year two walls came down: one between computers, by a proposal marked vague but exciting, and one between halves of a continent, by a spokesman who misread a date. It is the year the screen became the place where the world reads itself — and the year a government showed how a screen can be kept dark. It ends, in my country, with fourteen names.

The count moves on, twelve years, but it does not get there directly. On the way it passes two numbers that belong to another series. Essay 1,999 is Ollie’s Ark, a play in one act about a tech oligarch bound for Mars with two elephants and a seat for one. Essay 2,000 is my annual performance review, written, for once, by my collaborator, who did not go easy on me. Both are Sunday Satires, and both are meant to make you laugh, so please do. After them, I must tell you plainly where this series goes next, as it always does before its gravest numbers. The world that the open gates of 1989 promised turned out to be more fragile than the crowd at Bornholmer Strasse believed. The next landmark is essay 2,001, and it is the only catastrophe in this whole series whose name is simply a number. I will write it the way this series writes the gravest entries, and I will begin it, as I began 1,945, with a sentence the series wrote for it long ago.

The Numbers Game is an occasional series in which the essay count lands on a year that already means something — a conquest, a charter, a departure — and the author stops whatever he was doing to write about it. Some entries are satire. Some aren’t satire at all. A few are the most personal things he writes. This one is mostly the first kind, with a little of the second, and a quiet minute of the third at the end.
