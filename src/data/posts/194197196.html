The case that dismantled a century of campaign finance law began with a film that almost nobody watched.

“Hillary: The Movie” was a ninety-minute documentary produced by Citizens United, a conservative nonprofit organization, in 2008. It was, by any reasonable critical assessment, not a good film. It was an extended attack on Hillary Clinton’s character, record, and fitness for the presidency, assembled from interviews with conservative commentators and presented with the production values of a mid-budget political advertisement that had been stretched, somewhat arbitrarily, to feature length. Its thesis — that Hillary Clinton was dangerous, dishonest, and unsuitable for the presidency — was not a thesis so much as a premise, stated repeatedly and illustrated with selective evidence.

Citizens United wanted to distribute the film on cable television’s video-on-demand platforms and run advertisements promoting it during the 2008 Democratic primary season. The Federal Election Commission ruled that the film and its advertisements constituted electioneering communications under McCain-Feingold and that Citizens United could not use its corporate treasury funds to pay for them. Citizens United sued.

The case that followed — Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission — was decided by the Supreme Court on January 21, 2010. It was the most consequential campaign finance ruling since Buckley v. Valeo. It was, in the judgment of most campaign finance scholars, considerably more consequential than Buckley. And it was, in a detail that its critics found either ironic or entirely fitting, decided on a much broader basis than the narrow question the case actually presented.

The Court did not have to reach far to decide Citizens United. The question before it, as originally framed, was specific: was this particular documentary, distributed in this particular way, covered by McCain-Feingold’s electioneering communications provision? A majority of the justices could have answered that question — in either direction — without touching the broader constitutional questions that the case ultimately decided.

They chose not to answer the narrow question.

In June 2009, after the initial argument, the Court ordered the case reargued in September on a broader question: whether Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce — a 1990 precedent that had upheld restrictions on corporate independent expenditures — should be overruled, and whether the relevant portion of McCain-Feingold should be struck down entirely.

This was the Roberts Court reaching for a larger prize. The narrow question was whether Citizens United could distribute its film. The broader question was whether corporations could ever be prevented from spending their treasury funds on political advocacy. The Court had decided, before hearing the reargument, that it wanted to answer the broader question. The film was the occasion. The First Amendment was the instrument. The target was the entire framework of restrictions on corporate political spending that had existed, in various forms, since the Tillman Act of 1907.

Citizens United v. FEC — decided January 21, 2010. The case that began as a narrow question about one film became the vehicle for overruling a century of precedent.

The decision, handed down 5-4, was written by Justice Anthony Kennedy.

Its central holding was this: the government may not prohibit political speech on the basis of the speaker’s corporate identity. Corporations, like individuals, have First Amendment rights. The government’s interest in preventing corruption justifies contribution limits — direct donations to candidates. It does not justify restrictions on independent expenditures — money spent separately from any candidate’s campaign. Because independent spending cannot, by definition, involve a direct transaction with a candidate, it cannot constitute the quid pro quo corruption that is the government’s only legitimate interest. Therefore restrictions on corporate independent expenditures are unconstitutional.

Austin was overruled. The relevant portion of McCain-Feingold was struck down. A century of legal tradition — the Tillman Act, the Taft-Hartley Act, the Federal Election Campaign Act, all of them resting on the principle that corporate political spending posed a distinctive threat to democratic integrity — was declared unconstitutional in a single opinion.

The majority offered two assurances that were meant to limit the damage.

First: independent spending cannot corrupt, because corruption requires a transaction, and independence forecloses a transaction. The assumption embedded in this assurance — that super PACs and similar organizations would actually operate independently of the candidates they supported — was, as we will examine in the next essay, empirically false within a single election cycle.

Second: existing disclosure requirements would ensure transparency. Voters would know who was spending what, and could evaluate the messages accordingly. This assurance was also false, for reasons that the Court apparently did not anticipate and that the regulatory apparatus was not equipped to prevent.

Both assumptions were wrong. The decision stood.

Justice John Paul Stevens, age 89, authored the 90-page dissent — one of the most thorough warnings in Supreme Court history about the dangers of treating corporations as people, the narrow definition of corruption, and the coming era of dark money.

Justice John Paul Stevens was eighty-nine years old when he wrote his dissent in Citizens United. He had served on the Court since 1975, appointed by Gerald Ford one year before Buckley v. Valeo was decided. He had watched the campaign finance system evolve for thirty-five years. He had, in that time, developed views about money and politics and corporations and democracy that were precise, well-documented, and almost entirely absent from the majority opinion.

His dissent runs to ninety pages. It is one of the most comprehensive documents in the history of American campaign finance law, and it is worth engaging with seriously rather than simply citing as an authority, because its arguments were not merely legal. They were empirical, historical, and, in retrospect, predictive.

Stevens argued that the majority had fundamentally misunderstood the nature of corporations. Corporations are not people. They are legal fictions — entities created by law, granted specific powers and protections for specific purposes, with no independent existence outside the legal framework that defines them. To say that a corporation has First Amendment rights equivalent to those of a natural person is to confuse the creation with the creator. The rights belong to the people who own and operate corporations, not to the corporations themselves.

He argued that the majority’s definition of corruption — limited to explicit quid pro quo transactions — was artificially narrow. Corruption, in the real world of politics, operates through relationships, expectations, and mutual understandings that rarely require explicit statement. The billionaire who spends $50 million electing a senator has not necessarily handed that senator a check with conditions attached. But the senator knows who spent the money. The senator knows what that person wants. The senator knows what happens to senators who disappoint their largest supporters. This is corruption in the functional sense even if it is not corruption in the transactional sense, and a legal framework that recognizes only the transactional kind is a framework that permits the functional kind without limit.

He argued that the majority’s faith in disclosure was misplaced. The campaign finance system already had significant disclosure gaps, and the decision would create new vehicles for political spending that would exploit those gaps systematically. Dark money, in other words — though Stevens did not use that phrase — was a foreseeable consequence of the decision, and the majority’s assurance that disclosure would protect against it was, at best, optimistic.

Stevens retired four months after Citizens United was decided. He later said, in interviews and in his memoir, that the decision was the one he most regretted not being able to prevent. He had been in the minority. He had written the map. Nobody in a position to follow it did.

The immediate reaction to Citizens United sorted along predictable lines. President Obama, in his State of the Union address delivered six days after the ruling, criticized the decision with the justices sitting in front of him — an unusual breach of the separation of powers theater that normally governs such occasions. Justice Alito, who had been part of the majority, was visibly seen mouthing the words “not true” in response.

President Obama criticizes the ruling in his 2010 State of the Union address, with the justices seated directly in front of him — an extraordinary public rebuke.

The exchange was revealing. Obama said the decision would “open the floodgates for special interests — including foreign corporations — to spend without limit in our elections.” Alito disagreed. The subsequent ten years of election spending suggests that Obama was closer to correct, with the caveat that the floodgates opened primarily for domestic billionaires and corporations rather than foreign ones, though the dark money opacity made it difficult to be certain even about that.

Mitch McConnell, who had spent a decade fighting campaign finance reform, called the decision “a great day for the First Amendment.” He was not wrong that the decision expanded First Amendment protections. He was, in the judgment of anyone who has watched the subsequent decade of American elections, somewhat selective in his assessment of what those expanded protections had produced.

What is most striking about Citizens United, viewed from a distance of sixteen years, is not the decision itself but the confidence of its assumptions. The majority genuinely believed — or at least genuinely argued — that independent spending could not corrupt, and that disclosure would be sufficient to protect against whatever influence the new landscape might generate. These were not cynical arguments. They were arguments made by justices who thought they understood how political spending worked.

They did not understand how political spending worked.

Within two election cycles, super PACs were spending billions. Single-candidate super PACs — organizations devoted entirely to electing one specific person — were being run by that person’s former campaign staff. Candidates were uploading footage to YouTube so their super PACs could use it in advertising without technically coordinating. The dark money infrastructure was routing contributions through layers of nonprofit organizations to make tracing impossible. The disclosure requirements that the majority had relied upon as a check on abuse were being systematically circumvented through mechanisms that the FEC lacked both the authority and the appetite to stop.

The majority had assumed good faith. The political money system had no mechanism for requiring good faith, and no shortage of people willing to operate without it.
