Jeanette Jerome — Jennie, as she was known for the whole of her life — was born in Brooklyn on January 9, 1854, the eldest of Leonard and Clara Jerome’s surviving daughters, and by the time she was nineteen years old she had already been shaped by the consequences of her father’s Trieste posting more thoroughly than by anything American at all. The family had relocated to Paris in the 1860s, in keeping with Clara’s post-Trieste enthusiasm for European society, and Jennie was educated there until the Franco-Prussian War forced the Jeromes to relocate again, this time to England. It was in England, at a sailing regatta on the Isle of Wight during Cowes Week, on August 12, 1873, that she was introduced — at a reception hosted by the Prince of Wales, the future Edward VII — to Lord Randolph Henry Spencer-Churchill, the second son of the 7th Duke of Marlborough.

The courtship that followed was, by the standards of Victorian aristocratic engagements, indecently fast. Winston Churchill himself later described it, in his memoir “My Early Life,” as love at first sight followed within months by marriage — and the record largely bears this out. Randolph proposed within days of the initial meeting. What followed the proposal was not romance but negotiation, and it is here that the transactional character of the marriage becomes impossible to soften. The Duke of Marlborough objected strenuously to an American daughter-in-law, not on grounds of Jennie’s character, which by every account was formidable, but on the straightforward grounds that his family needed capital and an American commoner’s dowry was an unglamorous way to acquire it, however necessary. Leonard Jerome, for his part, had recently suffered financial reversals of his own, and the negotiations over the settlement were, in the words of one biographer, “ungentlemanly” — a genteel way of saying that both fathers spent months arguing over money before either side would let the wedding proceed. The final terms settled a dowry of £50,000 — roughly $250,000 at the time — on the couple, with the specific stipulation, inserted at Jerome’s insistence, that £1,000 a year of it be paid directly to Jennie rather than absorbed into Randolph’s general finances. That single clause tells you a great deal about what Leonard Jerome had already learned, watching his own fortunes rise and fall on Wall Street, about what happens to money that isn’t ring-fenced.

The Duke also required, as a condition of his consent, that Randolph actually stand for a parliamentary seat before the marriage could go forward — evidently unwilling to hand over his family’s future daughter-in-law to a son who had not yet demonstrated he could hold public office. Randolph obliged, contesting the family borough of Woodstock, which included Blenheim Palace itself, in the general election of February 1874, and won by a margin of only 165 votes. Two months later, on April 15, 1874, Randolph and Jennie were married at the British Embassy in Paris — not in England, and not with the Duke or Duchess in attendance, a detail that speaks to just how contested the match had been right up to the ceremony itself.

Winston was born seven and a half months later, on November 30, 1874, at Blenheim Palace, delivered prematurely after Jennie, out on a shooting party, went into sudden labor. The premature timing fueled speculation for more than a century about whether the child had, in fact, been conceived before the wedding — a question Churchill’s own biographers have generally treated as unresolvable and, more to the point, beside it. What mattered more than the exact arithmetic of the pregnancy was the environment Winston was born into: a marriage six weeks old, between two people who had known each other for roughly a year, financed by a transatlantic dowry negotiation, in a family that had not wanted the match and had only consented to it under considerable financial duress. It is not a scandalous origin story by the standards of the aristocracy of the period. It is, however, a considerably less romantic one than the “love at first sight” framing that both Winston and later admirers, Scaramucci among them, have preferred.

Jennie was not alone in making this particular bargain. Her two sisters, Clara and Leonie, crossed the Atlantic alongside her in the 1860s and married into English and Irish society within a few years of Jennie’s own wedding, and the contrast between the three marriages is instructive for anyone tempted to read Jennie’s story as either uniquely fortunate or uniquely difficult. Clara married an English adventurer named Moreton Frewen in 1881, a man so reliably unsuccessful in business that his own relatives called him “Mortal Ruin” — and Clara spent much of her married life managing the financial consequences of his optimism in a way that made Jennie’s own struggles with Randolph’s declining fortunes look, by comparison, almost orderly. Leonie married into the Leslies, an Anglo-Irish landholding family, over her new in-laws’ initial disapproval, and built what appears, from the surviving correspondence between the sisters, to have been the most conventionally stable of the three marriages. Jennie’s own match, for all its early friction with the Marlborough family, produced by far the most historically consequential outcome of the three — which is either evidence that Leonard Jerome’s parenting produced one genuinely well-matched marriage among three gambles, or evidence that history simply remembers whichever gamble happens to produce a wartime prime minister and quietly forgets the other two. I suspect it is closer to the latter.

It is worth dwelling for a moment on precisely what Jennie was walking into at Blenheim Palace itself, because the physical scale of the place tends to get lost in accounts that treat the marriage purely as a social and financial transaction. Blenheim was, and remains, one of the largest private houses in England, built originally for the 1st Duke of Marlborough in the early eighteenth century as a gift from a grateful nation after his military victories over the French — a palace, not a country house, with room enough that an entire wing could be given over to a young couple without either the Duke or Duchess needing to alter their own daily routine in the slightest. Jennie arrived there in the spring of 1874 as a twenty-year-old American who had spent most of her adolescence in Paris, married into a family that had resisted the match for the better part of a year, and installed in a household run by a formidable mother-in-law who had every reason to regard her new daughter-in-law’s American money more warmly than her American manners. The couple’s arrival at Blenheim six weeks after the wedding was, by contemporary accounts, warmly received by the local tenantry and townspeople of Woodstock — the church bells reportedly rang late into the evening the night Winston was born there that November — but warmth from the tenants was not the same as warmth from the family itself, and Jennie spent much of her early married life navigating a household that had accepted her dowry considerably more readily than it had accepted her.

What followed for Jennie was not an easy marriage. She and Randolph settled initially at Blenheim, where she was, by her own later account, largely confined to the company of her mother-in-law while Randolph pursued his rapidly ascending parliamentary career — a career we will examine in full in Part 4, and one that would consume nearly all of Randolph’s attention and, eventually, ruin him. Jennie was, by the standards of a Victorian aristocratic wife, an indifferent and often absent mother; she handed both Winston and his younger brother Jack to nannies and governesses with a consistency that Winston himself would later write about with a mixture of understanding and evident, lifelong hurt. His actual maternal attachment, by his own account, was to his nanny, Elizabeth Everest, whose death in 1895 he described as one of the genuine emotional catastrophes of his young adulthood — considerably more felt, in his own retelling, than the death of either of his actual parents.

It would also be a mistake to read Jennie only through the lens of an absent mother in an unhappy marriage, because it flattens a woman who, once released from the immediate constraints of that marriage, demonstrated real ambition and real competence in her own right. After Randolph’s death in 1895, Jennie became an active political operator on her son’s behalf, campaigning for him during his early parliamentary contests and using her still-considerable social connections on both sides of the Atlantic to advance his career in ways that went well beyond conventional maternal support. She founded and edited a literary quarterly, the Anglo-Saxon Review, in 1899 — an ambitious, expensively produced publication that folded within two years but that nonetheless marked her as a woman with intellectual and entrepreneurial appetites of her own, not merely a vessel for dowry money and heirs. During the Second Boer War, she helped organize and personally oversaw the fitting-out of a hospital ship, the Maine, funded largely by American donations she solicited herself, and sailed with it to South Africa in December 1899 — a piece of genuinely hands-on wartime service that predates, by four decades, the more famous wartime contributions of her son. It is easy, writing a series organized around inheritance, to reduce every family member to what they passed down to the next generation. Jennie’s own record, considered on its own terms rather than as a way station between her father’s money and her son’s fame, was considerably more active than the popular “society beauty” caricature usually allows.

None of this is offered to indict Jennie personally. She was navigating a role — American money married into English title, expected to produce heirs and manage a household while contributing nothing of substance to the actual decisions that governed her life — that offered her very little room to be anything other than what she became: a woman whose considerable intelligence and social skill were spent almost entirely on maintaining appearances and managing a husband whose political career would ultimately collapse under its own miscalculations. Jennie remarried twice after Randolph’s death in 1895 — first to George Cornwallis-West in 1900, a man twenty years her junior, and later to Montagu Porch in 1918 — and she died in London on June 29, 1921, having outlived Randolph by twenty-six years and having spent much of that time, by all accounts, rather more freely and rather more happily than she had spent her first marriage.

The inheritance Jennie passed to Winston is harder to pin down than Leonard Jerome’s temperamental gift for recovery, but it is there in the record all the same: an early and thorough lesson in the gap between how a family’s public arrangement is described — a love match, a merger of two great nations in miniature, the “special relationship” before the phrase existed — and how it actually functioned day to day, which was considerably more transactional, considerably lonelier, and considerably more dependent on money changing hands than the official version ever let on. Scaramucci’s essay calls the alliance a bet on character. Jennie’s marriage was also, quite literally, underwritten by a settlement document with specific numbers attached to it. Both things were true of the family relationship, and both things, I would argue, ended up true of the national one it later came to symbolize.

There is one further thread worth pulling before this part closes, because it recurs at nearly every generation of this series: the question of what happens to the women who marry into or are born into a family whose public story belongs almost entirely to its men. Jennie’s own writing, late in her life, shows a woman keenly aware of exactly this dynamic — aware that history would remember her, if it remembered her at all, as Winston’s mother and Randolph’s widow, rather than as the woman who ran a hospital ship, edited a literary review, and navigated two further marriages on her own terms after the first one ended in ruin and grief. Mary Soames, Jennie’s granddaughter-in-law by a different line and this family’s most dedicated internal historian, would later devote much of her own career to correcting exactly this kind of erasure — a project we will examine properly in Part 12. For now, it is enough to note that the pattern started with Jennie, continued through Clementine, and has not, by the evidence available in this series’ final movement, entirely ended yet.
