Postmark: Sunnyvale, California. Tokyo, Japan. A hospital room. A nursing home corridor at dusk.

Surgeon at the da Vinci console: hands move in their natural range, the tremor is filtered out, and what remains is scaled for precision inside the patient

The human hand is a miracle of evolution. Twenty-seven bones. Thirty-four muscles. More than a hundred ligaments and tendons. An interface so sensitive it can read the texture of a surface through a thin rubber glove, so dexterous it can thread a needle by feel, so strong it can grip a rope and hold a person’s weight.

It also trembles. Not perceptibly — not to the naked eye — but at the microscale level where a surgeon works, the human hand oscillates continuously, driven by the involuntary firing of motor neurons, the rhythm of the heartbeat, the accumulated fatigue of hours at the operating table. A senior neurosurgeon with thirty years of experience and hands that look absolutely steady is still producing motion artifacts in the two-to-eight hertz range during the finest parts of a procedure. That tremor cannot be trained away. It is physiological. It is human.

The da Vinci surgical system filters it out entirely.

At the console, the surgeon’s hands move in their natural range. The software interprets those movements, scales them down, filters out the tremor frequency, and translates what remains into the precise, tremor-free motion of instruments one centimetre across operating inside a human body through incisions smaller than a keyhole. What the surgeon intends to do and what the instruments actually do are the same thing — without the noise that the human nervous system cannot help introducing.

This is not a robot performing surgery. The da Vinci system is not autonomous. Every movement originates with a human surgeon at a console. But it is the most sophisticated tool ever placed between a surgeon’s intention and a patient’s body, and it has fundamentally changed what surgery is.

Seventeen Million Procedures

Intuitive Surgical launched the first commercially available da Vinci system in 2000, receiving FDA clearance as the first robotic surgical platform in history. Twenty-five years later, surgeons have used da Vinci systems in nearly seventeen million procedures worldwide. In 2024 alone, more than 2.68 million procedures were performed using the platform — a seventeen percent increase over the previous year. In 2025, that number grew by a further eighteen percent. More than 9,900 systems are installed across hospitals worldwide. More than 90,000 surgeons have been trained to use them.

The scale of adoption is remarkable not because robots performing surgery is new — the da Vinci system has been in operating theatres for a quarter century — but because the rate of adoption is accelerating. Hospitals that installed their first system a decade ago are now deploying their third or fourth. Procedures that were once performed robotically only in specialist centres are now standard practice in regional hospitals. The technology that was a curiosity in 2000 is an expectation in 2026.

The da Vinci 5, launched in 2024, represents the latest generation. It includes more than 150 enhancements over its predecessor, among them force feedback technology — the ability for the surgeon to feel resistance in the instruments they are controlling, approximating the sense of touch that conventional robotic surgery cannot provide. A 2024 pre-clinical study showed that surgeons using force feedback during simulated suturing tasks applied lower forces to tissue, with greater consistency, than those operating without it. The soft robotics problem — giving a machine the sense of touch — is being solved, instrument by instrument, procedure by procedure.

Da Vinci instruments operating through keyhole incisions — tremor-free and scaled for millimetre precision in the confined pelvic cavity.

What the Evidence Shows

The clinical case for robotic surgery is strongest in procedures where confined spaces and millimetre-level precision matter most. Prostate cancer surgery is the canonical example.

Open radical prostatectomy — the traditional approach — requires a large abdominal incision, significant blood loss, substantial post-operative pain, and a hospital stay typically measured in days. Recovery to normal function takes weeks, sometimes months. The operation targets tissue immediately adjacent to nerve bundles that control urinary continence and erectile function, and preserving those bundles requires exactly the kind of precision that fatiguing human hands in a confined pelvic cavity cannot consistently provide.

Robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy changes the equation. The incisions are keyhole-sized. Blood loss is substantially reduced. Hospital stays average one to two nights. The three-dimensional, magnified view and tremor-filtered instrument control that the da Vinci system provides make nerve-sparing dissection not just possible but reproducible — achievable by a skilled surgeon in good conditions, rather than by the most exceptional surgeon in ideal conditions. Long-term follow-up studies have confirmed that cancer-specific mortality at eight years was lower in the robotic group, particularly among high-risk patients, while functional outcomes for continence and erectile function were meaningfully better than open surgery comparisons.

The limitation — and it is a real one — is that the da Vinci system is not a surgeon. It does not make decisions. It does not recognize unexpected anatomy. It cannot adapt in real time to a complication it has not been programmed to anticipate. The surgeon remains essential. What the machine provides is not intelligence but precision — an extension of the surgeon’s intent, faithful to that intent in ways the human hand alone cannot be.

That distinction matters as robotic surgery extends into more complex procedures and less experienced hands. The outcomes data from centres of excellence do not automatically transfer to community hospitals. The learning curve is real. The equipment costs between half a million and two million dollars per system, with recurring instrument and maintenance costs that make robotic surgery economically significant even before it is clinically significant. Access is unequal, globally and domestically. These are not arguments against the technology. They are arguments for honest accounting of what it is and what it is not.

The Competition Arrives

For twenty-five years, Intuitive Surgical operated in surgical robotics with essentially no serious competition. The da Vinci system was first to market, first to scale, and so thoroughly embedded in hospital capital expenditure plans and surgeon training programs that switching costs were prohibitive. A hospital that has trained its surgeons, bought its instruments, and built its workflow around da Vinci does not casually adopt a competitor platform.

That monopoly is ending. In late 2024 and 2025, Medtronic’s Hugo soft tissue robotic system, CMR Surgical’s Versius Plus, and SS Innovations International’s SSi Mantra system all received FDA clearance, entering the US market directly against da Vinci for the first time with meaningful commercial backing. Johnson & Johnson’s Ottava system is in late-stage development. The surgical robotics market, valued at roughly $18 billion in 2024, is projected to reach $83 billion by 2032 — growth that will be driven not by da Vinci alone but by a competitive field that is finally, after a quarter century, genuinely competitive.

For patients, competition means lower prices, broader access, and faster innovation. For hospitals, it means genuine procurement choices. For the field as a whole, it means that surgical robotics is no longer the story of one company’s long dominance — it is the story of a technology reaching the stage where the market opens and the real work of dissemination begins.

The Other Theatre

An elderly resident interacts with PARO, the therapeutic baby harp seal designed for companionship in elder care settings.

The operating room is not the only place where robots are changing what medicine looks like.

In a quiet nursing home in Tokyo’s Setagaya ward, a different kind of robot makes its rounds. PARO is a therapeutic robot in the shape of a baby harp seal. It is approximately the size of a real seal pup — soft, white-furred, with large dark eyes and whiskers that respond to touch. When stroked, it responds with sounds and movements. When spoken to, it turns toward the voice. It cannot speak. It cannot diagnose or treat. It does not perform any clinical procedure.

What PARO does is keep people company.

Japan arrived at PARO through necessity. The country has the oldest population in the world — 28.7 percent of citizens are aged 65 or older, a proportion that continues to rise. By 2025, 17.5 percent of the population was aged 75 or older. The nursing sector has one applicant for every 4.25 available jobs. The projected shortfall of care workers reached 380,000 by 2025. Social security expenditures related to aging — pensions, medical care, long-term care — account for roughly one-third of Japan’s entire public expenditure. The mathematics of the crisis are not complicated: there are not enough human caregivers for the number of elderly people who need care, and the gap is widening faster than training programs, wages, or immigration policy can close it.

Japan has tried both approaches simultaneously. In 2019 it passed immigration legislation allowing visas for foreign workers in elder care, reversing decades of restrictive policy. In the first year, 60,000 visas were granted. The number barely touched the shortfall. Robotics, to many Japanese policymakers, is not an alternative to human caregivers but an acknowledgment that human caregivers alone will not be enough.

PARO was developed by Japan’s National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology over more than two decades. It is classified as a medical device in Japan and the United States. Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated clinically meaningful outcomes: reduced depression and loneliness in dementia patients, lower caregiver burden in facilities using the robot regularly, reduced anxiety and, in some studies, reduced need for psychotropic and analgesic medications. An eight-week intervention study found significant improvements in depression and loneliness, with effects that persisted four weeks after the intervention ended.

Classified as a medical device in Japan and the US, PARO has been shown in randomized trials to ease depression, loneliness, and anxiety in dementia patients.

These are real results. They are also results that require careful interpretation. PARO does not cure dementia. It does not replace human contact — the same studies that show PARO’s benefits also show that the residents who respond most strongly to the robot are those with more severe cognitive impairment, for whom the distinction between a robot and a living animal may be less legible. The ethical question — whether it is appropriate to provide something that simulates companionship as a substitute for companionship — is one that the clinical literature acknowledges but does not resolve. Japan has largely decided the question pragmatically: given a choice between a robot seal and nothing, a robot seal is better than nothing. Whether that framing is acceptable depends on whether you believe “better than nothing” is the right standard for the care of elderly people with dementia.

The Exoskeleton Problem

Between the operating theatre and the nursing home lies a third territory: rehabilitation.

Every year, millions of people survive strokes, spinal cord injuries, and other neurological events that leave them with impaired movement. The traditional rehabilitation pathway is labour-intensive and progress-dependent — a therapist works with the patient, hour by hour, session by session, helping them relearn movement patterns through repetition and correction. Progress is slow. Therapist time is expensive. The number of sessions a patient can access is limited by cost and availability. The gap between what optimal rehabilitation looks like and what most patients can access is wide.

Robotic exoskeletons address part of that gap. Devices like Ekso Bionics’ EksoGT and ReWalk Robotics’ ReWalk system are wearable robotic frameworks that support the weight of the lower body and assist with the mechanics of walking, allowing patients with spinal cord injuries or stroke-related weakness to stand, step, and move through rehabilitation exercises with a consistency and volume that human-only therapy cannot match. The robot does not make the patient walk — it creates the conditions in which the patient’s own nervous system can relearn the pattern of walking through repetition. The machine provides the scaffolding. The patient provides the effort.

A robotic exoskeleton assists gait training for stroke and spinal cord injury — providing the consistent repetition human therapists alone cannot match. The machine provides the scaffolding; the patient provides the effort

The outcomes data is promising. Multiple studies have found that exoskeleton-assisted therapy produces measurable improvements in walking speed, stride length, and balance in stroke patients compared to conventional therapy alone. For spinal cord injury patients, even incomplete restoration of function represents an enormous quality-of-life improvement — the difference between standing for a conversation and spending that conversation in a wheelchair.

The limitation here is again cost. Exoskeleton systems currently price between $70,000 and $150,000, placing them well beyond the reach of most rehabilitation facilities and entirely beyond the reach of individual patients. That cost is falling — the manufacturing economics of any technology improve with scale and time — but not fast enough to make exoskeleton rehabilitation standard care in the near term. The technology works. The distribution problem has not been solved.

Soft robotic rehabilitation offers a partial alternative. Wearable gloves and sleeves made from soft materials rather than rigid exoskeleton frames can assist hand and arm rehabilitation at a fraction of the cost, targeting the upper-body motor function that stroke often impairs most. Research groups at Harvard’s Wyss Institute and elsewhere have demonstrated that soft robotic rehabilitation garments can improve grip strength and range of motion in stroke patients through home-based programs, without the clinical supervision that exoskeleton systems typically require. The geography of access — who can reach a specialist rehabilitation centre and who cannot — remains a constraint that soft robotics begins to address.

The Question the Theatre Raises

The operating theatre, the nursing home corridor, and the rehabilitation ward all share a common feature that distinguishes the From the Theatre postcards from all the others: the stakes are personal in a way that the factory floor is not.

A robot that picks strawberries less efficiently than a human picker is an economic problem. A robot that assists in surgery less effectively than a skilled surgeon is a patient’s life. A robot that provides companionship to a person with dementia in lieu of human contact is a question about what we owe the vulnerable. The proximity to the human body — to pain, to isolation, to death — makes every design choice in healthcare robotics an ethical choice simultaneously.

And yet the alternative to engaging with these technologies is not a world of purely human medicine. It is a world of insufficient medicine — of surgical queues that stretch too long, of elder care facilities with too few staff, of rehabilitation programs cut short by cost. The question is not whether robots belong in healthcare. They are already there, and they are already helping. The question is how to deploy them equitably, govern them responsibly, and ensure that the drive to scale does not outpace the evidence for safety.

This is not a reason to move slowly. The costs of not deploying these technologies are also human costs — the prostate cancer patient who undergoes open surgery when robotic assistance was available but inaccessible, the dementia patient who spends her days in silence when PARO might have given her something to hold, the stroke survivor who receives twelve sessions of rehabilitation when twenty-four would have made the difference.

The Asimov Gap opens widest here. The question of what rules should govern machines that touch the human body — that assist in cutting it open, that keep it company in its most vulnerable moments, that help it relearn how to walk — is not a theoretical governance question. It is a practical and urgent one, playing out in hospital procurement committees and clinical trial designs and insurance reimbursement decisions and regulatory frameworks that were written for a world that did not have these machines.

The hands that never shake are already in the operating room. The seal that never leaves is already in the nursing home. The exoskeleton that never tires is already in the rehabilitation ward. The question is not whether they belong there. The question is what we owe the people they are there to serve.
