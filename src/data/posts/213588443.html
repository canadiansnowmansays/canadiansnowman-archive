Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word essay in August arguing that leading AI labs were consolidating power behind a narrative of “doom,” warning — without naming Anthropic or its chief executive, Dario Amodei — that if such labs kept leading, power would tilt toward large institutions rather than individuals. As The New York Times’ Eli Tan and Kalley Huang reported on August 27, 2026, the essay landed at almost exactly the moment internal Meta projections showed the company could spend as much as $10 billion a year on Anthropic’s own AI models — a sum that would have represented a meaningful share of the roughly $65 billion in annual run-rate revenue Anthropic itself estimated in July.

That contradiction is the whole story, and this series should resist the temptation to flatten it into simple hypocrisy. It’s stranger and more interesting than that.

Mr. Zuckerberg returned to the theme within weeks. After Mr. Amodei called for the industry to slow frontier development, a call that drew support from Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis, Mr. Zuckerberg answered on X. Benzinga reported on September 15, 2026 that he wrote that “every lab has the responsibility and incentive to move at the pace required to train its models safely,” that Meta had delayed its own Muse agent by months for safety work, and that “we didn’t call for everyone else to do this before we would. We just did it as part of our day-to-day work.” His closing argument was that “any lab that doesn’t focus on alignment will fall behind.” The jab was aimed at Anthropic’s public posture. It was published by the chief executive of a company whose engineers were, by the Times’ account, among Anthropic’s heaviest users.

The frenemy problem is structural, not personal

Meta and Anthropic are far from a unique case. Google and Amazon have together committed $73 billion to Anthropic while building their own competing models in-house. Microsoft was one of OpenAI’s earliest and largest backers and now goes out of its way to advertise its independence from the company it helped fund. Nvidia runs multibillion-dollar partnerships with both Meta and Google even as each works to design its own chips and reduce Nvidia dependency. As Tomasz Tunguz of Theory Ventures put it, watching this ecosystem is like watching dueling generals share a supply line — everyone is simultaneously trying to win the war and keep the other side’s factories running, because right now they need the output more than they need the win.

What makes Meta’s version of this pattern worth its own essay is the specificity of what it was doing with Anthropic’s tools, and how directly that use fed into the product now positioned to make Anthropic’s tools unnecessary.

Hatch, tested on the rival it’s meant to replace

Meta has used Anthropic’s models to power and test Hatch, the “24/7” personal AI agent Mr. Zuckerberg has described as tasked with improving a user’s health, relationships, and finances. According to the Times’ reporting, the version of Hatch that eventually launches publicly will run on Meta’s own model rather than Anthropic’s — meaning Anthropic’s technology served, functionally, as the scaffolding Meta built its own competing product on top of before swapping the foundation out. Nat Friedman, Meta’s head of AI product, has reportedly told colleagues that continuing to rely on Anthropic’s or OpenAI’s coding tools rather than Meta’s own could reduce Anthropic’s revenue ahead of a listing that could value the company at roughly $2 trillion — an acknowledgment, from inside Meta, that its own purchasing decisions are material enough to move the valuation of a company it is simultaneously trying to out-compete.

Hatch is also meant to make money directly. Forkast News, summarising reporting by The Information, wrote on August 25, 2026 that Meta was targeting a late-August or early-September launch and had considered a premium tier priced as high as $199.99 a month, with agents that could carry out tasks across services such as DoorDash, Etsy and Yelp from inside WhatsApp. That timetable slipped. Meta did launch a consumer agent, Muse, in early September, and it was downloaded more than five million times in its first month, The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong and Charlie Warzel reported on October 16; the reporting does not say whether Muse is the product developed under the Hatch name.

The “tokenmaxxing” detail captures the scale better than any dollar figure: at the start of 2026, as Meta engineers ramped up their use of Claude Code, internal leaderboards tracked who was burning through the most Anthropic tokens. Costs rose fast enough that Meta pulled the leaderboards in June and told employees it needed a better system for managing the spend — a company literally gamifying its own dependency on a rival’s product before deciding the dependency had become embarrassing to display internally.

The part the Times piece didn’t connect: June’s distillation lockdown

There is an earlier chapter to this story the August 27 piece doesn’t mention, and it changes how the “tokenmaxxing” period should be read. In late June 2026, internal Meta documents obtained by The Information, and reported by trade outlets including The Decoder on June 29, 2026, showed the company’s Applied AI Engineering division instructing engineers to limit or halt certain uses of Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex entirely. The stated concern was distillation: the risk that code, architectural suggestions, and reasoning generated by a rival’s model could work its way, intentionally or by accident, into Meta’s own training data for its Llama model family, exposing the company to both an intellectual-property dispute and a subtler contamination of its own model’s provenance. An internal memo reportedly warned of “serious escalations with partner companies” if this happened. The Next Web added on June 30, 2026 that the worry was sharpest because Meta was building its own in-house coding system, MetaCode, and that Google had separately capped how much Meta could use Gemini models for coding and chatbots, citing capacity constraints. Meta was short of supply from one rival and wary of contamination from two others, all while its own tool was unfinished. Every major lab’s terms of service — Anthropic’s, OpenAI’s, Google’s — explicitly bars using their models’ outputs to train a competing system, and Anthropic has separately and publicly accused several Chinese labs, including DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Alibaba, of doing exactly that at industrial scale through fraudulent accounts.

Put the two stories side by side and Meta’s relationship with Anthropic resolves into something more coherent than plain contradiction: a company restricting one internal division’s engineers from touching Claude’s raw output over contamination fears in June, while other parts of the same company ran up a token bill approaching $10 billion a year using the identical tool for the identical underlying reason — because it was, by every account, simply the best coding tool available. That is not hypocrisy so much as an organisation large enough to hold two incompatible risk assessments simultaneously, in different buildings, answering to different budgets. Meta’s own words to the Times — that it has “clear rules for the responsible use of AI tools” — turn out to be true and largely beside the point; the rules existed, and the spending happened anyway, because the rules were never really designed to stop it.

The circularity thread gets a new entry

This series has spent three parts tracking how AI capital increasingly finances itself in a loop — Nvidia backing OpenAI’s compute purchases in Part III, the Piketon lease guarantee in Part V, Oracle’s leveraged bet in Part VI. Add one more strand: in June 2026, Anthropic reportedly offered to buy up to $10 billion in computing power from Meta’s own data centres over two years. Reuters, citing the Times, reported on July 17, 2026 that Anthropic would make monthly payments over the two-year term, that either side could end the arrangement early, and that the deal would give Meta a way to earn money from its infrastructure beyond advertising, putting it in competition with rental specialists such as CoreWeave and Nebius. As of early October 2026, no deal had been announced. When Reuters reviewed Anthropic’s confidential prospectus in late September, the compute commitments itemised in InvestmentNews’s account of that reporting on September 29, 2026 ran to Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Broadcom, xAI and AMD, and Meta was not among them. If a deal happens, the arrangement would run the circularity this series has documented among chipmakers and hyperscalers through a genuinely new pairing — the AI lab paying its own major customer to rent back the infrastructure that customer built, partly, on the back of the token spending that customer is now trying to wean itself off. Meta, in this scenario, would be simultaneously Anthropic’s landlord, its rival, its former largest buyer, and — through Hatch’s development history — its beneficiary.

What the pattern says about the IPO

None of this changes the arithmetic underlying Anthropic’s public offering, for which it filed on October 26, but it does complicate the story that offering has to tell. A $2 trillion valuation rests partly on revenue growth, and Meta’s pullback — real, if partial, and running alongside a new coding tool, Muse Code, that Meta’s own engineers are increasingly choosing over Anthropic’s — is exactly the kind of customer-concentration risk a prospectus has to disclose and a market has to price. The pullback is now on the record: Meta, one of Anthropic’s largest customers, had projected spending as much as $10 billion a year on its technology “but pulled back as it switched to other A.I. models,” the Times reported in its account of the filing. It was one of the clues the same reporting offered for a sharp slowdown in Anthropic’s growth, from 141 per cent quarter on quarter in the second quarter to 45 per cent in the third, and some investors have since begun discussing a valuation of $1.5 trillion to $1.7 trillion rather than $2 trillion. Anthropic’s confidential prospectus had already disclosed the risk, in general terms. Reuters’ Echo Wang reported on September 28, 2026 that nearly a quarter of Anthropic’s 2025 revenue came from two customers, and that many of its largest clients lack long-term contracts and could cut or stop spending. Reuters did not name the two customers. Meta’s own in-house frontier model, internally called Watermelon, paused and resumed a stage of training in July, which delayed its release. It finally arrived on October 12, as Eli Tan reported for The New York Times: two models, Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, which Meta says score nearly as well as Anthropic’s top models on tests Meta itself supplied, with the Full version reserved for subscribers to Muse Code. Benchmarks a company supplies about its own product are not independent evidence, so Meta’s ability to fully replace what it spends on Anthropic remains unproven. Until it is proven, Meta’s public criticism and private cheque-writing will keep running on parallel tracks, each one true, neither one the whole picture on its own.
