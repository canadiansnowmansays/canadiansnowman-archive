It is generally understood in Elsinore that the swans belong to Elsinore. This understanding is warm, civic, and entirely without legal foundation — a combination that describes a surprising number of the things that hold small Ontario cities together, and which nobody examines too closely for the same reason you do not examine the structural integrity of a chair you have been sitting in comfortably for forty years.

The swans arrived in the 1970s, when the city acquired a breeding pair as a contribution to the Elizabethan theme, which was a contribution to the Festival, which was the reason the Elizabethan theme existed in the first place. The city paid for them. The city provided the river. The city built the Elizabethan Gardens along the south bank and positioned the benches at the angles someone determined, thirty years ago, were correct. The swans arrived, assessed the situation, and decided it was acceptable, which is the closest thing to civic participation that swans engage in and which Elsinore has always chosen to interpret generously.

Their descendants — born free on the Avon, raising their cygnets in the reed beds upstream, proceeding along the river with the composed authority of creatures who have been here longer than anyone on the Tourism and Culture Committee and intend to remain longer still — have been understood, by everyone in Elsinore, to belong to Elsinore in the same way the river belongs to Elsinore and the Festival belongs to Elsinore: completely, obviously, and without the need for documentation.

It had not occurred to anyone to check the documentation until Bradley Fenn joined the Tourism and Culture Committee.

Gerald has known since 2006. The folder has been in the filing system under Swans — Legal Status — Perth County Avon River Elsinore. He was not asked. He is ready.

Bradley had been reviewing the municipality’s asset register in preparation for his efficiency proposals. He did this the way he does everything: thoroughly, systematically, with the particular attention of a man who believes that what is not accounted for is not managed and what is not managed is a problem waiting to announce itself.

The swans were not on the asset register.

He raised this at the April meeting of the Tourism and Culture Committee, on a Tuesday evening, with a slide.

The slide showed Wayne’s quarterly visitor review analysis, which Bradley had obtained from the tourism development files Gerald maintains, and which indicated that the swans appeared in forty-three percent of positive visitor reviews of Elsinore, making them, by Wayne’s own figures, the second most frequently cited positive feature of the visitor experience after the Festival itself and ahead of the Elizabethan Gardens, the Literary River Tour, the Avon Table, and the butter tart coupon from the Perth County Pantry.

If the swans were a tourism asset of this significance, Bradley said, they should appear on the asset register. He asked, specifically, who owned the Elsinore swans.

The room considered this question in the way that rooms consider questions that have never previously been considered, which is with the particular quality of silence that falls when something everyone has always known turns out, upon examination, to be something nobody has verified.

Mayor Doug said he believed the city had always had a very good relationship with the swans and that he didn’t see why that needed to change. He wore his good coat. He looked at the table in front of him in the way of a man who is hoping the question will resolve itself if he gives it sufficient time.

Wayne said he had always assumed the swans were a municipal asset and had treated them as such in his marketing materials, his website, the Literary River Tour laminated route map, and the Gold Level commemorative certificate. He acknowledged that he had not verified this assumption. He said this with the equanimity of a man noting a gap in his research rather than a man experiencing distress, because Wayne’s relationship with unverified assumptions is the relationship of someone who moves quickly and corrects course when necessary and has always found this approach adequate.

Alistair, who attended the committee meetings as the Festival’s representative and who had been looking at the ceiling since Bradley’s slide appeared, said that the question of who owned the swans was, in his view, a category error, and that some things existed outside the logic of ownership, and that the Avon River and the morning light on it were not on the asset register either and nobody had proposed putting them there. He said this in a single complete paragraph and then returned his attention to the ceiling.

Bradley noted that the river and the morning light were not tourism assets in the quantifiable sense that the swans demonstrably were.

Alistair said he was not sure Bradley had been to the river in the morning.

Mayor Doug thanked both of them.

Gerald Fitch had been very still since Bradley raised the question. He had his folder in front of him — the dark blue one, two inches thick, which he brings to committee meetings containing the relevant background documentation for all anticipated agenda items and several items that have not yet been anticipated. He had known the answer to Bradley’s question since 2006, when he had researched it for reasons he has never explained to anyone and filed the results under Swans — Legal Status — Perth County Avon River Elsinore. The folder has been in his filing system for twenty years. It has never been requested.

He was waiting to be asked.

He was not asked. The question was tabled pending research, which in committee language means that someone will be asked to produce a report, which in Elsinore means that Gerald will produce the report, which means that Gerald will open the folder he has had for twenty years and type up what is in it, which he will do at his own pace and which will be ready when it is ready.

He walked home after the meeting. He made a note to himself. He filed the note.

The Swan Warden heard about the meeting from Gerald the following morning in the municipal hall parking lot, both of them arriving for separate reasons at the same moment, as they occasionally do.

She listened to Gerald’s account of the question and the discussion and the tabling.

She nodded.

She said: “I’ve known since 2004.”

She went inside.

Gerald did not ask why she had not raised it in 2004, or in the twenty-two years since, because Gerald does not ask questions he already knows the answers to, and the answer in this case was that the Swan Warden raises things when the raising serves a purpose, and for twenty-two years the question of swan ownership had not required raising because nobody had tried to put the swans on an asset register, and now someone had, and she would raise it at the next meeting, which she did.

The legal position of mute swans in Canada is, in Darlene Potts’ four-page summary, as follows.

In England, unmarked mute swans in open waters belong to the Crown. This is a prerogative right dating to the twelfth century, rooted in the medieval understanding that swans were a valuable food source and that valuable things in open water were, by the logic of the time, royal property. The right has been maintained through the centuries not because anyone particularly needs to eat a swan but because ancient prerogative rights, once established, require active effort to extinguish and no one has ever felt sufficiently strongly about swan ownership to make that effort. There is an annual ceremony on the Thames called Swan Upping, in which the Royal Swan Marker — a position held by a specific person appointed by the Crown — counts and marks the cygnets with the assistance of the Swan Uppers of the Worshipful Company of Vintners and the Worshipful Company of Dyers, who hold historic rights to a share of the Thames swans. This has been happening since the fifteenth century. It will presumably continue.

In Canada, the Crown prerogative over swans was never formally transferred at Confederation and has never been asserted. Canadian mute swans are protected under the Migratory Birds Convention Act, which gives the federal government jurisdiction over their welfare and management but does not establish ownership in any person, institution, or municipality. The original Elsinore swans, acquired by the city in the 1970s and paid for from the municipal budget, might be argued to be municipal property in the way that any purchased animal is property of its purchaser. Their descendants — born free on the Avon, banded under the federal migratory bird program, protected by the federal government but owned by no one — are, in the technical legal sense, nobody’s swans.

The Swan Warden presented this summary at the May committee meeting in the same voice she uses for habitat survey results: without drama, without editorial, with the precision of someone who has been sitting on accurate information for twenty-two years and is now, the moment having arrived, providing it.

She sat down.

The room received this information in the way that rooms receive information that is both completely surprising and, upon reflection, entirely obvious.

Gerald opened his folder. He confirmed that the Swan Warden’s summary was consistent with his own research from 2006. He had the relevant sections of the Migratory Birds Convention Act paper-clipped to the inside cover of the folder, where they had been for twenty years, awaiting this meeting.

Bradley said: so the swans cannot be placed on the asset register.

The Swan Warden said: you cannot put a migratory bird on an asset register.

She said this in the voice she uses for facts that are not subject to committee discussion.

Bradley made a note.

Mayor Doug said he thought the important thing was that Elsinore and the swans had always had a very good arrangement and he didn’t see why that needed to change. He looked genuinely relieved. He mentioned, without being entirely sure why he mentioned it, that King Charles was by all accounts a fine man. He wore his good coat.

Alistair, who had returned his attention from the ceiling to the room upon hearing the Swan Warden’s summary, said that he had always felt the swans’ relationship with Elsinore was the correct one for this latitude and this river and this particular quality of morning light, and that the legal confirmation of their freedom was, if anything, the right outcome. Nobody was entirely sure what this meant. It sounded correct.

Wayne excused himself shortly after the meeting. That evening he removed the swans from the Literary River Tour asset valuation spreadsheet. He created a new column labelled Ambient Assets — Unowned. He entered the swans. He looked at the column for a moment. He considered it one of his better ideas. He saved the file.

Carly had covered the committee meeting for the Beacon. She had sat in the back row with her notebook, writing quickly, and had understood within the first three minutes that the story was not Bradley Fenn’s question but the Swan Warden’s answer — specifically, the fact that the Swan Warden had known since 2004 and had said nothing, and what that meant about how knowledge operates in a small city and who holds it and when they choose to use it.

She wrote the piece in two hours. She filed it to Norman at nine in the evening.

He changed the headline. Her headline was: “Who Owns the Elsinore Swans? Nobody, It Turns Out, and the Swan Warden Has Known Since 2004.” His headline was: “The Swans Belong to No One. This Is Fine.”

She read his headline. She thought it was better than hers. She did not say this. She was learning.

The piece was the most-read thing the Beacon had published since Alistair’s fourteen-hundred-word letter about civic institutions. It was shared forty-seven times. Three of the shares came from South Korea, from people who had been following along since the paddle boat photograph and who had apparently developed a specific interest in the Swan Warden.

Gerald read the piece and confirmed to Carly, when she called to verify, that his research was from 2006. She asked why he had never raised it. He said he had been waiting to be asked. She wrote this down. She put it in the piece. Norman left it in.

Jim read Carly’s piece on Sunday morning. He read it at the kitchen table before his six-fifteen walk, with his coffee, in the particular quiet of early Sunday morning when the street outside has not yet decided what kind of day it is going to be.

He thought about the swans. He thought about the asset register. He thought about the column Wayne had created, Ambient Assets — Unowned, which Carly had included in her piece because Gerald had mentioned it and Carly had called Wayne to verify and Wayne had confirmed it without apparent embarrassment, because Wayne has never been embarrassed by a good idea.

He thought about what it meant for something to be unowned and protected simultaneously. He thought about this in the way he thinks about most things: without announcing it, reaching a conclusion, filing it.

He walked the dogs at six-fifteen. He went past the river. One of the swans was on the south bank, near the second bench, in the April light.

He watched it for a moment.

The swan did not acknowledge him. It was not required to acknowledge him. It belonged to nobody and went where it pleased, which this morning was the south bank in the April light, and it was there with the completeness of something that has found its right place and does not need to be told so.

Jim walked on.

He mentioned the piece to Maggie at Sunday dinner.

Maggie said she’d thought the Swan Warden knowing since 2004 and saying nothing for twenty-two years was the best part.

Jim said he thought the part about Gerald having the folder since 2006 was the best part.

They agreed both parts were good.

The boys ate their dinner.

A Note for the Record

In England, the King owns the swans. He has owned the swans, or his predecessors have owned the swans, since the twelfth century. There is a ceremony. There is a Royal Swan Marker. There is a tradition of asserting that certain beautiful things in open water belong to the Crown by virtue of the Crown being the Crown, and the Crown having always owned these things, and the ownership having never been formally questioned, and the questioning never having seemed worth the effort involved.

The swans did not vote on this arrangement. They were not consulted. Ownership was asserted by the Crown and the swans continued, as swans do, proceeding with their business on the Thames as they had always proceeded, in the apparent indifference to human governance that is either a natural quality of swans or a considered philosophical position, depending on how much time you have spent watching them.

In Elsinore, the swans belong to nobody. They are protected by the federal government and owned by no one. This is either a legal nicety or a philosophical position, and it is, in any case, the correct relationship between a municipality and its wildlife — which is to say, the wildlife is not on the asset register, the asset register does not govern the wildlife, and nobody has suggested that the wildlife might be rebranded or monetized or renamed for a donor. The question has not arisen. Darlene Potts would note it if it did.

Bradley Fenn has made a note to revisit the question after the next election. He has a slide prepared.

The swans have not been informed.

They are on the river.

They are proceeding.
