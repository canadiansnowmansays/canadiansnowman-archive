By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

On the morning Jack Schlossberg launched his campaign for Congress, his team had a plan.

Aides had lined up calls with media outlets, Democratic luminaries, and a roster of wealthy donors. The choreography was careful, the goal clear: demonstrate on Day One that the grandson of a president was a serious candidate ready for a grueling race in the most competitive congressional district in America.

By midday, Schlossberg had revised the agenda. He needed a nap. He then effectively disappeared for the rest of the day, leaving his team to manage the silence.

This detail, reported in the New York Times on May 14th — forty days before the June 23rd Democratic primary — is the kind of detail that, once read, cannot be unread. It does not prove that Jack Schlossberg is unfit for Congress. It does not settle the question of his candidacy. But it does something almost more consequential in a primary race: it gives the story somewhere to go.

The Times piece, by Nicholas Fandos, is a careful and largely devastating portrait of a campaign in which the candidate’s talent and the campaign’s competence have rarely occupied the same room at the same time. It is not an opposition hit. It is, as best I can read it from Toronto, a fair accounting of what happens when a famous name meets an unfamiliar discipline.

The Twelfth District, which was always going to be interesting, has become something else entirely: a test case for whether charisma plus dynasty can survive a newspaper’s scrutiny forty days out.

The Inventory of Chaos

Let me be precise about what the Times reported, because precision matters when a story of this kind lands in the final stretch of a primary.

Schlossberg had at least two campaign managers in six months. He had two field directors. He fired an ad maker and a mail consultant. Some staff were let go after weeks. Others, in a detail that reads like something from a Leacock sketch, stayed on for weeks after he had decided to dismiss them — because he never got around to telling them.

One canvassing coordinator, Jorge Muñiz Reyes, departed last week. His assessment of the campaign was not measured: it was, he said, like a dollar-store flower bouquet. The colours might be nice to look at for a few days. But since the flowers lack roots, they cannot last very long.

In November, Schlossberg posted a video to nearly 900,000 Instagram followers about Trump’s military provocations in Venezuela. It was shared widely. Aides to Representative Seth Moulton of Massachusetts — a Marine combat veteran who had posted a nearly identical message the day before — were taken aback. Schlossberg’s campaign, when contacted, acknowledged the candidate had copied the video because he liked it. He updated the post to credit Moulton. Moulton chose not to go public.

And then there is the Zoom interview. A woman who interviewed for a job with the campaign described an experience that began as courteous and substantive. Toward the end of the session, she said, Schlossberg slammed his hands on the table, pushed his face close to the camera, and addressed her by name — repeating that he wanted her, that he needed her. She believed he was referring to the campaign. The tone made her uncomfortable. She did not take the job.

His current campaign manager, Paige Phillips, said she sits in on every interview and that this did not happen.

The woman said Phillips was not on the call.

The Humanizing Fact

Mr. Schlossberg, right, with his parents, Caroline Kennedy and Edwin Schlossberg, and his sister, Tatiana, who died of cancer in December.Credit...Steven Senne/Associated Press

None of this, in fairness, exists in isolation.

Tatiana Schlossberg — Jack’s younger sister, an environmental journalist and author — died in December at thirty-five, after a battle with acute myeloid leukemia. She died while her brother was building a congressional campaign. She died young and by any account unexpectedly quickly, and she died with opinions about the race: the last thing she said to him, Schlossberg told CBS Sunday Morning, was you better win.

The campaign’s current manager cited Tatiana’s death as context for the period when sources described Schlossberg as having effectively gone missing. If an anonymous source thought he disappeared at any point, she said, they should remind themselves of that fact.

This is a legitimate point. Grief is not a campaign liability. It is a human condition, and anyone who has lost a sibling — or watched someone lose one — understands that the machinery of a congressional campaign is not the first thing that reasserts itself in those weeks.

But the Times also reported that the erratic behaviour — the blown strategy meetings, the long unexplained absences, the habit of greenlighting plans and then scrapping them hours later — predated December. The nap on launch day was in the fall. The Moulton incident was in November. The pattern, as reported, is not grief. It is character.

Both things can be true simultaneously. That is not a contradiction. It is a portrait.

What Pelosi Actually Endorsed

In February, Nancy Pelosi endorsed Jack Schlossberg for the Twelfth District. The endorsement was framed as a recognition of his passion, his ability to inspire young people, and his capacity to give voters something to believe in again.

It is worth reading the endorsement carefully for what it does not say.

Pelosi noted, in her statement, that she had served in Congress alongside several of Schlossberg’s relatives: the late Senator Ted Kennedy, and former Representatives Patrick Kennedy, Joe Kennedy, and Joseph P. Kennedy III. That is not a résumé endorsement. That is a bloodline endorsement, dressed in the language of inspiration.

At eighty-five, Pelosi remains the Democratic Party’s most formidable institutional operator. Her endorsement of Schlossberg signals something real about how the party’s old guard intends to manage the succession question in a Manhattan seat it has held for decades. Continuity. Familiarity. A name that opens doors talent alone cannot.

In the Twelfth District — the most educated congressional district in the country, a place where voters attend candidate forums the way other cities attend ball games — a Pelosi endorsement carries less organizing muscle than it might elsewhere. Local analysts have noted that endorsements in this district matter most when they bring volunteers, neighbourhood relationships, and club infrastructure. What Pelosi brought was a political ad and a famous face.

Schlossberg has a narrow polling lead. He has held it since the race began, through multiple rounds of polling, which is not nothing. Name recognition in a low-information early primary environment is a real advantage, and Schlossberg has more of it than any candidate in the field.

But the Times piece does something to a polling lead: it converts it from a number into a question. How many of those 25-percent voters — surveyed before this story landed — were voting for Jack Schlossberg the candidate, and how many were voting for Schlossberg the cultural event? And when the cultural event develops a complications column, what happens to the lead?

The Money Behind the Curtain

While the principal characters in this drama have been consuming most of the oxygen, a parallel story has been unfolding in the financial undergrowth of the Twelfth District race — and it is, from my vantage point in Toronto, the most interesting subplot in the field.

Michael Bloomberg’s super PAC has spent $4.3 million in support of Micah Lasher. That number has been widely reported and fits neatly into the establishment-candidate narrative: the former mayor, who once governed this city, backing the most institutionally connected candidate in the race.

Less widely noted is what is happening on the Bores flank.

An OpenAI-backed PAC has spent $2.4 million in ads attacking Alex Bores. An Anthropic-backed PAC has spent $1.2 million in ads supporting him. The artificial intelligence industry — the two companies most publicly identified with the question of whether AI serves democratic or oligarchic ends — has decided that a Manhattan congressional primary is worth nearly $4 million of combined attention.

Bores himself has theorized, with some wry logic, that the spending against him stands to help as much as hurt. In the Twelfth District, where voters are constitutionally suspicious of outside money and fluent in its dynamics, being the target of an OpenAI-funded attack campaign is not obviously a disadvantage. It implies you are the candidate someone powerful enough to fund a super PAC is afraid of.

I have spent considerable time in this newsletter examining the Asimov Gap — the distance between what artificial intelligence can do and what democratic governance has figured out how to manage. That gap is, it turns out, also a campaign finance gap. The same companies whose governance models are in active democratic dispute are now funding congressional races to shape the composition of the body that might one day regulate them.

This is worth more than a parenthetical. It will not be the last time we see it.

The Stage Magician’s Problem

I wrote in Part II of this series that Jack Schlossberg is a stage magician — someone whose trick works at distance, whose mechanism can be crude if the scale is large enough. The Twelfth District, I suggested, was about to decide whether close-up competence or stage-scale charisma was what it wanted in Congress.

The Times piece does not resolve that question. But it does clarify the stage magician’s central vulnerability: when the audience moves closer, the mechanism becomes visible.

Ron Klain — the former White House chief of staff who taught Schlossberg at Harvard Law and agreed to help him with policy positions — told the Times he came away impressed by Schlossberg’s acumen in their weekly calls. “Of all the congressional candidates I’ve ever worked with,” Klain said, “Jack is one of the ones who has been most serious about policy.”

This is a genuine data point, and it deserves to sit alongside the rest. A candidate who takes weekly policy calls with a former White House chief of staff and impresses him is not a political tourist. He is, at minimum, a serious student of what he does not yet know.

But a serious student and a functioning campaign are not the same thing. The 435 members of Congress do not govern alone — they govern through staff, through institutional relationships, through the grinding machinery of constituent service and committee work and floor votes and the thousand small decisions that never make it onto social media. The question the Times is implicitly raising is not whether Schlossberg understands policy. It is whether he can run an organization.

The Moulton incident is instructive here. Copying a congressman’s social media post — a Marine combat veteran’s post about military policy, no less — and posting it to 900,000 followers without credit is not a policy failure. It is an operational failure. It is the kind of thing a functioning campaign catches before it happens.

The staff churning is also instructive. Two campaign managers in six months is not, on its own, disqualifying. Campaigns are chaotic organisms and sharp reversals are, as Phillips noted, frequently a feature. But the details matter: staff who stayed on for weeks after being dismissed because the candidate never got around to telling them. That is not decisiveness. That is avoidance.

The Forty-Day Question

There are forty days between the publication of the Times piece and the June 23rd primary. In the compressed, high-attention environment of a Manhattan Democratic primary — a district that reads the Times the way other places read box scores — forty days is a long time.

The question is not whether the piece damages Schlossberg. It does. The question is what it damages and how durably.

If his support is primarily name-recognition support — voters who know the Schlossberg name and have positive feelings toward the Kennedy legacy — then the Times piece may not move them. Name-recognition voters are, by definition, not processing detailed campaign reporting. They are voting for the feeling.

If his support includes a meaningful slice of voters who have been watching the race closely, attending forums, reading endorsements, and making a more deliberate choice — the Twelfth District is full of them — then the piece lands differently. These voters have an alternative. Several, in fact.

Micah Lasher, whatever his polling position, has spent thirty years building the kind of institutional competence the Times implicitly contrasts with Schlossberg’s campaign chaos. Alex Bores has the fundraising lead and the East Side infrastructure and, apparently, the distinction of being considered threatening enough to attract $2.4 million in AI industry opposition spending.

The race that political insiders described from the beginning — an East Side versus West Side contest between two Assembly members, with Schlossberg and Conway providing the glamour — may be reasserting itself. The question is whether it does so in time.

The View From Here

I am watching this from Toronto, where I started this series because a Kennedy was running for Congress and the dynasty question seemed worth following.

The dynasty question is still worth following. But a different question has emerged alongside it: what does the Democratic Party owe its voters in a primary race, and what do voters owe themselves when they pull a lever for a famous name?

The Times piece is, at its core, a story about the gap between performance and governance — between the capacity to generate attention and the capacity to manage an organization. In the Trump era, the Democratic Party has sometimes confused the two. The ability to generate content, to go viral, to function in a low-attention economy has been treated as evidence of political fitness. It is not. It is evidence of media fitness, which is a related but distinct skill set.

Schlossberg has media fitness in abundance. He has 1.7 million followers. He surfs. He mocks his cousin. He gave his sister’s final words to a television camera and made the country feel something real, because those words were real.

Whether he can run a congressional office — manage a staff, maintain institutional relationships, show up to committee hearings, keep a scheduler, return phone calls — is a different question entirely.

On June 23rd, the Twelfth District will answer it.

The flowers, as one former staffer observed, may be nice to look at. The question is whether they have roots.
