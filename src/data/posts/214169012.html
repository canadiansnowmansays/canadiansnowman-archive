A few years ago I sat in a Toronto theatre beside my son Jim and cried without embarrassment. The show was “Come From Away,” and by the time the cast reached the number where the town of Gander, Newfoundland, simply decides — without a vote, without a committee, without asking what was in it for them — to take in nearly seven thousand strangers whose planes had been ordered out of American skies, I was no longer watching a musical. I was watching an argument about what a country is for. I left that theatre with what I can only call a buoyed sense of pride, the kind that has nothing to do with flags and everything to do with knowing, in your chest, that this is what your people did when it mattered.

On September 4, 2026, one week before the twenty-fifth anniversary of the attacks, the New York Times’ Michael Paulson filed a report from Gander itself, where “Come From Away” is once again playing down the street from the airport that made it necessary. This year the run has been extended, for the first time, through September 13, to hold the anniversary inside it. Around the world, 146 productions are opening or continuing this month — in Poland, Portugal, Singapore, on Cunard cruise ships, and in more American regional theatres than any title except “A Christmas Carol.” A story that began as one small act of decency has become, by any reasonable accounting, one of the most-produced pieces of theatre on Earth.

As Paulson reported for The New York Times, the show’s durability rests on an inversion nobody in Gander asked for or expected. Thirty-eight commercial flights were grounded there on the morning of September 11, 2001, dumping roughly seven thousand travellers — more people than the town itself contained — onto a runway built, ironically, for a Cold War that never needed it. What Gander did next was not policy. It was reflex. Residents opened their homes and fed strangers in numbers that strained the town’s entire capacity, for days, with no idea when it would end or whether their guests could ever repay the favour. Claude Elliott, the town’s mayor at the time and now a character in the musical he has seen 139 times, put the town’s philosophy to Paulson in five words that carry the whole show’s argument: the average Gander resident, a quarter century on, would still take an American into their home “and look after them today,” even with the two governments locked in a tariff war, “because you’re still our friends and you’re still our neighbours.” Oz Fudge, the town’s police officer in 2001 and another character who has now seen the show 110 times, said the story “means as much today as it did twenty-five years ago, given the climate that we’re in,” because “every time you turn on TV, all you see is hatred, and this little show is doing something.”

That distinction — between a people and their government — is one this series has made about Canada’s own position more than once. What I want to argue here is that Gander was never an aberration. It is the most famous entry in a ledger that runs back more than a century, and Washington’s current conduct toward Canada reads very differently once you actually open that ledger.

Start with the two world wars, where the arithmetic alone should embarrass anyone who calls Canada an ungrateful dependent. Canada entered the First World War in August 1914, when Britain’s declaration bound the empire’s dominions with it; the United States did not enter until April 1917, nearly three years later. By the time American troops reached the trenches in numbers, Canada — a country of not yet eight million people — had already suffered the bulk of a death toll historians place between 61,000 and 67,000, at Ypres, the Somme, Vimy Ridge and Passchendaele. The Second World War repeats the pattern with Canada acting entirely on its own authority this time: Ottawa declared war on Germany on September 10, 1939, a full week after Britain and more than two years before Pearl Harbor brought the United States in. Roughly 45,000 Canadians did not come home from that war, a toll paid, again, before Washington sent a single soldier.

Korea, a war Americans mostly forget and Canadians call their own forgotten one, cost 516 Canadian lives between 1950 and 1953, out of more than 26,000 Canadians who served alongside American forces under the UN flag — including at the Battle of Kapyong, where a badly outnumbered Canadian battalion held a line that stopped a Chinese offensive from breaking through to Seoul.

And then there is Vietnam, where Canada’s most memorable contribution to the American war effort was declining to have one. Ottawa never sent troops and never chose a side out loud, but its actual legacy from that decade runs in the opposite direction of every other entry on this ledger: between 1965 and 1973, somewhere between 30,000 and 100,000 draft-age Americans — the estimates vary this wildly because Ottawa quietly filed them as ordinary immigrants rather than as refugees, which was itself the point — crossed the border rather than report for a war a great many of them, and eventually a great many Americans, came to regret. Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver absorbed them as students and, later, taxpayers; more than half stayed for good even after President Carter’s 1977 pardon reopened the road home. In fairness to the ledger’s honesty, Ottawa was not above quietly selling Washington the munitions and aircraft parts covered under the two countries’ defence production-sharing agreement throughout the very war it was refusing to join — Canada’s conscience about Vietnam, like most consciences, came with a wholesale discount. But the plainer fact, the one a certain generation of Americans still remembers fondly, is that when tens of thousands of their sons didn’t want to go, Canada was the place that let them not go, no questions asked at the border and, for once, no invoice afterward either.

Then there is Tehran. When Iranian students seized the US embassy on November 4, 1979, six American diplomats slipped away before the building was overrun and had nowhere to go. Canada’s ambassador, Ken Taylor, hid them in his own residence and his deputy’s home for nearly three months, arranged genuine Canadian passports for them under forged pretences, and helped the CIA extract all six to Switzerland on January 27, 1980, in an operation later dramatized, at some historical remove, in “Argo.” Congress awarded Taylor its Gold Medal for it. The episode is remembered in Canada, correctly, not as an act of espionage but as an act of hospitality that happened to require diplomatic cover.

Afghanistan is the most recent blood debt on the ledger, and it was paid in direct response to what was done to America, not to Canada. Ottawa joined the US-led coalition within weeks of September 11, 2001, and stayed until 2014. Canada’s own war museum records that of more than 40,000 Canadian Armed Forces members who served there, 158 died — a per-capita casualty rate higher than any other coalition partner besides the United States itself, concentrated in the brutal fighting around Kandahar that Canada, alone among the smaller allies, chose to hold.

None of that is ancient history, and none of it stopped when the guns did. Canada and the United States have operated a formal mutual-aid arrangement on wildfires since 1982, and it runs in both directions depending on which country’s forests are burning worse in a given year. In September 2020, as fires consumed more than a million acres of California, sixty Quebec firefighters and two water-bomber aircraft crossed the border at Governor Gavin Newsom’s request; Canada’s ambassador to Washington called it simply “neighbours helping neighbours.” In January 2025, as the Palisades and Eaton fires tore through Los Angeles, Canada mobilized 250 firefighters and equipment from Ontario, Quebec and Alberta within days of the request — at the exact moment president-elect Donald Trump was publicly floating the use of “economic force” to annex the country whose crews were already airborne. The two governments’ relationship was already curdling. The firefighters went anyway.

And when Hurricane Katrina drowned New Orleans in August 2005, Canada’s response was not a phone call or a fundraiser, though it was also that. Three Canadian warships and a Coast Guard vessel, carrying roughly a thousand Canadian Forces personnel, Sea King helicopters and pallets of supplies, sailed from Halifax within days under what the military called Operation Unison. A Vancouver search-and-rescue team pulled 119 stranded people out of St. Bernard Parish before the US military could get there. Air Canada ran cargo shuttles of bottled water into New Orleans. Canadians donated more than a million dollars to the Red Cross inside the first week. Prime Minister Paul Martin told the departing sailors that Canada was “a helping nation” and that aid in a crisis “has to extend beyond this country’s borders.” Nobody in Ottawa attached a tariff schedule to the offer.

I list all of this not to keep score — the ledger was never meant to be settled, which is exactly the point — but because “Come From Away” tends to get treated, even by people who love it, as a lovely and slightly improbable exception: the one time Canadian kindness became famous enough to win a Tony. It was not an exception. It was the visible tip of a pattern that has held for well over a hundred years, through two world wars Canada entered before the United States did, through a forgotten war in Korea, through a war Canada refused to fight but still found a way to help with — by giving the reluctant an address — through a hostage crisis in Tehran, through a war in Afghanistan that Canada joined because of what happened to America, through burning California hillsides and a drowned Gulf Coast city. Gander simply happened to do it all at once, in one week, in front of enough journalists that somebody eventually wrote a musical about it.

This is the ledger the current administration in Washington is choosing not to read. For the better part of this year, the government of the United States has told Canada, repeatedly and in writing, that it is not a partner but a supplicant; not a neighbour but, in the president’s own words from Truth Social, a country that “will be treated like a State no longer.” It has renamed Lake Ontario. It has called Canadian negotiators “clowns” from “among the worst Nations in the World to deal with.” It has floated annexation as a punchline that keeps not landing as a joke. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, by his own account, wants automobile production moved out of Canada entirely. None of this is metaphorical. It is the documented record this series has been building for twenty-seven parts.

Today on the twenty-fifth anniversary of the day the American aviation system emptied itself into a Newfoundland gravel runway, a Canadian town is once again feeding the ghosts of that morning for free, eight performances a week, because it turns out to still believe that is simply what a decent place does — the latest instalment of a pattern that includes two wars fought partly on Washington’s behalf before Washington itself had joined them.

“Come From Away” survives — not merely endures, but multiplies, 1,000-plus productions and counting, mostly since 2025 — because it describes an exchange with no transaction in it. Nobody in Gander asked what the stranded passengers could offer in return, exactly as nobody at Vimy Ridge, or Kapyong, or the Canadian ambassador’s residence in Tehran, or aboard HMCS Athabaskan off the Mississippi coast, waited for an invoice before acting. Compare that to the vocabulary this administration has used all year toward the same country. Section 338. Reciprocal tariffs. “WE DON’T NEED CANADA, THEY NEED US.” A demand that Canada align its own trade policy with Washington’s as a condition of being spoken to civilly. Every interaction reduced to the question of who has leverage over whom, right down to a White House fact sheet — chronicled in Part XXVI of this series — that cited a chatbot conversation as a source because no human-authored one would say what it needed said. This is a government that cannot conceive of an exchange that isn’t a negotiation, and so it has spent a year negotiating with a neighbour whose defining historical habit, documented across a century and at least six separate conflicts and disasters, is showing up without being asked — or, on at least one occasion, quietly holding the door open for those who didn’t want to go.

There is a musical motif in the show, noted in Paulson’s piece, that I think does more work than its writers may have intended it to. “You Are Here,” borrowed from the language of locator maps in airport terminals, recurs as both a literal stage direction and an emotional one. I would gently suggest that this is precisely the sentence the current White House cannot bring itself to say to Canada. “You are here” would require admitting that Canada is a place with its own standing, its own sovereignty, its own right to say no to a deal that costs it its cultural protections or its steel industry — rather than a subsidiary awaiting instruction, which is the phrase Mark Carney himself used, almost word for word, to describe how American negotiators had been treating his country in the final hours before the August 21 collapse this series covered in Part XXIII.

Stephen Marche’s August 24 essay in the Times, discussed in Part XXIV of this series, located Canadian toughness in the capacity to endure rather than to dominate — the coureur de bois model of national character, not the conqueror’s. Every entry on the ledger above is that same instinct running in a different key: endurance not as grim survival but as open-handed generosity, offered again and again to the very country that is now, a quarter century after Gander’s finest week, threatening to rename one of Canada’s own lakes out of spite.

I confess to a certain longing, reading Paulson’s piece, for something I know the current administration is constitutionally unable to produce: a moment of genuine unguarded feeling about what actually happened to America that morning, untethered from grievance, untethered from leverage, untethered from the question of who owes whom. The people of Gander wept alongside their unexpected guests in 2001 and, by every account in that theatre lobby, still do at every performance. I wish, without much hope of it happening, that the men currently deciding tariff schedules on steel and softwood lumber could sit through two hours and forty minutes of a show about a town that asked nothing of stranded Americans except the chance to help, and come out the other side remembering the century of ledger entries behind it. I doubt it would change a single tariff rate. But Oz Fudge is right that something in the current climate needs undoing, and I don’t know a better place to start than a folding chair in a 392-seat theatre in Gander, watching people who owe you nothing decide to give you everything anyway — again.

You are here, the show keeps insisting, long after the last plane has taken off again. On the twenty-fifth anniversary of the morning that made the phrase necessary, it would not hurt Washington to read the rest of the ledger too.
