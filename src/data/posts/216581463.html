by Stephen Gordon

Essay 1,707 concerns the rarest operation in the political ledger. Nations subtract from one another constantly; it is their favourite arithmetic, and this series has covered the subtractions at Hastings and Mactan and Gravelines and will cover more before the count runs out. Nations multiply, in the colonial manner, and divide, usually with casualties. But addition — two sovereign countries voluntarily summing themselves into one — is so rare that the seventeen hundred years of this series’ territory offer only a handful of clean examples, and the cleanest of them took effect on May 1, 1707, when the kingdoms of England and Scotland, after four centuries of intermittent war and one century of awkwardly sharing a monarch, merged their parliaments and became Great Britain.

I said voluntarily, and I heard the readers of Scottish descent clear their throats, and they are right to, because the story of how Scotland came to the table is the real Numbers Game entry here, and it begins — where else — with a catastrophic investment.

In the 1690s, Scotland was a sovereign nation with a chronic problem: it was poor, and its richer neighbour controlled the trade routes. The proposed solution was audacious — Scotland would found its own colonial empire, at a stroke, by planting a trading colony on the Isthmus of Darien in Panama, the narrow waist of the Americas, positioned to broker the commerce of two oceans. The scheme was promoted by William Paterson — a name the finance readers will know, because this same Scot had just founded the Bank of England, and I ask you to hold that dual credential in mind: the era’s most sophisticated financial engineer, designing his own nation’s leap into empire. The subscription was a patriotic frenzy. By the standard estimates, something between a fifth and a quarter of all the liquid capital in Scotland — the savings of nobles, merchants, widows, towns — went into the Company of Scotland and sailed for Panama.

What Panama supplied in return was fever, famine, Spanish hostility, and English obstruction — the English crown, at war with France and courting Spain, ordered its colonies to render the Scots no aid — and within two years the venture had collapsed entirely, with some two thousand dead and the money simply gone. Not lost in the ordinary manner of bad investments, where it at least becomes someone else’s money. Gone — spent into the jungle. A quarter of a nation’s capital, subtracted to zero. Scotland at the turn of the century was a sovereign state that had, in the accounting sense, failed; and it is in that condition that she entered negotiations with the neighbour whose obstruction had helped fail her, which is the sort of negotiating position this series usually covers under the heading of the Strait of Hormuz.

Now the treaty, and the number I have been saving, because it is the most precisely satirical figure in eighteen centuries of entries. The Union was an exchange: Scotland surrendered her parliament and accepted a share of England’s national debt and taxes; and in consideration, England paid over a lump sum, calculated by the actuaries — Paterson himself among the calculators, the man auditing the wreck of his own scheme — to offset the debt assumption and, crucially, to compensate the Darien investors. The sum was called, with magnificent bureaucratic poetry, the Equivalent, and it came to exactly £398,085 and ten shillings.

Look at that number the way this series has taught you to. Not four hundred thousand pounds — that would be a payment. £398,085 and ten shillings is a reconciliation: a figure computed, line by line, to the last coin, as if the independence of a thousand-year-old kingdom were an invoice with a remittance slip. And in substance, a great deal of it was: the Equivalent flowed in large part to the shareholders of the Company of Scotland, which is to say that the sovereign debts of the Darien scheme were settled by the surrender of the sovereignty that had incurred them. The men who voted the Union through in the Scottish Parliament included, unsurprisingly, a healthy representation of Darien creditors, and the arrangement has been described in one immortal audit line that arrived eight decades later, when Robert Burns wrote of a parcel of rogues bought and sold for English gold. I quote the poet at less than his full length and refer you to him for the rest; he needs no help from me, and the line has been doing its work at Scottish gatherings for two hundred and thirty-five years.

The sum, I should add, did not arrive as an abstraction, and its arrival is the scene I would have filed had I been on the beat. In the summer of 1707 the Equivalent came north to Edinburgh physically — a convoy of wagons under dragoon guard, hauling chests of coin up the road to the castle, the balance made up in exchequer bills that the Scots, with a burned investor’s instincts, deeply distrusted, paper from London having recently demonstrated its properties. And the crowd that met the wagons did not cheer the settlement of the national accounts. The crowd stoned the wagons. I ask you to hold the image, because this series will not supply a better one: a nation’s independence, computed to the ten-shilling piece, rolling up the High Street under armed escort while the nation in question threw rocks at the arithmetic. Every subsequent constitutional crisis in the English-speaking world is in that scene somewhere. And the ledger was not done compounding: the apparatus set up to administer the Equivalent’s debts — the society of its creditors — was chartered twenty years later as a bank, and the bank exists today, and it is the Royal Bank of Scotland. The purchase price of Scottish sovereignty became, by direct corporate descent, the seed capital of one of the world’s great banks — a bank which, I note for symmetry, required its own rescue by the taxpayers of the united kingdom three centuries on. The money has never stopped commenting on the transaction. It is the most articulate sum in this series.

The seats tell the same story as the shillings. In the new united Parliament, Scotland received 45 members in a Commons of 558, and sixteen elected peers in the Lords. The ratio was defensible on the taxable-capacity arithmetic of the day — England’s economy was many times larger — and indefensible on any other, and both parties knew it, which is why the treaty’s drafters wrote so much of Scotland’s continuing distinctness into the deed itself: the separate Kirk, the separate courts, the separate law, guaranteed in perpetuity. The addition, in other words, was never a full merger of the books. It was a consolidation with protected subsidiaries — and I write those words as a citizen of a country that is itself a consolidation with protected subsidiaries, two languages, two legal traditions, one chronically renegotiated ledger, and I will say more about how my own country’s addition was done when the count reaches 1,867, an entry I have been carrying toward for a long time.

Here is the idea at the centre of this essay: addition is the only arithmetic operation that nations perform which requires the consent of both numbers — and the consent is never fully given, only continuously renewed. A conquest is finished when the fighting stops; essay 1,066 was over by nightfall. An addition is never finished. The sum of 1707 was tested by rebellion within a decade, and twice more at sword-point in the following forty years; it was tested at the ballot in 2014, when Scotland voted on subtraction and stayed, on a division of roughly 55 to 45 — and I note, because the Numbers Game notices such things, that the margin of the union’s survival after three centuries almost exactly matched Scotland’s original share of the Commons: forty-five. The books of 1707 are still open. They are open by design; that is what a voluntary union is — a ledger that either party may, in principle, demand be re-audited, forever, and whose continuation is therefore a daily plebiscite conducted mostly in silence.

And I will permit myself one paragraph of the present, by contrast, in the manner this series prefers. Set the Union of 1707 beside the modern proposal to add a certain northern country as a fifty-first state, and observe what is missing. The Union, for all its gold and its rogues, was a negotiated instrument between two parliaments, with the smaller party’s church, courts, and law entrenched in the deed, a computed Equivalent paid to the shilling, and the whole submitted to — and repeatedly surviving — the continuing consent of the added. The modern proposal contains, by my count, none of these: no negotiation, no entrenchment, no equivalent, and a consent that polls somewhere between refusal and laughter. It is not an offer of addition. It is a subtraction wearing addition’s clothes — and a Canadian can identify the costume at a glance, having spent a hundred and fifty-nine years learning what genuine addition costs: everything, continuously, in patience. The fifteen minutes that made such learning necessary are two essays away.

Dior, for the record, understands unions perfectly, having entered one with this household some years ago on terms she would describe, if pressed, as a treaty between sovereign equals with protected subsidiaries — her crate, her bowl, the good spot by the window, entrenched in perpetuity. She surrendered her parliament and retained her law. The Equivalent is paid daily, in kibble, calculated to the gram, and I am given to understand from the set of her ears that the books remain open and the plebiscite continues, mostly in silence, renewable at every mealtime. Three hundred years of constitutional theory, and the husky had it solved by instinct: the union survives exactly as long as the Equivalent keeps arriving.

The count moves on, as it does. The next landmark is essay 1,752, and the long-fused readers have been waiting for it since essay 1,582: the year Britain — having refused, for one hundred and seventy years, on principle, to accept a calendar correction because a pope had signed it — finally submitted to the arithmetic, deleted eleven days from a September, and grumbled so memorably that the grumbling entered legend, and the legend, as usual in this series, is better documented than the riot.

Two kingdoms did addition and the sum is still being checked. That is not the failure of the arithmetic. That is the arithmetic.

See you at 1,752, when the eleven days finally fall due.
