I have a problem with numbers.

I established this at essay 1,066, when I confessed that I had spent the better part of a year counting toward 1,000, achieved 1,000, felt nothing in particular, and immediately began counting toward the next number. I blamed William the Conqueror. He seemed like a reasonable scapegoat — a man who crossed the Channel in 1066, rearranged England by force, and left behind a number so memorable that a Canadian on Substack would still be invoking it ten centuries later.

William would have liked that. He was, by all accounts, exactly the kind of person who tracked his numbers obsessively.

Here we are again.

Essay 1,215. I did not plan this. That is what I want you to understand. I did not sit down in January and calculate that if I maintained my current pace, I would arrive at a four-digit number that happens to correspond to one of the most consequential documents in Western legal history. I am not that organized. I am, however, exactly the kind of person who notices, ninety essays out, that a milestone is approaching, and immediately abandons whatever he was writing to make something of it well in advance.

Dior watched me realize this. She did not seem surprised. She has the particular resignation of a husky who has lived with a number-counter for long enough to recognize the facial expression.

It was June 15, 1215. The meadow at Runnymede was damp, as English meadows tend to be, and King John of England was having what historians would generously call a difficult morning.

The barons had had enough.

This is worth dwelling on, because “the barons had had enough” is not a sentence that emerged overnight. King John had spent years doing what powerful men with unchecked authority tend to do: taxing arbitrarily, imprisoning without cause, selling justice to the highest bidder, and operating under the sincere belief that being king meant the rules applied to everyone except him. He had lost Normandy. He had quarreled with the Pope. He had alienated virtually everyone within quarreling distance.

The barons, who were themselves not exactly saints — let’s not romanticize a group of armed medieval landlords — had nonetheless arrived at a position that turned out to be historically important: someone had to say no.

Not politely. Not through strongly worded letters. With an army, in a meadow, with a document already drafted and a very clear message: you will sign this, or we will finish what we started.

John signed.

He had no choice. That is the thing about the Magna Carta that tends to get lost in the reverential glow of retrospect: it was not a gift from an enlightened monarch. It was a surrender document. The Great Charter was great precisely because a king was forced — by force — to acknowledge that even he was subject to the law.

It is also worth noting that John did not write a word of it himself. The document was drafted largely by Archbishop Stephen Langton and a committee of barons, then placed in front of a king who signed what was put before him without, by most historical accounts, reading it especially closely. There is something almost comforting about discovering that a tradition of powerful men signing documents they have not fully absorbed predates the photocopier by eight centuries.

He hated it. He immediately appealed to the Pope to have it annulled.

I’ve written before about a framework I call TACO: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. I did not realize, when I coined it, that I was simply describing King John’s itinerary for the summer of 1215. He threatened the barons. He accommodated when the army arrived. He capitulated and signed. And then, with admirable speed, he obfuscated his way to Rome to get the whole thing annulled. Eight hundred years before the acronym existed, John was already running the play.

The Pope, who owed John nothing and the barons nothing either, obliged.

The First Barons’ War followed. John died the next year, possibly of dysentery, possibly of the general consequences of being King John.

But the document survived. That is the part that matters. The idea outlasted the man who signed it under duress and spent his remaining months trying to escape it. Eight hundred and eleven years later, its core principle — that no one, not even the sovereign, is above the law — is still the thing that distinguishes functional democracies from the alternatives.

I find this oddly comforting, given the current alternatives.

Magna Carta, 1215. The document that survived a reluctant king, a papal annulment, and a war — because someone wrote it down.

There is a man in Washington who also has trouble with the word no.

His name is Donald John Trump.

I want to pause on that middle name, because history occasionally arranges things with a precision that satirists can only envy. The forty-seventh President of the United States shares his middle name with the king who was dragged to Runnymede in 1215 and forced, at sword-point, to acknowledge that even sovereigns are subject to the law. King John. The original. The one who signed the charter, hated it, appealed to the Pope to annul it, and died having accomplished nothing except the permanent association of his name with reluctant accountability.

Eight hundred and eleven years later: Donald John Trump.

He has heard no from judges. He has heard it from courts. He has heard it from the Constitution, which is, in its way, the American Magna Carta — a document that exists specifically to tell powerful men what they cannot do. He finds the document inconvenient. He has said so, in slightly different words, many times.

The first King John would have understood him completely. They would have had a lot to talk about — the burdens of sovereignty, the ingratitude of institutions, the sheer unreasonableness of being told that laws apply to you specifically. Two men named John, separated by eight centuries, united by the sincere conviction that the rules were meant for other people.

Eight hundred years before the phrase “self-pardon” existed, John was already testing how not to be bound by a document he’d just signed. He failed, eventually, though it took the Pope, an army, and his own death to make the point stick. I do not know how the modern version of this story ends. I have my suspicions.

I have also tried, on occasion, to imagine how the second King John would describe the Magna Carta if he had been the one to negotiate it. Something, I suspect, along the lines of: the greatest charter in the history of charters, signed under the most unfair circumstances ever inflicted on any monarch, by far, and frankly a disgrace that anyone would even ask him to sign it, but he did it anyway because he loves this country. The thirteenth-century version did not have Truth Social. History is poorer for it.

The difference is that in 1215, the barons showed up with an army.

The courts said no. The barons said no. So, reportedly, did Barron — just probably not about war powers.

The modern version — judges, courts, constitutions, the slow machinery of democratic accountability — is less dramatic. It moves through filing deadlines and appellate dockets rather than through armed men in a meadow. It is, consequently, easier to dismiss. Easier to call rigged. Easier to treat as an obstacle rather than a foundation.

But it is the same argument. It has always been the same argument. The only question, in any era, is whether the institutions hold.

The Magna Carta held. Eventually. Imperfectly. With considerable violence in the interim.

I am choosing to find that encouraging rather than alarming. It is a choice I make daily, and I will not pretend it gets easier.

I should tell you about the counting.

I am at 1,125 essays as I write this — which means I am drafting essay 1,215 approximately ninety essays in advance, because that is the kind of person I have become. A man who pre-writes milestone essays and then postdates them with the serene confidence of someone who is absolutely certain he will not slow down in the interim.

I briefly considered accelerating. Ninety essays in eleven days — purely so that essay 1,215 would land on June 15th, eight hundred and eleven years to the day after John pressed his seal into the wax at Runnymede. I ran the numbers. I reconsidered. The last time I ran at that pace I arrived at 1,000 gasping, realized I had published approximately forty essays no one needed quite that urgently, and spent a month walking it back. The Magna Carta does not require my assistance with its anniversary. It has been doing fine without me since 1215.

This is, I recognize, not normal behaviour.

I will say, in my own defence, that my numbers are at least honest. I am not claiming 1,215 is bigger than it is. I am not telling you it is the largest essay count in the history of essay counts, by far, nobody has ever seen numbers like this. It is 1,215. I counted it myself, one essay at a time, and I am prepared to be corrected if I miscounted. There is a man currently occupying the White House who could learn something from that approach, though I suspect the lesson would not take.

But here is what I have learned from the counting: the numbers that mean something are not the ones you choose. 1,000 felt significant because I assigned it significance, and when I arrived there it felt like — a number. 1,066 arrived and gave me William the Conqueror and a whole meditation on conquest and obsession and the husky’s dwindling patience.

It is now June 19th. I am four days late. I would like to point out, in my defence, that this essay belongs to the Sunday Satire series, and Friday the 19th is not a Sunday — which means I am not late at all. I am, in fact, early for next Sunday. The Magna Carta, I feel certain, would understand. King John was rarely on time for anything either.

1,215 arrived the same way. Not because I planned it. Because I was counting, and the count landed somewhere history had already been.

There is a version of this that is pure coincidence. A man writes a lot of essays, the numbers get large, and occasionally a number with historical resonance appears. The laws of probability guarantee it. You could make the same observation about a random number generator and a history textbook.

But I prefer the other version. The one where the counting is, in its small and slightly unhinged way, a form of faith — in the idea that words accumulate, that arguments compound, that the work of explaining what is happening and why it matters is itself a kind of participation in the long argument that started in a meadow in Surrey and has not finished yet.

The Magna Carta was, at its core, an act of documentation. Someone wrote it down. Someone said: here is what was agreed, here are the principles, here is what cannot be taken from you. The writing was the thing. The charter survived because it was written, copied, carried, read, cited, and — eight centuries later — still invoked by people who need to explain why no one gets to be above the law.

I am not writing the Magna Carta.

I am, however, writing. Every day. In the perhaps-delusional belief that documentation matters. That the record matters. That someone should be paying attention and putting it down.

Dior is still not impressed. She was hoping I had reconsidered the whole enterprise. I have not.

I will see you at essay 1,492.

That, as it happens, is the year a Genoese sailor employed by the Spanish Crown sailed west, got lost, landed somewhere he had never been, declared it somewhere he had always intended to go, and called it a triumph.

He did not know where he was. He died not knowing where he was. The continent he stumbled upon was named after someone else.

And yet — here we are, still counting from the year he didn’t know what he’d found.

Numbers, as I have established, are like that.
