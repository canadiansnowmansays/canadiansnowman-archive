There is a particular kind of bookkeeping that governments never publish, because nobody would like the totals. It records not what a policy promised but what it bought, and for whom. This series has been keeping such a ledger since Part I, on the theory that the original sins of this conflict have been charging compound interest ever since, and that the interest is always paid by someone other than the sinner.

Most of that ledger has, rightly, been about the land itself: the expulsions of 1948, the occupation of 1967, the settlements, the American veto, the slow financial strangulation of the West Bank that Part XIV described. But a ledger has two columns. On October 13, 2026, Karim Sadjadpour of the Carnegie Endowment published an essay in The Atlantic titled “Fighting Israel to the Last Palestinian,” and it is, in effect, an audit of the other column: the account held in Tehran in the name of the Palestinian cause.

His conclusion is blunt. After nearly half a century, the Islamic Republic’s campaign against Israel has inflicted far more suffering on Palestinians, Arabs and Iranians than on Israelis, and its principal political beneficiary has been the Israeli right.

Fourteen days before Israelis go to the polls, that is worth sitting with.

The Balance Sheet

Sadjadpour opens with a detail that should be better remembered. In the hours after Hamas’s October 7, 2023 attack, he notes, only one head of state publicly celebrated it: Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who expressed the hope that what he called the Zionist cancer would be eradicated.

Three years on, consider what the celebration purchased. Gaza is in ruins. Hamas’s senior leadership is dead. Hezbollah has been decapitated. Bashar al-Assad is in Moscow. Iran is fighting the second of two wars with Israel and the United States, its military largely destroyed, its economy crippled, and Khamenei himself among the dead. Readers of the Iran Crisis series will know the rest of that story in more detail than they might wish.

And Benjamin Netanyahu still governs Israel.

The heaviest entry, though, belongs to the people in whose name all of this was done. Sadjadpour puts the Palestinian death toll at an estimated 74,000. Those Palestinians were killed by Israeli bombs and Israeli bullets, and nothing in this essay is meant to blur that fact. But a strategy that invites a catastrophe and then claims the victims as martyrs to its cause cannot also claim to have been on their side.

Anti-Israel Is Not Pro-Palestine

The core of Sadjadpour’s argument is a distinction that ought to be obvious and somehow never is: being against Israel is not the same as being for Palestine. Too often, he argues, it has been the opposite.

The historical record is unkind to the self-appointed champions. Saddam Hussein paid the families of suicide bombers, reportedly up to $25,000 a family during the Second Intifada, while doing nothing whatsoever to build a Palestinian state. Muammar Qaddafi expelled thousands of Palestinian workers from Libya in 1995, leaving many stranded at the Egyptian border, as a gesture of protest against the Oslo Accords. That is, he punished Palestinians for the crime of other Palestinians negotiating. And Bashar al-Assad laid siege to the Yarmouk refugee camp outside Damascus, once the largest Palestinian community in Syria, until its residents were starving.

Tehran’s contribution to this tradition is a slogan. As Sadjadpour observes, the Islamic Republic’s official chant has always been “Death to Israel.” It has never been “Long Live Palestine.”

The difference is not a matter of phrasing. A movement built around a state you want to create must eventually concern itself with schools, hospitals, courts, sewers and elections. A movement built around a state you want to abolish needs only enemies and martyrs, and it can go on needing them indefinitely.

“What Good Would That Have Done for Us?”

The single most damning passage in Sadjadpour’s essay is an anecdote, and it should be read as one: a single conversation, reported by one participant. More than a decade ago, at a Track II diplomacy meeting in Liechtenstein, he asked an Iranian official whether the billions Tehran had spent arming Hezbollah, Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad might have done more for the Palestinian cause if spent training tens of thousands of Palestinians and Lebanese as doctors, lawyers and engineers.

The official, by Sadjadpour’s account, asked what good that would have done for Iran. Educated young people, he reasoned, would not come home to southern Lebanon and Gaza to fight Israel. The official, Sadjadpour adds drily, held a doctorate from a British university himself.

If the account is accurate, and Sadjadpour has spent a career earning the benefit of the doubt on Iran, it is as candid a statement of proxy strategy as one is likely to find. The strategy did not fail to produce Palestinian doctors and engineers. It was designed not to. Its success required chaos, misery and the absence of social mobility, because those are the conditions in which young men pick up rifles rather than stethoscopes.

An Enmity Neither Ancient Nor Natural

It is worth pausing on how strange this feud is. Israel and the Palestinians fight over the same land. Russia and Ukraine share a border; China and Taiwan share a strait and a history. Iran and Israel share nothing. They have no common frontier, no territorial dispute and no competition for water or oil.

What they do share is a long and, for most of it, friendly past. Twenty-five centuries ago, Cyrus the Great of Persia conquered Babylon and allowed the exiled Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild their Temple. The Book of Isaiah calls him God’s anointed, an honour the Hebrew Bible bestows on no other gentile king. Under the Shah, Iran and Israel were quiet partners, bound by oil, intelligence cooperation and a shared wariness of Arab nationalism. Iran’s Jewish community, though much diminished, remains the largest in the Middle East outside Israel.

All of that changed in 1979, when Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini built a revolutionary theocracy whose founding document, his 1970 treatise on Islamic government, is laced with hostility toward Jews. Sadjadpour quotes it at length; the short version is that Khomeini saw a Jewish conspiracy against Islam itself and cited, approvingly, the Prophet’s elimination of a Jewish tribe in Medina. Khamenei inherited the worldview and spent four decades and tens of billions of dollars acting on it.

The point is not that Iranians hate Israelis. Most evidence suggests ordinary Iranians are rather more interested in inflation, blackouts and their children’s emigration prospects. The point is that this enmity is a choice, made by a particular regime for its own purposes, and it is barely older than Netanyahu’s political career.

The Kingmaker in Tehran

Here Sadjadpour makes the argument that gives this essay its title and its timing. Critics of military action against Iran have long warned that bombing strengthens Tehran’s hard-liners, who thrive on insecurity. The reverse, he argues, has received far less attention: Tehran’s threats have strengthened Israel’s hard-liners.

The evidence begins in 1996. Yitzhak Rabin had been murdered the previous November by a Jewish extremist opposed to the Oslo process, and Shimon Peres, Oslo’s architect, entered the election campaign with a comfortable lead. Then, between late February and early March 1996, a wave of suicide bombings by Hamas and Islamic Jihad, both backed by Iran, tore through buses in Jerusalem, a junction in Ashkelon and a shopping centre in Tel Aviv, killing about sixty people. On May 29, 1996, Netanyahu won the first direct election for prime minister by fewer than 30,000 votes out of more than three million cast.

Peres understood what had happened. On the eve of the vote, Sadjadpour notes, he warned that the real threat to his victory was not Netanyahu but Iran and Islamic fundamentalism. He was right. A few dozen bombers did what the Likud campaign could not.

For the next three decades, Tehran supplied Netanyahu with the ideal foil: a nuclear programme, Holocaust denial, an annual parade of missiles with anti-Israel slogans painted on them, and a ring of militias on Israel’s borders. Netanyahu, in turn, supplied the rhetoric. “It’s 1938 and Iran is Germany,” he told an American Jewish audience in 2006. He brought a cartoon bomb to the United Nations General Assembly in 2012. Each warning had a domestic dividend: it raised Israeli fear, pushed the Palestinian question off the agenda and, eventually, helped persuade Gulf monarchies that feared Iran more than they cared about Palestine to normalise relations with Israel without securing a Palestinian state.

Every time Israelis and Palestinians came close to a settlement, in Madrid, Oslo, Camp David or Annapolis, Tehran was funding someone determined to blow it up. The two governments agree on almost nothing. But the Islamic Republic did more than any Israeli voter to make Netanyahu the longest-serving prime minister in his country’s history.

The Other Half of the Partnership

This is where Sadjadpour’s essay, excellent as it is, needs a second column of its own. He is an Iran specialist writing about Iran, and the agency in his account sits almost entirely in Tehran. But this series has spent seventeen parts documenting that Israeli governments, and Netanyahu’s above all, made choices too.

The relationship ran in both directions. For years, Netanyahu’s governments allowed Qatari cash to flow into Hamas-run Gaza, on the widely reported theory that a strong Hamas in Gaza and a weak Palestinian Authority in Ramallah made a Palestinian state impossible. In 2019, Haaretz reported that Netanyahu had told a Likud gathering that anyone opposed to a Palestinian state should support strengthening Hamas, a claim his office disputed. The settlements expanded in every one of his terms. The West Bank financial chokehold described in Part XIV was his government’s policy, not Iran’s.

So the fairer summary is not that Tehran is to blame and Jerusalem is the victim. It is that the Islamic Republic and the Israeli right have been, for thirty years, each other’s most reliable recruiting sergeants. Iran needed an Israel that bombed Gaza; Netanyahu needed an Iran that threatened annihilation. Each delivered. The Palestinians, the only party that needed a state, were the only party that got nothing.

A reader of The Atlantic’s comment section put it well enough, in passing: the two hard-line camps feed on each other. Sadjadpour comes close to saying the same thing, observing that Iran is one of the very few governments on earth whose ambition to abolish another country has eclipsed its ambition for its own.

What Palestinians Think

There is a habit, on all sides of this argument, of speaking for Palestinians rather than asking them. Fortunately, they have been asked.

Sadjadpour cites a 2024 poll in which only 28 percent of Palestinians held a favourable view of Iran, and a May 2025 survey by the Palestinian Center for Policy and Survey Research in which 54 percent said Iran’s role was harmful to the Palestinian cause, against 32 percent who called it beneficial. The office of Palestinian Authority President Mahmoud Abbas, admittedly no friend of Hamas, has accused Khamenei of seeking to sacrifice Palestinian blood and destroy Palestinian land.

Honesty requires adding that the picture is not static. A later PCPSR survey, fielded in October 2025 and reported by the Jerusalem Post, found Iran’s favourability among Palestinians had risen to 42 percent, even as about 70 percent still called Iran’s political influence in the region a critical threat to Palestinian interests. The Arab Barometer recorded a similar rise in approval of Iran among Palestinians, from 21 percent in 2023 to 36 percent in 2025. People under bombardment tend to warm to whoever appears to be shooting back. That is not an endorsement of Tehran’s strategy; it is a measure of how desperate the alternatives have become.

Israelis, for their part, have no ambivalence at all. A 2025 survey by the Pew Research Center found that 87 percent of Israelis considered Iran harmful to the prospects for peace, 80 percent very harmful. On that one point, Netanyahu has never needed to persuade anyone.

October 27 and the Missing Foe

Which brings us to the election, and to the question Part XVII left open.

For the first time in Netanyahu’s long career, Israelis will vote with Iran’s “Axis of Resistance” in pieces. The man who called for Israel’s eradication is dead. Hezbollah is leaderless, Hamas is broken and Assad has fled. If the foil was the secret of Netanyahu’s success, what happens when the foil is gone?

Netanyahu’s answer, so far, is to insist that it is not gone. Launching Likud’s campaign in Tel Aviv on September 17, 2026, he gave a speech devoted entirely to security, as La Dernière Heure reported from a Belga dispatch. Only his camp, he said, could guarantee Israel’s safety; the mission had to be finished, and that meant bringing down the Iranian regime and destroying Hezbollah and Hamas entirely. In August, as Ajel reported, he told Israelis that Iran had tried to kill one of his sons. The enemy, in other words, must remain both defeated and dangerous: defeated enough to credit him, dangerous enough to need him.

The polls suggest the trick is harder to pull off this time. Successive surveys since late 2023 have shown his bloc short of a majority, with the race tightening between Netanyahu’s coalition and the opposition led by Gadi Eisenkot and the Bennett-Lapid list. A Kantar poll for the public broadcaster Kan on September 27 found him ahead of Naftali Bennett as preferred prime minister, 42 percent to 33, with a quarter of respondents choosing neither. That is a lead, but it is a lead built on a war he has promised to finish rather than one he has won.

It would be pleasant to report that the end of the Iranian foil means the end of the politics it sustained. It would also be naive. Eisenkot and Bennett are hardly doves; Bennett has spent his career opposing a Palestinian state. A post-Netanyahu Israel may still offer the Palestinians very little. But it would at least be a government whose security doctrine was not built, for thirty years, on a symbiosis with the regime it claimed to be fighting.

Judged by Outcomes

Sadjadpour closes with a principle that this series could adopt as a motto: policies should be judged by their outcomes, not their slogans.

By that standard, nearly five decades of “Death to Israel” have produced the following. Israel exists, larger, richer and more heavily armed than in 1979. Gaza is rubble. The West Bank is being annexed by inches. Iran, a country blessed with oil, gas and one of the best-educated populations in the region, is impoverished, isolated, bombed and bleeding its brightest young people to Toronto, London and Los Angeles. And the Israeli politician most opposed to Palestinian statehood has governed for longer than anyone else in his country’s history, partly on Iran’s account.

That is the compound interest on a slogan. The Palestinians paid the principal and the interest both. Tehran collected nothing but a few years of regional swagger, now spent. And Netanyahu, the one man who profited handsomely, will spend the next fourteen days trying to persuade Israelis that the debt is still outstanding.

Sadjadpour expects Tehran’s surviving rulers to double down rather than take stock. He is probably right. The more interesting question is whether Israelis, on October 27, will take stock on their behalf.
