Starship V3 takes flight for the first time last Friday

In 2001, Elon Musk tried to buy a rocket from Russia.

He was not trying to launch a satellite or win a government contract. He was trying to send a small greenhouse to Mars — a plant growing in Martian soil, photographed and transmitted back to Earth, intended to reignite public interest in space exploration by showing something green and alive on a red and dead planet. He called the project Mars Oasis. The greenhouse was the easy part. The rocket was the problem.

Musk flew to Moscow with Adeo Ressi and Jim Cantrell, met with Russian aerospace companies, and inquired about purchasing a refurbished Dnepr intercontinental ballistic missile — a decommissioned nuclear weapon repurposed as a launch vehicle. The Russians quoted him $8 million per rocket. Musk thought it was too much. He went back. They raised the price. One account has a Russian executive spitting on him to indicate what he thought of the young American’s seriousness. Another account has Musk being offered vodka at breakfast and the Russians dismissing him as a dilettante.

On the flight home from the second or third Moscow trip — the details vary by source, the outcome does not — Musk opened a spreadsheet on his laptop and began calculating the cost of building a rocket from scratch. He priced the raw materials: aerospace-grade aluminium, carbon fibre, rocket-grade kerosene, liquid oxygen. He mapped the components: engines, avionics, fuel tanks, payload fairings. He discovered something that the aerospace industry had apparently known but never acted on: the raw materials of a rocket constituted approximately 2% of the typical launch price. The other 98% was overhead, margin, institutional inefficiency, and the cost of doing business with organisations that had never been asked whether rockets needed to cost what they cost.

Elon Musk as a young man in the 1990s, working at an early computer setup — emblematic of the self-taught, first-principles mindset that led from code to rockets.

This is the first principles moment that created SpaceX and, by extension, the entire Musk stack. The question was not: how do we make rockets slightly cheaper? The question was: what does a rocket actually cost if you build it from the physics up rather than from the procurement catalogue down?

The answer was: far, far less than anyone was charging.

Space Exploration Technologies Corp. — SpaceX — was incorporated in May 2002 in El Segundo, California, funded with approximately $100 million of Musk’s PayPal money. The founding team was small. The ambition was not.

Musk’s initial pitch to engineers was disarmingly simple: the reason humans are not a multi-planetary species is not that the physics prevents it. It is that launches cost too much. Reduce the cost of launch by a factor of ten, and everything changes — communication, exploration, colonisation, scientific research, national security. The cost of launch is the single variable that gates everything else.

Every design decision at SpaceX flowed from this premise. Where NASA and traditional aerospace contractors like Boeing and Lockheed Martin operated through vast supply chains with thousands of subcontractors — each adding margin, each operating to their own schedule — SpaceX would build as much as possible in-house. Vertical integration. The same philosophy that would later define Tesla’s battery production, chip design, and manufacturing strategy originated in a rented warehouse in El Segundo where engineers built rocket engines from raw metal.

SpaceX’s Merlin engine during a static fire test. Designed and built in-house, it became the workhorse powering the Falcon family

The Merlin engine — SpaceX’s workhorse — was designed and manufactured internally. The avionics were designed internally. The flight software was written internally. When SpaceX needed a valve and the aerospace supplier quoted a six-month lead time at $50,000, SpaceX engineers built the valve themselves in weeks for a fraction of the cost. This is not a company that outsourced. This is a company that treated outsourcing as an admission of failure.

The first three Falcon 1 launches failed. The first, in March 2006, ended twenty-five seconds after liftoff due to a corroded nut on a fuel line. The second, in March 2007, reached space but experienced a roll control malfunction during second-stage burn. The third, in August 2008, failed when the first and second stages collided during separation — a timing error caused by upgraded Merlin engines whose residual thrust had not been accounted for.

Three launches. Three failures. Approximately $90 million spent. Roughly $10 million remaining — enough for one more attempt.

Part III of this series documented the pattern: survive the gap between investment and proof of concept. The gap for SpaceX was six years and three explosions. Musk has said he had planned for three failures and budgeted accordingly, which either means he was a prudent planner or a man who retroactively imposed structure on a period of controlled panic. Either reading is plausible. Both may be true.

The fourth launch, on September 28, 2008, succeeded. Falcon 1 reached orbit. It was the first privately developed liquid-fuelled rocket to achieve orbital insertion. Musk reportedly went to the factory floor, addressed the staff, and cried.

Falcon 1 ascending — the rocket that nearly bankrupted SpaceX before its historic fourth-flight success in September 2008

Three weeks later, NASA awarded SpaceX a $1.6 billion Commercial Resupply Services contract to deliver cargo to the International Space Station. The company that had been weeks from bankruptcy was now a NASA contractor with a billion-dollar backlog.

The proximity to extinction deserves emphasis because it is routinely glossed over in the mythology. In December 2008, Musk had approximately $20 million left across his personal finances and company reserves. He was borrowing money from friends. His marriage to Justine was ending. Tesla, his other company, was simultaneously on the brink of collapse. He has described this period as the worst of his life. The fourth Falcon 1 launch was not a business milestone. It was a survival event. Everything that followed — the Falcon 9, the Dragon capsule, the Starship, the Starlink constellation, the IPO — exists because a rocket worked on a Tuesday in September 2008 when the man who built it had enough money for exactly one more attempt.

The lesson Musk drew from this experience was not caution. It was that the margin between failure and world-changing success is thinner than anyone outside the room understands, and that the only rational response is to push harder, faster, and with more capital concentrated into fewer bets. Whether this lesson is courage or compulsion is a question his biographers will debate for decades. What it produced is not debatable.

The vertical integration at SpaceX mirrors and predates Tesla’s approach — and may, in fact, be the template from which Tesla’s strategy derived.

SpaceX builds approximately 80% of its rocket components in-house. The Merlin engines, the Raptor engines (powering Starship), the avionics, the flight computers, the guidance systems, the payload fairings, the composite structures — all designed and manufactured at SpaceX facilities. The company pours its own carbon fibre. It machines its own turbopumps. It writes its own flight software. When an aerospace supplier quoted a $120,000 radio and a nine-month delivery, SpaceX engineers built an equivalent unit for $5,000 in weeks.

The principle is identical to what Tesla would later apply to batteries, chips, and charging infrastructure: every outsourced component is a dependency, every dependency is a constraint, and every constraint that physics does not require is an opportunity to reduce cost, accelerate iteration, and control quality. The aerospace industry had spent decades building elaborate supply chains across hundreds of subcontractors. SpaceX collapsed the supply chain into one building.

This vertical integration is what makes the Starlink economics work. SpaceX manufactures Starlink satellites at its Redmond, Washington facility — not purchased from a traditional satellite manufacturer at traditional satellite prices, but built in-house at a pace and cost that reflects SpaceX’s manufacturing culture rather than the aerospace industry’s procurement culture. SpaceX then launches those satellites on its own Falcon 9 rockets at marginal cost. The entire value chain — satellite design, satellite manufacturing, launch vehicle design, launch vehicle manufacturing, launch operations, network operations — is internal. No middlemen. No markup. No supplier dependency.

This is why no Starlink competitor can match the economics. Amazon’s Project Kuiper must purchase launch services at market rates. OneWeb (now part of Eutelsat) must purchase launches from Arianespace or SpaceX itself. Every competitor faces a cost structure that includes supplier margins SpaceX does not pay. The vertical integration is not just a business advantage. It is a structural moat that widens with every launch.

What followed was a decade of engineering that I will not pretend to adjudicate technically but that I can describe in its outcomes, because the outcomes are the point.

Falcon 9 replaced Falcon 1 as SpaceX’s primary launch vehicle. Its nine Merlin engines — where Falcon 1 had one — delivered payloads to low Earth orbit at costs dramatically below the industry standard. The rocket was designed from inception for reusability: the first stage would launch the payload, separate, flip, reignite its engines, and land vertically — either on a ground pad or on an autonomous drone ship at sea.

A Falcon 9 first stage standing tall on the autonomous drone ship “Of Course I Still Love You” after a successful sea landing

The first successful Falcon 9 first-stage landing occurred on December 21, 2015, at Cape Canaveral. The footage — a column of fire descending from the sky and settling onto a concrete pad with the precision of an elevator — remains one of the most striking visual demonstrations of engineering achievement in the twenty-first century. The aerospace industry had said reusable orbital-class rockets were impractical. SpaceX landed one on a Tuesday.

As of 2026, SpaceX has completed over 400 successful Falcon 9 landings. Individual boosters have flown and landed more than twenty times each. The cost per kilogram to low Earth orbit has dropped from approximately $54,500 on the Space Shuttle to under $2,700 on Falcon 9 — a reduction of more than 95%. Musk’s original spreadsheet on the flight home from Moscow has been vindicated at a ratio that even he likely did not anticipate.

Dragon became the first commercial spacecraft to dock with the International Space Station in 2012, and Crew Dragon became the vehicle that returned American crewed launch capability from American soil in 2020 — ending a nine-year period during which NASA depended on Russian Soyuz capsules to reach the station it largely built and funded. The irony that Musk’s company replaced Russian rockets after Russian aerospace executives declined to sell him one is the kind of narrative symmetry that fiction would consider heavy-handed and reality delivers without comment.

Crew Dragon module attached to the ISS, with Earth in the background — ending reliance on Russian Soyuz for U.S. astronauts.

Starship is SpaceX’s next-generation launch vehicle: a fully reusable, stainless-steel rocket standing approximately 120 metres tall, designed to carry over 100 tonnes to low Earth orbit and — critically — to be refuelled in orbit for missions to the Moon and Mars. Starship is the vehicle Musk intends to use for human Mars colonisation. It is also the vehicle that will launch the next generation of Starlink satellites and the orbital AI compute infrastructure that TeraFab’s chips are designed to power.

The scale of Starship defies easy comparison. It is taller than the Statue of Liberty. Its Super Heavy booster generates approximately 74 meganewtons of thrust at liftoff — roughly twice the thrust of the Saturn V that carried Apollo astronauts to the Moon. It is designed to launch, land, be restacked on its booster, and launch again within hours rather than months. If the turnaround targets are achieved, Starship will reduce the cost per kilogram to orbit by another order of magnitude below Falcon 9 — bringing space access costs to levels that make orbital data centres, lunar bases, and Mars settlements economically conceivable rather than merely physically possible.

On May 22, 2026 — two days after SpaceX filed its IPO prospectus with the SEC, in the kind of scheduling coincidence that only a man who controls both the rocket and the roadshow calendar could engineer — Starship flew for the twelfth time. It was the first flight of the V3 variant: new Raptor 3 engines, a redesigned booster with three larger grid fins replacing the previous four, simplified aft sections, increased propellant capacity, and new hardware for future orbital refuelling. The rocket stood 408 feet tall and produced eighteen million pounds of thrust. It launched from the brand new Pad 2 at Starbase. Nicki Minaj — the woman who sang “Starships” — was in attendance. You cannot make this up.

The flight did what SpaceX flights do: it partially succeeded, partially failed, and produced data worth more than either outcome considered alone.

The Super Heavy booster lost an engine during ascent. It lost several more during its boostback burn and crashed into the Gulf of Mexico instead of landing — though it was not planned for recovery on this flight. The upper stage, Ship 39, lost one of its six Raptor engines but completed most of its planned objectives regardless: it reached its suborbital trajectory, deployed twenty-two Starlink simulator satellites (including two equipped with cameras to photograph the heat shield during reentry), survived reentry, and splashed down in the Indian Ocean as planned. SpaceX had intentionally removed a heat shield tile to test effects on adjacent tiles, painted several tiles white as imaging targets, and stressed the rear flaps at maximum dynamic pressure. This was not a rocket trying to perform perfectly. This was a rocket trying to learn.

Musk called it “epic.” Flight 13 — the second V3 test — is already scheduled for June. The pattern from Falcon 1 is repeating at a scale that dwarfs the original: iterate, fail partially, learn completely, fly again before the analysis is cold. The gap between Flights 11 and 12 was seven months. The gap between 12 and 13 will be weeks.

The context for this flight matters as much as the flight itself. NASA has selected Starship as the Human Landing System for Artemis IV, targeting a crewed Moon landing in 2028. SpaceX has yet to demonstrate a full orbital flight, in-orbit refuelling, or docking with an Orion capsule. Flight 12 proved that V3 can fly, survive reentry, and deploy payloads under engine-out conditions — which is the foundation on which every subsequent objective must be built. It is not the destination. It is proof that the vehicle can leave the driveway. But two days after an IPO filing at a $1.75 trillion valuation, proof that the vehicle can leave the driveway was exactly what the roadshow needed.

SpaceX is the purest application of first principles thinking in the Musk stack, and it deserves to be described as such without qualification.

The company identified a genuine constraint — launch cost — that prevented an entire domain of human activity from advancing. It decomposed the problem to its physical fundamentals. It discovered that the cost structure was primarily institutional rather than physical. It built a vertically integrated alternative that addressed the actual constraint. It iterated through failure. It proved the approach worked. And it did so while creating more value — strategic, economic, and scientific — than arguably any other private company founded in the twenty-first century.

This is not an argument that SpaceX is perfect. The company’s labour practices have drawn criticism. Its environmental impact assessments for Boca Chica launch operations have been contested. Musk’s management style — characterised by extreme work demands, public firings, and the expectation that employees treat the mission as more important than personal well-being — raises legitimate ethical questions about the human cost of engineering achievement.

But the engineering achievement is real. The reusable rockets land. The cost curve has declined by the factor Musk predicted. The Dragon capsule delivers astronauts to the station and returns them safely. Starship, despite delays and test-flight explosions that Musk treats as learning opportunities, is further along than any competing super-heavy-lift programme. Flight 12 — engine failures, booster crash, and all — demonstrated that the V3 architecture works, that the vehicle can survive the loss of engines and still complete its mission, and that the iterate-and-fly-again philosophy that saved the company in 2008 remains its operating system in 2026.

When this series evaluates Musk’s ventures against the first principles standard — asking whether each venture represents authentic first principles reasoning or a departure from it — SpaceX earns the highest mark. The question was right: what does a rocket actually cost? The analysis was correct: far less than anyone charges. The execution, despite three early failures and countless subsequent setbacks, delivered the answer the physics predicted.

SpaceX is what Musk looks like when he is doing what he is best at: asking a question no one else thought to ask, building the answer from raw materials, and having the capital, the risk tolerance, and the engineering judgement to survive the gap between the question and the proof.

The question this series must grapple with in later essays is why the man who built SpaceX also built X. Why the mind that asked “what does a rocket actually cost?” also asked “what if I bought Twitter?” One question was first principles. The other was something else entirely. And the distance between those two questions is the distance between the best and worst of the Musk stack.

But SpaceX is the best. And intellectual honesty requires saying so.

There is, however, a caveat that transforms SpaceX from an engineering story into a power story, and it is the caveat that this series exists to examine.

On May 20, 2026, SpaceX filed its S-1 registration statement with the Securities and Exchange Commission — the IPO prospectus for what is expected to be the largest initial public offering in history. The company will list on the Nasdaq and Nasdaq Texas under the ticker SPCX, targeting a valuation of approximately $1.75 trillion and seeking to raise between $75 and $80 billion. The investor roadshow begins June 5. The listing is expected June 12. Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, and JPMorgan Chase are underwriting.

The prospectus is glossy — literally. Colour photographs of rockets and space, pull quotes from Musk’s musings, the kind of document that a company produces when it wants investors to feel the grandeur before they read the numbers. But the numbers are where the story lives, and the numbers tell a story considerably more complicated than “rocket company goes public.”

SpaceX generated consolidated revenue of $18.7 billion in 2025, with an operating loss of $2.6 billion and a net loss of $4.9 billion. In the first quarter of 2026 alone, the company lost $4.3 billion. This is not what a profitable rocket company looks like. This is what happens when you fold a money-losing social media platform and a money-losing AI chatbot into a profitable rocket-and-internet company and take the whole bundle public as a single entity.

The corporate archaeology is worth tracing because it reveals the architecture of the offering. Musk acquired Twitter in October 2022 for $44 billion and rebranded it as X. In March 2025, xAI — Musk’s artificial intelligence company, which operates the Grok chatbot — acquired X. In February 2026, SpaceX acquired xAI. The result is a Russian-nesting-doll corporate structure in which a social media platform lives inside an AI company that lives inside a rocket company, and all of it is being sold to investors as a single stock at a $1.75 trillion valuation.

The S-1 breaks the company into three segments, and the contrast between them is the financial expression of the tension this series has been documenting since Part I.

Estimated Total Addressable Market from the SpaceX S-1 — showing the enormous ambition (and valuation) behind the IPO.

The Connectivity segment — Starlink — is the engine. It generated $11.4 billion in revenue in 2025 with adjusted EBITDA of $7.2 billion. Starlink had 10.3 million subscribers across 164 countries at the end of March 2026, double the 5 million it served a year earlier. Starlink is a real business with real margins, built on real engineering, delivering real value to real people in 164 countries. It is what first principles looks like when it reaches scale. It is also financing everything else.

The Space segment — the actual rocket business, including Falcon 9, Dragon, and Starship — generated $4.1 billion in revenue but posted a $657 million operating loss. The reason: $3 billion in Starship research and development in 2025 alone, with another $930 million in the first quarter of 2026. SpaceX has spent over $15 billion developing Starship — more than originally budgeted. This is not mismanagement. This is what building the most powerful rocket in history costs. But it means the rocket business that defines SpaceX’s identity is not the business that funds SpaceX’s operations.

The AI segment — xAI, Grok, and the remnants of X — is the black hole. It lost $6.35 billion from operations in 2025 and consumed $12.7 billion in capital expenditure. In the first quarter of 2026 alone, AI capital expenditure hit $7.7 billion — an annualised pace above $30 billion. In 2024, before the xAI merger, SpaceX was profitable, posting $791 million in net income. The merger turned a profitable company into a loss-maker overnight. By design.

Starlink’s profits are subsidising xAI’s losses. The internet business built on first principles engineering is bankrolling the AI chatbot that was built to compete with ChatGPT after Musk fell out with Sam Altman. Whether this represents strategic vision or an expensive grudge laundered through corporate structure is a question the market will price on June 12.

The governance provisions in the S-1 deserve their own paragraph because they answer a question this series has been circling: what happens when the man who controls the rockets also controls the shareholders? Musk holds 85% of SpaceX’s shareholder voting power. He will serve simultaneously as chief executive officer, chief technology officer, and chairman of the board. He could only be fired by voting to fire himself. This is not a publicly traded company with a controlling shareholder. This is a personal fiefdom with a stock ticker.

The related-party transactions are clarifying. SpaceX spent nearly $700 million on Tesla Megapack battery products between 2024 and 2025. It spent an additional $131 million on Cybertrucks — the slab-sided electric pickup that Tesla has struggled to sell to anyone who is not Elon Musk. That $131 million may represent between 6% and 9% of all Cybertruck sales in 2025. One Musk company is purchasing inventory from another Musk company, and the shareholders of the purchasing company — soon to include the public — are paying for it. This is legal. It is disclosed. And it is the kind of transaction that 85% voting control ensures will never be challenged.

Then there is the Anthropic deal, which may be the single most revealing item in the prospectus — not for what it says about SpaceX, but for what it says about the AI industry’s infrastructure dependency. Anthropic — the company that makes Claude, a direct competitor to Musk’s Grok — has agreed to pay SpaceX $1.25 billion per month through May 2029 for compute capacity at the Colossus and Colossus II data centres. The total contract value exceeds $40 billion. Either party can terminate with ninety days’ notice.

Visual representation of the SpaceX S-1 filing and the complex corporate structure bundling rockets, Starlink, xAI, and X.

Read that again. Anthropic is paying Musk’s company $1.25 billion a month to rent computing infrastructure that was built for Grok but that Grok does not fully utilise because Grok’s usage has declined. SpaceX is monetising the unused capacity of its own AI division by leasing it to a competitor. The S-1 describes this as a “dual monetisation strategy.” A less charitable description would be: the AI chatbot that Musk built because he was angry at OpenAI has failed to attract sufficient users, so SpaceX is renting its servers to the competition and booking the revenue to improve its IPO numbers.

The deal also includes both companies exploring the development of “multiple gigawatts of orbital AI compute capacity” — data centres in space, powered by solar panels, cooled by the vacuum of space, launched on SpaceX rockets, connected through Starlink’s satellite network. SpaceX filed for FCC approval in January to launch up to one million data-centre satellites, with deployment potentially beginning as early as 2028. The company stated in the S-1 that it believes orbital AI compute is “an incredibly difficult technical challenge that only we can solve at scale in the near term.”

This is the convergence point of the entire Musk stack. The rockets launch the satellites. The satellites provide the internet. The internet connects the data centres. The data centres run the AI models. The AI models are trained on the compute infrastructure. And all of it — rockets, satellites, internet, data centres, AI — is controlled by one man who holds 85% of the voting power and cannot be removed from any of his three simultaneous executive positions.

SpaceX operates the majority of active satellites in Earth orbit. It is the sole provider of American crewed orbital launch capability. It holds contracts with NASA, the Department of Defence, and the intelligence community. Its Starship rocket is the only vehicle with the payload capacity to build the orbital infrastructure that Musk’s broader empire requires. And the person who controls all of this is the same person who posts conspiracy theories at three in the morning, who gutted content moderation on his social media platform, who names his children with alphanumeric codes, and who walked into the federal government with a chainsaw emoji and accomplished less in months than his rocket engineers accomplish in an afternoon.

The rocketry is brilliant. The concentration of strategic power that the rocketry enables is unprecedented. And the S-1 prospectus — filed two days before a Starship test flight, timed to a roadshow that begins two weeks later, structured to bundle a social media platform and an AI chatbot into a rocket company’s valuation — is the document that makes the concentration legible in financial terms for the first time.

SpaceX is the purest application of first principles thinking in the modern world. It is also the foundation of the most concentrated portfolio of strategic power any private citizen has ever held. And in approximately three weeks, it will be a publicly traded company whose controlling shareholder cannot be outvoted, whose related-party transactions cannot be blocked, and whose corporate structure bundles the rockets, the internet, the AI, and the social media platform into a single entity valued at $1.75 trillion.

Both of these statements are true. The tension between them is the subject of this series.
