When Donald Trump first heard Jensen Huang’s name, by his own account, he was not impressed. “Who the hell is he? What’s his name?” he has recalled telling associates. “His name is Jensen Huang of Nvidia. I said, ‘What the hell is Nvidia?’”

The president tells the story with relish, according to Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan, Tripp Mickle and Julian E. Barnes in The New York Times on November 12, 2026, because of how it ends. Within a year, the man he had never heard of had become, in the president’s mind, a modern-day Henry Ford. On the Tuesday before Huang resigned, Trump telephoned him and urged him to keep fighting for his job.

Part I followed the woman U.S. intelligence came to believe was sent to get close to Huang. Part II follows the other courtship, the one conducted in public: how the head of a company that had barely bothered with Washington became the most influential business figure in the second Trump administration, and how a ban on selling A.I. chips to China became, within eight months, a deal in which the U.S. government took a quarter of the revenue.

The man who wasn’t at the inauguration

Huang came late to politics. The Nvidia founder had no real presence in Washington during Trump’s first term. He did not lobby or give to campaigns. The Biden administration’s sweeping chip restrictions forced him to pay more attention, but he still did far less politicking than his rivals, and he was among the few prominent tech executives who did not attend Trump’s second inauguration in January 2025.

His first two encounters with the new president went badly. About two weeks after the inauguration, on January 31, 2025, the two men struggled to connect in the Oval Office, according to Tripp Mickle and Ana Swanson in The New York Times on November 19, 2025. Afterwards Trump told reporters he could not say whether he would ban more of Nvidia’s chip sales to China.

The second meeting, at Mar-a-Lago, was no better. In April 2025 the administration warned Nvidia that it planned to shut down sales of the H20, the last A.I. chip the company still sold in China. As Tripp Mickle reported in The New York Times on July 17, 2025, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick invited Huang to Mar-a-Lago to make a last-ditch appeal. On April 4, on the sidelines of a candlelit donors’ dinner reported to cost $1 million a head, Huang and his wife, Lori, met the president. Huang argued that the H20 was far less powerful than what Nvidia sold elsewhere, and that losing China would hurt American companies and help Chinese rivals.

Officials briefed afterwards thought he had undersold the chip. The H20 was packed with memory well suited to running A.I. systems, and Chinese customers were spending billions on it. On April 15, the administration sent Nvidia a letter shutting down H20 sales to China.

Paying for the relationship

What changed the relationship was not an argument. It was a pledge.

On April 30, 2025, Huang stood at a White House lectern and announced that Nvidia and its suppliers would invest up to $500 billion in American manufacturing. In what Nvidia executives later said were unplanned remarks, he credited the president’s “strong encouragement — and I mean his strong encouragement,” and Trump smirked.

The pledge offered Trump something his first term had failed to deliver. He had promised to bring back coal and steel jobs, and neither industry returned to its old strength. A.I. offered another route to the reindustrialisation he had promised voters. People close to him said he came to believe the rush to build data centres, with their enormous appetite for electricity, could produce a manufacturing revival and keep a shaky economy running hot. That made the leaders of the A.I. companies the most valuable executives in the country, and Huang perhaps the most valuable of all.

In May 2025 Huang joined Trump on his first international trip, to the Persian Gulf, and helped bring about more than $200 billion in chip sales to Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. “What a job you’ve done,” Trump told him in a speech in Riyadh. “We are proud to have you in our country.” The president started calling him “my friend.” At Nvidia, people felt that word was the breakthrough.

The flattery ran both ways. “America’s unique advantage that no country could possibly have is President Trump,” Huang said in July 2025. Nvidia later gave at least $20 million to the president’s ballroom fund, The New York Times reported on October 29, 2026. Later reporting put the commitment at $25 million, made in October 2025, two months after Trump let Nvidia resume H20 sales to China for a 15 percent cut, according to The New York Times.

The ally inside

Huang also found an ally inside the building. David Sacks, the venture capitalist who had become the White House’s A.I. and crypto czar, was more receptive to Huang’s view of China than other officials. He disliked a Biden-era rule that capped A.I. chip sales to most countries, and he questioned Washington’s consensus that selling chips abroad was bad for the United States. After Huawei unveiled an A.I. system competitive with American products, Huang’s warnings began to resonate with him.

Sacks helped dismantle the Biden chip-cap rule, which cleared the way for the Gulf deals. He and Huang began making the same argument: to win the A.I. race, the United States should encourage the world to buy American technology rather than give it reasons to buy Chinese. “The American tech stack should be the global standard, just as the American dollar is the standard,” Huang said on a think-tank podcast in July 2025.

In public Huang went further. “China is not behind,” he told a Washington conference in April 2025. At a Taiwan computer show in May, he said American export controls had only made Chinese companies stronger. “All in all, the export control was a failure.” He once called the label “China hawk” a “badge of shame,” and when the CNN host Fareed Zakaria asked whether it mattered if the United States or China won the A.I. race, he replied, “I don’t think it does,” as Julian Gewirtz recalled in The Atlantic on November 12, 2026.

China hawks on the MAGA right, Stephen K. Bannon among them, wanted chip sales to China banned outright. Huang and Sacks persuaded Trump to take the other side, the unpopular one with the populist base.

Nvidia’s spending followed its ambitions. The company spent $640,000 on federal lobbying in 2024 and $4.97 million in 2025, according to OpenSecrets, and $4.69 million in the first three quarters of 2026, as Cecilia Kang and David McCabe reported in The New York Times on November 12, 2026. Samuel Hammond of the right-leaning Foundation for American Innovation, a critic of Nvidia’s campaign against export controls, put its aim bluntly: “His No. 1 prerogative has been gaining market access to China, by hook or by crook.”

The reversal

On July 10, 2025, Huang met Trump in the Oval Office, with Sacks seated nearby. By the end of the nearly hourlong meeting, Trump had agreed that Nvidia’s chips could return to China. On July 14, as Tripp Mickle reported in The New York Times, Nvidia announced that the administration had assured it licences for the H20 would be granted. The ban had lasted three months.

Lutnick explained the reasoning on CNBC. The approval, he said, was linked to trade talks with China, which had just agreed to supply rare-earth magnets to American companies. The idea was to sell Chinese businesses Nvidia’s fourth-best chip so that “they get addicted to the American technology stack.” Six months earlier, at his confirmation hearing, the same man had said Nvidia and others “need to stop helping” China. Part IV will return to that journey.

Days after the reversal, Huang held a news conference in Beijing to tell Chinese customers Nvidia was open for business. The mood was jubilant. Asked whether he had changed the president’s mind, he demurred: “I don’t think I changed his mind. It’s my job to inform the president about what I know very well.”

In August came the arrangement that would define the relationship. Trump suggested the government take a cut of Nvidia’s chip sales to China, and Huang agreed. The figure began at 15 percent. Critics noted that U.S. law prohibits charging fees for export licences. Huang said the administration was working on a regulation to make it possible.

“You’re taking over the world, Jensen”

By the autumn of 2025 Huang had become one of the president’s regular travelling companions. At a state dinner during Trump’s visit to Britain in September, the president mused that A.I. “is taking over the world,” then turned to Huang. “You’re taking over the world, Jensen,” he said. “We both hope you’re right.”

Nvidia’s chips had also become a tool of foreign policy. After the administration helped broker peace between Armenia and Azerbaijan, officials discussed A.I. technology exchanges with both. Kazakhstan announced a $2 billion deal for Nvidia-powered data centres in the same week that it joined the Abraham Accords. A U.S. official said the chips played only a minor role, but they had joined Boeing planes on the list of things America sells to its friends.

In late October 2025 Huang returned to the Oval Office with a gift: a shiny silicon disk, among the first examples of Nvidia’s new A.I. chip made in Arizona. He was lobbying at the time for permission to sell a version of Blackwell, Nvidia’s most advanced chip, to China. On October 29, flying to South Korea to meet Xi Jinping, Trump told reporters: “We’ll be speaking about the Blackwell.”

That was a step too far. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Trade Representative Jamieson Greer raised national security objections, and lawmakers rebuked him. By the flight home Trump had reversed himself: he and Xi had discussed chips, “not the Blackwell.” It showed the limits of Huang’s influence, though only on the most advanced chips.

A quarter of the revenue

On December 8, 2025, Trump announced that Nvidia could sell the H200, its second-most-powerful chip, to approved customers in China, as Tripp Mickle and Ana Swanson reported in The New York Times. In a social media post he wrote that “$25% will be paid to the United States of America,” ten percentage points more than the August arrangement. Commerce Department lawyers were tasked with finding a way around the law against export taxes.

Six senators, including the Republican Pete Ricketts and the Democrat Chris Coons, had just introduced a bill to limit A.I. chip sales to China. Senator Elizabeth Warren wrote to the president urging him to “stop ignoring the input of bipartisan members of Congress and your own experts in order to cut deals that trade away America’s national security.” Nvidia’s spokesman, John Rizzo, called the decision “a thoughtful balance that is great for America.”

Administration officials privately marvelled at how much sway Huang seemed to have. Some had worried about him for a long time, and not only about his views on China policy. They saw him as, at best, naïve and careless in his dealings with Chinese executives and local officials, with whom he kept close ties.

What the president already knew

The calendar of that autumn carries an irony that would not become public for a year.

In October 2025, the month Huang presented his Arizona chip in the Oval Office and Trump floated selling Blackwell to Xi, intelligence officials briefed the president about a Chinese businesswoman named Alice Huang. They told him U.S. agencies had concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset, and that surveillance showed she had a close relationship with Nvidia’s chief executive. Two months later, Trump approved the H200.

His advisers have since offered explanations. He saw Nvidia as essentially too big to fail, bound up with the stock market and the economy. He had invested heavily in Huang personally. And he was never a China hawk in any conventional sense. His hostility to Beijing has largely been confined to trade, and even there he prefers a deal with Xi to a long fight. Asked in 2020 why he had not sanctioned China over the mass detention of Uyghurs, he replied: “Well, we were in the middle of a major trade deal.”

The courtship worked because both men wanted the same thing: Nvidia’s chips selling everywhere, and the headlines that came with it. What neither of them advertised was what the president had been told about the woman at the edge of the photographs. Part III follows what he did with that warning, and with the one that came after it.
