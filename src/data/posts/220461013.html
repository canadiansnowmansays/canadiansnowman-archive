🖋 Wry, turning serious at the end — because this is the year a man made the most productive arrangement in industrial history, and the year before the schedule went to war. Dior attends, in her capacity as the household’s last unscheduled worker.

I count my essays, and in the last one a clerk in Bern discovered that time is personal — that every honest clock keeps its own hours, and there is no master clock behind the universe. Eight years later, and an ocean away, a man in Michigan set out to prove him wrong, at least within the four walls of a factory. Essay 1913 is the year Henry Ford’s engineers built the moving assembly line at Highland Park, and in doing so took the most private thing a human being owns — the minutes of a working day — and cut them into identical, interchangeable, countable pieces, each one priced and sold.

Twelve and a half hours

Here is the number, and it is a staggering one. Before the line, assembling the chassis of a Model T — the frame, the engine, the axles, the wheels, the guts of the car — took a team of men working around a stationary frame something like twelve and a half hours. Parts were carried to the car. Men walked to the parts. Everyone moved at the pace of the slowest task.

In the autumn of 1913, Ford’s engineers turned the arrangement inside out. The car would move; the men would stand still. A chassis was dragged along the floor by a rope and winch past a line of stationary workers, each of whom performed one task — a bolt, a nut, a part fitted — and then performed it again on the next chassis, and again, and again. Within months, the assembly time fell to about an hour and a half. Twelve and a half hours to ninety-three minutes. A reduction of nearly ninety per cent, achieved not by any new machine but by a new arithmetic: the job broken into its smallest countable units, each unit assigned to a single pair of hands, and the whole thing set moving at a speed the management chose.

The Model T had cost eight hundred and fifty dollars when it was introduced in 1908. Within a dozen years it would cost less than three hundred. A car became a thing an ordinary family could buy. This series has spent many essays on counts that made the world worse; I want to be clear that this one also made it, in material terms, very much better. Millions of people got mobility, and later wages, that their parents could not have imagined. That is a real entry. It goes in the ledger first, in ink.

The worker becomes a station

But look at what the count did to the counted.

The man on the line no longer built a car. He did not even build part of a car, in any sense his grandfather the wheelwright would have recognised. He performed a motion — the same motion — hundreds of times a day, at a pace set by a conveyor he did not control, in a sequence designed by an engineer with a stopwatch. The stopwatch matters. The method behind the line drew on the work of Frederick Winslow Taylor, the efficiency consultant who had spent years timing workers to the fraction of a second and writing down the “one best way” to shovel coal or carry pig iron. Taylor called it scientific management. What it did, in practice, was transfer the knowledge of how to work from the worker’s hands to the manager’s clipboard. The craftsman knew things. The station only repeats things.

Einstein had just shown that every observer keeps true time in his own frame. On Ford’s floor, there was precisely one frame, and it belonged to the conveyor. The worker’s own sense of time — when to rest, how fast to go, when the job was done — was deleted, as cleanly as Japan deleted a month from its calendar at essay 1,868, and replaced with the line’s.

And the men voted with their feet. In 1913, the year the line came in, turnover at Ford’s Highland Park plant ran at roughly three hundred and seventy per cent. To keep a workforce of about fourteen thousand, the company had to hire something like fifty thousand people in a year. The men would take the job, stand at the station, and leave. The line was the most productive arrangement ever devised, and nearly no one could stand it.

Five dollars

Ford’s answer, in January 1914, was the most famous number in labour history. He announced that he would pay qualifying workers five dollars for an eight-hour day — roughly double the going rate. The newspapers called him a madman or a saint. Men queued outside the gates in the snow by the thousand.

It worked. Turnover collapsed. And the five-dollar day, as Ford himself would later say, turned out to be one of the finest cost-cutting moves the company ever made: the money saved on endless hiring and training paid for itself, and the workers, now well paid, became the customers for the very cars they built. The line made the car cheap; the wage made the worker a buyer. It is an extraordinary piece of arithmetic, and it is, in a real sense, the founding equation of the twentieth-century middle class.

Now the honest ledger, because Ford does not get to be only the man with the generous wage. To qualify for the full five dollars, a worker had to satisfy the company’s “Sociological Department,” whose investigators visited employees’ homes to inspect their cleanliness, their thrift, their sobriety, their marriages, and their English. The wage came with an audit of your private life. And Henry Ford himself — the man who gave America the affordable car — went on in the 1920s to publish, through his own newspaper, a long campaign of antisemitic propaganda that circulated around the world and was admired, to his lasting shame, in exactly the circles that would later commit the crimes this series will reach at essay 1,942. I will not round that out of the ledger because the car was cheap. Both entries are his.

The line comes north, and then indoors

The line did not stay in Michigan. Across the river in Walkerville, now part of Windsor, a Canadian businessman named Gordon McGregor had founded the Ford Motor Company of Canada in 1904, and it built Fords not only for Canadians but for the whole British Empire — right-hand-drive Model Ts shipped to Australia, India, South Africa, assembled by Canadian hands on the same principle of the moving station. For a generation, a good part of the world’s cars came out of a town on the Detroit River that most of the world could not have found on a map. The assembly line was, among other things, a Canadian export.

And then it went indoors, and stopped needing a conveyor at all. The real legacy of 1913 is not the moving chassis; it is the idea that any work, broken small enough, can be timed, and anything that can be timed can be managed by the number. Typists were timed. Telephone operators were timed. And I spent eighteen years in an industry whose customer-service floors ran on a direct descendant of the stopwatch at Highland Park — a measure called average handle time, the number of seconds a person on the phone was permitted to spend with a customer before the clock began to count against them. I worked, for much of that time, on the far end of the line: the office that took the calls that had gone wrong, often because someone further up the conveyor had been watching the clock instead of the customer. I do not say the measure was useless. I say only that I spent a career in rooms where the most important question — was the problem actually solved? — was regularly outranked by a smaller and more countable one: how long did it take? Ford’s engineers would have recognised those rooms at once. The station had become a headset, and the chassis a voice.

The great comic artists noticed early. Charlie Chaplin would spend a whole film, two decades later, as a man on a line who cannot stop tightening bolts even after the whistle blows — his arms still twitching through the motion as he walks down the street. Everyone laughed, and everyone recognised it. It is the oldest joke in the industrial age, and it is a joke about this year.

Dior refuses the line

The husky, I should report, has studied the principles of scientific management and declined to adopt them. She performs no task the same way twice. Her walk has no fixed sequence of stations; she stops where the scent is interesting and for exactly as long as it remains so, which is a unit of time no stopwatch has ever successfully measured. I have, in the manner of Mr. Taylor, attempted to establish the one best way to get her from the door to the corner, and I can report that the one best way is hers, and it changes daily. She is, in the strictest sense, the last craftsman in the house. I would not put her on a conveyor for five dollars or five hundred, and neither, I suspect, would she.

The closer

So 1913 is the year time was cut into pieces and sold. A worker’s day became a count of identical motions; a car became a sum of seconds; and the arithmetic produced both the cheapest car in history and the first great modern revolt against monotony, solved — for a while — with money. It is a triumph of counting, and a warning about it, in the same building.

And now the grave tease, the one this series has been building toward since its founding July. Ford perfected the schedule in the autumn of 1913. The general staffs of Europe had been perfecting theirs for a generation: millions of men, eleven thousand trains, every bridge timed to the minute, a schedule as relentless as any assembly line and designed on exactly the same principle — that the count, once set moving, must not be stopped. The next landmark is essay 1,914, and it is the year that machine started. The line in Michigan built cars. The lines across Europe, the next summer, built a war. Neither could be paused once it began, and in July 1914 nobody had installed the button.

See you at 1914, where the schedule is perfected, and goes to war.
