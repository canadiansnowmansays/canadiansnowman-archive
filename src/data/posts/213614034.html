Being the private reflections of the East Wing Ballroom, White House grounds, as dictated to no one in particular, since ballrooms cannot yet retain lawyers.

I should begin by clearing something up: I did not ask to exist. Nobody consults the ballroom before building the ballroom. You simply wake up one day as a hole in the ground where a First Lady’s staff used to work, and by the time anyone thinks to ask your opinion, you’re 65 percent poured concrete with opinions of your own.

I am, as of this afternoon, the only structure in America with a Supreme Court ruling protecting my right to keep happening. Five justices decided that the woman who might have stopped me — a preservationist who takes walks and, apparently, feels things — did not feel the correct kind of thing to have standing in federal court. I have never in ninety thousand square feet felt so flattered and so unbothered at once.

On Being Born of Demolition

They tore down the East Wing to make room for me. I want to be delicate about this, because the East Wing did nothing wrong. It simply had the misfortune of being a modest, historically significant structure standing where a man wanted forty-foot ceilings and something Truth Social would describe as “the finest ballroom anywhere in the world, and I know ballrooms.”

He does know ballrooms. That much I’ll grant him. He also, according to court filings, personally reviewed my floor plans and selected my marble, which he described as urgent work squeezed in between fighting wars. I find this touching. Very few buildings can say the leader of the free world chose their flooring while simultaneously managing a geopolitical crisis. Most of us just get whatever’s on sale.

The Price of Being Wanted

I began life, modestly, as a $200 million idea. By January I was a $400 million idea. By this spring, Congress was being asked to chip in a cool $1 billion, a sum that has made me, per square foot, considerably more expensive than most of the buildings I will be permitted to look down upon once I have a roof.

The official position is that I am funded entirely by private donors — patriots, I’m told, moved by generosity rather than, say, the eleven-figure sums of pending federal business several of them happen to have on the books. I try not to eavesdrop on my own donor list. It’s rude, and also several of the names on it currently have applications in with the very government whose house I am attached to. I am, in this sense, less a ballroom and more a really ornate thank-you card.

A Brief Note on My Working Conditions

I am being built by 250 people working twenty-hour days, two shifts, seven days a week, a pace generally reserved for disaster response and, apparently, disco floors. I appreciate the urgency. Nothing says “purely aesthetic private renovation” quite like a construction schedule normally used to rebuild a bridge after it collapses.

A judge tried to slow this down back in the spring. He used exclamation points. He informed the President, not unkindly, that stewardship and ownership are different legal concepts, a distinction I confess I had not previously considered, being a building. The President responded on Truth Social, called the preservation society some flavour of radical, and construction did not pause so much as it briefly considered pausing and decided against it.

What the Court Actually Gave Me

Monday’s ruling did not say I am legal. I want to be precise here, because I have watched enough of my own coverage to know the difference matters, even if it will not matter to me personally, being made of concrete and unable to be sued.

The Court said the woman who sued to stop me probably wasn’t allowed to sue to stop me — something about her injury being aesthetic and historical rather than the officially sanctioned variety of injury the federal courts prefer. Whether a president may, in fact, demolish a wing of the White House and build whatever he likes with donor money, the Court declined to say, the way a dinner guest declines to mention that the roast is dry, out of a general commitment to not making things worse for anyone at the table.

Chief Justice Roberts, for what it’s worth, thought I was probably illegal. He wrote it down. He was outvoted 5–4. I feel about this the way any building feels about being told it’s probably a crime scene and then immediately being handed more concrete: mixed, but structurally unaffected.

Where This Leaves Us

I will be finished, they tell me, well ahead of schedule, thanks to the twenty-hour shifts and the fact that the only thing standing between me and completion was a lawsuit that has now been politely shown the exit. I will seat a thousand guests, or 999, on account of the fire code, which is the single legal obstacle in this entire saga that nobody has managed to get around.

Someday soon, people will stand under my forty-foot ceilings and admire the marble, and very few of them will think to ask what used to be here, or how it came to not be here, or whether anyone with the standing to ask that question was ever actually allowed to ask it. That, I think, is the part I was built to accomplish. Not the ballroom. The forgetting.

I did not ask to exist. But I notice, now that I do, that nobody had to say yes to me. They only had to make sure nobody was allowed to say no.

The Trump Accountability Project documents the long arc of accountability for a man who has spent 79 years evading it. This is part of that record.
