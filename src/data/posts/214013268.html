This is a new series, and it deserves one paragraph of introduction before it gets to work. Tesla has launched Cybercab in Austin, and this launch actually prompted me to look at the broader picture worldwide. Everything Tesla-specific — Cybercab, Optimus, the chip roadmap, the stock — has a home in the Musk Stack Substack series. Everything about AI safety, governance, and consciousness has a home in the Asimov Gap. This series is about the rest of it: the global robotaxi race being run by companies that aren’t Tesla, in countries that aren’t the United States, at a pace and with a volatility most American coverage of this industry still hasn’t caught up to. It starts, appropriately, with the country running furthest ahead — and with the crisis that nearly stopped it cold.

The numbers nobody in the American conversation seems to know

Baidu’s Apollo Go — the clear leader among China’s robotaxi operators — runs in more than 20 Chinese cities, including Wuhan, Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, Chongqing, and Guangzhou, CnEVPost has reported. By the end of October 2025, its global autonomous driving mileage had passed 240 million kilometers across more than 17 million orders. By the first half of 2026, cumulative service volume had crossed 100 million rides. Robin Li, Baidu’s co-founder and chief executive, has said the company’s ambition is 65 cities and eventually 100 by 2030, with tens of thousands of vehicles on the road and fares eventually landing at roughly half what a traditional taxi costs today. China, taken as a whole, now hosts more active commercial robotaxi deployments than the United States does — a claim worth measuring against the country most American readers assume leads this race. Waymo’s entire national fleet numbers around 4,000 vehicles, with roughly 1,600 of them operating across a 707-square-kilometer San Francisco Bay Area footprint serving 3.5 million residents, as analyst Junko Yoshida has documented. Pony.ai alone runs about 1,700 robotaxis across a comparably sized 2,048-square-kilometer geofenced zone in China’s own Greater Bay Area, serving 10.4 million residents — more than triple Waymo’s home-market population, on a single Chinese operator’s fleet that isn’t even the largest of the three.

The head start behind those numbers is a decade old, not a recent surge. Baidu first achieved full autonomous driving under mixed city and highway conditions in late 2015 — before most Americans had heard the word robotaxi. What’s happened since is the compounding effect of ten years of testing, regulatory iteration, and fleet deployment that Western coverage, fixated on whichever single-city pilot dominates the news cycle at home, has never fully priced in.

The morning a city’s robotaxis stopped moving

None of that scale insulated the industry from its worst public moment to date. South China Morning Post reported that on March 31, 2026, more than 100 of Baidu’s Apollo Go vehicles in Wuhan simultaneously malfunctioned during evening rush hour — a cloud-system failure that left passengers stranded in the middle of the road for up to two hours and snarled traffic across the city. No injuries were reported. The optics were brutal regardless: a fleet marketed as intelligent, sitting frozen and driverless in the street, with nobody behind any wheel able to simply pull over and wait it out.

Beijing’s response was swift and national in scope. On April 29, 2026, China suspended the issuance of new autonomous-driving permits across the entire country — not just Wuhan, not just Baidu, but every operator, covering fleet expansions, new pilot programs, and new city-level launches nationwide. The freeze held for roughly three months, lifted in late July following what regulators described only as a completed industry-wide safety review, the specific findings of which have not been made public. China has since resumed issuing permits, phased back in gradually rather than all at once.

What’s notable is how differently the three major operators weathered the freeze. Caixin Global reported that WeRide, whose technical architecture is less centrally dependent on the kind of cloud infrastructure that failed Baidu, kept expanding through the pause — reaching approximately 1,000 domestic vehicles by late April 2026, adding roughly 200 in the weeks immediately following the freeze, apparently without triggering the same regulatory scrutiny. Pony.ai likewise kept adding vehicles, reaching more than 1,700 by late May and raising its year-end 2026 fleet target from 3,000 to 3,500, with CFO Wang Haojun framing the company’s strategy as concentrating first on dense first-tier cities before pushing into second-tier markets. The incident, in other words, didn’t just cost Baidu three months of new-market growth — it sorted the field, rewarding operators whose systems failed more gracefully and punishing the one whose system failed all at once, in public, during rush hour.

The standard nobody in Washington has matched yet

China’s regulatory answer to the Wuhan incident wasn’t limited to a temporary freeze. Junko Yoshida has reported that this year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology approved GB 44721—2026, a mandatory national safety standard for automated driving systems in Class M and Class N intelligent connected vehicles, with implementation set for July 1, 2027. That is a single, unified, national technical standard — the kind of thing the United States, where autonomous-vehicle regulation remains a fragmented patchwork of state-by-state pilot programs and city-level permitting fights, does not yet have and shows no near-term sign of producing. China’s regulatory instinct, observed by analysts covering the Wuhan freeze, runs toward decisive, fast, top-down correction — freeze first, investigate, then codify a permanent national rule — rather than the incremental, litigation-prone, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction process that governs Waymo, Cruise, and Tesla’s robotaxi ambitions in the U.S. Whether that model produces better safety outcomes over the long run is an open question this series will keep tracking. What isn’t in question is that it produces a faster, more centrally coordinated regulatory response than anything currently on offer in Washington, Sacramento, or Austin.

The jobs question China can’t outrun either

The freeze wasn’t purely a technical response. China’s robotaxi expansion has run into the same labor backlash this Substack has already tracked extensively on the American side of the AI boom — just with a different regulatory reflex attached to it. Baidu learned this in 2024, when Apollo Go’s below-market pricing in Wuhan provoked a public letter from a local taxi company warning that the technology, rather than improving lives, was starving people at the bottom of the labor market, as The Wire China detailed. The municipal government responded by dialing back its support and slowing Apollo Go’s citywide expansion — a full two years before the Wuhan cloud failure gave regulators a second, more dramatic reason to hit pause. China’s own youth unemployment data, tracked by Bloomberg, adds real weight to that anxiety: joblessness among 25-to-29-year-olds climbed to 7.7 percent in March 2026, up from 7.2 percent a year earlier and the highest figure since the National Bureau of Statistics revamped its employment reporting. A state simultaneously pushing to dominate AI and robotics while presiding over rising youth unemployment has an obvious incentive to slow-walk exactly the kind of labor displacement robotaxis represent — the same tension this Substack’s Capex Ledger series has documented repeatedly in American data-center and AI-labor contexts, arriving here in a Chinese industrial-policy register instead.

Tony Han, WeRide’s founder and chief executive, addressed the safety half of that tension directly at the Fortune Global Forum in Riyadh this year, per Fortune’s reporting, arguing that autonomous vehicles will likely never reach zero risk but should be roughly ten times safer than human drivers within a decade — an argument for patience and proportion, delivered by the executive whose company came through the Wuhan freeze with the least damage. Whether Chinese regulators, taxi unions, and an anxious labor market grant the industry that decade is the more interesting question, and not one this series can answer yet.

Three companies, three different bets, one shared setback

Apollo Go’s advantage remains scale and data — the largest fleet, the deepest operating history, and the resulting cost curve, now recovering from its worst quarter. Pony.ai is running a different playbook entirely: partnerships with established automakers, including Toyota and China’s BAIC — its GAC Toyota joint venture has already produced a mass-market robotaxi built on Toyota’s bZ4X EV platform — positioning itself as a licensable autonomy stack rather than a single-brand operator, the same model Wayve is pursuing in Britain.

WeRide has taken the most diversified route of the three, running Robobus shuttles at Resorts World Sentosa in Singapore, at Roland-Garros in Paris, and across multiple sites in Riyadh, alongside its core robotaxi business — a diversification that, this spring, turned out to double as insurance against exactly the kind of single-point-of-failure event that hit Baidu.

The underlying economics, freeze notwithstanding, have genuinely turned a corner. Early autonomous vehicles, weighed down by expensive LiDAR arrays and redundant safety computing, cost more than one million yuan each — roughly $139,000 per car. In the first half of 2026, unit economics crossed the breakeven line in specific Chinese cities for the first time, driven by improving ride density and utilization rather than a single hardware breakthrough. That inflection, not the Wuhan incident, is the more durable story: a technology that spent a decade being expensive and experimental became cheap and commercial in the same twelve-month window that also produced its most public failure to date.

Where this connects to what this Substack has already covered

Readers of Quiet War, this Substack’s six-part account of China’s March 2026 Five-Year Plan, will recognize the machinery underneath this expansion without this essay needing to re-explain it. That series documented the state subsidies — more than $20 billion — flowing into China’s humanoid robotics sector, and the industrial-policy logic of treating automation as compensation for a shrinking workforce rather than a threat to existing jobs. Robotaxis are not humanoid robots, and this series will not re-tread Quiet War’s ground on that architecture. But the same national posture toward automation — build first, regulate decisively when something breaks, treat scale as the primary competitive asset — runs directly beneath everything in this essay, from Robin Li’s 100-city target to the speed of the April freeze and the July restart.

The route that matters next

All three companies are already moving well past their home market, freeze or no freeze. Gasgoo’s AutoNews has reported that Apollo Go partnered with Dubai’s Roads and Transport Authority in March 2025 for its first fleet outside China, targets 100 fully autonomous vehicles there by the end of 2026 and 1,000 by 2028, and has separately announced plans — through partnerships with Uber and Lyft — to begin unmanned testing and service in London before this year is out. Pony.ai is pursuing the Middle East and Southeast Asia in parallel, with testing permits already secured in Dubai, Doha, and Abu Dhabi. WeRide’s Grab partnership has already put an 11-vehicle autonomous shuttle fleet on the road in Singapore’s Punggol district — the city’s first autonomous service in a residential area — with Southeast Asian expansion continuing from there.

The London detail is the one worth sitting with longest, because it means this series’ next essay — on Uber and Wayve’s robotaxi debut in London this week — is not actually a story about Britain’s AV market in isolation. It’s the opening chapter of the same city becoming the next contested ground between a British AI start-up, an American ride-hailing giant, and at least one Chinese operator that has already said, on the record, that London is next. This series will take that story on its own terms when it arrives. For now: China didn’t just start first, and it didn’t even need a flawless run to stay ahead. It survived its worst month yet, wrote a national rule out of it, and kept expanding anyway — which may be the more instructive story than the head start itself.
