On a morning in March 2019, Kilmar Abrego Garcia went to a Home Depot parking lot in Hyattsville, Maryland, looking for day labour. This is what construction workers without papers do in Prince George’s County — they stand in parking lots and wait for someone to offer them a day’s work. It is a scene you can find outside any Home Depot or Lowe’s in the greater Washington area on any given morning: men in work boots, nursing coffees from the 7-Eleven next door, waiting for a contractor’s truck to pull up. Abrego Garcia was standing with three other men he recognised from previous mornings at the same spot. As a NewsNation investigation would later document, his lawyers said “he had never interacted with them in any other context.” A police officer pulled over to question them. He described them as “loitering.”

What happened next would determine the next seven years of his life. Prince George’s County police arrested three of the four men. The evidence for determining Kilmar Abrego Garcia’s gang affiliation, as documented on a Gang Field Interview Sheet, was as follows: he was wearing a Chicago Bulls hat. He was also wearing a hoodie that displayed rolls of money covering the eyes, ears, and mouths of different presidents — imagery that officers wrote was “indicative of the Hispanic gang culture” and represented the MS-13 code of “ver, oír y callar” — see no evil, hear no evil, say no evil. As The Intercept reported in its examination of the evidence, officers noted that “wearing the Chicago Bulls hat represents that they are a member in good standing with the MS-13.”

Day laborers waiting outside a Home Depot in the Washington area. This is where Kilmar Abrego Garcia stood on an ordinary morning in March 2019 — until a police stop turned a Chicago Bulls hat into alleged proof of MS-13 membership.

A Chicago Bulls hat. You can buy one at Foot Locker. You can buy one on Amazon. Michael Jordan wore one. Scottie Pippen wore one. Millions of people across North America wear one because they like basketball, or because they grew up in the nineties, or because they found it on a sale rack and it fit. In Prince George’s County, Maryland, in March 2019, wearing one while standing in a Home Depot parking lot was enough to launch a gang field interview that would, six years later, reach the United States Supreme Court.

A confidential informant then told police that Abrego Garcia was a member of MS-13’s “Western” clique and held the rank of “Chequeo” with the street name “Chele.” As the Baltimore Banner documented in its investigation of the gang evidence, this informant was described by police as a “past, proven, and reliable source of information.” There was one problem with this identification, among several: the Western clique operates out of New York. Abrego Garcia had never lived in New York. Had never been to New York. Had never, by any account in the record, set foot in the state. His lawyers would later note that the government’s own evidence placed him in a gang chapter based in a city he had never visited, in a state he had no connection to, operating under a street name that no one who actually knew him had ever used.

The “evidence” that wasn’t: a Chicago Bulls hat and a hoodie with money imagery. Police interpreted these as MS-13 indicators. Federal judges later described the entire file as “nothing more than his Chicago Bulls hat and hoodie.

He was never charged with a crime. Two water bottles filled with cannabis were found at the scene but were not attributed to him or any specific individual. He had $1,178 on him — not an ICE criterion for gang activity, but noted in his record nonetheless, as though carrying cash while Hispanic were itself an indicator. He was turned over to immigration authorities.

It is worth pausing here to understand the machinery that was now in motion. Gang databases in the United States are notoriously unreliable. Across the country, people have been entered into gang databases on the basis of their clothing, their neighbourhood, their associates, their social media activity, and — in documented cases — their race and ethnicity. Once you are in a gang database, getting out is extraordinarily difficult. The designation follows you through every subsequent interaction with law enforcement and immigration authorities. It becomes, in effect, a permanent mark — not a conviction, not even a charge, but a label that carries the weight of both without any of the procedural protections that either would require.

Abrego Garcia was now in the database. And that database entry would follow him from a Home Depot parking lot in Hyattsville to a federal prison in El Salvador.

What followed was an eighteen-month legal odyssey through immigration court. Abrego Garcia was held in detention. He conceded his deportability — he was, after all, in the country without authorization — and applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The government argued he should remain detained, relying principally on the gang designation. The immigration judge — Judge Elizabeth Kessler — kept him locked up, finding that the confidential informant’s identification was “sufficient to support that the Respondent is a gang member.” But Judge Kessler also noted, as ABC News reported in its examination of the conflicting pictures of Abrego Garcia, that various representations given by the police were “at odds” with one another. The evidence pointed in multiple directions at once — strong enough to detain, too weak to charge.

In June 2019, while still in detention, Abrego Garcia married Jennifer Vasquez Sura through the glass barrier of his visitation room at Howard Detention Center in Maryland. He had missed the birth of their child. Think about that for a moment — not as a legal detail but as a human one. A man in an immigration detention cell, pressing his hand against glass, speaking vows to a woman on the other side, while somewhere in Prince George’s County a baby he has never held is sleeping in a crib. This is what the machinery of the state looks like at the human scale. It looks like a wedding through glass.

As Judge Xinis would later write in her memorandum opinion in the Maryland civil case, the evidence against Abrego Garcia consisted of “nothing more than his Chicago Bulls hat and hoodie, and a vague, uncorroborated allegation from a confidential informant.” That is a federal judge, appointed under Article III of the Constitution, reviewing the sum total of what the United States government had compiled against this man and finding it to be, essentially, nothing.

On October 10, 2019, following a full evidentiary hearing in which Abrego Garcia offered what the immigration judge found to be credible testimony, he was granted withholding of removal. The judge found that he had demonstrated a “well-founded fear of future persecution” from gangs in El Salvador — the very gangs that had driven his family from their pupusa shop, that had threatened his sisters with rape, that had chased him across neighbourhoods and borders and countries. The ruling did not grant him asylum. It did not give him a green card. It did not make him a permanent resident or put him on a path to citizenship. It conferred only one right: the government could not send him to El Salvador. It could, in theory, deport him to any other country willing to accept him. But El Salvador — the country where gangs had targeted him as a child, the country he had fled at sixteen — was off the table. A judge had said so. The law was clear.

As TIME reported in its investigation of what happened next, the Trump administration — the first Trump administration — had thirty days to appeal the ruling. The Bureau of Immigration Appeals database logs the status of each case by a unique identifier called an A-Number, short for Alien Registration Number. Abrego Garcia’s entry shows his application for withholding of removal was granted on October 10, 2019. Below that is a simple notation: “No appeal was received for this case.”

Think about that. The same administration that would later insist Abrego Garcia was a dangerous MS-13 operative, that would brand him on social media as a gang member and a human trafficker and a predator, looked at the evidence in 2019 and decided it wasn’t worth contesting. The hat. The hoodie. The informant from a state he’d never visited. They examined the case, they weighed their options, and they let it go. They let it go because the evidence was not there. It was not there in 2019, and it would not be there in 2025, and it would not be there in 2026 when a federal judge in Tennessee would dismiss the indictment they built on it.

Abrego Garcia was released. He went home to Maryland. He went back to work in construction — eventually as a union sheet metal apprentice, a skilled trade, a job with a future. He checked in with immigration authorities annually, as required by the terms of his withholding of removal. He was, for five years, exactly what the system intended him to be: a man with a fragile but real legal status, living within the rules, showing up when told to show up, working, raising children, paying taxes into a system that had not decided whether he belonged to it.

And then, on March 12, 2025, while driving home from work with his five-year-old son in the car, ICE agents pulled him over in an Ikea parking lot and told him his immigration status had changed. Three days later, he was on a plane to the one country on earth that a federal judge had specifically prohibited the United States government from sending him to.

Immigration court. A federal judge granted withholding of removal in 2019, ruling that Kilmar faced a well-founded fear of persecution in El Salvador. The Trump administration reviewed the case — and chose not to appeal.
