In Westminster, the opposition has a simple fate. If it wins, it crosses the floor and becomes the government. Its leader moves into Number 10, or 24 Sussex, or wherever the job now sleeps, and the people who spent years asking the questions start answering them. There is no third option.

The United States has invented one. In January 2027, the Democrats will hold the gavels in both houses of Congress, and Donald Trump will still hold the executive branch. They will be the majority and still the opposition, with the power to subpoena but not to govern, to investigate but not to enforce. Westminster has no name for that. This series, which has spent fifteen essays asking whether the Democrats could function as a loyal opposition, now has to ask a stranger question: what does a loyal opposition do once it has won?

The scale of it

Start with what happened on November 3, 2026. As Isaac Stanley-Becker reported in The Atlantic on November 10, 2026, Democrats won their largest popular-vote margin in House races since 1976, when voters were also furious about prices and government malfeasance, and when Watergate was the word for both.

Two races mattered to this series more than any others. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed, whose one-point primary win over Haley Stevens opened Part I, beat the Republican Mike Rogers. With 85 per cent of the vote counted, the Associated Press called it at 51.9 to 46.1, the Michigan Advance reported. In Maine, Troy Jackson, the logger who replaced Graham Platner as nominee in July and whose party leadership troubles filled Part IV, defeated Susan Collins after nearly thirty years, as the Associated Press reported on November 4.

Then, on November 11, eight days after everyone else, came Mississippi. Scott Colom, a district attorney from Columbus, defeated Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 4,700 votes after more than a week of counting affidavit and mail ballots, The New York Times reported. No Democrat had won a Senate race in Mississippi since 1982, and Trump carried the state by 23 points two years ago. Colom becomes the first Black senator Mississippi’s voters have ever chosen; Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce, who held seats during Reconstruction, were picked by the state legislature. Three years ago Hyde-Smith blocked his nomination to the federal bench. He ran on wages, prices and health care, made a viral ad out of her advice that voters facing expensive beef should pick another protein, and courted Republicans openly, even working the line at a Trump rally. He said afterwards that he had always believed there were voters “who loved our state more than they loved a political party.” His win gives Democrats 55 Senate seats, with Alaska still being counted.

The candidates whom the party establishment feared were unelectable were elected, and so was one it had barely noticed.

Part XV ended on the eve of the vote with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s remark about El-Sayed, and the claim behind it that the right Democrat could win anybody, anywhere. The results suggest she was closer to right than her critics. They do not settle the argument this series has tracked since August. They move it.

Lawyer up

The new argument is about what to do with the win, and it began on election night.

Representative Robert Garcia, who is set to chair the House Oversight Committee, issued a statement two words long: “Lawyer up.” At a Crooked Media convention the following weekend he joked that, being gay, pettiness came naturally to him. “I’m mad,” he said, and then corrected himself: it was not pettiness but “a desire for real justice.” Garcia has promised oversight in four areas: what he calls the “Trump Crime Family,” abuses at ICE and Homeland Security, corporate corruption, and the handling of the Epstein files. Representative Analilia Mejia of New Jersey went further on social media. “We’re going to toss out subpoenas like Mardi Gras beads,” she wrote.

That is one Democratic Party. Here is the other.

“I am not interested in retribution,” James Talarico, the new senator from Texas, told Fox News. “The people of Texas did not elect me last night for retribution.” Adam Hamilton, the Methodist pastor who won in Kansas, said his goal was to become Republican senator Jerry Moran’s best friend. Bernie Sanders, the movement’s elder, warned that accountability must not crowd out the economic agenda: “We cannot spend all of our time investigating Trump.” And Chuck Schumer, who expects to be majority leader, told The New York Times that “if we just did corruption, we’d be a loser.”

The same split opened within days over Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the health secretary whose vaccine policy most Democrats abhor. Senator Angela Alsobrooks of Maryland wrote that he "must resign or be fired. End of discussion." Annie Andrews, the pediatrician elected to the Senate from South Carolina, ran on impeaching him. Tim Ryan, the former Ohio congressman, answered Alsobrooks directly: "This makes no sense. Why wouldn't we work together to see what wins we can get?" His point, as Sheryl Gay Stolberg reported for The New York Times on November 12, is that Kennedy's food and environmental agenda, stalled by his own White House, has a natural home with Democrats and with the health-focused voters who helped elect Trump. Even Chellie Pingree of Maine, a regenerative farmer, wanted to ration the fight: "if there are any impeachments, it should start with Secretary Hegseth." Kennedy, for his part, has decided the defeat freed him. "The Senate no longer has any leverage over me," he told supporters.

Readers of this series will recognise the shape. In August it was the fight between purity and coalition (Part I). In September it was the fight between the big tent (Part VII) and the wrecking ball (Part XIII). The question now is whether the party fights or governs. The same people are mostly on the same sides.

What the swing seats said

The best evidence for the cautious side comes from before the election. Matt Viser reported for The Atlantic on October 2, 2026 on a survey of likely voters in the 37 districts that the Cook Political Report then rated as competitive. Only 21 per cent wanted a candidate who would be a check on Trump. Thirty-five per cent wanted someone who would work with him. The largest group, 43 per cent, wanted someone who would focus on changing the political system altogether.

On its face, that is a warning against a season of subpoenas. Representative Jason Crow of Colorado, one of the managers of Trump’s first impeachment, drew that lesson. Impeachment animated the base, he told Viser, but it did not achieve its goal. “We’re going to be methodical about it; we’re going to be intentional about it,” he said. “We can’t do everything at once.” Suzanne Bonamici of Oregon offered a word for the mood: not retribution, she said, but a reckoning.

Read the number again, though. The plurality did not ask for compromise with Trump. It asked for the system to change. That is not an argument against investigations. It is an argument about which investigations, and why.

Corruption as a cost of living

This is where the winners of 2026 think they have found something the losers of 2018 did not.

Garcia put it in a single sentence to Stanley-Becker: taking on corruption “will make the lives of American families more affordable,” because it will “stop the Trumps from stealing from you and giving to themselves.” Jamie Raskin, the incoming chair of House Judiciary, reached further back. The worst price increases of the past two years, he argued, came from the war in Iran and Trump’s tariff war, both of them usurpations of Congress’s constitutional authority. He compared it to the Boston Tea Party. Did the Sons of Liberty throw the tea in the harbour because of its price, or because of an unjust tax and a state-backed monopoly? Both, said Raskin. “A corrupt political system makes life unaffordable for the vast majority of people.”

The new attorney general of Texas, Nathan Johnson, told Stanley-Becker the same thing from the campaign trail. The anti-corruption message worked, he said, because voters feel like prey: "There's a general feeling that we're all just prey and don't have control over our own economic lives." Johnson is expanding his office and hiring experts in consumer protection, antitrust and public contracting. He insists he is not on a mission to take down Elon Musk, who spent about $226 million on Texas races and lost every statewide contest. But there will be no force field around him either.

This is the fusion that the 43 per cent were asking for. It is not a choice between investigating Trump and lowering prices. It is a claim that the investigations explain the prices: crypto fortunes, pardons for donors, contracts for friends, gas prices set by companies the administration helped into Venezuela. Garcia wants to expose exactly that last one.

It also gives the cautious wing something to say yes to. Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to be speaker, put price gouging at the top of his letter announcing his bid. Garcia has added surveillance pricing, the practice of using personal data and AI to charge different customers different prices. Schumer, having warned against doing only corruption, has a bill to break up the meatpacking monopolies. Elizabeth Warren, in line to chair Senate Banking, has an anti-corruption package that would ban members of Congress, senior officials and judges from trading individual stocks. These are investigations and bills a Kansas pastor could vote for.

Bernie at the centre

The most striking feature of the new majority may be where Bernie Sanders now sits.

Russell Berman reported for The Atlantic on October 27, 2026 that Sanders, at 85, was in more demand in the final weeks than ever before. YouGov ranked him the most popular sitting elected official in America. He endorsed more than 80 candidates, and by his aides’ count nearly three in four won their primaries. He spent October in Maine for Jackson and gave $1 million to help El-Sayed in Michigan. Both won.

What matters for this series is not that Sanders was popular. It is how he used it. In House races he refused to demand purity. Several of his candidates were also endorsed by the Blue Dogs, the old fiscal hawks, a combination Berman reports has been nicknamed the “Bernie Dogs.” When Berman asked whether he worried about diluting his brand, Sanders said there was no litmus test. Of Rebecca Cooke, who is not running on Medicare for All in a conservative Wisconsin district, he said that she knew her district and he trusted her judgement. If she were running in New York City, he added, it might be different.

That is the big tent from Part VII, practised by the man who was supposed to be its enemy. It is also a reminder that Sanders has not gone soft. He told Berman that the Democratic Party was “significantly responsible for the rise of Trumpism.” He is in line to chair the Senate health committee in the new Democratic majority. And all four of his marquee Senate candidates, Jackson, El-Sayed, Peggy Flanagan and Angie Nixon, have called for replacing Schumer as leader. Sanders himself stopped short of that. He said he would back a leader who would fight for Medicare for All. Schumer has not.

So the warning that “we cannot spend all of our time investigating Trump” does not come from a moderate trying to protect donors. It comes from a populist who wants the time for something else.

The 1976 problem

There is a reason Stanley-Becker reached for 1976, and it is not encouraging.

The last time a party won a House popular vote by a margin like this one, the Democrats went on to hold the House, the Senate and the White House. They reorganised the civil service and created independent inspectors general. Then they ran aground. Tax reform, national health insurance and a consumer-protection agency all failed, sunk by stagflation, business lobbying and a bitter quarrel between Jimmy Carter’s centrists and the party’s anti-corporate left. Ron Dellums, a California congressman, described the stakes that year in words that could have been said this autumn: money concentrated in a few hands, “with the rest of us forced to fight each other for a share of what’s left.”

The Democrats of 2027 will not even have a trifecta. They will face a lame-duck president to whom the Supreme Court has granted broad immunity, and a Justice Department led by Todd Blanche, who said on CNN that it will defend officials the Democrats investigate. Contempt of Congress requires a U.S. attorney to bring charges, and the one in Washington is Jeanine Pirro. The machinery of enforcement belongs to the people being investigated. When Democrats pick their targets, they are also picking the fights they can actually finish. The companion series When Democracy Fights Back will take up that enforcement problem on its own.

The first test

The coalition’s first test came sooner than anyone expected, and it was not about subpoenas. It was about attendance.

On November 10, a week after the election, the House took up Thomas Massie’s articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Republicans moved to send them to the Judiciary Committee, where impeachments go to die, and won 211 to 204. Eleven Democrats missed the vote, as Robert Jimison reported in The New York Times. Their reasons ranged from a medical procedure and a personal emergency to rush-hour traffic after a fundraiser near the Capitol hosted by Katherine Clark, the party’s chief vote-counter. Nancy Pelosi was in California collecting a lifetime achievement award. One of the absentees was Haley Stevens, whose primary defeat by El-Sayed opened this series. El-Sayed’s verdict on CNN was blunt: “Show up and do your job.”

The arithmetic softens the embarrassment. Seven Republicans were absent too; had everyone turned up and voted with their party, Hegseth would still have survived. Pat Ryan of New York checked himself out of hospital after back surgery to cast his vote. But Hakeem Jeffries admitted that night that he did not yet know what had happened, and Clark opened the next morning’s caucus by scolding her colleagues. For a party about to ask voters to trust it with the gavels, it was the wrong week to lose track of its own members.

The other half of the story was on the Republican side. Massie, beaten in his primary by a Trump-backed challenger, had offered his colleagues what he called a life raft: a vote that would put some daylight between them and the president. Not one Republican climbed aboard, Catie Edmondson reported in The New York Times. Afterwards Richard Ojeda, a Democratic representative-elect from North Carolina, found Massie in a hallway and promised, with an expletive, that the new majority would finish the job next session.

That is the coalition’s problem in miniature. The other side is still bound to Trump. Whether this side can count to a majority on the days that matter is, after that Tuesday, an open question.

A Westminster footnote

From Canada, there is one more thing to say about the word at the top of this series.

A loyal opposition is loyal to the Crown, not to the government. Its job is to oppose the ministry of the day without ever questioning the legitimacy of the system it shares. In Ottawa or London, the opposition’s investigations are an ordinary part of politics. Ministers expect them and, by and large, answer them.

What the new Democratic majority faces is a president who has said, in so many words, that he regards investigation as persecution, and who has spent two years demonstrating that he will not answer. In that situation the loyal choice is not restraint for its own sake. It is to investigate in a way that the 43 per cent can recognise as fixing the system, not settling scores, and that the 21 per cent can recognise as a check. Garcia’s “lawyer up” and Talarico’s “not retribution” are both, in their way, attempts at the same job.

Part I warned that primary purity is not the same as electability. On November 3 the impure coalition won: Sanders’s populists, Hamilton’s pastors, Talarico’s seminarian and the Blue Dogs, together. The test now is whether it can stay together through two years of subpoenas, stonewalling and the temptation to mistake a big win for a mandate to do everything at once. Jason Crow has already said they can’t. He may be the most important Democrat in the room. Katherine Clark, who counts the votes, may be the second.
