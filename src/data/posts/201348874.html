They promised us flying cars.

That is the standard complaint, delivered with a rueful shrug, whenever someone wants to capture the gap between the future we were sold and the one we actually received. The Jetsons premiered on September 23, 1962 — twenty-four episodes, one season, an animated family floating above Orbit City in bubble-domed vehicles while Rosie the Robot handled the housework with Brooklyn-accented wit. It was set in 2062, one hundred years hence. The show’s creators gave themselves a full century of runway. They imagined flat-screen televisions, video calls, smart watches, talking alarm clocks, and a robotic domestic servant who could think for herself and learn new things. They got most of it right, and on an accelerated schedule that would have astonished them.

Rosie, though. Rosie they did not get right — not yet, not quite. She remains the unfulfilled promise, the benchmark against which the actual future keeps being measured and found wanting. We have the Roomba. We have the warehouse robot. We have the surgical arm operating with sub-millimetre precision inside a human body. But we do not yet have Rosie: a machine that moves through our domestic world with grace and judgment and personality, that knows when to make coffee and when to stay out of the way.

We are working on it. That is rather the point.

The Machine That Watched Tom Swift

Before The Jetsons there was Tom Swift — boy inventor, American archetype, hero of a dime novel series that began in 1910 and has never entirely stopped publishing. Tom Swift built electric runabouts and airships and photo telephones and undersea laboratories. He solved problems with ingenuity and optimism and a complete absence of existential dread about what he was creating. The machines in Tom Swift’s world were unambiguously good, extensions of human capability, servants of human purpose. The question was never should Tom build this. The question was always how.

That pre-anxiety relationship with technology feels almost foreign now. We have spent decades training ourselves to lead with the risk assessment. Every new technology arrives trailing a cloud of op-eds about what could go wrong. This is not entirely unreasonable — there are genuine things that could go wrong — but it has produced a cultural habit of meeting the future with suspicion before curiosity, with fear before wonder.

This series proposes a different starting position. Not naivety. Not the uncritical boosterism of a press release. But wonder first, analysis second — the Tom Swift orientation, updated for an era in which the machines are doing things Tom Swift never imagined and the ethical questions are considerably more complicated than anything he faced.

The future we were promised has arrived. It just arrived quietly, through the back door, assembled from off-the-shelf components in a grandparents’ backyard pool in Burlington, Ontario. It arrived in university labs where researchers watched geckos climb glass walls and thought: what if we could do that? It arrived in Ukrainian fields where drones built in sheds for a fraction of imported costs are rewriting the rules of warfare. It arrived in operating theatres where robotic arms complete surgeries with a precision no human hand can sustain across a six-hour procedure.

It did not announce itself with flying cars. It rarely announces itself at all. That is what these postcards are for.

What This Series Is, and What It Is Not

Postcards from Tomorrow is a companion series to The Asimov Gap. The relationship between them is deliberate and structural, not incidental.

The Asimov Gap asks the governance questions: what rules should we write for the machines we are building? Who is accountable when they fail? What does it mean that our legal and ethical frameworks were designed for a world that no longer exists? It operates in the legislature, the courtroom, the policy chamber. It is interested in the gap between the pace of technological development and the pace of human wisdom about how to manage it — the gap that Isaac Asimov intuited in 1942 when he sat down to write the Three Laws of Robotics for a pulp fiction magazine and accidentally produced the most durable framework for machine ethics that anyone has yet managed.

Postcards from Tomorrow goes into the field. It is interested in what is actually being built, by whom, and with what animating philosophy. Every postcard raises, at least implicitly, the governance questions that The Asimov Gap addresses directly. Read together, the two series form a call and response: here is what is being built; here is what we need to think about because of it.

But this series has its own distinct register. It is despatches from a place rather than arguments from a position. Each essay is built around something specific — a machine, a technology, a person, a moment — and uses that specific thing as a lens for looking at something larger. The specific is always earned before the general is claimed.

The series will range widely. From the garage to the operating theatre. From the factory floor to the ocean floor. From the wheat field to the battlefield. From the kitchen table to the lunar surface. The Canadian thread will run through it — not parochially, but because this country has a robotics story that is both genuinely significant and remarkably underreported, and because the outsider’s perch has always been the most useful place from which to observe the future taking shape.

What this series is not: a technical manual. A product review. A corporate press release dressed up as journalism. An exercise in techno-panic that treats every new machine as a harbinger of dystopia. The dystopia conversation is real and necessary — The Asimov Gap engages it directly — but it is not the only conversation available, and it should not crowd out the one that starts with curiosity.

The Asimov Connection

Isaac Asimov invented the word robotics. He was twenty-two years old, a Russian-born American biochemist moonlighting as a science fiction writer, when he introduced the Three Laws in a 1942 short story called Runaround. The Laws were elegant, hierarchical, and — as Asimov himself demonstrated through the very stories he wrote about them — fundamentally insufficient. Every story in the I, Robot collection is essentially a proof that the Three Laws fail in interesting ways. Asimov was not writing a policy document. He was writing philosophy disguised as pulp fiction, exploring what happens when you try to reduce ethics to an algorithm.

Eighty-four years later, the largest technology companies in the world are actively wrestling with the same questions he posed in a magazine that cost fifteen cents. That is either a remarkable coincidence or evidence that Asimov understood something fundamental about the relationship between humans and intelligent machines — something that the rest of us are only now being forced to grapple with, because the machines have arrived and they are not waiting for us to finish the argument.

Asimov’s lasting gift to the imagination, beyond the Three Laws, was his insistence that robots would be interesting rather than merely threatening. His robots were curious, constrained, sometimes tragic, occasionally wiser than the humans around them. He asked what it means to be a mind in a body — whether that body is biological or mechanical. He refused the easy story in which the machine is simply the enemy. That refusal is this series’ inheritance.

Tom Swift Meets the Governance Problem

The Tom Swift orientation — wonder, ingenuity, optimism, a machine-as-tool rather than machine-as-threat — is not sufficient by itself. We know this now in ways that 1910 did not. The same robotic systems that perform delicate surgery are being adapted for autonomous warfare. The same AI that helps a fifteen-year-old in Burlington build an ocean monitoring platform is being used to design weapons that select their own targets. The same dexterity that allows a robot to harvest a strawberry without bruising it could allow it to do other things that require no elaboration here.

The wonder lane is not a lane that pretends the dark lanes do not exist. It is a lane that begins with curiosity rather than fear, because curiosity is the more accurate starting position. Most of what is being built in robotics right now is not threatening. Most of it is farmers trying to solve labour shortages, surgeons trying to reduce recovery times, teenagers watching turtles and asking why machines have to be so noisy. The darkness exists and will be examined, with care and with the ethical seriousness it demands. But it does not get to set the tone for every conversation.

That, finally, is what separates this series from the techno-panic genre that dominates so much coverage of emerging technology. Techno-panic requires the threat to be the protagonist. These postcards require the builder to be the protagonist — the specific human being, in a specific place, with a specific problem they decided to solve. The machines are interesting. The people building them are more interesting still.

What Is Coming

The postcards will arrive from many different addresses. From a backyard pool in Burlington where a teenager watched a snapping turtle and reimagined ocean monitoring. From the 1981 Space Shuttle mission that put a Canadian robotic arm into orbit for the first time, starting a lineage that now reaches toward the Moon. From Ukrainian fields where the economics of survival have produced the most rapid robotics innovation in the history of warfare. From Japanese elder care facilities where a demographic crisis is forcing a reckoning with what it means to ask a machine to keep a person company. From Boston Dynamics’ labs where engineers spent years teaching a robot to do a backflip before asking what the backflip was for. From the surgical theatre where the da Vinci platform has now assisted in more than two million procedures annually, with a wave of competitors accelerating behind it.

From Waterloo, Ontario, home to the largest robotics research cluster in Canada, where the future is being assembled with a Canadian quietness that the rest of the world has not yet learned to notice.

The first postcard is already written and waiting to post. It comes from Burlington and was the inspiration for this new series.
