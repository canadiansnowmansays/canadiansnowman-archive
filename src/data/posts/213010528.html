While Washington waited for three judges to write an opinion, San Francisco held a hearing that mattered more.

On July 30, 2026, U.S. District Judge Rita Lin convened a hearing on cross-motions for summary judgment in Anthropic PBC v. U.S. Department of War — the companion case to the one this series has followed through the D.C. Circuit, filed under a different statute in a different court, but built on the same conduct. Four months earlier, Lin had granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction and called the government’s theory “classic illegal First Amendment retaliation.” The question in July was whether that impression would survive a fuller record, cross-examined by lawyers on both sides instead of decided on emergency papers.

It did. It got worse for the government.

As Lawfare’s Roger Parloff live-blogged the hearing, Judge Lin opened not with a question but with an impression: having reviewed the full record, she said, it looked to her largely the same as what she’d assessed at the preliminary injunction stage. If anything, she said, it had gotten worse for the government’s position. She saw no additional evidence improving the Department’s case. And she wanted an answer to a question that had apparently been bothering her since March: was the government’s actual position that a contractor who criticizes it in public can be branded untrustworthy and cut off, full stop?

That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is, more or less, what happened.

The Hypothetical DOJ Couldn’t Answer

Much of the two-hour hearing turned on a single doctrinal question: does Pickering v. Board of Education — the framework that gives government more latitude to regulate the speech of its own employees and contractors — apply to what the Department of War did to Anthropic? DOJ attorney James Harlow said yes, arguing that terminating a contract is the kind of ordinary personnel-adjacent action Pickering was built for.

Judge Lin did not let this stand unexamined. She built a hypothetical, piece by piece, and walked Harlow through it in real time: suppose the government announced it was terminating every defense contract held by anyone who had ever publicly criticized the President. Suppose it said the reason was that criticism now made the contractor’s loyalty suspect. Does Pickering still apply?

Harlow said yes.

Lin pressed further: would that scheme violate the First Amendment?

Harlow’s answer amounted to it depends on the facts the government can present — he would not concede the obvious point even as a hypothetical, insisting he didn’t want to foreclose an argument in some future case. Lin told him, without much diplomatic cushioning, that she was surprised he couldn’t give a straight answer to what she considered an easy question.

This is worth sitting with. A senior Justice Department lawyer, given the chance to say that a blanket loyalty test attached to public criticism would be unconstitutional, would not say it. He said it depends. The hedge is the story. If the doctrine genuinely protected Anthropic here, saying so would have cost the government nothing in the hypothetical — the actual case could still be argued as different on the facts. The refusal to concede even the hypothetical is what a lawyer does when the real facts are close enough to the hypothetical that conceding it would be dangerous.

Michael Mongan, arguing for Anthropic, made the same point more bluntly: the reason the government’s lawyer wasn’t giving Lin clean answers, he said, was that “he’s a good lawyer” and recognizes his own hypotheticals might be the government’s next case.

The Secondary Boycott Problem

The hearing also revisited a thread from Part I of this series: the “Hegseth Directive,” issued February 27, which purported to bar any contractor doing business with the U.S. military from also doing commercial business with Anthropic — a secondary boycott reaching companies that had no contract with the Department of War at all.

Lin pushed Harlow on whether that kind of order — announced publicly, aimed at deterring an entire industry rather than managing one contractual relationship — falls outside Pickering altogether, into the territory of the government exercising raw regulatory power rather than acting as a mere contracting party. Harlow eventually conceded that, “to the extent” the action had no nexus to any actual government contract, it moves toward “regulatory” territory the Pickering framework wasn’t built for.

That concession matters more than its hedged phrasing lets on. The Hegseth Directive, as originally written, was precisely that: a demand that nobody, anywhere, do business with Anthropic if they wanted to keep working with the Pentagon. Lin noted that the social media post is still sitting in the public record, unretracted, even if the government now says it never took formal effect. Mongan’s side pointed out that this piece of the record — the announcement designed to deter, not just to sever one contract — is exactly what makes this case different from an ordinary Pickering dispute.

The Question DOJ Wasn’t Authorized to Answer

The most consequential exchange of the hearing, for readers of this series specifically, arrived at Judge Lin’s fourth prepared question: has any federal agency expanded its use of Claude since the supply-chain risk designation was issued?

This is the Mythos contradiction from Part III of this series, now asked from the bench. If the Department’s position is that Anthropic’s models cannot be trusted — that something might be embedded in them that could compromise military operations — then continued or expanding use of those same models by other parts of the national security apparatus is either evidence the designation was pretextual, or evidence the government doesn’t actually believe its own risk theory. Lin framed it almost exactly that way: didn’t it seem inconsistent, she asked, for the Department of War to treat Anthropic as a national-security threat while, as reported, other parts of government were expanding their use of the same technology?

Harlow’s response was the closest thing to a stumble in the entire transcript. He said the “Mythos model” — his own phrase, on the record — wasn’t reflected in the administrative record before the court, and that he was “not authorized to give a substantive response today.” He asked for a week to confer with his clients and submit something afterward.

Lin didn’t let the moment pass without naming what it implied: sometimes, she observed, a party’s conduct after the disputed decision reveals something about its actual intent at the time. That is precisely the inferential move this series made in Part III, watching from the outside, based on public reporting about Anthropic engineers working inside the NSA on Mythos deployment. Now a federal judge has made the same inference available to the record, and gotten the government’s own lawyer to confirm — under questioning, not through anonymous sourcing — that the arrangement exists and that the Department is not yet willing to explain it in open court.

Mongan, for his part, said Anthropic would prefer to litigate this to a final judgment rather than wait on a supplemental filing, but didn’t object to the government taking a week if the court wanted more.

The Wind-Down Nobody Can Quite Describe

Question three covered less dramatic but genuinely useful ground: what is actually happening to Anthropic’s government footprint, agency by agency, while all of this litigation grinds forward? Harlow’s answer was a portrait of an executive branch that cannot coordinate its own retreat. The Department of War, he said, continues winding down its use of Anthropic products, on a schedule slated to complete by September 30. Other agencies are on pilot programs set to expire August 30. But — and this is the part worth underlining — he conceded that “several” agencies are still using Anthropic’s tools, some through third-party cloud vendors, and that the federal government generally is still “dipping its toe” into AI tools broadly, agency by agency, without central coordination.

Mongan corrected the record slightly on Anthropic’s side: as far as the company can tell, most agencies have continued working with Anthropic since the injunction, only two have paused contracts, and none have cancelled outright. Anthropic, he said, has only secondhand information about the extent of any “wind-down,” because it isn’t privy to internal agency planning.

Put simply: nine months after Trump’s February 27 order commanding every federal agency to “IMMEDIATELY CEASE” using Anthropic’s technology, the government’s own lawyer cannot say with precision which agencies have actually stopped.

The Classified Question

One new factual detail surfaced that hadn’t previously been reported in this series: among the “articulable facts” DOJ says justified the supply-chain designation, Harlow cited a moment when a senior Anthropic official questioned, internally, whether it was appropriate to use Claude in connection with a specific ongoing military operation. Lin asked directly what the question had actually been. Harlow said the substance wasn’t suitable for the public record — it concerned an active operation — but insisted the mere fact that an Anthropic executive had asked the question at all was itself evidence of a company that saw itself positioned to exercise “corporate moral judgment” over how its product gets used.

Lin, unconvinced, asked whether the record foreclosed the far more mundane possibility that the question was simply, “is this a good use of the product.” Harlow said yes, the record foreclosed that reading — a claim that asks a court to infer bad faith from a single question whose content the government won’t disclose. Mongan’s response, delivered later, made the obvious point: whatever the question was, Anthropic’s CEO personally met with Hegseth to assure him the company had no intention of interfering with any ongoing operation, and that assurance is also part of the record.

What Comes Next

Judge Lin took the matter under submission at the close of the hearing and will issue a written order. Both sides spent the final stretch arguing over remedy — whether any ruling against the government should include a formal remand to the Department of War to attempt a lawful re-designation, or whether the underlying errors are severe enough that no such do-over is warranted. Mongan called it hard to imagine more serious errors than these. DOJ argued for remand without vacatur on at least the narrower procedural questions.

Meanwhile, the D.C. Circuit — the court whose oral argument produced Judge Henderson’s “spectacular overreach” line in May — still has not issued its written opinion, more than three months after argument. Two federal courts, two statutes, the same underlying conduct, and neither has yet produced the final word.

What the July 30 hearing adds to this record is not a new fact so much as a new witness to the facts already assembled here: a sitting federal judge, working from the government’s own briefing, arrived independently at the contradiction this series identified in Part III — that the Pentagon cannot simultaneously insist Anthropic’s models are too dangerous to trust and expand its own reliance on those same models inside the National Security Agency. The government’s lawyer, asked to explain that contradiction under oath, asked for a week.

The case is still not over. Neither, it turns out, is the government’s story.
