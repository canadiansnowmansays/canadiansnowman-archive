There is a story about the end of the Silk Road that appears in a great many textbooks, and it goes roughly like this: the Ottomans took Constantinople in 1453, closed the overland trade routes to European merchants, cut off the supply of eastern spices and silk, and thereby forced Portugal and Spain to seek a sea route to the Indies — producing, as an incidental consequence, the European encounter with the Americas. It is tidy, it supplies a satisfying causal chain from a single dramatic date, and it is substantially wrong.

The problems start with the premise. The Ottomans did not close the trade routes, and had no conceivable interest in doing so. An empire straddling the junction between Asia and Europe derives enormous revenue from taxing goods that pass through it, and the Ottomans taxed that traffic assiduously and encouraged its continuation. Trade in eastern goods through Ottoman territory did not stop after 1453; by most measures it grew substantially over the following century, with Aleppo, Bursa, and Cairo functioning as major entrepôts and Venetian, Genoese, and later French and English merchants operating in Ottoman ports under negotiated commercial privileges. The spice trade through the Red Sea and Egypt was still running at high volume well into the sixteenth century, competing with and at times outperforming the Portuguese sea route. An empire that had closed the routes would not have needed to fight the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean for a share of the spice traffic, which the Ottomans repeatedly did.

The second problem is chronology. Portuguese exploration down the African coast began decades before 1453. Ceuta was taken in 1415. Portuguese ships reached Madeira and the Azores in the 1420s and 1430s, rounded Cape Bojador in 1434, and were trading for gold and slaves on the Guinea coast through the 1440s — all of it under the sponsorship of the prince conventionally called Henry the Navigator, and all of it before Constantinople fell. Whatever motivated the Portuguese, it was not an event that had not yet happened.

What was actually going on requires disaggregating several things the tidy story conflates.

The overland route genuinely did decline, but the decline began in the fourteenth century and had little to do with the Ottomans. Part XI of this series described the fragmentation of the Mongol empire into mutually hostile khanates from the 1330s onward, restoring the borders, tolls, and insecurity that unified Mongol authority had suppressed. Part VII described the pandemic that swept the same routes in the 1340s. Timur’s campaigns in the late fourteenth century devastated Central Asian and Persian cities and, though he rebuilt Samarkand spectacularly, left the wider region’s commercial infrastructure worse than he found it. By the time the Ottomans took Constantinople, the great overland caravan trade of the Mongol century had already been dead or dying for the better part of a hundred years.

What remained, and what Europe actually cared about, was not overland Chinese silk but maritime Asian spices — pepper above all, along with cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, and ginger — arriving from India and the Indonesian archipelago through the Indian Ocean, the Red Sea or the Persian Gulf, and then overland to Mediterranean ports. This traffic was maritime for most of its length and had been for centuries. Its European end was controlled, comprehensively and profitably, by Venice.

That is the actual grievance, and it explains Portuguese behaviour far better than any Ottoman policy does. Venice held negotiated arrangements with the Mamluk sultanate in Egypt that gave it privileged access to spices arriving at Alexandria, and it defended that position against European rivals with every commercial and diplomatic instrument available. A Portuguese or Castilian merchant wanting pepper bought it from Venetians at Venetian prices. Portugal, a small kingdom on the wrong end of the Mediterranean with an Atlantic coastline and a well-developed shipbuilding tradition, had every incentive to find a route that did not involve paying Venice, and precisely no ability to break Venice’s position by any other means. The Age of Exploration was, in its origins, a competitive manoeuvre by a peripheral European kingdom against a dominant European one, conducted by going around the entire arrangement.

They found the way around in 1498, when Vasco da Gama reached Calicut, having rounded the Cape of Good Hope that Bartolomeu Dias had passed a decade earlier. The Portuguese arrived in an Indian Ocean that had been a sophisticated, multi-polar, long-established commercial world for a thousand years — Arab, Gujarati, Tamil, Malay, and Chinese merchants all operating within it — and they arrived with an advantage that had nothing to do with commercial acumen. Their ships carried cannon, and the ports and shipping of the Indian Ocean did not, because the region’s maritime trade had developed for centuries without the kind of naval warfare that makes heavily armed merchantmen worth their cost.

What followed was less an exploration than a protection racket. Afonso de Albuquerque, appointed governor in 1509, pursued a strategy of seizing chokepoints rather than territory: Goa in 1510, Malacca in 1511, Hormuz in 1515, with an unsuccessful attempt on Aden. From these bases the Portuguese issued the cartaz, a pass that non-Portuguese ships were required to purchase in order to trade without being attacked, and attempted to enforce a monopoly on specified goods. It never worked as completely as Lisbon intended — the Ottomans contested it, Gujarati and Arab shipping evaded it, and the Red Sea route to Egypt continued carrying enormous quantities of pepper throughout the sixteenth century — but it was effective enough to redirect a substantial share of the trade around Africa and into Lisbon, and to make Venice’s position progressively less tenable.

The cannon are the connection back to the previous installment of this series, and it is worth stating explicitly because it closes a loop that took six hundred years. Gunpowder left China as an alchemical accident, travelled west with the Mongol conquests, was developed in Europe into naval artillery, and returned to Asian waters mounted on the ships that dismantled the trading system the original routes had built. The technology that made the Portuguese irresistible in the Indian Ocean had come out of the very civilization whose goods they were sailing there to acquire.

It is worth saying what the fall of Constantinople in 1453 actually did accomplish, since dismissing the textbook version should not become a claim that the event was unimportant. Mehmed II’s siege was a genuine turning point, conducted with an artillery train that included enormous bombards cast by a Hungarian founder named Orban — who had, in a detail almost too neat to be true, offered his services to the Byzantines first and been turned down on grounds of cost. The walls that had held off besiegers for a thousand years came down in fifty-three days. The last Roman emperor died in the fighting, an institution with an unbroken line back to Augustus ended, and Greek scholars fleeing west with manuscripts contributed to the intellectual currents of the Italian Renaissance. Ottoman control of the straits gave the empire a stranglehold on Black Sea access that damaged Genoese interests severely, since Genoa’s trading colonies at Caffa and elsewhere depended on it. What the fall did not do was sever European access to eastern goods, because those goods were arriving overwhelmingly through Egypt and Syria rather than through the Black Sea, and the Ottomans wanted the customs revenue.

Ottoman commercial policy, in fact, ran in the opposite direction from the textbook claim. The capitulations — grants of trading privileges to foreign merchants, extended to Venice, then to France in the sixteenth century, and later to England and the Netherlands — established legal protections, reduced tariffs, and consular jurisdiction for European traders operating in Ottoman territory. These were issued from a position of strength, as favours a confident empire could afford to extend, and only became instruments of foreign leverage much later when the balance of power shifted. An empire spending diplomatic effort to attract European merchants was not an empire trying to shut them out.

There is one further counterfactual that hangs over this installment and deserves at least a mention. Between 1405 and 1433, the Ming admiral Zheng He commanded seven voyages that took Chinese fleets to Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, and the East African coast — fleets whose largest vessels, by the contemporary accounts, dwarfed anything Portugal would build for another century, and whose total complement ran into the tens of thousands. Then the voyages stopped. The court’s priorities shifted toward the northern frontier and the enormous expense of rebuilding the Grand Canal and the Beijing capital, factional politics turned against the eunuch establishment that had sponsored the fleets, and the records of the voyages were reportedly suppressed. Sixty-five years after the last voyage, da Gama arrived at Calicut with four ships. The temptation to build a grand narrative on this contrast is considerable and should mostly be resisted — the Ming had rational fiscal and strategic reasons for their choice, and the Portuguese did not sail out of superior vision but out of the desperation of a small kingdom cut out of a market. But the fact stands that the Indian Ocean the Portuguese entered had, within living memory, been visited by fleets that could have swept them from it, and was not defended when they arrived.

For the overland routes, the maritime turn was terminal in a way that the earlier disruptions had not been. A ship carrying several hundred tonnes of cargo with a crew of a few dozen, moving at the pace of the monsoon and paying tolls to nobody in particular, simply outcompetes a camel caravan on every metric that matters for bulk goods. The overland routes did not vanish — regional trade across Central Asia continued for centuries, and specific high-value, low-bulk commodities kept moving — but the long-distance, continent-spanning commerce that had defined the Silk Road for fifteen hundred years shifted onto water and did not shift back. Kashgar, Samarkand, Bukhara, and the oasis towns of the Tarim Basin became what geography made them once the traffic stopped: remote places, far from everything.

The overland world’s last significant commercial chapter belongs to Safavid Persia, and it illustrates how the surviving continental trade adapted to a maritime world. The Safavids, who established a Shia state in Persia from 1501 and spent much of the following two centuries in conflict with the Ottomans, held one commodity of genuine international value: raw silk, produced in quantity in the Caspian provinces of Gilan and Mazandaran. Shah Abbas I, who ruled from 1588 to 1629, made the silk trade a royal monopoly and set about routing it away from Ottoman territory, which meant away from the traditional Aleppo and Bursa markets and toward alternatives — Armenian merchant communities from New Julfa, a suburb Abbas built at his capital Isfahan and populated with Armenians deported from the Caucasus, were granted extraordinary commercial privileges and built a trading network running to India, Russia, and Europe. Abbas negotiated with the English East India Company, with the Dutch, and with Muscovy, all in the interest of finding a route to European buyers that did not enrich his Ottoman rivals. The New Julfa Armenians became, for roughly a century, one of the most effective long-distance commercial networks in the world, operating on family credit relationships across a span from Amsterdam to Manila. It is the same structural role the Sogdians had played a thousand years earlier, in the same part of the world, for much the same reasons.

That was, in effect, the last act. Safavid decline in the eighteenth century, Russian expansion into Central Asia in the nineteenth, and the hardening of borders that followed left the old overland corridor divided among empires with little interest in transit trade and considerable interest in keeping each other out. The oasis towns that had lived on caravan traffic for two thousand years spent the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as remote provincial settlements in somebody’s frontier zone, visited mainly by soldiers, administrators, and the occasional archaeologist removing manuscripts.

The Ottomans, meanwhile, did rather well for another two centuries, which is inconvenient for the textbook story in a final way. An empire supposedly rendered obsolete by the discovery of the Cape route went on to reach its greatest territorial extent in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and remained a major power into the eighteenth. Its eventual decline had a great many causes, most of them domestic and fiscal, and the redirection of the spice trade around Africa was a contributing factor rather than a fatal blow.

What the whole episode illustrates, and what makes the tidy version worth correcting rather than simply dismissing, is how badly single-cause explanations serve a system this complicated. The Silk Road did not end because someone closed it. It ended because the political conditions that had made it viable came apart over a century and a half, because a pandemic gutted the demand and the population at both ends, because a commercial rivalry between two European states created an incentive to find an alternative, and because a Chinese chemical curiosity had by then been refined into something that let small European ships impose terms on ports that had never needed to defend themselves. No one intended any of it. The route had been built by accident, as the Introduction to this series argued, and it was dismantled the same way.
