Alex Bond stood outside his home in York, Pennsylvania, and told a pair of political canvassers what worried him most. After gas prices and taxes, he named data centers — the energy-hungry warehouses that make AI function. “AI is terrible,” the 29-year-old account manager said. “And it’s probably going to kill us all.”

What Bond did not know, as Stuart A. Thompson reports in The New York Times today, is that his exact words were about to be processed inside one of the data centers he had just finished condemning. The canvasser, volunteering for the Democratic group Swing Left, recapped Bond’s comments into a phone app immediately after the conversation ended. An AI tool then analyzed that memo alongside hundreds of others collected the same way across Pennsylvania’s competitive Tenth Congressional District, synthesizing what voters like Bond were saying into reports the campaign could use to craft more persuasive messages aimed at turning him into a supporter.

“Everything a person is saying is a data point,” said Violet Kopp, one of the canvassers and Swing Left’s East Coast organizing manager.

This series has spent fifty-five parts asking who controls the AI systems that shape elections — the chatbots voters consult in Part 40, the super PACs spending tens of millions in Part 55. Thompson’s reporting reveals a third layer underneath both, one this series has not yet documented directly: campaigns are no longer just targets of the AI debate. They have become, almost universally and almost invisibly, AI’s most intensive day-to-day users — turning the same anxieties Bond voiced on his doorstep into the raw material used to manage him as a voter.

I. The Irony Is the Point, Not an Accident

Bond’s case is not an edge case this series is elevating for dramatic effect. It is, per Thompson’s reporting, the standard operating procedure of a modern door-knocking campaign. Canvassers record what voters say. AI tools synthesize the recordings across an entire district. The output becomes message-testing data, used to identify which framings move a persuadable voter and which do not.

The asymmetry here deserves to be stated plainly, because it is the cleanest illustration this series has found of the gap between what AI is publicly understood to be and what it is privately deployed to do. Bond’s stated position — AI is dangerous and existentially threatening — was not treated by the campaign as a political opinion to be respected or debated. It was treated as a data point: useful, processable, and ultimately a problem to be solved through better message targeting, generated by the very technology Bond was objecting to. The campaign did not need to agree with Bond, argue with him, or even directly engage his concern. It needed only to know that the concern existed, in order to construct language that would route around it. Whether that is good campaign practice or a small but telling demonstration of exactly the unaccountable, invisible AI deployment this series has spent fifty-five parts warning about is a question Thompson leaves to the reader — and a question this series does not think is close.

II. The Numbers Behind the Doorstep

Bond’s encounter is not an anomaly. It is the visible tip of an adoption wave that has moved further and faster than almost anything else this series has tracked.

A survey from the technology-and-politics newsletter Anchor Change found that 87 percent of campaign strategists and operatives now use AI daily in their work. A progressive nonprofit hosts monthly conference calls drawing hundreds of attendees who trade tips on using AI to refine campaign operations. American Bridge 21st Century, a Democratic-aligned opposition research group, has used AI to investigate roughly 250 Republican candidates and politicians, crediting the technology specifically with helping it dig into seventeen House Republicans in otherwise safe districts — seats opposition research previously had neither the staff time nor the funding to scrutinize closely. Pat Dennis, the group’s president, described the workflow precisely: “AI is surfacing flags to humans, who vet the projects, expand on them, and get them out the door.”

Kate Gage, a Democratic consultant and former Obama administration official who now runs the Higher Ground Institute, told Thompson that AI adoption consumes essentially all of her professional attention: “Basically my whole life right now is figuring out how to get campaigns to identify what the use cases are.” Her organization’s database now catalogs nearly one hundred distinct AI tools built specifically for campaign messaging or voter data management — among them, a technique for turning a single policy brief into a full week of social media content. The campaign of Saikat Chakrabarti, who ran unsuccessfully for a California House seat, went further still: it replaced nearly every piece of paid campaign software — canvassing apps, phone banking systems — with custom AI-built alternatives coded by a staff of three.

This is not a preview of how future campaigns might work. It is, per these numbers, already how most campaigns work, in the same election cycle in which the public told pollsters, repeatedly and emphatically, that it does not trust the technology at all.

III. The Asymmetry Nobody Is Voting On

Thompson’s reporting surfaces a partisan divide in AI adoption that has nothing to do with ideology about AI policy and everything to do with internal organizational friction — a distinction this series has not previously had occasion to draw, and one worth drawing carefully.

Polling shows Democratic voters more wary of AI than Republican voters, but the adoption gap inside the parties’ campaign operations runs in the same direction for a different reason: Democratic strategists report wrestling with internal pushback from staff and unionized campaign workers worried about AI displacing their own jobs, while Republican strategists report comparatively little internal resistance despite comparable voter-level skepticism among conservative voters. Eric Wilson, who directs the Center for Campaign Innovation and trains Republican operatives on AI adoption, frames the dynamic as a live competitive advantage: “Republicans are saying, ‘OK, how can we use this to help us win and give us an advantage?’” Of Democratic hesitation, he is blunter still: “I think it’s a strategic error on their part.”

Wilson’s own account of how political professionals are instructed to handle the ethics of the technology is the sharpest material in Thompson’s reporting, and it deserves to be quoted in full rather than summarized. Asked about the morality of AI use in campaigns, Wilson said: “I’ll be giving a talk, and someone will say, you know, what about the morality of it? And that’s a fine debate to have, but we’re trying to win elections.” That sentence is not a gaffe. It is a coherent, openly stated operating philosophy: ethical questions about a technology’s use are conceded to be legitimate and simultaneously treated as subordinate to the goal the technology is being used to pursue. This is the same logic Section III of Part 49 identified in the FDA paradox — a stated commitment to responsible governance that dissolves the moment it would require sacrificing competitive advantage — applied here not to a frontier AI lab, but to the machinery of electoral democracy itself.

The standards gap this produces is concrete and already operative. Wilson’s own written guidance holds that AI-generated video of a political opponent is acceptable so long as it accurately reflects something the opponent actually said. The National Democratic Training Committee’s course on AI takes the opposite position: campaigners should never create such content, full stop, because it “undermines democratic discourse and voter trust.” These are not two strategists disagreeing at the margins about tactics. They are two organizations training the next generation of American political operatives on fundamentally incompatible answers to the question of what synthetic media is allowed to do to a democratic election — and no law, regulation, or binding industry standard currently exists to settle which answer governs, the same regulatory vacuum this series identified at the federal level in Part 40’s treatment of election deepfake law.

IV. The Stakes Beneath the Stakes

It would be a mistake to read this purely as a story about campaign efficiency or partisan competitive advantage, because the underlying mechanism Thompson describes is the same one this series has tracked from the labor side in Part 50 and the cognitive side in Part 54, now operating on the electorate itself.

Bond’s words became a data point not because anyone acted in bad faith, but because the entire infrastructure of contemporary campaigning has been quietly rebuilt around treating voter sentiment as raw material for synthesis and targeting rather than as the substance of a democratic conversation. This is the accountability sink from Part 50, restaged at the scale of an entire electorate: when a campaign’s message ultimately moves a persuadable voter, no single person decided exactly which words would do the moving, or why, or whether the resulting message was a fair representation of the candidate’s actual position. The AI system surfaced the pattern. A human approved the output. The voter receives a message that feels personally targeted because, in a real and specific sense, it was — built from the verbatim words they used on their own doorstep, fed back to them in a form engineered to be more persuasive than anything a human strategist could have hand-crafted at the same speed and scale.

None of this requires any deception this series could point to and call fraudulent. Nothing here is a deepfake. Nothing here is a chatbot lying about a candidate’s record, the specific failure mode Part 40 examined. It is simpler and, in its way, more structurally significant than either: democratic persuasion itself, the basic mechanism by which campaigns are supposed to win arguments rather than win optimization contests, is being quietly re-engineered by a technology that the people on the receiving end of it overwhelmingly say they do not trust — and that they have no practical way to opt out of, short of declining to speak to a canvasser at all. Bond can refuse to use AI in his own life as a matter of principle. He cannot refuse to have his own stated objections to AI processed by the very systems he is objecting to, the moment he answers his own front door.
