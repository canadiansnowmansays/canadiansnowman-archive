An analyst from Evercore asked the only question that actually mattered.

On SpaceX’s August 4, 2026 earnings call, Kat Kunnarelli put it to Gwynne Shotwell directly: becoming a true fourth American carrier would imply well over a hundred billion dollars of mobile capital expenditure over time — auctioned spectrum, greenfield towers, the whole traditional buildout. Was that really the plan? Or was SpaceX weighing something closer to an MVNO arrangement, or an acquisition?

Shotwell’s answer didn’t fit either box. And once you understand why, the entire American half of this series’ central question — buy or partner — turns out to be a false choice. SpaceX isn’t planning to do what a normal fourth carrier would need to do. It’s planning to do something a normal carrier structurally can’t.

Five megahertz, then sixty-five

Start with the numbers, because Shotwell gave them precisely. “Currently the system that we’re operating uses about five megahertz of bandwidth, and we operate that through local telco providers,” she told analysts — an admission worth reading twice, because it is SpaceX’s own chief operating officer confirming, in plain language, exactly the MVNO-style arrangement this series has been describing from the outside. Five megahertz, borrowed from T-Mobile’s existing spectrum, is what powers today’s texting-and-basic-data service.

The EchoStar acquisition changes that math by an order of magnitude. “We have 65 megahertz of bandwidth available to us through the EchoStar spectrum, which gives massive increase in capability,” Shotwell said. “Another way to look at it is we will also probably 10x the number of satellites.” Stack the spectrum increase on top of the satellite increase, and her own framing is blunt: “you could look at the Starlink mobile leveraging the next generation satellite and the EchoStar spectrum as being 100 times better, 10 times 10 better than what we’ve got right now.”

That is a genuinely enormous jump, and it’s the part of the story most coverage has already caught. What most coverage missed is what comes next — the terrestrial half of the plan, which nobody had explained clearly until an analyst forced the issue.

The answer nobody expected

Shotwell didn’t dodge the build-versus-buy question. She reframed it. “The spectrum that we purchased from EchoStar does have terrestrial components, we definitely intend to build out the terrestrial component,” she confirmed — so this isn’t purely a satellite play. But when Kunnarelli’s question implied a hundred billion dollars of towers and greenfield infrastructure, Shotwell pushed back on the premise itself: “I don’t want to talk about the CapEx for that right now. Let me give a little hint, and that is you could put a cellular base station on basically the gear that holds a Starlink broadband dish. You can have kind of these little femtocells around the country, and you deploy that as you need it. You don’t have to necessarily spend... many billions of dollars in low-band spectrum all upfront before you deploy your system.”

A femtocell, for anyone who hasn’t run into the term, is a small, low-power cellular base station — the kind normally used to patch a single dead spot inside an office building or a basement. Shotwell’s suggestion is to turn that concept inside out: instead of building a small number of enormous towers, distribute a very large number of tiny ones, riding on hardware SpaceX has already sold and already installed. Musk elaborated on the same idea a few minutes later: “instead of having to deploy these very expensive and difficult-to-locate large cellular base stations, we feel reasonably confident that we can deploy a large number of sort of small stations essentially. They’re really just Starlink dishes that also provide connectivity in the mobile spectrum bands, and have them be all over the place. The Starlink antennas are located on the roofs of houses and businesses, so they’ve got a sort of clear view for providing connectivity directly to cell phones on the ground.”

Read that carefully, because it inverts the entire cost structure a normal telecom buildout assumes. A traditional carrier has to go find land, negotiate a lease or a purchase, get zoning approval, pour a foundation, and erect a tower — one at a time, for tens of thousands of locations, each one a discrete capital project with its own timeline and its own risk of local opposition. SpaceX’s alternative is to treat every rooftop that already has a Starlink broadband dish as a potential cell site, activated with what is presumably a hardware or firmware addition rather than a new construction project. The company isn’t proposing to build a terrestrial network from scratch. It’s proposing to notice that it already has one — several million of them, sitting on roofs across the country, originally sold for an entirely different purpose — and repurpose it.

Why Dish Network is the wrong comparison

Several analysts covering the story have reached for Dish Network as a cautionary tale: a company that spent years and billions trying to build a fourth national carrier and never gained meaningful traction against the incumbents. It’s an understandable comparison, and it’s the wrong one, for a reason that has nothing to do with execution talent and everything to do with what each company actually owned before it started.

Dish Network had to build its terrestrial network from a standing start — buy the spectrum, then build the towers, with no existing installed base to lean on. SpaceX, if Shotwell’s plan holds, doesn’t have that problem, because the installed base already exists. Every Starlink broadband subscriber’s rooftop dish is, in this telling, a future cell site that’s already deployed, already paid for by a different customer for a different reason, and already sitting exactly where a network planner would want a small cell anyway — on a roof, with a clear line of sight. Dish had to build a network from nothing. SpaceX is proposing to notice it already has the beginnings of one and simply switch it on, roughly the same trick Rogers pulled when it flipped on satellite connectivity for phones already in customers’ pockets, except run in reverse: instead of the satellite reaching down to an unmodified phone, the ground equipment reaches up into the mobile spectrum bands from a terminal that was already sitting there.

This is also, worth noting, a third model entirely, distinct from both halves of the buy-versus-partner framework Part I laid out. It isn’t spectrum ownership in the traditional sense — the femtocell trick works regardless of whose licensed spectrum the EchoStar deal delivers. And it isn’t the MVNO-style partnership described in Parts I and II either, since there’s no local carrier’s tower network being leased. It’s something closer to asset reuse at a scale no traditional telecom company has ever had available to it, because no traditional telecom company has ever had millions of customer-owned terminals already deployed on rooftops for an unrelated product line.

There’s a regulatory wrinkle buried in this plan too, and it’s one Shotwell didn’t address directly but that’s worth flagging. Traditional cell towers require zoning approval precisely because they’re large, visible, and often unwelcome new structures — which is exactly the process that slows a conventional buildout to a crawl, site by site, county by county. A femtocell riding on hardware a customer already owns and already installed, for a different licensed purpose, may or may not trigger the same category of review. If it doesn’t — if adding mobile-band transmission capability to an existing, already-approved satellite terminal counts as a modification rather than new construction — SpaceX may have found a way to sidestep the single slowest bottleneck in American telecom infrastructure almost by accident. That’s a genuinely open legal question, and one regulators haven’t yet had to answer at scale, because nobody has tried this particular trick before.

Skepticism worth taking seriously

None of this should be read as an assumption that the plan simply works because SpaceX said so. Turning a fixed satellite antenna, optimized for receiving a beam from a specific orbital position, into something that also reliably transmits and receives ordinary cellular traffic to and from phones on the ground is a nontrivial radio-frequency engineering problem — different antenna geometry, different interference profile, different power requirements. Shotwell’s own hedging on the question — “I don’t want to talk about the CapEx for that right now” — reads as much like a company still working out the engineering as it does a company protecting a trade secret. Investors on the call seemed to sense the same thing; SpaceX shares fell sharply after hours despite the revenue beat, with analysts explicitly citing execution risk across the company’s simultaneous bets on Starship, Starlink V3, mobile service, and AI infrastructure all at once.

There’s also a density problem the femtocell model doesn’t obviously solve. Rooftop Starlink terminals are heavily concentrated in exactly the places traditional cellular coverage is already good — suburban and rural homes with a clear view of the sky — and comparatively sparse in the dense urban cores where mobile traffic is heaviest and where satellite-friendly rooftop access is hardest to find. A femtocell network built on top of existing broadband subscribers might blanket the exurbs beautifully while leaving downtown Toronto or Manhattan no better served than before. If that’s how it plays out, “eliminating dead zones,” as Shotwell put it, may turn out to be a more honest description of the plan’s strength than “replacing your urban carrier.”

The size of the target

Shotwell was equally direct about how big she thinks the opportunity is. “The big three in the U.S., AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile, roughly between them, $600 billion a year,” she told analysts. “I anticipate us to be able to acquire quite a few of their customers because I think our service will be better. We will eliminate dead zones leveraging basically the satellites in orbit. It’ll be better during any sort of natural disaster because surprisingly, even though space movies make space look super dangerous, it’s a pretty quiescent environment.”

Six hundred billion dollars is not a rounding error, and it explains why AT&T and T-Mobile’s CEOs have both already, on their own earnings calls, ruled out offering SpaceX any kind of wholesale MVNO deal — and why all three national carriers moved so quickly in May 2026 to pool their own spectrum into a joint venture aimed at building a competing direct-to-device network before Starlink Mobile launches. You don’t form a defensive alliance between three companies that otherwise compete bitterly with each other unless you believe the threat is close to real. The femtocell plan is precisely the kind of threat that would justify that alliance: not a slow, capital-intensive buildout the incumbents could watch coming for a decade, but a fast, low-visibility deployment riding on infrastructure that’s already sitting on rooftops across the country, waiting for a firmware update.

What this means for the timeline

Bring this back to the question that’s run through the whole series: can SpaceX actually hit “commercial mobile service by the end of 2027,” as both Shotwell and Johnsen stated on the call? The femtocell strategy is, in a real sense, the only version of the plan that makes that date plausible. A conventional terrestrial buildout — permits, zoning, physical tower construction, one site at a time — simply doesn’t fit inside eighteen months at any scale that would matter to hundreds of millions of American mobile customers. A software- and hardware-upgrade rollout across an existing, already-permitted, already-installed base of rooftop terminals might.

Whether it actually works at the RF-engineering level — whether a broadband dish’s rooftop position and antenna geometry can be adapted cleanly to also serve as a cellular small cell, at a cost and a reliability that makes commercial sense — is a genuinely open technical question that Shotwell was careful not to over-promise on. But the strategic logic is airtight in a way the “just buy spectrum everywhere” version of the plan never was. It’s the one path that lets SpaceX be a vertically integrated owner in the one market where it wants to be an owner, without needing to build a single traditional tower to get there.
