I. The Mole in Reserve

Anyone who has stood at a carnival Whack-a-Mole machine knows the game’s one real secret. The moles are never defeated. They are simply waiting under the table for a different hole.

On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Trump administration showed the country what was waiting under the table. The Office of the United States Trade Representative proposed a new slate of tariffs on fourteen economies, among them China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, Vietnam and the twenty-seven members of the European Union, as Tony Romm reported for The New York Times. The legal vehicle is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, the unfair-trade-practices statute, invoked this time on the theory that America’s trading partners keep factories running that the world does not need and send the surplus to American shores.

Regular readers will know the statute. It is the same Section 301 the administration used in July to rebuild the global tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February. That first rebuild is still standing, just about, while a panel of judges at the Court of International Trade decides whether to knock it over.

So the administration has done what any experienced player does when the mallet is already in the air. It has put a second mole in the next hole.

II. Reading the Fine Print

The headline numbers sound like an escalation. China faces a proposed 20 per cent tariff, which the administration says could rise to 40 per cent if Beijing does not honour the terms of its trade truce with Washington. Vietnam faces 20 per cent. The European Union, Japan, Korea, Taiwan and the rest face what the Times called similarly high and potentially escalating rates.

The fine print tells a quieter story. A senior official told reporters that the new duties will not exceed the limits in the deals the administration has already negotiated, as RTÉ reported, carrying Reuters’ account of the briefing: 15 per cent for the EU, Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, 19 per cent for Malaysia, and 20 per cent for China under a truce that expires on January 10. The new tariffs will not stack on top of the national-security duties on steel and cars. Nor will they stack on the July forced-labour tariffs; where both apply, the higher rate wins. Only China gets a genuine pile-up, with the new 20 per cent landing on top of the roughly 25 per cent imposed in 2018, according to CNBC. About $30 billion of Chinese imports are set aside entirely while the two governments consider tariff cuts through a bilateral “Board of Trade.”

Strip away the drama and what is left is less a new tariff wall than a new legal address for an old one. The Turnberry deal with Brussels, the 15 per cent ceilings with Tokyo and Seoul, the truce with Beijing: all of them were negotiated in 2025 under the threat of tariffs imposed through the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. In February, the Supreme Court ruled that IEEPA never allowed tariffs in the first place. The threat that produced those deals was unlawful. The prices agreed under it were never handed back. Section 301 is now being asked to give them a lawful home after the fact.

That is the Whack-a-Mole pattern in its purest form yet. The court did not hit the tariffs. It hit the statute. The tariffs simply moved house.

III. The Court That Hasn’t Ruled

To see why the administration wants a second regime, look at the first.

The forced-labour tariffs took effect on July 24, the day the temporary Section 122 surcharge expired. They impose 10 or 12.5 per cent on goods from 60 trading partners whose offence, in the administration’s telling, is failing to ban or police imports made with forced labour. Canada and Mexico are spared for goods that enter duty-free under CUSMA, as Troutman Pepper noted when the tariffs took effect. Almost everyone else pays.

The economists were unimpressed. William Cline of the Peterson Institute for International Economics calculated that the rates were roughly 40 to 50 times what the forced-labour problem could justify. The judges did not seem much more impressed. When the Court of International Trade heard the consolidated challenge on September 30, the panel appeared mostly sceptical of the trade representative’s case, according to MLex. Nearly two months later it still has not ruled, and the Times reported that the judges had recently seemed poised to rule against the administration.

Now read the excess-capacity proposal again with that in mind. Its rates sit at or near the same levels. It replaces the forced-labour tariff wherever its own rate is higher. And if the forced-labour tariffs fall, the excess-capacity tariffs will be standing in the same spot, covering most of the same economies, at roughly the same price. It is a spare tyre bolted on before the puncture.

There is also the matter of speed. The statute gave the trade representative until March 11, 2027, to reach a determination in the excess-capacity case, a year after the investigation opened, as EcoVadis set out in its guide for importers. The administration is not using that time. It wants the hearings and public comment finished and the tariffs in force by January. Which raises the obvious question: what is happening in January?

IV. Three Clocks in January

Three things, as it happens.

On January 3, a new Congress is sworn in with Democratic majorities in both chambers, after a midterm election in which, as the Times put it, rising prices fed a backlash that stripped Republicans of their congressional majorities. Tariffs were among the forces driving those prices.

On January 10, the US-China trade truce expires, the agreement that holds China’s ceiling at 20 per cent. The proposed escalation to 40 per cent is plainly written with that date in mind.

And January is the earliest date the administration official would give for the new tariffs to take effect.

Before any of that comes December, and the G20 summit in Miami, where the official suggested the new duties could help produce more trade agreements. That sentence deserves a moment. Tariffs proposed in November, to take effect in January, to encourage deals to be signed in December, by countries that already signed deals at these exact rates in 2025. The mole is not only popping back up. It is asking to be paid again for the privilege.

V. The Congress That Can’t Unplug It

The incoming majority has been loud about its intentions. Ron Wyden, who takes over the Senate Finance Committee in January, has promised to use it to reassert congressional authority over trade, and said there is “overwhelming support for repealing the tariffs on products from Canada,” as The Financial Express reported. Chuck Schumer has said cutting tariffs will be a priority. The public is with them: a Reuters/Ipsos poll found 62 per cent of Americans would support repealing the tariffs.

Here is the problem. Section 301 tariffs do not need Congress’s permission to stay in place, and Congress cannot switch them off by a simple majority vote. Repeal means passing a law, and passing a law means surviving a veto. As the Associated Press pointed out after the midterms, overriding a presidential veto takes a two-thirds majority in both houses, and the Democrats do not have it.

This is the lesson Part VI drew from the Russia sanctions bill, when Congress handed the president one of the broadest tariff authorities he had yet received. Congress built the mallets. Section 301 was written in 1974 to give the executive a weapon against genuinely unfair foreign practices; Section 232 in 1962 for national security; Section 338 in 1930 for discrimination against American commerce. Each has now been picked up and swung at targets its authors never imagined.

A majority that wants the game to stop has two real options. It can write narrower statutes strong enough to survive a veto, which requires Republican votes. Or it can use subpoenas and hearings to make every swing expensive. The second option is available on January 3. The first may have to wait until 2029.

VI. Brazil, Again

Tucked beneath the main announcement was a smaller one. The trade representative’s office said it was considering raising tariffs on Brazilian sugar and aircraft, to bring Brasília back to the negotiating table.

The aircraft detail is the interesting one. When the administration imposed its first post-Supreme Court Section 301 tariff in July, a 25 per cent duty on a broad range of Brazilian goods from July 22, it took care to exempt Brazilian beef, coffee, rare earths, energy products, aircraft and aircraft parts, as Reuters reported at the time. American regional airlines fly Embraer jets. American manufacturers buy Brazilian aircraft parts. The exemption was not generosity. It was self-defence.

Four months later, the self-defence is on the table. And Brazil has never been the deficit villain of the administration’s speeches. The United States ran a $14.4 billion goods surplus with Brazil in 2025, up from $7.7 billion the year before, according to Al Jazeera. Whatever Brazil is being punished for, it is not for selling Americans more than it buys.

VII. The Number the Mallet Can’t Hit

Part VII ended on a scoreboard: Peter Navarro’s trade deficit, the one twenty months of tariffs were supposed to shrink, at a 17-month high. The figures released this month put the September deficit at $105 billion, a slight dip from August but still the second-largest since Trump returned to office.

The Times names one of the main forces behind it: artificial intelligence, as American companies buy expensive semiconductors and computing hardware from abroad. AI is the president’s own signature priority. So the administration finds itself trying to close a deficit that its favourite industry is widening, and the new tariffs cannot do much about it. Taiwan and South Korea, where those chips are made, are held to 15 per cent by the very deal caps the new tariffs promise to respect.

Meanwhile the bill lands where it always does. US consumer inflation ran at 3.7 per cent in September. The Federal Reserve believes most of the earlier tariffs have already worked through to prices; new ones could push them up again. And on Wednesday morning, stocks already seemed poised to open lower amid scepticism about AI, before the latest tariffs were added to the mix.

VIII. What Canada Should Notice

Canada is not on Wednesday’s list, and was not among the sixteen economies investigated. Canadian goods that qualify under CUSMA were also spared the forced-labour tariffs. That is the good news, and it is real.

The less comfortable news is that Canada already has a mole of its own. The 50 per cent Section 338 tariffs, imposed in August under a 1930 statute no president had ever used, are now in court as well. A New Jersey food importer, Gellert Global Group, filed the first challenge at the Court of International Trade on October 20, arguing that Section 338 is no longer valid because Congress later passed more specific trade laws, including the very Section 301 the administration now leans on, as Newsweek reported.

Count the holes. IEEPA: struck down by the Supreme Court in February. Section 122: found unlawful by the trade court in May, expired in July. Section 301 for forced labour: argued in September, ruling pending. Section 338 for Canada: challenged in October. Section 301 for excess capacity: proposed on November 25, in force by January if the schedule holds. Five tariff regimes in ten months. The table may run out of holes before the administration runs out of moles.

The Great Canadian Trade War will take up the Canadian thread in its next instalment. For this series, the lesson is simpler. Whatever the court decides about Section 338, Ottawa should assume a replacement is already being measured for the hole.

IX. The Scoreboard

Every round of this game ends the same way, so here is the scoreboard as of November 25, 2026.

The courts have struck down one tariff authority outright and found a second unlawful. Two more are waiting on judges. The administration has answered each loss not by lowering tariffs but by finding another statute to hold the same rates for a few more months. Its new proposal changes little for America’s allies, because their rates are capped by deals negotiated under a law that turned out to be illegal. It changes a great deal for China, whose truce runs out in January. And it is being rushed into place before a Congress arrives that has the votes to complain but not, without Republican help, the votes to stop it.

China’s foreign ministry spokesman, Guo Jiakun, called the overcapacity charge “a false proposition.” Beijing would say that. But it is worth noticing how the administration’s own officials described the tariffs: as a way to hold the existing deals in place and to press for new ones, not as a cure for anyone’s idle factories. Whatever these tariffs are, the evidence so far says they are not really about excess capacity.

They are about having something standing when the mallet comes down.
