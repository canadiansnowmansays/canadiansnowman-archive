For three years, the threat was the point. Prosecute Fauci. Handcuff Fauci. Put him in front of a jury and make him answer for the pandemic. Senator Rand Paul of Kentucky said some version of this so often that it stopped sounding like a promise and started sounding like weather — a thing that was simply, permanently forecast. On August 6, 2026, he finally got the closest thing to a delivery mechanism he was ever going to get: his own Homeland Security Committee, voting 8-7 along strict party lines, held Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress for invoking the Fifth Amendment during a hearing on the origins of American pandemic policy.

Part IX of this series covered that vote in detail — the constitutional argument Paul made for why a man who has been formally, irrevocably pardoned should still be compelled to testify against himself, and the legal scholars who found that argument considerably weaker than Paul’s press conferences suggested. What this addendum covers is what happened next, because “what happened next” turns out to be the most honest verdict anyone has rendered on this case yet.

Nothing happened next. Or rather: the people with the actual power to make something happen looked at the file and, by every available account, decided to let it sit.

As Adam Kinzinger reported on his Substack on August 28, 2026, Trump administration officials are privately questioning whether the contempt referral would survive contact with a courtroom. The framing on his own show was blunt: it is a great deal easier to threaten a prosecution on cable news than to defend one in front of a judge. The Wall Street Journal’s reporting, which Kinzinger cited, describes a Justice Department quietly concluding that Paul’s referral has more theater in it than law.

The theater starts with the procedure Paul chose. A congressional contempt referral is supposed to travel a specific road: the committee votes, then the full chamber votes, and only after that does the recommendation land at Justice for a prosecutor to decide what to do with it. Paul skipped the middle step. He knew, or should have known, that a floor vote in the Senate would never clear the sixty-vote threshold needed to send a contempt citation forward — not with Democrats unified against it and not enough Republicans willing to spend political capital handcuffing an 85-year-old retired public health official over a Fifth Amendment invocation. So instead of losing that vote, he simply didn’t hold it. He sent the committee’s recommendation straight to the Justice Department and asked Vice President Vance, in his capacity as president of the Senate, to certify it as though the full chamber had already spoken.

This is, by the assessment of people who track congressional procedure for a living, not how any of this has ever worked before. It is a workaround for a vote Paul knew he would lose, dressed up as a technicality. And it leaves the White House holding a referral that, if it were ever challenged in court — which it certainly would be — arrives without the one thing that has always given contempt-of-Congress prosecutions their legal weight: an actual vote of the body allegedly held in contempt.

Then there is the case itself, which does not improve on closer inspection. Fauci did not refuse to answer questions out of general obstinacy. He invoked the Fifth Amendment specifically because the pardon issued to him by President Biden on January 19, 2025, on his way out of office, covers past conduct — it does not and cannot immunize him against a false-statement or perjury charge for something he might say in a hearing room in 2026. Legal scholars interviewed after the committee vote made this point repeatedly: a pardon that covers your past does not entitle Congress to set a perjury trap for your present. Asking a grand jury to indict a man for correctly exercising a right the Constitution gives him, at the request of the senator who has spent years publicly promising to get him, is not the kind of case career prosecutors line up to try. It is, if anything, the kind of case that makes a defense attorney’s year.

And here is the detail that tells you everything about how seriously anyone in this administration actually believes their own rhetoric: Donald Trump, who has never once in his second term shown any reluctance to phone his Attorney General about a prosecution he wants pursued — who has treated the Justice Department less as an independent institution than as a personal switchboard — has not raised the Fauci referral with his own AG at all. Not once, by any reporting available. The president who renamed Lake Ontario this same week out of what appears to be pure pique at Canada, who tracks his approval ratings obsessively, who has never met a grievance he didn’t want prosecuted, has looked at the single case Republicans spent three years building and decided it isn’t worth a phone call.

This is, on its own, a strange kind of restraint from a Justice Department that has not otherwise been shy. The same building that has pursued the sprawling docket this newsletter has tracked under the name The Enemies List — prosecutions of former officials, of critics, of people whose primary offense appears to be having said something Donald Trump didn’t like — apparently drew a line at an 85-year-old immunologist invoking a constitutional right. That contrast is worth sitting with. It is not that this Justice Department has suddenly discovered a commitment to prosecutorial restraint as a general principle. It is that even a department willing to stretch the law for other targets looked at this specific file and found it too thin to stand on. The Fauci referral did not survive scrutiny not because this DOJ has scruples, but because there was simply nothing there — no floor vote, no underlying crime beyond the exercise of a right, no political upside sturdy enough to justify the embarrassment of losing in open court.

There is also a midterm calculation buried in this decision, and it cuts against the instinct one might expect. With Republican prospects for November looking increasingly grim — falling approval numbers, a war in Iran with no exit ramp six months after it was promised to last five weeks, corporate donors quietly hedging their bets toward Democrats — one might assume the administration would welcome any distraction with the emotional charge Fauci still carries for the base. Handcuffing the man conservative media spent half a decade turning into a supervillain would, in a purely tribal sense, be a gift. That the White House has declined the gift anyway is its own small data point: even a White House this comfortable with spectacle apparently understood that losing a Fauci prosecution in open court, on the record, with cameras present, would do more damage to the “we go after our enemies and we win” brand than simply letting the referral gather dust ever could. Silence, it turns out, is the safer performance.

That is the actual finding here, and it is worth sitting with, because it settles a question this series has been circling since the Introduction: was any of this ever about accountability, or was it always about the performance of accountability? Part VIII covered the twenty-one words Fauci repeated across that hearing — the same short invocation, offered dozens of times, each repetition itself becoming a kind of evidence for how carefully counseled and how legally sound his position was. Part IX covered the vote that followed and the legal scholars who doubted it would hold, including the pointed observation that Congress has reached for contempt citations before in moments that had far more to do with settling scores than establishing facts, going back to the Lois Lerner precedent this series flagged at the time. This is the chapter where the people whose job it is to actually try the case looked at what Paul built and quietly, without a press conference of their own, put it in a drawer.

It is worth pausing, too, on what this does to Rand Paul specifically. He is the senator who, over years of interviews, insisted Fauci’s pardon stripped him of any right to silence and promised there would be consequences once the matter reached people with actual prosecutorial power. He built the case, skipped the vote he knew he would lose, and delivered the file to the one desk that could turn his promise into an indictment. That desk has now, by every available account, declined to act on it — not through any Democratic obstruction, not through a court ruling, but through the quiet judgment of his own party’s Justice Department. If there is a single sentence that captures three years of promised repercussions, it may be this: the people best positioned to know whether a case is real looked at Paul’s and decided it wasn’t worth trying.

There is no triumphant ending available here, and it would be dishonest of this series to reach for one. Fauci has not been vindicated by a court, because there is no court date, and there may never be one. Nothing has been formally withdrawn. Rand Paul has not held a press conference walking back three years of promised repercussions, and there is no reason to expect he will; the referral can simply persist, unprosecuted and unresolved, a threat that never quite becomes a case and never quite goes away either. That in-between state — not cleared, not charged, just permanently pending — is its own kind of cruelty for an 85-year-old man, whatever one thinks of his record.

But it is also, in its own quiet way, the most accurate verdict this saga has produced. Three years of promised handcuffs arrived at the one office in Washington that actually has the authority to use them, and that office looked at the file and found a procedural shortcut instead of a vote, a Fifth Amendment invocation instead of an obstruction, and an 85-year-old man instead of a flight risk. Even a Justice Department that has spent a year and a half showing very little restraint elsewhere balked at this one. That is not exoneration. It is something more useful: it is the system’s own lawyers concluding, without being asked to concede anything in public, that there was never enough here to prosecute — only enough to threaten.

The nonpolitical man spent four decades trying to keep his work out of the partisan crossfire. He did not entirely succeed, and this series has not pretended otherwise. But the final image this addendum leaves us with is almost fitting: not a trial, not a pardon, not a vindication, but a referral sitting untouched on a desk in Washington, because even the people who wanted it most couldn’t quite make themselves pick it up.

It is tempting to call this an ending, and it isn’t one. Referrals can sit for a year and then suddenly move, especially in an administration this responsive to news cycles and this willing to revisit settled questions when the political weather shifts. Rand Paul retains his committee gavel and his grievance, and there is no rule against him trying the same maneuver again closer to an election, when the appetite for a distraction might outweigh the embarrassment of a losing case. Nothing in this addendum should be read as a promise that the matter is closed. What it documents, instead, is a moment — one Friday in late August 2026 — when the machinery built to punish Anthony Fauci looked at itself honestly enough to hesitate. Whether that hesitation holds through November is a question for a future entry in this series, not this one. For now, the handcuffs Republicans spent three years promising remain exactly what they have always been: a threat that outlived its own evidence.
