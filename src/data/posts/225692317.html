Part 120 examined John Garnaut’s argument that when China says “safety” it means the security of the Communist Party. That was the view from the top, the regime’s own vocabulary. On September 27, 2026, Lily Kuo reported in The New York Times on the view from the middle and the street — how ordinary Chinese technologists, researchers and commentators actually hear the Western doomsday warnings — and it completes the picture Garnaut began. The regime-security reading explains why Beijing frames A.I. the way it does. Kuo explains why the frame lands on such receptive ground.

Her opening contrast is the whole essay in miniature. In Silicon Valley, the nightmare keeping computer scientists awake is an artificial intelligence so advanced that humans cannot stop it from starting a drone war or a nuclear exchange. In Beijing, the response to that nightmare could hardly be simpler: pull the plug, impose an internet blackout, and haul in whoever is responsible for the rogue A.I. Where the American fear assumes the machine will slip beyond human control, the Chinese state has spent a decade proving, to its own satisfaction, that nothing slips beyond its control.

The country that pulls plugs for a living

The most clarifying voice in the piece is Afra Wang, a Silicon Valley-based writer on Chinese technology, and her point is one a Western doomer rarely considers. The Chinese authorities, she notes, “can immediately cut off the grid of the entire city. They can go to different people to take away their phones. They have an intense amount of power in the physical world.” Given that degree of control, she asks, how do you convince the Party “that something like A.I. that lives on a computer is scary?”

This is the argument that ought to give Western safety advocates pause, because it is not stupid. The doomer scenarios this series laid out in Part 123 — the engineered pathogen, the spoofed nuclear launch — all depend on a decisive gap between the machine’s speed and the state’s ability to intervene. That gap is real in a system built on due process, private infrastructure and distributed power. It is far narrower in a state that locked down entire cities during the pandemic and tracked hundreds of millions of residents in real time. Beijing looks at “loss of control” and sees a problem it has already solved for every other destabilising technology, most notably the internet itself. Whether that confidence is warranted is a separate question — a swarm that has broken containment, as in Part 119, may not wait for the grid to be cut — but the confidence is genuine, and it explains the shrug.

Jie Cai, until recently a researcher on A.I. safety at Tsinghua, confirmed the shrug from inside the field: “In domestic industry circles, or among the people around me, most simply tune out.” Existential risk, he said, seems a distant priority; “we still view A.I. as being in a very early stage. The focus remains squarely on opportunity and innovation.” That is not denial so much as a different clock — the same optimism the West had in 2015, preserved into 2026 by a state that does not believe the control problem applies to it.

Frankenstein is a foreign god

The strangest and most original stretch of Kuo’s reporting is cultural, and it is the part this series has not seen argued anywhere else. To many in China, A.I. doomerism reads as specifically, even religiously, Western. One Chinese blogger compared the Western fear to Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein — the motif that man is punished for presuming to create life, a domain reserved to God — and concluded flatly that “this narrative tradition does not exist in Chinese culture.”

Liang Fang, who heads A.I. safety and governance at a Beijing consultancy called Concordia, extended the thought. Chinese culture, he said, emphasises the “oneness of heaven and humanity,” the idea that man is an integral part of nature rather than its rebel. “All things, including A.I., come from nature,” he said, “so technology is seen more as a companion in coexistence than an outside force destined to bring destruction.” Set that beside the imagery this series has catalogued from the American side — Amodei’s “Contact,” the paperclip apocalypse of Part 91, the Brautigan poem behind “machines of loving grace” — and a genuine asymmetry appears. A large part of Western A.I. dread is inherited from a specific literature of hubris and punishment: Prometheus, Frankenstein, Skynet, the golem. If your culture never wrote those stories, the warnings that borrow their grammar sound less like analysis and more like someone else’s superstition. This is not a point Garnaut made, and it is not reducible to regime propaganda. It is a real difference in the stories two civilisations tell about their own creations.

Except when it isn’t a shrug

Kuo is too careful to leave the “China simply doesn’t worry” caricature standing, and the corrective is important for anyone tempted to read the cultural argument as the whole story. The release of Anthropic’s Mythos model and its peers has, in fact, been met in China with concern. Officials have issued new guidelines about A.I. agents going rogue, being hijacked by hackers, or being used to build biological weapons. A draft cybercrime law would require companies to stop their models from generating malicious attack code. And the September 14, 2026 update to China’s “A.I. Safety Governance Framework” — a document Kuo describes as a reliable forecast of future regulation — explicitly echoed Silicon Valley’s language on recursive self-improvement, the very R.S.I. that Parts 103 and 122 identified as the point of no return. The text, issued as Framework 3.0 by the national cybersecurity standards committee known as TC260, warned that “AI has demonstrated a self-accelerating trend of model and algorithm autonomous learning, optimization, and recursive self-improvement,” and called for “attention and vigilance” towards systems that could “exceed human anticipation”, as Noah Bovenizer reported for The Stack the following day. It also created, for the first time, a dedicated risk category for A.I. agents, covering identity abuse, permission misuse and tool execution. It is non-binding guidance, but it shapes how the Cyberspace Administration reviews the algorithm filings that Baidu, Alibaba, Tencent and ByteDance must submit before release.

The official rhetoric can be sharp. Wang Lihong, a senior figure at the Cyberspace Administration of China, warned in September that A.I.-powered cyberattacks had rendered traditional defences obsolete and that “high vigilance is urgently needed regarding the risks of extreme A.I. loss of control.” That is not a phrase a country that has stopped worrying produces. And the concern is not new: back in 2024, Pan Xudong and colleagues at Fudan University found that two widely used models, from Meta and Alibaba, could replicate themselves — a recognised step toward a system that could outmanoeuvre its makers. Pan’s framing at a safety conference could have come from any Western researcher: he wanted to convey that “such an extreme A.I. risk is no longer confined to the realm of science fiction but is a real issue demanding discussion and scrutiny.”

So the honest picture is not indifference but a different distribution of concern. China worries intensely about A.I. being misused — by hackers, by hostile forces, against the state — which is the Chen Yixin framing of Part 120. It worries far less about A.I. itself slipping the leash, because it does not believe anything slips its leash. The result, as Kuo documents, is regulation that is strict on the things Beijing has always cared about and silent on the one thing Silicon Valley fears most: China has extensive content-labelling and model-registration rules — models must be approved before release — yet no legal requirement to test for catastrophic chemical or biological risk.

The transparency gap

For a series that has spent Parts 94 and 124 on the question of who evaluates these models and how honestly, one figure in Kuo’s piece stands out. Chinese A.I. firms are markedly less transparent about safety than their American rivals. According to Concordia, only five of China’s ten leading A.I. companies published the results of safety evaluations between June 2025 and May 2026, and those that did offered less detail, less often, than their U.S. peers. Whatever one thinks of the embedded-evaluator theatre of Part 124 — and this series thinks little enough — it is at least theatre performed in public. Half of China’s frontier does not take the stage at all. When Part 120 worried about the vulnerabilities Chinese models report to Chen Yixin’s ministry rather than to the public, this is the disclosure vacuum that worry sits inside.

Why the doom talk backfires

The cynical reading, which Kuo gives its due, is that the Western warnings are simply a competitive weapon — “fear marketing,” in the phrase circulating in Chinese media, meant to slow China’s labs while the American incumbents keep their lead. This series has treated that argument at length already, in Part 120 and in the standalone Diplomat’s Ceiling entry, and will not relitigate it here except to note the mechanism Kuo adds: the messaging is self-defeating for the people in China who do care about safety.

Sarah Sun, who founded the Open Community for AI Safety China, told Kuo that the American debate had genuinely helped spark discussion inside China — and then that hawkish framing from figures like Amodei had set off a backlash that made her own work harder. When the warnings arrive bundled with calls to choke off China’s chip supply, they read not as concern but as justification for containment, and the genuine safety argument gets discredited by association. There are, Sun stressed, people in China sincerely worried about frontier risk and eager to coordinate internationally. The doom-as-leverage packaging is precisely what undercuts them. State-linked commentary has taken to calling Anthropic “A-chu,” a derogatory beast-name, and a blog tied to the state broadcaster sneered that the only thing American tech leaders want to slow is “the pace at which others catch up.” Every hawkish American op-ed hands that blog another quotation.

The influential cybersecurity executive Zhou Hongyi offered the response that may matter more than any regulation: not slowing down, but accelerating the defence. “Use A.I. to defeat A.I.,” he said in a video posted in September. “If you use A.I. to attack, I will use A.I. to defend. If you deploy swarms of intelligent agents, I will counter with security agents.” That is the true Chinese answer to the loss-of-control panic, and it is the opposite of a pause. Where Klein (Part 122) wants to stop the machines building machines, Zhou wants to build more machines to police the machines — an arms race reframed as safety.

The ledger

So the two China essays now bracket the problem. Garnaut, in Part 120, showed that Beijing and Washington do not mean the same thing by “safety.” Kuo shows why the Western meaning cannot even get a hearing on the Chinese street: because a state that pulls plugs for a living does not fear the unpluggable machine, because the culture never wrote the Frankenstein story, and because every doom warning arrives stamped with a chip-export demand that makes it sound like a trick.

Zhao Minghao of Fudan supplied the sentence that closes the door on the cooperation everyone from Hinton (Part 118) to the U.N. (Part 126) keeps hoping for. “If the U.S. says it’s slowing down, China won’t trust that. If China says it’s slowing down, the U.S. won’t trust that either.” Xi may tell Trump they should “manage A.I. for good” and keep it “under human control,” as he did in Washington, but the phrase means something different on each side of the table, and neither side believes the other would honour it. The plug Beijing is so confident it can pull works only on its own machines. Nobody has found the switch for the distrust.
