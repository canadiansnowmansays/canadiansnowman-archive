Elon Musk and Douglas Adams — one built the rockets, the other wrote the manual. Or so it seemed.

“Everyone has their sort of favourite philosopher, but my favourite philosopher is Douglas Adams.”

— Elon Musk, CBS Sunday Morning, July 2019

Douglas Adams died on May 11, 2001, at a gym in Santa Barbara, California. He was forty-nine years old. He had written five novels in a trilogy, co-authored a book about endangered species with the zoologist Mark Carwardine, created an interactive computer game, been the first European to buy an Apple Macintosh, and produced the most quotable body of English-language science fiction since anything by anyone. He did not live to see the iPhone, Twitter, ChatGPT, or any of the other developments that would have provided him with material for another five books at minimum.

He also did not live to see a bullied South African teenager he never met build, piece by piece, the actual infrastructure his novels had satirised.

This essay is about the relationship between Douglas Adams’s imagination and Elon Musk’s engineering — and about what happens when a satirist’s jokes are adopted as blueprints by someone with the capital to build them.

When Elon Musk was twelve or fifteen — he is imprecise about the age, which may itself be significant — he experienced what he has described as an “existential crisis.” He was living in Pretoria, bullied at school, alienated from his father, and grasping for something that would give the universe meaning. He tried philosophy. He read Nietzsche and Schopenhauer and found them “really negative.” He has said he would not recommend them to teenagers, which may be the single most universally applicable piece of advice he has ever given.

Then he read The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy.

“It highlighted an important point,” Musk said in a 2013 interview, “which is that a lot of times the question is harder than the answer. And if you can properly phrase the question, then the answer is the easy part.”

This is Musk’s foundational intellectual claim, and it maps directly onto what he calls first principles thinking. Don’t start with the conventional answer. Start with the right question. Why are rockets expendable? Is that physics or habit? Why are electric cars slow? Is that batteries or design assumptions? Why is the meaning of life elusive? Maybe because, as Adams wrote, nobody knows what the actual question is.

In the novel, a race of hyper-intelligent beings builds a supercomputer called Deep Thought to answer the Ultimate Question of Life, the Universe, and Everything. Deep Thought processes for 7.5 million years and returns the answer: 42. The beings are outraged. Deep Thought explains, with considerable patience, that the answer is useless because they never knew what the question was in the first place. To find the question, Deep Thought designs an even larger computer — the planet Earth — which runs for ten million years before being demolished five minutes before completing its programme, to make way for a hyperspace bypass.

Adams wrote this as a joke. A very good joke — possibly the best joke in English-language science fiction — about the futility of assuming that computational power can solve existential problems. The more processing power you throw at the wrong question, the more absurd the result. The answer isn’t wrong. The question is missing. And the universe is indifferent to your frustration about it.

Musk read this joke and concluded: ask better questions. Which is a defensible reading, as far as it goes. But it does not go where Adams went. Adams’s deeper point was not that we need better questions. It was that the search for a single definitive answer to everything is itself the comedy. The joke is not that Deep Thought gave a bad answer. The joke is that anyone thought a definitive answer was possible.

Musk appears to have missed this layer entirely. He has been building increasingly powerful computers to answer increasingly large questions for his entire adult life, apparently without noticing that Adams was making fun of exactly this impulse. xAI’s Grok is — functionally, architecturally, and philosophically — Deep Thought. It is a machine built to answer questions about the universe. It is named after a Heinlein term meaning “to understand intuitively and completely.” The ambition embedded in that name is precisely the ambition Adams spent five novels demolishing with laughter.

But Grok is not the most Adams-relevant piece of the Musk stack. That distinction belongs to Grokipedia.

The Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy — the book within the book — is a reference work. It is the most popular publication in the known universe, having outsold the Encyclopedia Galactica by a wide margin despite being frequently inaccurate. It is carried by travellers everywhere. It contains entries on every subject imaginable, written with opinions baked in, and its editorial standards are, to put it diplomatically, inconsistent. Its cover bears the words “DON’T PANIC” in large, friendly letters.

Grokipedia is an AI-generated encyclopedia designed to replace Wikipedia. It is accessible worldwide through Starlink’s satellite network. It contains entries on every subject imaginable, generated by an AI system whose editorial calibration reflects the politics of its owner. It will be carried on every smartphone connected to Starlink’s direct-to-device service. Its editorial standards are, to put it diplomatically, determined by the man who also controls the social media platform where public discourse about those editorial standards takes place.

Adams invented the Guide as satire. An unreliable reference book carried everywhere by everyone, produced by underpaid researchers and powered by an editor more interested in lunch than accuracy. The comedy was that the galaxy ran on bad information cheerfully delivered. The Guide’s entry on Earth in its entirety: “Mostly harmless.”

Musk is building the Guide sincerely. Grokipedia will be, for hundreds of millions of Starlink-connected users worldwide, the reference source. Not a reference source. The reference source — because when your internet connection is provided by Starlink, and Starlink’s terms of service include zero-rated access to X and Grok (as the T-Satellite partnership already demonstrates), and Grokipedia is integrated into Grok, then the path of least resistance for any question about the world leads to Musk’s answer.

Adams would have written this scenario. He would have placed it in a chapter where Arthur Dent discovers that the Guide’s parent company has been acquired by a narcissistic galactic tycoon who uses it to promote his own products, and the entry on his competitors now reads “Mostly harmful.” The chapter would have been very funny. It would also have been a warning.

The parallels extend beyond the Guide itself.

Adams imagined the Babel fish — a small, yellow, leech-like creature that, when placed in your ear, translated any language in the universe by feeding on brain wave frequencies. The Babel fish was so useful that it inadvertently proved the non-existence of God (the argument being that something so useful could not have arisen by chance, therefore it must be proof of a creator, but proof denies faith, and without faith God is nothing, therefore the Babel fish proves that God does not exist, “and God promptly vanishes in a puff of logic”). Adams used the Babel fish to make a point about the unintended consequences of universal translation — that removing all language barriers simultaneously removed the only thing preventing every civilisation from understanding what every other civilisation was actually saying about them, which led to more wars than anything else in history.

Grok provides real-time AI translation across X. Starlink will deliver it globally. The unintended consequences of universal AI-mediated communication are already visible: algorithmic amplification of outrage, the collapse of shared epistemic frameworks, and an information environment in which everyone can hear what everyone else is saying and respond instantly, which has not — as the techno-optimists predicted — led to greater understanding but to greater conflict. Adams predicted this. He called it a Babel fish. He noted that it started wars. He was joking. The joke landed.

Adams imagined the Heart of Gold — a spaceship powered by the Infinite Improbability Drive, which worked by passing through every point in the universe simultaneously and was stolen by Zaphod Beeblebrox, the two-headed, three-armed President of the Galaxy who was, by his own admission, primarily interested in having a good time. Beeblebrox won the presidency largely on his charisma and his ability to distract the public from what he was actually doing, which turned out to involve the theft of the most powerful ship in existence.

The Heart of Gold. Powered by the Infinite Improbability Drive. Musk nearly named his Mars ship after it. That detail tells you everything you need to know about both men.

Musk almost named his first Mars-bound rocket after the Heart of Gold. He put “DON’T PANIC” on the dashboard of the Tesla Roadster he launched into solar orbit. These are not incidental references. These are a man telling you, in public, that his engineering programme is modelled on a novel about a charismatic narcissist who steals the most powerful machine in the galaxy while the public isn’t paying attention.

I want to be careful here. I do not think Musk sees himself as Beeblebrox. I think Musk sees himself as the builder of the Heart of Gold — the engineer who makes the Improbability Drive work while Beeblebrox clowns around at the controls. The problem is that Musk is both the engineer and the clown. He builds the rockets and posts the memes. He designs the chips and tweets conspiracy theories at three in the morning. The two-headed quality of Beeblebrox — one head for the public, one head for the scheme — maps onto Musk’s own oscillation between genuine engineering brilliance and the performative chaos of his public persona.

Adams would have been fascinated by this. Adams was fascinated by technology — he was an early internet adopter, a devoted Apple user, and a man who understood that the tools we build reshape how we think. He was also a committed environmentalist, a humanist, an atheist who thought the universe was wonderful precisely because no one was in charge of it, and a man whose entire body of work is animated by the conviction that certainty is comedy and humility is wisdom.

Everything Adams valued — uncertainty, humility, the suspicion that anyone claiming to have the answers is probably selling something — is absent from Musk’s public persona. Musk deals in certainty. Mars by 2030. Full autonomy this year. One billion robots. A terawatt of compute. The language of first principles becomes, in Musk’s hands, a language of inevitability. If the physics allows it, it will happen. If I can ask the right question, the answer follows. The universe is an engineering problem, and I am the engineer.

Adams’s universe is not an engineering problem. Adams’s universe is a comedy in which engineering problems are a subset of much larger questions about meaning, kindness, mortality, and the proper temperature at which to serve tea. The most powerful computer in Adams’s universe spent ten million years computing and returned “42.” The most powerful human in Musk’s universe is spending $25 billion building a chip fabrication plant and expects to return a terawatt. Both responses are answers to questions that were never properly asked. Adams saw the humour in this. Musk does not.

There is one more Adams creation that maps onto Musk’s empire with uncomfortable precision.

In The Restaurant at the End of the Universe, Adams introduces the Sirius Cybernetics Corporation — a company whose products are universally despised, whose marketing department is the first against the wall when the revolution comes, and whose fundamental design philosophy is that their products should appear to have a personality. The company’s customer service division is described as “a conditions-of-conditions-of-conditions clause in a conditions clause.” Its most famous product is Marvin the Paranoid Android, a robot of vast intelligence and bottomless depression, cursed with a “Genuine People Personality” that makes him aware of how much he is wasting his potential on menial tasks.

Tesla is building a humanoid robot called Optimus. It has a Samsung OLED face capable of displaying expressions and status communication. It is designed for general-purpose tasks — folding laundry, factory assembly, household chores. It will have, in the language of Tesla’s own marketing, a personality interface designed to make it “approachable.” It will be mass-produced. It will be, if Musk’s projections are correct, ubiquitous.

Adams invented Marvin as a joke about what happens when you give a machine intelligence and a personality and then ask it to open doors. The robot becomes depressed because it can feel the gap between its potential and its function. Musk is building Marvin — without, apparently, having read the chapters where the joke lands.

I suspect Adams would have looked at the Musk stack — Grokipedia as the Guide, Grok as Deep Thought, Starlink as the Sub-Etha Net, Optimus as Marvin, Musk himself as Beeblebrox — and written one more novel. A very funny novel. In which a man builds every single thing the Guide describes, sincerely, at enormous expense, and the universe responds with its customary indifference. The man becomes the most powerful person in the galaxy, and none of it answers the question he has been asking since he was twelve years old in Pretoria, lonely, beaten up, and reading a book about a man who was flung into space and discovered that the answer to everything is 42.

The answer is still 42.

The question is still missing.

And the man with all the satellites, all the robots, all the chips, and all the rockets is still looking.

Douglas Adams was born in Cambridge, England, in 1952. He was six foot five, chronically late with manuscripts, passionate about conservation, and the owner of a guitar collection he could not play well but refused to stop buying. He died too young in a country that was not his own, leaving behind books that have sold more than fifteen million copies and a philosophical framework that a bullied teenager in South Africa would one day adopt as the blueprint for the most ambitious engineering programme in human history.

Adams’s last book, published posthumously, was called The Salmon of Doubt. It was unfinished. The title referred to an old Irish myth in which the salmon of knowledge swims upstream against all obstacles, and whoever catches it gains all the wisdom of the world. Adams, characteristically, suspected the salmon was a red herring.

Musk is still chasing it. The salmon does not care.
