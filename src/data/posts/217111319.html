I did not go looking for this series. It found me in the middle of an ordinary conversation with Rikesh, the way the best ideas usually do — we were talking about Trump, about the United States more generally, and at some point the comparison that came up, unprompted, was Britain. Not modern Britain. Nineteenth-century Britain, the Britain of gunboats and unequal treaties and a navy that could simply decide what terms a smaller country would accept. It struck me as the right comparison the moment Rikesh said it, and then it struck me as an insufficient one almost as quickly, because the more I turned it over the more obvious it became that Britain was not a special case. Rome did this. Germany did this, briefly and catastrophically. The Soviet Union did this. The United States has done this more than once, under more than one flag of convenience. The pattern is old, it recurs across empires that had almost nothing else in common, and naming it properly seemed more useful than leaving it as a single passing comparison in a conversation with a friend.

I am not, it turns out, the only person reaching for this comparison this particular week. In among the reporting on President Trump’s address to the United Nations General Assembly on Tuesday, 22 September 2026 — the one in which he told the assembled leaders of 193 countries that he faced a choice between letting Iran “rebuild” or, in his own words, annihilating it — the New York Times’ own White House correspondents described another line from the same speech, Trump’s boast that “to the victor belong the spoils” over Venezuela’s oil, as language “drawn from the days of American gunboat diplomacy more than a century ago.” That is not my framing. That is the Times’ own reporters, on their own initiative, reaching for the exact same historical shorthand Rikesh reached for in conversation, the same week, independently. When a comparison surfaces twice from unrelated directions inside a matter of days, it is usually worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as rhetorical flourish.

Why “many nations qualify” is the problem, not the excuse

The obvious risk with a series like this one is scope. Every great power in recorded history has, at some point, used its strength against a weaker state to get something it wanted. If that were the entire argument — powerful countries sometimes push weaker countries around — it would not be worth fifteen essays. It would be a single paragraph, true of nearly every century and nearly every continent, and therefore true of nothing in particular.

So this series works from a rubric rather than a vibe. A case qualifies as an instance of nation-bullying, for the purposes of what follows, only if it satisfies all five of the following:

Overwhelming asymmetry. The power gap between the bully and the target has to be real and essentially uncontested — not a close contest that one side happened to win, but a mismatch clear enough that the outcome was never seriously in doubt.

Coercion as first resort, not last. Threats, blockades, or force arrive before serious negotiation is attempted, not after negotiation has genuinely failed.

Extraction is the actual point. Territory, resources, trade terms, tribute, or strategic position change hands, and the stated public justification and the real, operative goal diverge in a way that can be documented rather than merely suspected.

A self-justifying rhetoric dresses the extraction up. Civilisation, liberation, security, or some other elevated language is deployed publicly to describe what is, underneath, an act of taking.

Resistance gets punished. A target state that says no, rather than simply losing, is made an example of — economically, militarily, or both — in a way meant to be visible to everyone else watching.

Score a historical episode against all five and it belongs in this series. Score it against three or four and it is probably just an ordinary exercise of power in an unequal world, which is a different and less interesting subject. The rubric is doing real work here: it excludes a great deal of history that looks superficially similar but isn’t, and it is the reason this series can stop at fifteen essays rather than sprawling into every war of conquest ever fought.

A quick example of the rubric actually excluding something is more useful here than another paragraph describing it in the abstract. Consider the Marshall Plan — American money, on a genuinely enormous scale, reshaping the postwar economies of Western Europe in ways that plainly served American strategic interests as well as European recovery. Does it qualify? Run it against the five points and it mostly doesn’t: the recipient governments requested the aid and negotiated its terms rather than having it imposed by threat; there was no coercion preceding the offer, no invasion or blockade that produced compliance; and while the American strategic interest was real, it was also publicly stated rather than concealed behind a different justification. The Marshall Plan involved an enormous asymmetry of power used self-interestedly — but self-interest used through negotiated agreement is a different animal from the pattern this series is tracking, and the rubric is precisely what lets the difference be stated clearly rather than argued about in general terms. That is the kind of discipline the whole project depends on.

What this series is not claiming

It is worth being explicit about the limits of the argument before the history starts, because the alternative is readers filling in a stronger or weaker claim than the one actually being made. This series is not an argument that all the powers it covers are morally equivalent to one another, or that history is simply a closed loop with no genuine differences between empires, ideologies, or eras. Rome’s tribute system, the Holocaust, and a 2026 tariff threat over a small country’s rare-earth deposits are not the same thing, and nothing in the chapters that follow will pretend they are. What they can share, despite everything that separates them, is a recognisable behavioural pattern — the five points above, in roughly the same sequence, aimed at roughly the same kind of target. Naming that recurring pattern is the entire project. It is not a verdict that everyone who has ever qualified for it is equally culpable, and it is not a claim that the pattern is somehow inevitable or unstoppable, either. Britain’s own chapters, as it happens, end with the clearest demonstration in this whole series that the pattern can simply stop working — which is worth knowing going in, since it means this is not a story that only ever ends one way.

The shape of what follows

The series opens with Rome, less because Rome invented the pattern — it almost certainly didn’t — than because Rome perfected the specific combination of tribute extraction and civilising rhetoric (“Romanisation”) that every later case in this series echoes in some form, right down to the language Trump used at the United Nations on 22 September 2026.

Britain gets more room than any other power in this series, and deliberately so: no other case here spans as many centuries, as many continents, or as many genuinely distinct rubric-qualifying episodes under a single flag. Five parts follow Britain roughly in chronological order — starting closest to home, with Ireland, arguably the oldest and starkest case in the entire series, where grain continued to leave a starving country because the market said so; through the Opium Wars, where the practice the phrase “gunboat diplomacy” later came to name took its fullest form; through the Raj, where the civilising rhetoric was not merely implied but written down as policy, and later set to verse; through the Boer War, whose civilian internment camps brought the term “concentration camp” into common English use; and closing with Suez in 1956, the one moment in this entire series where a great power tried the old playbook one more time and simply watched it fail, forced into retreat by the same postwar order this Substack’s UN series has spent all year examining from the inside.

From there the series widens out geographically and chronologically: the Scramble for Africa, where multiple great powers bullied simultaneously and simply divided the results among themselves by treaty rather than fighting each other for them; Wilhelmine and Nazi Germany, included specifically as the cautionary case, where the first premise of the rubric — that the power gap has to be real — turned out not to hold, and the result was not a client relationship but a catastrophe; and the Soviet Union in Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and Afghanistan, read here for the bullying pattern itself rather than the Security Council mechanics this Substack’s UN series has already covered from a different angle.

The American material then runs in three parts, deliberately kept distinct because the method changes even where the pattern doesn’t. First, Theodore Roosevelt’s “big stick” era — the engineered secession of Panama from Colombia in 1903, timed precisely to the US Navy’s position off the isthmus, followed within days by a canal treaty with the government that secession had just produced; and the 1904 Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine, which turned the whole approach into published, standing policy rather than an improvised one-off. This is the overt, saber-rattling American case, closest in spirit to the British chapters that precede it. Then the Cold War, where the first resort was as often a covert coup as a gunboat — United Fruit’s Central America, Iran in 1953, Chile in 1973. Then the post-Cold War case, Iraq in 2003, the clearest recent instance of a stated justification (weapons that did not exist) diverging from what was actually extracted.

The series closes with the case that started this whole project in a conversation with Rikesh: Trump’s second term, scored against the same five points as every case before it — Greenland, the rhetoric around the Panama Canal (which, as it happens, closes an almost perfect loop back to Roosevelt’s own justification for taking it in the first place), Venezuela’s oil claimed openly as spoils, and the Iran ultimatum delivered from the UN’s own podium on 22 September 2026. A concluding essay then lays every case in the series side by side against the rubric and asks the one question the whole project exists to answer: is anything about the current American case actually structurally different from what came before — the existence of the UN itself as a nominal check, the scale of nuclear-era consequences, the speed of a news cycle that turns a threat into a headline within minutes — or is it, underneath the faster media, simply the same five-point pattern history keeps producing.

A note on companions

Readers of this Substack’s UN series will recognise some of the territory ahead — Hungary, Suez, and Iraq all appear there too, examined from the institutional side: what the Security Council could and couldn’t do about each of them. This series is not a retelling of those essays. It is the companion argument, looking at the same events from the other direction — not what the institution failed to stop, but what the bully was actually doing and why the pattern is old enough to have a name before the UN ever existed to fail at stopping it. Readers of “The American Contradiction” will find a related but distinct thread as well: that series traces America’s own internal cycles of exclusion and inclusion; this one is concerned with what the same country, and others like it, have done to people entirely outside their own borders, where the domestic reckoning that “The American Contradiction” tracks has generally not reached at all. The two series share a subject without duplicating an argument, and I’d rather say that plainly now than have a reader wonder later whether one has simply been folded into the other.

One more thing worth stating before the history starts: this series will not spare Canada an entry if a genuinely qualifying case turns up in the research. I do not currently have one in the outline above, and I am not going to manufacture one to prove even-handedness where the rubric doesn’t actually support it. But if Part VII’s research into the Scramble for Africa, or any later chapter, turns up a case where Canada itself scores against all five points — and Canada’s own historical treatment of Indigenous nations is the obvious candidate that would need honest reckoning if it belongs here — I would rather add it than pretend the rubric only ever points outward.

I do not know yet exactly when this series will begin in earnest; there is a great deal else in motion at the moment, and this one can wait for room. But the outline is set, the rubric is set, and I wanted it on the record before the idea went the way ideas from conversations sometimes do, which is nowhere.
