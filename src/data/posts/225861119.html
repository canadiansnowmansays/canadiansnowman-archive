Near the end of an 88-minute news conference at the White House on November 20, 2026, the day after he flew home from Shenzhen, Donald Trump looked out over the room and spotted somebody he wanted to show off. “By the way,” he said, “we have the most powerful man in the world in the room right now, chief of staff, Stephen Miller. Do you have anything to say?”

Miller had something to say. According to the transcript posted by Roll Call’s Factba.se, he spoke for 128 seconds and got through 367 words, which is a pace normally reserved for auctioneers and the small print at the end of car commercials. When he finished, the President announced, “We have breaking news,” asked Miller whether he wanted to run for president, and declared him “better than these two guys”, meaning the Vice President and the Secretary of State, who were standing a few feet away and smiling with the fixed expressions of men who have just been ranked.

Then, a few minutes later, Trump told the room to “forget about his, uh, little statement.”

It is worth not forgetting it. Miller had been chief of staff for eleven days. He got the job, as Reuters reported, when Trump promoted him after Republicans lost control of Congress in the midterm elections of November 3. This was his first chance to make the case, in public and on camera, for the presidency he now runs on Trump’s behalf. What he delivered was a highlight reel: every play that went right, run at double speed, with the losses edited out.

Stephen Leacock once observed that advertising is the science of arresting human intelligence long enough to get money from it. Miller’s list is political advertising in the same spirit. It deserves to be watched at normal speed.

The list

Here is what Miller said President Trump had achieved in less than two years: more than any president “by far in American history.” Net deregulation, at ten regulations removed for every new one, “never happened before in history.” DEI ended. “Transgender insanity” ended. The border “fully secured.” Record deportations. Record low crime and a record low murder rate. Record production of American fossil fuels and of American coal. “The first new nuclear reactors to go critical since the 1970s.” Every global trading relationship rebalanced. The entire military rebuilt, and the entire federal law-enforcement apparatus. Lethal force against the drug cartels “for the first time in American history.” Venezuela taken over “in less than an hour, doubling U.S. oil reserves overnight.” The relationship with Russia and China reformed. And all of this while “producing the strongest economy in American history, the fastest wage growth in American history,” and defeating the “20-plus percent year-over-year inflation” inherited from Joe Biden.

The culture-war items, DEI and “transgender insanity,” are statements of intent rather than claims of fact, and there is nothing to check except the vocabulary. The rest can be checked, and it sorts into three piles: what holds up, what bends, and what breaks.

What holds up

Credit where it is due. The murder rate is genuinely, historically low. Stateline reported in August that the FBI’s figures for 2025 showed the murder rate falling 18.1 per cent, to 4.1 killings per 100,000 people, tying the lowest rate since national estimates began, last seen in 1955 and 1956. Violent crime fell faster than in any year on record. That is good news, and no honest critic should pretend otherwise.

What Miller left out is the timeline. The decline did not begin on January 20, 2025. The crime analyst Jeff Asher, speaking to NPR on June 30, traced the drop back to the spike of 2021 and the long unwinding that followed, and he described 2025 as the third straight year of record declines. A president can fairly claim to have presided over a falling murder rate. He cannot claim to have started one that was already falling when he took the oath.

Deportations hold up too, with an asterisk. The Department of Homeland Security announced that ICE carried out 483,200 deportations in fiscal year 2026, which Al Jazeera reported broke every record in the agency’s 23-year history. But CBS News found that only 63 per cent of those deportations in July and August 2026 were formal removals; the rest were voluntary departures and returns. CBS also noted that broader government-wide measures of deportation were higher in parts of the early 2000s. The record is real if you count the way ICE counts. It is also less than half the million removals a year that Trump campaigned on, a target PolitiFact reported on September 8 was out of reach. Miller did not mention it.

What bends

The 10-to-1 deregulation ratio. The rule is real: an executive order early in the second term required agencies to repeal ten rules for every new one. The administration then announced it had beaten its own target by a mile, reporting 646 deregulatory actions against five new regulations, a ratio of 129 to 1. The George Washington University Regulatory Studies Center took the arithmetic apart in March 2026 and concluded that the White House regulatory office had gamed its methodology to produce the higher number. Counting only the actions that actually changed the federal rulebook, the ratio fell to 43 to 1. Government Executive reported in April that experts across the ideological spectrum agreed the administration had overstated its success. The deregulation is real. “Never happened before in history” is the bit that bends.

The nuclear reactors. This is the line that should have stopped the room. The first new commercial reactor built from scratch in the United States in decades, Unit 3 at Plant Vogtle in Georgia, reached self-sustaining fission in March 2023, as Fortune reported on March 7, 2023. Its twin, Unit 4, followed. CNBC noted the same day that it was the first new reactor start-up in nearly seven years, the previous one being Watts Bar Unit 2 in Tennessee in 2016. None of this happened in the 1970s, and all of it happened before Trump’s second term.

What the administration did achieve is narrower and still worth something. Under a Department of Energy pilot programme set up by executive order in May 2025, four small experimental reactors reached criticality by July 4, 2026, according to Idaho National Laboratory. These were zero-power test reactors at laboratory sites, not power stations feeding the grid. Miller turned a respectable laboratory milestone into a historic first by moving it back half a century.

Venezuela. The first half of the sentence is close enough. Energy Secretary Chris Wright told Fox News that the operation that removed Nicolás Maduro on January 3 took about 30 minutes. The second half, “doubling U.S. oil reserves overnight,” is fiction on two counts. The oil arrangement came nearly eight months after the raid, when Trump announced on August 28 that the United States would take majority control of more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan reserves, as Aamer Madhani reported for the Associated Press. And it was not an addition to American reserves in any ordinary sense. The Honolulu Star-Advertiser reported that the announcement gave few details about the fields, the companies involved, or how the United States would actually exercise control. Barrels in the ground in someone else’s country, under a partnership nobody has fully described, are not the same thing as barrels you own.

What breaks

“The strongest economy in American history, the fastest wage growth in American history.” On November 6, two weeks before Miller spoke, the October jobs report came out. It was a good report: 165,000 jobs and unemployment down to 4.1 per cent. But as Paul Wiseman reported for the Associated Press, hourly wages had risen just 3.1 per cent over the year, and once inflation was taken into account, average hourly earnings fell 0.7 per cent in September, the sixth straight year-over-year decline. Real wages that have been falling for six months are not the fastest wage growth in American history. They are not wage growth at all.

“Inheriting 20-plus percent year-over-year inflation.” This is the plainest falsehood in the speech. The annual inflation rate peaked at 9.1 per cent in June 2022, the highest in about 40 years. By January 2025, the month Trump returned to office, it was 3 per cent. FactCheck.org has spent a year pointing out that the highest twelve-month inflation in American history was 23.7 per cent, set in 1920, a century before Joe Biden. No year-over-year reading under Biden came close to 20 per cent. Miller seems to have taken the cumulative price rise over four years and relabelled it as an annual rate, which is a bit like announcing that it was 80 degrees all week because the daily highs add up to more than 500.

Nor has inflation been “defeated.” The last annual reading, for September 2026, was 3.7 per cent, according to Stock Analysis, higher than the rate Trump inherited. The Bureau of Labor Statistics could not publish an annual figure for October because the 2025 government shutdown left no October 2025 data to compare against, but the one category it could measure, gasoline, was up 37.4 per cent on the year.

“Lethal force against the drug cartels for the first time in American history.” What Miller is describing is the campaign of military strikes on small boats in the Caribbean and the eastern Pacific that began in September 2025. PBS NewsHour, republishing PolitiFact, reported that a strike on May 30, 2026 brought the death toll to 205. Human Rights Watch has called the strikes extrajudicial executions. In the very first strike, as NPR reported, the military killed the survivors with a follow-up attack. Whether this is a first in American history is beside the point. A chief of staff boasting that the government has killed more than two hundred people without charge or trial, under a secret Justice Department memo the public is not allowed to read, as ABC News has reported, is not making the case he thinks he is making.

The things not said

A highlight reel is defined by its edits, and the largest thing cut from Miller’s list was a war.

The United States and Israel went to war with Iran on February 28, 2026. It is the reason gasoline is up by more than a third. It is the main reason, by the White House’s own reckoning, that Republicans lost the House and the Senate. On November 4, the Vice President wrote on X that “the reason we lost is the war,” as Axios reported. He added that anyone who wanted to argue that losing the Senate for six years was worth it should go ahead and argue it. The AP Voter Poll, a survey of more than 100,000 voters, found that about six in ten voters thought the war was the wrong decision.

That same Vice President was standing a few feet behind Miller while he spoke. Earlier in the same news conference, Trump himself, asked about Vance’s statement, called it “a very accurate statement” and said the war had driven up fuel prices. And then the new chief of staff stood up and listed the administration’s achievements without mentioning the war once. Not as a triumph, not as a cost, not at all. It was a list of everything except the thing that decided the election.

Then there are the omissions that follow from the war.

The voters. Miller called the past two years the greatest in American history, seventeen days after the country handed both chambers of Congress to the other party. In the days after the election, Newsweek reported, Trump’s approval fell to 32 per cent in the Economist/YouGov poll, the lowest of either of his terms.

The oil. Miller boasted about Venezuelan reserves the United States does not own and left out the reserve it does. The same Associated Press report on August 28 noted that the Strategic Petroleum Reserve had fallen below 300 million barrels, down more than 100 million since the start of 2026, drained to cushion the price shock of the war. While the President talks about doubled reserves, the actual national emergency stockpile is being used up.

The coal. “Record production of American coal” sat oddly beside another item in the Associated Press live news file of November 16, reporting that the Energy Department had defied a court order to keep a Michigan coal plant running past its retirement date.

The new Congress. The same file reported a memo from White House counsel Will Scharf telling every agency to get clearance from his office before agreeing to testify before Congress. That is the building Miller now runs, preparing for the investigations that a Democratic House and Senate are about to open. It was not mentioned among the achievements, but it tells you more about the next two years than anything that was.

And Miller himself. The architect of the deportation campaign he was boasting about is also the official who, after a second American citizen was shot dead by a federal agent in Minneapolis in January 2026, called the victim, Alex Pretti, an assassin with no evidence, as The Daily Beast reported. Record deportations come with records of another kind.

The next two years

Miller ended with a promise: “the next two years are going to be the greatest two years in American history,” and he was proud to be the chief of staff overseeing the staff “to keep delivering wins for this extraordinary American leader.”

It is worth looking at what those two years are actually going to involve. A Congress controlled by the opposition, with subpoena power. A president who said at the same news conference that he intends to veto whatever they send him for “two years and one month.” A war that the Vice President blames for the election loss and that the President says he would start again. Inflation above where it was when he took office. A boat-strike campaign under legal challenge. And a chief of staff whose public debut was a list of achievements in which at least four claims fall apart on inspection, and the single most consequential decision of the term never appears.

From up here in Canada, there is something almost touching about the performance. Miller is not trying to persuade the people who voted against his party on November 3. He is speaking to an audience of one, standing a few feet away. In that sense the speech worked. The President called it better than anything the Vice President or the Secretary of State could manage, and then, a few minutes later, told everyone to forget it.

The voters, as it happens, had already heard the list. They had been hearing it for two years. Seventeen days before Miller read it out at double speed, they gave their answer, and it is the one item no amount of editing can remove.
