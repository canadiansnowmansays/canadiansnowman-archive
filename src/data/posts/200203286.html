In George Orwell’s vision of the Ministry of Truth, the most important room was not the one where propaganda was written. It was the one where the questions were never asked.

Pete Hegseth has been building that room for eighteen months. On Monday, he finished it.

The New York Times has reported The Defense Department announcement that its press office — the institutional space whose entire purpose, whose only reason for existing, is communication with the public — has been designated a classified space. Off limits to journalists. Available, in the words of acting press secretary Joel Valdez, “by appointment only.”

The justification offered was that speechwriters handling classified material had been moved into the area. The press office. The public affairs office. The one staffed by people whose job titles include the word “public.”

Welcome to Part 10 of the Ministry of Truth.

The Architecture of Enclosure

This did not happen in a single day. It happened in steps, each one triggered by a court defeat, each one dressed in the language of security and procedure.

October 2025: The Pentagon imposed a comprehensive set of restrictions allowing it to designate journalists as “security risks” and revoke their press passes.

Late 2025: The New York Times sued, arguing the policy violated the First and Fifth Amendment rights of its journalists. A federal judge ruled in the Times’ favor and tossed out the October policy.

The Pentagon’s response was not to accept the ruling. It was to construct a new policy achieving the same ends through different means — mandatory escorts for all journalists anywhere in the building, and the closure of Correspondents’ Corridor, where reporters from major news organizations had maintained designated desks for decades.

April 2026: A divided three-judge appellate panel allowed the escort requirement to remain in place while the Trump administration appealed.

May 2026: The Times filed a second lawsuit, describing the interim policy as “patently retaliatory” and noting that the escort requirement rendered its journalists’ press passes “essentially worthless.”

June 1, 2026: The press office itself becomes classified space.

Each step followed a legal setback. Each step was accompanied by an explanation that inverted its own meaning. Each step closed off another avenue through which a reporter might ask a question and receive an answer from a human being not pre-approved to give it.

This is not bureaucratic drift. This is architecture. Someone designed these rooms.

The Department of War

One detail in Monday’s announcement deserves particular attention, because it appears in a statement issued by the office of public affairs — the office now classified — and it tells you everything you need to know about the project underway.

Acting press secretary Joel Valdez referred, in his official statement, to “the assistant to the secretary of war for public affairs.”

The Department of War. Not the Department of Defense, which is the department’s legal name, the name Congress gave it, the name that requires an act of Congress to change. The Trump administration’s preferred name. Used officially, in a public statement, by the person whose job is to communicate with the public, to describe the office that has just been declared off-limits to the public’s representatives.

In Orwell’s original, the Ministry of Truth was called the Ministry of Truth because it did the opposite of what its name suggested. The Department of War is called the Department of War because this administration wants you to know, with unusual candor, exactly what it is.

The press office calls itself the office of public affairs while becoming inaccessible to the public. The Department of Defense calls itself the Department of War while refusing to account for what its wars have done.

The biggest lie, as this series has argued from its first installment, is always in the name itself.

The Question Hegseth Cannot Answer

There is a reason the press office has been classified. There is a reason journalists require escorts. There is a reason Correspondents’ Corridor has been shuttered, the October security-risk policy replaced by an interim policy that achieves the same ends, and that interim policy now supplemented by the declaration that the room where spokespeople work is too secret for reporters to enter.

The reason is the boat strikes.

Since this series began tracking the Pentagon’s press restrictions and the military campaign being conducted under their cover, more than 200 people have been killed in strikes against boats the U.S. military accuses of carrying drugs in the waters off South America. More than 60 strikes. Strikes shrouded, as the New York Times has reported, in near-total secrecy — few bodies recovered, scant physical evidence of debris or the drugs the administration claims the boats were transporting.

The military has, by its own account, increased strike capacity. More fixed-wing attack aircraft and armed MQ-9 Reaper drones operating from bases in El Salvador and Puerto Rico. A suspected drug boat’s chance of evading the military, a U.S. military official told the Times, has dropped from roughly 50 percent to roughly 25 percent. The killing has accelerated.

And cocaine is still arriving in the United States.

A wide range of legal experts have said the strikes are illegal — the military is prohibited from deliberately targeting civilians, even those believed to have committed crimes, unless they pose an immediate threat. The strikes have been condemned by Colombia’s president as murder. Coastal fishing communities in Colombia and Ecuador have been devastated, with fishermen abandoning the ocean entirely because the small speedboats used by traffickers are indistinguishable from those used to catch fish. Ghost towns where there were once fishing villages. Men who once fed their families now picking up plastic bottles on beaches.

Zero evidence of impact on cocaine supply.

Here is the question Pete Hegseth cannot answer in a room with reporters present: If this is working, why won’t you tell us how?

And here is the question that follows immediately: If this is not working, why are you still doing it?

The classified press office is the answer to both questions. It is not a security measure. It is a silence measure. You do not classify the room where you announce victories. You classify the room where you cannot explain the losses.

What Gets Hidden When Nobody’s Watching

This series has tracked, across ten installments, the relationship between press restriction and operational impunity. The pattern is not subtle.

Parts 6, 7, and 8 documented how the Pentagon’s secrecy around the boat strike campaign made it functionally impossible to verify the administration’s claims about who was being killed, whether drugs were actually being transported, and what impact, if any, the strikes were having on cocaine flow into the United States. The military’s own social media posts — grainy videos of boats exploding — constituted the entirety of the official record. No names. No independent verification. No bodies, in most cases, to count.

Legal experts told the Times that the strikes appear to violate international law. The Times itself, through months of investigation, found the first physical evidence of a strike — a burned boat and two bodies — washed ashore on Colombia’s Guajira peninsula last December. The Colombian government’s forensic agency still has those bodies in custody. It has not been able to establish their identities.

Two hundred people killed. Two bodies identified. Zero confirmed drug seizures from the targeted boats. Zero documented impact on cocaine supply.

This is what operational impunity looks like. And operational impunity requires, as its precondition, the absence of reporters in the rooms where the decisions are made.

Hegseth has now completed that precondition.

The Times and the Building

There is a particular irony in the fact that the New York Times — the organization currently in federal court fighting for the right to have its journalists walk unescorted through the Pentagon — is also the organization that has done the most to document what the Pentagon is hiding.

The reporting on the boat strikes. The reporting on the promotion purges. The reporting on Hegseth’s intervention to put his personal aide on the Navy one-star list, and his subsequent denial to Congress. The reporting on officers being removed from promotion lists because their names appeared on anonymous watchlist websites. All of it produced by reporters working under escort requirements, with press passes described in their own lawsuit as “essentially worthless.”

Imagine what they might find if they could walk freely through Correspondents’ Corridor again.

This is precisely what Hegseth is imagining. The difference is that he finds the prospect alarming rather than reassuring.

Judge Robert Wilkins, writing for the appellate majority that today blocked the removal of 28 transgender service members, observed that the Trump administration’s military policies appeared “driven by the bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group” rather than evidence of operational necessity. The same observation applies to the press restrictions. There is no operational necessity served by classifying the press office. The speechwriters could be given a different room. The classification serves one purpose: fewer questions, fewer answers, fewer opportunities for the public to learn what is being done in its name, with its money, to people in boats off the coast of South America.

The Room Where It Happens

In Orwell’s Ministry of Truth, Winston Smith’s job was to rewrite the historical record — to make the past conform to whatever the present required it to have been. The work happened in small cubicles, out of sight, with no one watching.

Hegseth has not quite achieved that. The Times is still reporting. The federal courts are still, occasionally, ruling against him. Two hundred dead is a number that exists in the world, even if the names do not.

But he has classified the room. He has put the spokesperson on appointment-only access. He has renamed the department after what it does. He has moved the speechwriters in among the public affairs officers and called the resulting space too secret for the press.

The Ministry of Truth does not need to eliminate journalism entirely. It only needs to make journalism difficult enough, slow enough, expensive enough in legal fees and court time, that the questions arrive after the decisions are irreversible and the bodies have long since washed ashore.

Two hundred dead. Cocaine still flowing. Press office classified.

This is what it looks like when asking questions becomes, if not yet a crime, something close enough that the difference is difficult to explain to the families in Siapana and Puerto López who are still waiting to learn whose sons washed up on their beach.
