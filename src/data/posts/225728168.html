The new Congress will not be sworn in until January 3, 2027. The White House did not wait. On November 9, 2026, White House counsel William Scharf sent a letter to Representative Robert Garcia of California, the incoming chair of the House Oversight Committee, to “remind” him of “the foundational limits that the Constitution imposes on congressional investigations and subpoenas, which you and members of the new Congress have evinced a clear intent to transgress.”

A reminder is a curious thing to send someone who has not yet done anything. The letter, which the California Globe published in full, builds its case not on any subpoena (none had been issued) but on footnotes. Representative Emily Randall’s Bluesky post promising to pass out subpoenas “like candy.” Representative-elect Katy Padilla Stout telling future witnesses to “lawyer up, clear your schedules, & put some makeup on for the cameras.” Representative-elect Ojeda’s hope to see Secretary Hegseth “squirm.” It is the first constitutional warning I can recall that cites social media posts the way a law review cites the Federalist Papers.

Then came the line that made the news. Oversight conducted to harass or frighten, Scharf wrote, “may cross the line into blatant criminality,” and subpoenas issued to deprive citizens of their rights “may warrant further investigation.” As ABC News reported, Garcia answered that “Congress has wide latitude to conduct investigations and oversight, an authority inherent in the power to legislate.”

Part LXV of this series, “Lawyer Up,” recorded Garcia’s warning to the administration. This part asks the harder question, the one the Scharf letter was written to raise before anyone could answer it: what happens when the people told to lawyer up simply decline to show up?

In TACO terms (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate), the letter is the Threaten stage. The strategy behind it is pure Obfuscate. The goal is not to win the argument. It is to make sure the argument takes longer than two years.

Power, and the Process to Enforce It

Emily Bazelon and David French spent the first half of their conversation in the New York Times on November 19, 2026, circling exactly this problem, and French reached for the right case. It is ninety-nine years old.

In 1924, as the Teapot Dome scandal spread through the Harding administration, a Senate committee began investigating Attorney General Harry M. Daugherty’s failure to prosecute the men who had looted the Navy’s oil reserves. The committee subpoenaed the attorney general’s brother, Mally S. Daugherty, a bank president in Washington Court House, Ohio. Mally Daugherty did not appear. So the Senate sent its deputy sergeant at arms, John J. McGrain, to Cincinnati, where he took Daugherty into custody.

The case reached the Supreme Court as McGrain v. Daugherty, and in 1927 the justices upheld the arrest. “We are of opinion,” the Court wrote, “that the power of inquiry — with process to enforce it — is an essential and appropriate auxiliary to the legislative function.”

French’s point is that the two nouns in that sentence travel together. A power without a process is a suggestion. If Congress can investigate but cannot compel, then the subject of the investigation decides its scope, and, as Bazelon put it, that leaves the president defining the limits of Congress’s own powers. No one designing a system of checks and balances on purpose would build it that way.

The Scharf letter quietly concedes the first noun. It does not argue that Congress lacks investigative power; it argues about purpose, leaning on the Court’s insistence that inquiries serve a legislative end. The real fight is over the second noun. Congress has the power. The administration intends to deny it the process.

Three Doors, All Stuck

On paper, Congress has three ways to make a subpoena mean something. In practice, each one has a lock the executive branch holds the key to, or a hinge nobody has oiled in ninety years.

The first door is criminal contempt. A witness who defies a congressional subpoena commits a federal misdemeanour, and the House can vote to refer the case to the U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia. But the U.S. attorney works for the Justice Department, and the Justice Department works for the president. The history is not encouraging even under presidents who never boasted of their control over prosecutors. When the House held EPA administrator Anne Gorsuch Burford in contempt in 1982, the U.S. attorney declined to take it to a grand jury. When the House held Eric Holder in contempt in 2012, the Justice Department declined. When it held William Barr and Wilbur Ross in contempt in 2019, nothing happened. When it referred Mark Meadows and Dan Scavino in 2022, the department again declined.

The two people who did go to prison for contempt of Congress, Steve Bannon and Peter Navarro, each served four months in 2024. Both were out of government when they defied their subpoenas, and both were prosecuted by a Justice Department run by the other party. Neither condition will hold between 2027 and 2029. French laid out what he expects the playbook to be: claim that oversight threatens the presidency, use that claim to refuse cooperation, and pardon anyone cited for contempt. A prosecutor who will not prosecute is one lock. A pardon waiting behind it is a second. The door does not open.

The minority will be led by a man who has tested this door from the other side. This month House Republicans chose Representative Jim Jordan of Ohio, a close ally of the president, to lead them in the new Congress, as Tim Balk reported for The New York Times. In 2022 Mr. Jordan was one of five sitting Republican members subpoenaed by the House committee investigating January 6. He did not comply. The committee never held him in contempt. It referred him and the others to the House Ethics Committee, and the referral expired with that Congress. The man who will lead the opposition to the next round of subpoenas has already shown how little happens when a member of Congress simply ignores one.

The second door is inherent contempt, Congress’s own power to arrest and detain, which is what the Senate used against Mally Daugherty. It is constitutionally real and historically dusty. The last time either chamber used it was the Senate’s arrest of William MacCracken, upheld by the Supreme Court in Jurney v. MacCracken in 1935. In theory the House sergeant at arms could take a defiant cabinet secretary into custody. In practice, nobody wants to find out what happens when the Capitol Police arrive at a federal agency guarded by people who answer to the president. A power whose use might start a confrontation between two armed federal forces is not a process. It is a dare.

That leaves the third door: ask a court to enforce the subpoena. This is where Bazelon’s reading of the first Trump term becomes the essential piece of the argument.

The Clock Is the Defence

The House Judiciary Committee subpoenaed former White House counsel Don McGahn in April 2019. The administration ordered him not to testify, claiming “absolute immunity” for senior advisers. The committee sued. A district judge ruled for the House in November 2019, memorably writing that presidents are not kings. The case then wandered through the appeals court, panel and full bench, for more than a year. McGahn finally answered questions in June 2021, behind closed doors, under a settlement that limited what he could be asked, in a different Congress and under a different president. As Bazelon noted, the settlement produced very little that was new.

The Mazars subpoena for Trump’s financial records, issued in April 2019, went to the Supreme Court, which in July 2020 sent it back with a new four-part test for subpoenas aimed at a president’s personal papers: whether the information could be had elsewhere, whether the request is no broader than necessary, whether Congress has shown a detailed legislative purpose, and what burden it places on the president. The committee and Trump did not settle until 2022. By then the information was about history, not legislation.

And the border-wall case, in which the D.C. Circuit held that the House could sue over spending Congress had never approved, simply evaporated. The Biden administration stopped building the wall, told the Supreme Court the House cannot sue the president, and the Court vacated the ruling as moot without ever answering the question. Bazelon calls it what it is: a giant separation-of-powers dispute left unsettled.

Put the three together and the shape of the strategy is plain. A Congress lasts two years. Enforcement litigation, run with ordinary diligence and the occasional strategic appeal, lasts longer than that. Every subpoena the new House issues in January 2027 can be met with a claim of privilege, a lawsuit, and a stay. If the lawyers are patient, the 120th Congress will end before the courts finish. In a 2028 election year, the window shrinks further.

This series has spent most of its run tracking friction as a strategy against voters: purge notices, citizenship checks, investigations that need no court’s permission to start and no verdict to keep going. Part LI called the Fulton County ballot seizure “the investigation that doesn’t need to end.” Here is the mirror image. The administration’s own investigations run on no clock at all. Congress’s investigations run on a hard one. Delay costs one side nothing and the other side everything.

David Frum raised the same worry with Neera Tanden on his podcast for The Atlantic on November 18, 2026, arguing that Mazars had weakened subpoena enforcement. A ruling that looked at the time like a House win also gave every future target a four-part list of objections, each one good for a fresh round of litigation. The Scharf letter reads like someone has been studying that list for a while.

Bazelon’s Worry, French’s Bet

Congress could try to force the question: sue fast, sue often, and push the core issue (can a chamber of Congress sue the president to enforce its subpoenas?) to the Supreme Court. Bazelon is not sure that is wise, and her reason deserves to be taken seriously.

Trump, she observed, has a gift for pushing past the point where earlier presidents stopped, which forces courts to resolve questions everyone had assumed were settled. Before he argued that a former president could not be prosecuted for crimes committed in office, the working assumption was that he could be. That assumption was a useful unwritten deterrent. Then came Trump v. United States on July 1, 2024, and the unwritten rule became a written one, pointing the other way. An unanswered question can be a kind of protection. Ask this Court whether Congress can sue the president, and it might tell you no.

French is more hopeful. He predicted, with what he admitted might be regrettable confidence, that the Court will endorse Congress’s powers of inquiry along with McGrain’s “process to enforce it.” Bazelon called it a brave prediction. French’s reply is the best sentence in the piece: “Naïveté in the defense of the Constitution is no virtue.”

I find myself somewhere between them, which is where a Canadian usually ends up in an American argument. French is right on the law. McGrain is a strong precedent, and Mazars itself, for all its new hurdles, reaffirmed that Congress may subpoena even a sitting president’s records. Bazelon is right about the calendar. A favourable ruling in 2029 is a fine thing for constitutional scholars and no use at all to the 120th Congress.

The Doors Nobody Can Lock

The good news, as Bazelon points out, is that the stuck doors do not end the story. They only change which rooms Congress can enter.

Private citizens are not covered by executive privilege. Chief executives, contractors, donors, and the businesses that have grown rich alongside the president’s family can be subpoenaed, and the president cannot order them to stay home. The administration’s own departures are another source. Former officials who resigned or were fired over the past two years have, as Bazelon puts it, stories to tell, and many of them will tell them willingly. A hearing room is still a hearing room. The cameras Padilla Stout mentioned will be there whether or not the White House sends anyone.

Then there is the purse. Appropriations bills can carry conditions, and a House that controls spending can decide how generously to fund offices that refuse to answer its questions. Bazelon is right that whether Congress can enforce that power in court is also unsettled, after the border-wall case ended the way it did. But a budget is not a lawsuit. It does not need a judge's permission to pass. And the House writing this one will be unusually large. With 259 seats, the largest House majority in three decades, as The New York Times reported on 24 November, Hakeem Jeffries can lose votes on either flank without sinking a bill. Kevin McCarthy, who needed fifteen ballots to win the speakership in 2023, put it in four words: "It gives real power."

From where I sit, it is worth remembering how differently this works under a Westminster system. In April 2010, Speaker Peter Milliken ruled that the House of Commons had the right to see uncensored documents on the treatment of Afghan detainees, whatever the government’s claims of national security. In March 2011, when Stephen Harper’s government still would not produce the costing figures for its crime bills and fighter jets, the Commons found it in contempt of Parliament, the first such finding against a government in Commonwealth history, and the government fell on the same day’s confidence vote. Our ministers sit in the chamber that demands their papers. Theirs sit down the street and send letters.

The Honour System

French’s bleakest observation is also his most accurate. The American Constitution depends heavily on an honour system: presidents produce what Congress asks for because they always have, prosecutors pursue contempt because the law says they should, pardons are not used to protect a president’s own witnesses because no president has been shameless enough to try. Bazelon’s dry addition is that the honour system has become pretty dishonourable on many fronts.

The Scharf letter is what the end of the honour system looks like when it is written on letterhead. It does not say the White House will comply with lawful subpoenas. It says the White House will decide what is lawful, and it suggests that the people who disagree might themselves face investigation. It was sent nearly two months before the Congress it addresses even exists.

But a threat is also a confession. Nobody spends that much legal firepower warning off an institution whose investigations they expect to be harmless. Garcia’s reply put the constitutional point plainly: Congress’s authority comes from Article I, not from the White House’s permission. McGrain put the practical one in 1927. A power of inquiry has to come with a process to enforce it, or it is not a power at all.

The 120th Congress will spend two years finding out how much of that process still works. The White House is betting that the answer arrives after the Congress has gone home. The voters who gave Democrats a House popular-vote margin that French notes has now reached fourteen points, and 259 seats, were placing a different bet on November 3, 2026. The first subpoenas go out in January.
