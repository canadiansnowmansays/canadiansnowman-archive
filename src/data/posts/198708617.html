When we last checked in on Texas, the story was about building. James Talarico had won the Democratic primary outright, a 33-year-old machinist had flipped a Fort Worth Senate seat by 14 points in a district Trump carried by 17, and a party that hadn’t won a statewide race in thirty years was asking itself whether persuasion — not just turnout — might actually work in the Lone Star State.

That was the Democratic side. The Republican side is a different kind of story. This one is about demolition.

On May 19, with early voting already underway for the May 26 runoff, Donald Trump endorsed Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton over four-term incumbent Senator John Cornyn. In a lengthy Truth Social post, Trump praised Paxton as “a true MAGA Warrior who has ALWAYS delivered for Texas” and took a direct shot at Cornyn, saying the senator had not supported him “when things were tough.”

Cornyn’s crime? In 2023, he told reporters: “I don’t think President Trump understands that when you run in a general election, you have to appeal to voters beyond your base.” He also supported a bipartisan gun safety bill Trump opposed. He voted with Trump more than 99% of the time. It wasn’t enough. In the loyalty economy, 99% is a failing grade.

The endorsement landed like a grenade in the Senate Republican caucus — which is exactly what it was designed to do.

Not About Texas

Here’s what makes this endorsement different from the usual primary-season loyalty test: it wasn’t really about Texas at all.

Steve Bannon said the quiet part at full volume: “This is as much a vote of no confidence in John Thune as it is a vote of confidence in Ken Paxton.” Thune, the Senate Majority Leader, found out about the endorsement the same way everyone else did — by reading Truth Social. “I found out, I think, the way everyone else did,” he told reporters, with the carefully calibrated flatness of a man swallowing something sharp.

Sources told NBC News that the endorsement was driven by Trump’s fury over a series of Senate Republican defections. Thune had called the administration’s $1.776 billion “anti-weaponization fund” — designed to compensate Trump allies who believe they were targeted by government investigations — “a bad idea.” Senate Republicans were resisting Trump’s demand to fire the parliamentarian over her ruling that White House ballroom funding couldn’t be included in a budget reconciliation bill. The filibuster remained intact. The Save America Act was stalled. From Trump’s perspective, his own Senate majority was the problem.

So he punished it.

The Paxton endorsement was a disciplinary action wrapped in populist language. A reminder to every Republican senator that loyalty is the only currency that matters — and that the man who counts the currency can devalue your career with a single social media post.

The Other Candidate

Senator John Cornyn. Twenty-three years in the Senate. 119 bills signed into law. Voted with Trump 99% of the time. Still not loyal enough.

Before we get to the man Trump endorsed, let’s talk about the man he didn’t.

John Cornyn is a San Antonio native whose father flew B-17s in the Mighty Eighth Air Force during World War II. He studied journalism at Trinity University, earned a law degree from St. Mary’s, and later — while already sitting on the bench — went back to school for a Master of Laws from the University of Virginia. He was elected a district judge in Bexar County at 32, won a seat on the Texas Supreme Court at 38, and in 1997 became the first Republican elected Texas Attorney General since Reconstruction.

He has served in the United States Senate for twenty-three years. In that time, he has been the primary sponsor of 119 bills signed into law. He authored the Justice for Victims of Trafficking Act, which passed the Senate unanimously. He championed the OPEN Government Act, the most significant reform of the Freedom of Information Act in a decade. He helped lead passage of the First Step Act, the landmark bipartisan criminal justice reform. He co-authored the bipartisan gun safety bill after the Uvalde massacre — at a school in his own state, twenty-one people dead, most of them children. He served as Republican Whip, the second-highest ranking position in the Senate conference, and chaired the National Republican Senatorial Committee through two election cycles. He currently sits on Finance, Judiciary, Intelligence, Foreign Relations, and Budget.

He has never been indicted. He has never been impeached. He has never had his divorce records unsealed by court order. He voted with Donald Trump more than 99% of the time.

None of it was enough. In 2023, Cornyn made the mistake of saying what everyone in his party knew to be true: “I don’t think President Trump understands that when you run in a general election, you have to appeal to voters beyond your base.” For that single act of candour — since vindicated by every general election poll in Texas — he is now the other candidate. The one without the endorsement. The one the president of his own party told to go away.

The Trump Candidate

Ken Paxton: If you were designing a Senate candidate to maximize Democratic chances in November, you could hardly do better than this

Now meet the man Trump chose instead.

Start with the impeachment. In 2023, the Texas House — controlled by Paxton’s own party — impeached him for corruption and bribery related to his relationship with Austin real estate developer Nate Paul. The allegations included using the powers of the attorney general’s office to investigate Paul’s enemies, delay foreclosures on Paul’s properties, and interfere with police investigations. In return, according to impeachment testimony, Paul paid for Paxton’s home renovations and — in a detail that even a satirist would hesitate to invent — hired Paxton’s girlfriend so the two could meet in secret in Austin.

The Republican-controlled Senate acquitted him. Only two Republican senators voted to convict. Angela Paxton, a state senator and Ken’s wife of 38 years, attended the trial but was barred by Senate rules from voting or participating in deliberations. She sat and watched.

Two years later, in July 2025, Angela Paxton filed for divorce. “Today, after 38 years of marriage, I filed for divorce on biblical grounds,” she wrote on social media. The divorce records were initially sealed — unsealed only in December 2025 after media organizations sued, producing roughly 300 pages of documents revealing finances, property disputes, and the details of what “biblical grounds” meant.

Then there’s the securities fraud. Paxton was indicted in 2015 — his first year as attorney general — on felony charges of defrauding investors in a Dallas-area tech startup. The case dragged on for nine years before prosecutors agreed to drop the charges after a settlement. A separate federal investigation into bribery and corruption, prompted by whistleblower complaints from Paxton’s own former aides, was also eventually dropped. A State Bar of Texas attempt to sanction him over his effort to overturn the 2020 presidential election was overcome.

This is the man Trump called a “Fighter” who “knows how to WIN.”

The Math

The March 3 primary ended with Cornyn at 42% and Paxton at 41% — close enough that no one won outright, forcing the May 26 runoff. Wesley Hunt, the third-place finisher, endorsed Paxton within hours of Trump’s announcement, urging his nearly 300,000 primary voters to consolidate behind the attorney general.

A University of Houston poll conducted in late April showed Paxton leading the runoff 48-45, within the margin of error. But it’s the general election numbers that should make Republican strategists reach for something stronger than coffee. In hypothetical matchups, Democrat James Talarico led Cornyn 44-41 and Paxton 46-41. That was before the Trump endorsement — which, whatever it does for Paxton in the runoff, does nothing to improve his standing with the moderate suburban voters who will decide November.

The Republican response was remarkable for its candour. “This race could be the reason we lose the Senate,” one Republican familiar with state politics told NOTUS. Lindsey Graham acknowledged the seat would be harder to hold with Paxton as the nominee. Susan Collins said she couldn’t understand Trump’s thinking. Bill Cassidy called the anti-weaponization fund “a slush fund” and said he wouldn’t vote for the ballroom funding.

A Republican operative described the situation as a long-term “epic botch job” by the White House, noting that the party had already spent over $100 million in the primary — making it one of the most expensive Senate primaries in American history — and would now have to spend more. The operative also raised a concern that should keep Texas Republicans awake at night: if Paxton drags down the ticket in November, the GOP could lose some of the very congressional districts it redrew during Trump’s mid-decade gerrymander.

Think about that. The redistricting we documented in “When Democracy Fights Back” — the lines drawn to lock in Republican advantage for a decade — could be undermined by the party’s own presidential endorsement. The gerrymander, designed to be fireproof, may have met its accelerant.

The TACO Framework

For readers who have been following this analytical device across the series, the Paxton endorsement is a textbook case.

Threaten. Trump spent months signalling that an endorsement was coming and that whoever didn’t receive it should drop out. The implicit message to Senate Republicans: fall in line on the filibuster, the parliamentarian, the ballroom, and the anti-weaponization fund — or face consequences.

Accommodate. Thune tried. He got behind the $1 billion ballroom security request after a closed-door Secret Service briefing. He navigated carefully on the reconciliation bill. He spent months privately lobbying Trump to endorse Cornyn, or at least to stay out.

Capitulate. Not fast enough. The Senate caucus balked at the anti-weaponization fund, resisted firing the parliamentarian, and wouldn’t eliminate the filibuster. Partial capitulation bought no goodwill.

Obfuscate. The endorsement itself. A punitive act against the Senate majority leader, dressed up as populist empowerment. “Ken Paxton has gone through a lot, in many cases, very unfairly, but he is a Fighter.” The framing transforms a scandal-plagued candidate into a victim-hero, and a loyalty purge into a democratic mandate.

The View from the Other Side

James Talarico. Already running the general while Republicans settle scores in the primary. “It doesn’t matter who wins this runoff”

While Republicans were absorbing the shrapnel, James Talarico issued a statement that should be studied by every Democratic strategist in the country:

“It doesn’t matter who wins this runoff. We already know who we’re running against: the billionaire mega-donors and their corrupt political system.”

That’s a nominee who is already running the general election while his opponents are still bleeding in the primary. He’s not attacking Paxton or Cornyn by name. He’s running against the system — which means that whichever Republican emerges wounded from the runoff walks directly into a framing that was built before he arrived.

Democrats have long sought to flip Texas. They came within 3 points with Beto O’Rourke in 2018. They invested and lost with MJ Hegar in 2020. The difference this cycle is structural: a strong nominee who won his primary outright, a Republican opponent who will be either a four-term incumbent rejected by his own president or a scandal-plagued attorney general rejected by his own wife, and a national environment where Trump’s approval ratings are underwater.

Cook Political Report still rates the seat “Likely Republican.” But “likely” is not “safe.” And the party that’s supposed to be defending the seat just spent $100 million trying to destroy its own nominee.

Five Days

Early voting is underway. The runoff is May 26. By this time next week, we’ll know whether the most powerful endorsement in Republican politics can overcome a decade of scandal — and whether the party that built the gerrymander will have to live in the house it burned down.

Everything’s bigger in Texas, including the consequences.
