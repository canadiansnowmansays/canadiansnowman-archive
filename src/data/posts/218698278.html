This is satire. The inspection report, the Division, the inspector and the Committee are invented. The quotations from Ken Paxton, Donald Trump, John Cornyn and Nick Maddux, the dates, the polling, the ad spending and the history of Senate Bill 12 are real, as reported by the New York Times and others during the week of September 28, 2026. The illustrations are caricatures.

OFFICE OF THE ATTORNEY GENERAL OF TEXAS Division of Performance Standards (Senate Bill 12 Enforcement Unit)

FIELD INSPECTION REPORT

1. Background and Authority

Senate Bill 12 took effect on September 1, 2023. Its sponsors cited “a recent cultural trend” of drag shows accessible to children. It authorizes the Attorney General to seek injunctions and civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation. The Fifth Circuit allowed enforcement in February 2026. A federal judge blocked the law again on August 25, 2026, at which point the Attorney General announced he would appeal “immediately.”

The Division therefore has the statute, the resolve and the paperwork. All it has been missing is a performance.

2. Nature of the Complaint

On Thursday, September 24, 2026, at a private fund-raiser in a conference room near Capitol Hill, an attendee asked the complainant: “Do you feel like he’s a little bit of a drag?”

The complainant replied: “So to be honest with you, the numbers — when we did that convention — it dropped our numbers. Everybody’s numbers dropped. Right now, yeah, not good.”

The Division accepts this as a sworn complaint, sworn as far as anything said to a dozen lobbyists is sworn. It notes that the complainant has identified a drag operating in Texas, has described its effect on audiences, and has requested no remedy. The complainant’s senior adviser, Nick Maddux, has since described the complaint as “more manipulated nonsense.” The Division notes that this is the first time the complainant’s office has described a complaint by the complainant in those terms. Usually it reserves them for judges.

3. Statutory Definitions

The Division regrets that Senate Bill 12 does not, strictly speaking, contain the word “drag.” Its drafters preferred “sexually oriented performance,” which turned out to be harder to prove in court. For the purposes of this inspection, the Division has therefore adopted the definition its own complainant used:

Drag (n.): a performer whose appearance on the bill causes “everybody’s numbers” to drop.

Under this definition, a drag queen is a drag who insists on being the headliner.

4. Inspection Checklist

The inspector attended the matinee and followed the act across the Red River to its evening engagement. The Division acknowledges that it has no jurisdiction in Oklahoma. It went anyway, as is the Division’s custom.

5. Evidence of Drag

The Division collected the following evidence that the performer’s presence reduced the size of the audience:

Exhibit A, the box office. Every public poll of the Texas Senate race in September showed the complainant behind: Emerson, Marist, Texas Public Opinion Research, NBC 5/Telemundo, Big Data Poll. The Cook Political Report has rated the race a Toss-Up since August 20.

Exhibit B, the walkouts. Hispanic voters in the Rio Grande Valley, who first joined the audience in 2024, are leaving at intermission. The New York Times reported that the Trump flags have vanished from the Valley’s front yards. A Harlingen pastor described the new audience as “baby Republicans.”

Exhibit C, the empty seat. On September 30, the Vice President opened his Harlingen rally by saluting the complainant. The complainant was not there.

Exhibit D, the reviews. Senator John Cornyn, who previously held the complainant’s role in this production, offered a one-word notice: “Whoops!”

Exhibit E, the house. By mid-September, conservative super PACs had spent $98.5 million keeping the theatre open. The complainant’s own campaign had spent $465,000. On September 30 the campaign announced $20 million of its own. In the Division’s experience, a production does not paper the house this heavily when the headliner is selling out.

6. Findings

  1. The performance on October 1 does not meet the statutory definition of a sexually oriented performance. The Division wishes to be absolutely clear on this point. It wishes the image to leave everyone’s mind as quickly as possible.

  2. The performance does meet every definition of a drag recorded by this Division, all of which were supplied by the complainant.

  3. The performer insisted on top billing at an event that was nominally about someone else. Under Section 3, that makes him a drag queen.

  4. The Division notes for the record that the professional drag performers of Texas rehearse, hit their marks, tip their crews and do not blame the audience when the house is thin. They have asked, through counsel, not to be associated with this act. The Division considers the request reasonable.

7. Conflict of Interest

The Division is required to report that the complainant, the inspecting authority and the co-billed performer are the same person. He has told donors the act is hurting him, told the press that saying so was nonsense, and told the act’s management he will “close strong” alongside it.

The Division considered referring the matter to the Attorney General’s ethics officer. It decided against this on the basis of past experience.

8. Recommended Remedy

The Attorney General is authorized to seek civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation. The Division estimates the number of violations at one per public appearance, which yields a total far smaller than the outside spending now required to offset them.

The Division therefore recommends no penalty. Instead it refers the matter to the only review board in Texas with jurisdiction over this production: the voters. Early voting opens October 19. Election Day is November 3.

The Division notes that the review board has already seen the show.
