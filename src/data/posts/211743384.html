The United States Department of Agriculture was founded by Abraham Lincoln in 1862, in the middle of a civil war, on the theory that a nation feeding itself was a nation that could survive anything. Its banner now reads “USDA — GROWING AMERICA SINCE 1862,” and beneath that founding claim hangs a portrait of Donald Trump three storeys tall, staring down at the sidewalk like a shareholder inspecting a subsidiary.

A few blocks away, the Robert F. Kennedy Building — the Department of Justice’s headquarters, named for a slain attorney general who spent his tenure prosecuting organized crime and defending civil rights — now flies a banner between its columns showing Trump’s face over the words “MAKE AMERICA SAFE AGAIN.” The Department of Labor has its own: “AMERICAN WORKERS FIRST,” Trump’s portrait above a small stitched flag reading “Freedom 250.” At the Department of the Interior’s chief peace agency, the building itself has been renamed outright — the Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace, its founding mission carved into stone beneath a name that had nothing to do with founding it.

This series has spent eleven parts tracing a single compulsion: the need to see one’s own name on something permanent. What Part XII adds to the record is how far that compulsion has travelled since Part I. It no longer needs a casino, a tower, or a golf course. It has moved into the buildings that belong to everyone, that predate the man by decades or centuries, and that carry missions he did not write and does not administer day to day. It has moved, in other words, from ownership into occupation.

The banners are not decoration

It would be easy to read the USDA, DOJ, and Labor banners as ordinary administration messaging — presidents have long put their faces on department websites and press-briefing backdrops, and every White House decorates for its own priorities. But a banner is not a press release. A press release disappears from the news cycle in a day. A banner hung between the columns of a building that has stood since the New Deal is a different kind of object: it is meant to be seen by everyone who works there, every day, for as long as it hangs, and by everyone who walks past on the way to somewhere else.

The choice of buildings matters as much as the banners themselves. The Robert F. Kennedy Building is not an obscure annex — it is the physical seat of American law enforcement, named for a man whose death is itself part of the nation’s civic memory. Draping “Make America Safe Again” beneath his name is not simply decoration; it is a quiet argument about whose safety, and whose justice, the building now serves. The USDA banner performs a similar sleight of hand: it keeps Lincoln’s founding date as a badge of legitimacy while replacing the implicit subject of that legitimacy with a living president’s face. The institution’s history becomes backdrop for the individual’s brand.

The Institute of Peace, revisited

Readers of Part VI will recall how the U.S. Institute of Peace — an independent, non-partisan body created by Congress in 1984 to fund conflict-resolution research — became, through a hostile takeover of its board and a legal fight over its Washington headquarters, the Donald J. Trump United States Institute of Peace. The building’s new signage, carved in the same stone lettering as the institution’s name, makes the renaming permanent in a way a banner is not: banners come down; engraving does not, short of the kind of court order that took Trump’s name off the Kennedy Center this spring, only for the board to find a workaround and put it back as an inscription instead.

The Institute of Peace case previewed exactly what has now happened at USDA, DOJ, and Labor: the renaming did not require a scandal or even much public debate. It required control of the institution’s leadership, patience, and the understanding that once a name is carved into a federal building, removing it becomes someone else’s expensive, litigation-prone problem for years to come.

The roads, the bridge, the airport, and the wall

Beyond Washington’s departmental buildings, the more familiar instrument of the compulsion — the infrastructure sign — continues its steady multiplication. A highway sign now welcomes travellers to “President Donald J. Trump International Airport.” A green road sign somewhere in the country reads simply “President Donald J Trump Bridge.” In Florida, the 2025 legislature designated a “President Donald J. Trump Boulevard,” the designation printed directly onto the sign as if daring a future legislature to explain why it should ever come down. At one intersection, a municipal street sign has been amended rather than replaced outright: “Sea Grape Dr” retained on top, “President Donald J Trump Dr” added beneath it — two names for the same stretch of pavement, the older one demoted to a historical footnote on its own signpost.

There is also an older image circulating again this year: a plaque at the border wall near Yuma, Arizona, installed in June 2020, listing Trump’s name above the acting DHS secretary, the acting CBP commissioner, the Border Patrol chief, and the chief patrol agent for the Yuma sector — a chain of command reduced to a hierarchy of credit, with the builders and the funders and the agents who actually manned the wall listed only as supporting cast beneath the name at the top. That the plaque predates this year and is being recirculated now, alongside a fresh wave of banners and renamings, suggests less a new phase than a settled habit: infrastructure completed under this presidency, whenever it is completed, gets a name attached before it gets a ribbon cut.

When the name is implied rather than printed

Not every structure gets Trump’s name outright, and that is its own kind of tell. Renderings of a new domed stadium for the Washington Commanders — the team whose return to a rebuilt RFK Stadium site in the district became a personal cause of the administration’s, with the president publicly involving himself in the negotiations over the deal — carry no Trump signage at all; the branding is the team’s own burgundy-and-gold “W.” The absence is conspicuous precisely because everything else in this essay has taught the reader to expect the name to appear. It has not, yet. Whether it eventually does likely depends less on modesty than on leverage: a stadium deal with layers of federal, district, and team ownership involved is a harder surface to inscribe than a department banner that requires nobody’s permission but the administration’s own.

The renderings released for a proposed White House ballroom and a renovated airport terminal follow the same ambiguous pattern — palatial gold-and-marble spaces, the kind of architecture that announces status through material rather than lettering. Trump was photographed holding printouts of the ballroom renderings in the Oval Office, visibly pleased, gesturing at gilded ceiling coffers and crystal chandeliers reflected in checkerboard marble floors. No name is carved into these renderings yet, but the pattern established by the Kennedy Center, the Institute of Peace, and the departmental banners suggests that absence is provisional, not principled. A building this size, championed this personally, tends to acquire a name eventually — the only open question is whether it arrives as a plaque, an inscription, or a piece of legislation that makes the renaming someone else’s fight to win back.

The navy and the skies

The compulsion has also found its way into hardware that moves. A promotional rendering circulating this year depicts a warship — sleek, angular, firing missiles into a storm-lit sea — under the banner “TRUMP CLASS: USS DEFIANT,” with a small circular insignia showing Trump’s raised fist against an American flag mounted on the hull itself, as though the ship’s registry number were secondary to its patron. Naval vessels have carried presidential names before — the USS George H.W. Bush, the USS Gerald R. Ford — but those namings came posthumously, conferred by a Navy honouring a life already concluded. A “Trump Class” of warship, named and insignia’d while the man is still in office and the ships themselves may still be renderings rather than steel, collapses the usual distance between service and self-tribute into something closer to sponsorship.

A second rendering shows a stealth aircraft’s angular silhouette staged inside a hangar beneath a full-size American flag, lit for what is unmistakably a reveal event rather than a routine unveiling — the kind of theatrical presentation more familiar from a product launch than a defence contractor’s press briefing. The aircraft’s name and programme details are not the point of the image; the staging is. It is built to be photographed with a president standing in front of it, and the flag behind the aircraft does the same work the flag does behind every banner catalogued above: it borrows the nation’s own symbol to frame an individual as its embodiment.

Whether either object ever exists as more than a rendering matters less, for this series’ purposes, than the fact that the renderings were made and released at all. A warship named after the sitting president, insignia’d with his campaign gesture, is not military branding. It is the same instinct that put his face on the USDA banner, aimed at the Navy instead of the Department of Agriculture.

The pattern beneath the pattern

What connects the department banners to the airport signs to the ballroom renderings is not vanity in the ordinary sense — every politician enjoys seeing their name attached to something. It is the specific, patient exploitation of the gap between what a name change requires legally and what it requires practically. A statute can forbid an “additional memorial or plaque.” It cannot easily forbid a banner, which is temporary by definition until it has hung long enough to become permanent by habit. A board can be sued over a building’s name. It cannot as easily be sued over stone lettering installed while the lawsuit is still pending. A department’s founding mission cannot be legally altered by a banner strung between its columns — but the visual message received by every employee and visitor for as long as it hangs is that the mission and the man are now the same thing.

This is the throughline from Part I to Part XII: a psychology that first expressed itself in casinos and university branding has been given, in this second term, an entire federal apparatus to work with — buildings whose histories run deeper than any businessman’s, whose names once belonged to a president who freed the enslaved, a Congress that funded peace research, and a brother who prosecuted the mob. The buildings are still standing. What they say has changed.

What is most striking, cataloguing this material in the summer of 2026, is simply the volume of it. Part I of this series treated a single Trump Tower or a single Trump University lawsuit as evidence enough to build an essay around. Twelve parts later, the evidence arrives faster than any one instalment can hold it — a department banner here, a road sign there, a warship rendering, an airport terminal, a stadium under negotiation, a ballroom renovation, all surfacing within the same few weeks. A compulsion that once required a real-estate deal to express itself now requires only a press office and a print shop. That acceleration is itself the finding: the appetite has not changed since Part I, but the number of surfaces willing to be inscribed has grown far faster than the number of court challenges willing to stop it.
