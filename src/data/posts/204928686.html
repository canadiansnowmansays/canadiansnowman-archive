by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. You know this by now. It is the premise of everything I write in this series — that the number climbs, and every so often it lands on a year that was already carrying something, and I stop and tell you what.

This is essay 1,347.

I have been walking toward this number since 1313, when I sat at my desk in the small hours and felt the whole primal catalogue of fear go off at once, and told you there was a ship already at sea, a rat already in the fur, a number coming up the count that I did not want to be the man to write. I made a small dark joke of it then, because 1313 was allowed its jokes. This one is not. I want to say that plainly at the top, so you know what kind of essay you are holding. Every other entry in this series has been written with at least one eye on the wink. This one I am writing with my hands still.

Because the ship arrived. In October of 1347, twelve Genoese galleys put in at the harbour of Messina, in Sicily, and the men aboard them were already dying. What they carried came ashore with them, and within five years it had killed — the historians still argue the figure, and the arguing is itself a kind of horror — somewhere between a third and half of everyone alive in Europe. Twenty-five million people. Perhaps more. A number so large that it stops being a number and becomes a texture, a change in the light over an entire continent.

I do not know how to round that. I said at 1337 that I would not be rounding anything when I got here, and now that I am here I understand why. There is no rounder number for this. There is no version of twenty-five million that sounds better. It is the one count in all of history that refuses every instinct I have ever had about numbers — you cannot inflate it, you cannot brand it, you cannot make it land as anything but what it is. It simply sits there, too big to hold and too true to look away from.

Here is what the people of 1347 did not have.

They did not have a germ. They did not know there was such a thing as a bacterium, would not know for five more centuries. They did not know about the flea, or what rode in the flea, or that the small grey shape at the edge of the granary was the doorway the thing came through. They had no word for contagion that meant what we mean by it. What they had, instead, was a theory about bad air — miasma — and a conviction that the sickness was punishment, and a rising, desperate need to find someone to blame for it, which they satisfied in the worst ways available to them, and I will not detail those ways here except to say that the plague’s cruelty was not only in the dying. Frightened people, told nothing true about what was killing them, reached for the oldest and ugliest explanation, and murdered their neighbours over a disease none of them understood. That is its own essay, and a darker one, and I am not writing it today.

What I want to tell you about instead is the one thing they got right. Because they did get one thing right, and it is the reason this essay belongs in a series about counting.

They learned to wait. And they measured the waiting.

It happened a little after the worst of it, in the trading cities that had the most to lose and the most to fear from a ship arriving full of the dead. Ragusa — the city we now call Dubrovnik — passed a law in 1377 that any vessel arriving from a place of plague had to sit apart, offshore, untouched, for thirty days before anyone could come ashore. Thirty days. A trentino. Long enough, they had noticed — without knowing why, without any theory of why it should be so — for a sick ship to reveal itself. If death was aboard, thirty days would bring it out. If the thirty days passed and no one died, the ship was clean, and could enter.

Venice, watching, extended it. Thirty days became forty. A quarantino — from quaranta, forty — and that word, worn smooth by six hundred years of use, is the word we still say. Quarantine. Every time we have used it, in our own recent years of fear, we have been reaching back, without knowing it, to a group of frightened medieval traders who could not see the enemy and so did the only thing they could: they counted. They set a number against the catastrophe. They said, we do not understand this, we cannot see it, we have no defence that works — but we can wait, and we can count the waiting, and we can hold the line at a number and see what the number reveals.

Forty days. It is the most human thing in the whole terrible story. It is arithmetic used as a shield. Not to inflate, not to brand, not to make anything sound bigger or better than it was — just to hold, in the dark, against a thing too large to fight, a number you could actually keep. You cannot count twenty-five million. The mind slides off it. But you can count to forty. You can stand at the harbour and count to forty, and in the counting there is something that is almost hope: the belief that if you measure carefully enough, and wait patiently enough, the truth will come out of hiding and you will know what you are dealing with.

I have spent this whole series being a little glib about numbers. I have caught a president inflating them and a chronicler rounding them and I have made, I hope, some decent jokes at the expense of people who treat arithmetic as a mood. I stand by all of it. But 1347 is where I have to admit the other thing about numbers, the thing underneath the jokes, which is that counting is also how human beings have always tried to survive what they cannot comprehend.

We count because the alternative is to stand in front of the uncountable and go mad. We count the days since we quit something. We count the essays. We count to forty at the harbour while the ship sits offshore and we wait to learn whether it has brought the end of the world. The counting does not stop the plague. Nothing they had could stop the plague. But the counting was theirs — the one act of order they could impose on a year that had taken everything else — and I find, sitting here nearly seven hundred years later, that I cannot be glib about it at all. I am a man who counts things. So were they. They counted in the worst year there had ever been, and the number they arrived at saved lives, and gave us a word, and stands as proof that even the most helpless people in history found, in the simple act of measuring, something to hold on to.

So this is essay 1,347, and I have not made it funny, because it cannot be funny, and the fact that this series can hold an essay like this without breaking is, I think, the whole reason I split it off and gave it its own name. Some numbers are jokes. Some numbers are kings and sailors and marketing decisions that undersold a war. And some numbers are twenty-five million people, and a ship at Messina, and forty days at a harbour in the dark.

I will see you at the next one. It will be lighter; they mostly are. But I wanted this one to sit here, unrounded and unlaughed-at, because the people of 1347 deserve at least that from a man who makes his living counting — that when the count reached them, he stopped, and did not reach for the joke, and simply told you the number, and what it cost, and what they did with the only tool they had left.

They counted to forty. It is the bravest arithmetic I know.
