Governor Ron DeSantis signs the new congressional map, drawn in his office using partisan data — the map Florida voters explicitly banned in 2010.

Florida voters thought they had solved this.

In 2010, by ballot initiative, they amended their state constitution to prohibit exactly what happened to them last week. The Fair Districts Amendments were explicit: congressional districts may not be drawn to favour or disfavour a political party, and they must preserve the ability of minority communities to elect candidates of their choice. The voters wrote the rule themselves. They wrote it into the founding document of their state. They did it precisely so that no governor, no legislature, and no party could do what was done on June 10, 2026.

On that day, the Florida Supreme Court allowed it anyway.

The Ruling

In a 6-1 decision, the court permitted Florida’s new Republican-drawn congressional map to be used in the 2026 midterms, denying the challengers’ request for a temporary injunction without ruling on the merits of the case. The map will stand for November. The underlying lawsuit will continue afterward, in the way that lawsuits continue after the election they were meant to affect has already been decided.

The lone dissenter was Justice Jorge Labarga — the only member of the court not appointed by Governor Ron DeSantis. Labarga argued the court had jurisdiction and faulted the lower appellate court for failing to expedite review. He was outvoted by six justices, three of whom DeSantis had appointed directly.

The procedural posture is worth pausing on, because it is becoming the signature move of this entire redistricting era. The court did not declare the map constitutional. It simply declined to stop it before the election, knowing that the qualifying deadline for House candidates closed at noon that Friday — leaving no time for any court to rule on the merits before the field was locked. The map gets used. The question of whether it is legal gets answered later, when the answer no longer matters. This series documented the identical mechanism in Alabama, where the election calendar resolved what the Supreme Court would not. Florida ran the same play, in a different uniform.

2010 Fair Districts Amendments support map. Florida voters passed the anti-gerrymandering reforms with nearly 63% approval, writing the rules directly into the state constitution.

The Confession

What makes Florida distinct from the other states in this series is not the outcome. It is the candour.

In Louisiana, Jay Morris admitted on the record that he drew the map for partisan advantage. In Florida, the admission goes higher and runs deeper. DeSantis’s own aide, Jason Poreda, told lawmakers he drew the new map using partisan data. The governor’s office sent the map to Fox News with partisan colouring, displaying that Republicans could flip up to four seats based on 2024 presidential voting patterns. The map was drawn inside the governor’s office, against the wishes of his own legislature — DeSantis vetoed the map his own Republican lawmakers initially sent him, then handed them one drawn entirely by his staff.

And when accused of violating the Fair Districts Amendments, DeSantis did not deny it. He argued instead that the map does not need to comply with Fair Districts at all — because, he claimed, the amendment’s minority-protection language is itself at odds with federal law.

Read that argument slowly, because it is the through-line of this entire series. DeSantis is making the Callais argument at the state level. The Supreme Court ruled in April that race-conscious districting designed to protect minority representation is constitutionally suspect. DeSantis has taken that logic and turned it into a weapon against a state constitutional provision that voters adopted to protect themselves. The protection for minority voters is no longer a shield. In DeSantis’s hands, it is the very thing that makes the voter-approved anti-gerrymandering rule unenforceable. The amendment protects minority districts; minority-district protection is now legally suspect; therefore the amendment cannot bind him. The safeguard has been converted into the loophole.

This is what the National Redistricting Foundation’s Marina Jenkins meant when she called the 2026 plan the most pro-Republican congressional map in modern history for any state with fifteen or more districts. It was not an accident of geography. It was the stated intention, drawn with partisan data, defended with the argument that the rule against partisan drawing does not apply.

Florida’s new congressional map cracks former majority-Black districts, including the one long held by Rep. Al Lawson, redistributing voters to dilute Democratic-leaning areas.

The Human Arithmetic

Behind the procedural language sits a familiar redistribution. The map dismantled the district long held by former Representative Al Lawson, a majority-Black seat in north Florida, by redistributing its Black voters across four surrounding districts. A circuit judge — before being overruled by a DeSantis-appointed appellate court — had concluded the map was likely unconstitutional precisely because it diminished the political power of Black voters by scattering them.

This is the Louisiana pattern, transplanted east. In Louisiana, Cleo Fields’s majority-Black district was clustered out of existence. In Florida, Lawson’s was cracked apart. Different verbs, same grammar. The Voting Rights Act was written for the South, and as the New York Times has reported, roughly 60 percent of Black Americans now live in the South — a share growing as the Great Migration reverses. The map redrawn in Tallahassee hit the part of the country where Black Americans are increasingly building their lives, removing the representation just as the population concentrates.

The NAACP called the decision a blow to Black and Latino voters. Florida Rising’s Moné Holder framed it in the language this series keeps returning to: the question is whether voters get to shape democracy, or whether politicians can redraw the rules whenever it helps them stay in power.

In Florida, the voters had already answered that question in 2010. The court overruled the answer.

The Filthy Beast, in a New Habitat

Part XXXV traced the gerrymander back to Elbridge Gerry in 1812 and the pattern that has held for two centuries: every reform designed to constrain the manipulation eventually becomes the instrument of the next one. Florida is that pattern in its purest modern form.

The Fair Districts Amendments were the reform. Voters passed them in 2010 with nearly two-thirds support, an explicit, constitutional, citizen-authored attempt to slay the beast. For a decade they held, more or less. Then the legal landscape shifted — Callais weakened the federal protections, the state supreme court filled with appointees of the man drawing the maps — and the reform did not constrain the manipulation. It was simply read around. DeSantis did not repeal the Fair Districts Amendments. He did something more durable: he persuaded a court that they need not be obeyed.

Stoughton Cooley warned in 1892 that districting corrodes the political culture itself, that it teaches voters their participation is futile. The Floridians who passed the Fair Districts Amendments in 2010 were the opposite of futile. They organised, they campaigned, they amended their constitution. They did everything a citizenry is supposed to do. And in 2026 they watched a court decide it did not matter, on procedural grounds, with no ruling on whether they had been wronged, days before an election in which they will now vote under the map they had explicitly forbidden.

Where Democracy Fights Back

Florida voters fought for fair elections in 2010. In 2026, the constitutional protections they wrote were walked around by the very institutions meant to uphold them

Every instalment of this series asks the same question.

In Florida, the most honest answer is that democracy already fought back — in 2010, at the ballot box, in the most direct and legitimate way a people can act. They wrote the rule. The failure is not that the citizens did not resist. The failure is that the resistance was overruled by the institution meant to enforce it.

That is the darkest variant this series has documented. In Alabama, the courts at least blocked the map repeatedly before the calendar overtook them. In South Carolina, a state senate refused to act and the threatened maps did not materialise. In Florida, the people had already done the work, years in advance, and the court read their own constitution back to them and told them it did not bind the governor.

The underlying case continues. Common Cause, the Campaign Legal Center, and Equal Ground Florida will press it through the courts, and someday a ruling on the merits will arrive. It will arrive after November. It will arrive after the four flipped seats have been counted, after the representatives have been seated, after the map has done its work.

Justice Labarga, the one justice DeSantis did not appoint, wanted the court to move faster. He understood what the majority understood and chose to permit: that in redistricting, delay is not neutral. Delay is the decision. A map allowed to stand “for now” is a map that wins, because the election it governs cannot be un-held.

The voters of Florida built a wall against partisan gerrymandering and wrote their names on it. The machinery did not break the wall.

It walked around it, and the court held the door.
