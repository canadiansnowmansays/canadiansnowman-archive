When this series began, I assumed it would be a catalogue of Democratic programmes undone by a Republican president. That is how the reversals are usually described, by those who celebrate them and those who mourn them alike. The introduction admitted that the Reversal Ledger did not cooperate. This part sets out how badly.

Of the 122 reversals now in the ledger, 68 undid something created by Democrats. Thirty-six undid something bipartisan: a law passed with support from both parties, or a policy kept by presidents of both parties. Eleven undid something older than the modern party lines, a norm or an institution nobody had thought to call partisan. Seven undid something Republicans built.

Fifty-four out of 122. Nearly half of what has been reversed was never a Democratic agenda.

What Republicans built

Start with the seven that belong to the party now undoing them, because they are the most striking.

Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty in 1987. It was the first agreement of the Cold War to eliminate an entire class of nuclear weapons rather than merely limit them. It was abandoned in 2019. The Open Skies Treaty was negotiated under George H.W. Bush and signed in 1992; it was abandoned in 2020. The National Climate Assessment exists because Bush signed the Global Change Research Act in 1990, requiring one every four years; its authors were dismissed in 2025. The Environmental Protection Agency, whose research office was closed in 2025, was created by Richard Nixon in 1970. The regulation defining “harm” to an endangered species to include destroying its habitat was written under Gerald Ford in 1975, and its repeal was finalised in July 2026. In 1971 Elliot Richardson, Nixon’s Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, committed his department to taking public comment on its grant and benefit rules even where the law did not require it; that commitment was rescinded in March 2025. In 2004, under George W. Bush, the immigration service told its officers to defer to their own earlier approvals when a visa came up for extension; a rule proposed in October 2026 would end the practice.

To these one could add an eighth, which the ledger records as bipartisan but which belongs, in a sense, to Trump himself. Operation Warp Speed, the programme that produced the first COVID-19 vaccines in record time in 2020, was one of his first administration’s proudest achievements. In August 2025 his second administration cancelled twenty-two mRNA vaccine research projects worth about $500 million, part of the scientific line descended from it.

What both parties built

The thirty-six bipartisan items are where the pattern becomes a portrait.

The civil service. The Pendleton Act of 1883, which created a merit-based civil service after a disappointed office-seeker assassinated President Garfield. The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. Collective bargaining for federal workers, granted by John F. Kennedy in 1962. The Inspector General Act, also of 1978. All have been reversed or hollowed out.

Civil rights. Executive Order 11246, which Lyndon Johnson signed in 1965 and eleven presidents of both parties kept, barring discrimination by federal contractors. The disparate-impact standard in civil rights enforcement, used since the 1960s and written into employment law in 1991 under George H.W. Bush. The order requiring agencies to serve people with limited English, signed by Bill Clinton in 2000 and kept by George W. Bush. The Disadvantaged Business Enterprise programme for federal transport contracts, authorised in 1983 under Reagan.

The world. The World Health Organization, which the United States helped found in 1948. The United States Agency for International Development, created under Kennedy in 1961. The President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, created by George W. Bush in 2003 and credited with saving millions of lives. Voice of America, on the air since 1942. Membership of UNESCO, which Reagan left in 1984 and George W. Bush rejoined in 2003. Membership of GRECO, the Council of Europe’s anti-corruption body, joined under Clinton in 2000 and kept by every president since, including Trump in his first term. The Refugee Act of 1980 and the Temporary Protected Status programme created by the Immigration Act of 1990, which George H.W. Bush signed.

Science and the environment. Federal funding of university research, a partnership dating to 1945. The National Institutes of Health’s grant system. The National Environmental Policy Act, signed by Nixon. Fuel economy standards, signed by Ford. Appliance efficiency standards, created in 1975 and strengthened under Reagan in 1987. California’s authority to set its own vehicle emission standards, dating to 1967.

Money and accountability. The Impoundment Control Act of 1974, passed after Nixon refused to spend money Congress had voted. The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977. Public Service Loan Forgiveness, signed by George W. Bush in 2007. The Corporate Transparency Act of 2021, passed by a large bipartisan majority over Trump’s own veto as part of that year’s defence bill. Public broadcasting, created by Johnson in 1967 with support from both parties.

Names. The renaming of army bases named for Confederate officers, recommended by a commission Congress created in the same 2021 defence bill, also passed over Trump’s veto. The Kennedy Center’s name, set by a 1964 statute. The name of the Department of Defense, settled by amendments to the National Security Act in 1949.

What nobody thought of as partisan

The eleven longstanding items are, in some ways, the most revealing, because most of them were never written down as policy at all. They were assumptions.

That the Bureau of Labor Statistics, publishing official numbers since 1884, would not have its commissioner fired on the day of a bad jobs report. That the senior officers of the armed forces would not be removed for their perceived politics. That the Gulf of Mexico was the Gulf of Mexico. That the Smithsonian, founded in 1846, decided its own exhibitions. That the members of independent regulatory commissions could not be dismissed at will, as the Supreme Court had held since 1935. That the Federal Reserve was insulated from the president. That children born in the United States were citizens, as the Fourteenth Amendment had said since 1868. That people convicted of attacking the Capitol would serve their sentences. That federal land in the West would remain federal. That utilities would decide when their coal plants were too old to run. That a refugee told his status was being ended would have thirty days to answer, rather than the ten he has had since October 2026.

Three of those eleven were defended successfully: the Fed, birthright citizenship and the public lands, as Part V described. The rest have gone, or are going.

The settlement

Put the bipartisan and longstanding items together and a shape appears. They are not a programme. They are a settlement: a set of understandings that formed after the Second World War and were maintained, with adjustments, by presidents of both parties for most of the following eighty years.

The settlement held that the federal government should be staffed by professionals hired on merit and protected from political dismissal. That it should be watched by independent inspectors and its numbers published by independent statisticians. That it owed fair treatment to people who were not its friends, including contractors’ employees, people who spoke little English and refugees. That the United States should lead international institutions rather than leave them, and negotiate arms control even with its adversaries. That science deserved public funding at arm’s length from politics. That the environment was a legitimate subject of federal law. That some things, such as the military, the courts and the national accounts, should be kept out of party fights altogether.

Republicans built much of that settlement and defended it for decades. Eisenhower expanded it. Nixon added the EPA and NEPA. Reagan negotiated the INF Treaty and strengthened the appliance standards. The first Bush signed the climate research law and the Open Skies Treaty. The second Bush created PEPFAR. Conservative thought, in the tradition of Edmund Burke, has generally been suspicious of tearing down institutions that took generations to build, on the grounds that their purpose may not be obvious until they are gone.

What the ledger records is not only the reversal of a Democratic agenda. It is also the abandonment of that settlement by the party that helped write it.

The caveat

This series promised honesty, and honesty requires two qualifications.

The first is that not every reversal ran against popular opinion. Most Americans, in poll after poll, favour limits on transgender athletes in female sports, and the Supreme Court has now approved state laws imposing them. Some of the Democratic items in the ledger were themselves contested, issued late in a term, or struck down by courts on their merits. A reader who supports some of these reversals is not thereby a radical.

The second is that a settlement is not sacred because it is old. Institutions decay. Agencies grow complacent. Rules accumulate past the point of usefulness. A serious conservative case for reform could have been made against parts of the postwar government, and many conservatives have made one. But reform and demolition are different things. Reform asks what an institution is for and tries to make it work better. The ledger records, in most cases, no such question. It records the institution’s removal.

A Canadian’s view of the shore

From Toronto, the bipartisan settlement looked for most of my life like the American version of something Canada also had: a professional public service, a statistics agency nobody would dream of interfering with, a shared commitment to the institutions of the postwar world. Canadians have argued about the details as fiercely as Americans. But we assumed, as I suspect most Americans did, that the foundations were not on the table.

The ledger shows that they were. In Poe’s story, the walls of the cell were the Inquisition’s own construction, built by the institution that now used them against the prisoner. The walls of the American settlement were built by both parties. One of them is now taking them down.

The next part turns to the cost: what has been lost in the process that no future government, of either party, can put back.
