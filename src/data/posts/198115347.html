On Tuesday, the President of the United States was asked whether the economic hardship Americans are feeling would motivate him to make a deal to end the war with Iran (To Critics, Trump Remarks Reveal a Billionaire Out of Touch, New York Times, May 17, 2026).

“Not even a little bit,” he said.

“I don’t think about Americans’ financial situation,” he continued. His only consideration, he said, was preventing Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon. “That’s all.”

That same day, the Labor Department reported that inflation in the United States accelerated at its fastest rate in three years (For Trump, Soaring Prices Test Voters' Finances and Patience, New York Times, May 17, 2026). The average gallon of gasoline reached $4.52 — a more than 40 percent increase since the bombing campaign began in February. Grocery prices surged faster in April than any month in nearly four years. Consumer confidence dipped to an all-time low. A CNN poll found that 77 percent of Americans, including a majority of Republicans, believed the president's policies had increased the cost of living in their communities.

James Carville — the man who told Bill Clinton’s war room that the election was about the economy — called it “the greatest admission against interest in the history of the presidency.”

Vice President Vance, asked whether he agreed with the president, said: “I don’t think the president said that. I think that’s a misrepresentation of what the president said.”

Trump then went on Fox News with Bret Baier and doubled down.

“That’s a perfect statement,” he said. “I’d make it again.”

While the president was explaining that he does not think about Americans’ financial situation, the war he launched was dismantling a sovereign economy six thousand miles away.

Qatar — a desert peninsula the size of Connecticut that spent three decades converting natural gas into one of the highest per-capita incomes on earth — has been functionally severed from the global economy since February (The Iran War Is Crippling One of the World's Wealthiest Nations, New York Times, May 17, 2026). The closure of the Strait of Hormuz means virtually no gas has left Qatar's shore for more than two months. Ras Laffan, the nation's industrial centre for gas production — spanning more than 100 square miles of processing and liquefaction facilities — is shuttered. Loading cranes stand paralysed. Roads are blocked.

Then came the missiles.

Iranian strikes on Ras Laffan damaged critical infrastructure and reduced Qatar’s production capacity by 17 percent — damage that analysts say will take years to repair even if the strait reopens tomorrow. QatarEnergy, the state-owned energy giant, has lost billions since the war started. Every day the strait remains closed, Qatar hemorrhages hundreds of millions more in lost sales and shipping charter fees.

The International Monetary Fund expects Qatar’s economy to shrink 8.6 percent this year. The country imports 90 percent of its food. Fresh produce from Europe and grain from the Americas, which once arrived by sea, are now being diverted to costly airfreight routes or trucked overland through Saudi Arabia. Avocados airlifted from Tanzania. Government subsidies absorbing the price shocks so far — but for how long?

Qatar’s $600 billion sovereign wealth fund — with stakes in everything from Heathrow Airport to the Empire State Building — provides a deep cushion. S&P has maintained its sovereign rating. But the diversification strategy Qatar spent a decade building — tourism, international business, Formula 1, the most expensive World Cup in history — depends on the perception of stability. Images of Qatar’s airport under air raid warnings and Ras Laffan under missile attack, broadcast worldwide, are, as one analyst wrote, “incompatible with that perception in ways that are slow to reverse.”

The war has harmed Qatar’s hydrocarbon and post-hydrocarbon economic foundations simultaneously. Three decades of nation-building, suspended by a conflict in which Qatar is not even a combatant.

Lusail is home to a Parisian-style mall and a theme park with artificial snow.Credit...Erin Schaff/The New York Times

At Souq Waqif, Doha’s traditional market, vendors report far fewer international travellers in the closing weeks of what is usually peak tourist season. In the city of Lusail — home to a Parisian-style mall and a theme park with artificial snow — a choreographed fountain show at the Place Vendome mall on a recent Wednesday afternoon drew a single spectator, slumped against a stone wall, eating a sandwich.

That image will stay with me.

On Sunday — the day the president warned Iran that “the Clock is Ticking” and there “won’t be anything left of them” — a drone struck the Barakah Nuclear Power Plant in Abu Dhabi (Drone Strike Sparks Fire at UAE’s Barakah Nuclear Power Plant, Al Jazeera, May 17, 2026).

Read that sentence again. A drone struck a nuclear power plant.

Barakah is the first nuclear power station on the Arabian Peninsula. It supplies 25 percent of the UAE’s electricity and eliminates 22 million tonnes of carbon emissions annually. Three drones entered from the western border. Two were intercepted. The third hit an electrical generator outside the plant’s inner perimeter, starting a fire. Emergency diesel generators are now powering Unit 3. The UAE’s Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation confirmed no radiation was released. The IAEA’s director general expressed “grave concern” and called for “maximum military restraint near any nuclear power plant.”

The UAE’s foreign minister called it a “treacherous terrorist attack” and a “dangerous escalation” — and reserved the Emirates’ full right to respond (UAE Investigates Source of Drones After ‘Treacherous Terrorist Attack’ on Barakah Plant, The National, May 17, 2026). No country has claimed responsibility. The UAE has not publicly blamed Iran directly, though a diplomatic adviser to the UAE president hinted at Tehran, referring to “the principal perpetrator.”

The same day, Saudi Arabia intercepted three drones entering from Iraqi airspace (UAE and Saudi Arabia Report Drone Incidents as Iran War Deadlock Drags On, CNBC/Reuters, May 17, 2026). From Iraqi airspace. The same Iraqi airspace under which, as we will see, covert Israeli bases have been operating for over a year — bases that Washington almost certainly knew about and Baghdad’s own radars were shut down to conceal.

The IRGC spokesperson, Ebrahim Zolfaghari, warned that U.S. military bases in the southern Persian Gulf would be “deactivated.” A senior spokesperson for the Iranian armed forces told reporters that if Trump’s threats were carried out, the United States would “face new, aggressive, and surprise scenarios, and sink into a self-made quagmire.”

Oil responded instantly. Brent crude rose to $111.42 a barrel. West Texas Intermediate hit $107.98 — the highest this month. The International Energy Agency, in its latest monthly update, cautioned that global oil inventories are depleting at a record pace. UBS projected inventories would near all-time lows of 7.6 billion barrels by end of May (Oil Jumps as Trump Warns ‘Clock Is Ticking’ for Iran, CNBC, May 17, 2026).

And the president’s response to all of this? A Situation Room meeting, scheduled for Tuesday, to discuss military options (Trump Warns Iran ‘Clock Is Ticking’ as He Weighs Harder Strikes, Axios, May 17, 2026). Not diplomatic options. Not de-escalation. Military options. Regional intelligence officials told Fox News that Iran is pursuing a deliberate strategy of “deception and delay,” calculating that it can stretch the crisis past the World Cup and America’s 250th anniversary celebrations — events that would make restarting the bombing campaign politically untenable.

The ceasefire, which Trump last week declared was on “life support,” is now a diplomatic fiction sustained by the absence of anything to replace it. The president called Iran’s latest peace proposal “a piece of garbage.” Iran’s semi-official media described it as “reasonable and responsible.” The gap between those characterisations is the space in which the next escalation is being manufactured.

A drone hit a nuclear power plant. The president is convening a war council. And gas is $4.52.

And then there is the shepherd.

A shepherd with his herd in the Najaf desert in southwestern Iran on Tuesday. Israel operated at least two secret outposts in the desert during the war with Iran.Credit...Qassem Al-Kaabi/Agence France-Presse — Getty Images

In Iraq's western desert, on March 3rd, a 29-year-old Bedouin named Awad al-Shammari set off in his pickup truck on a grocery run to the nearest town of al-Nukhaib (In Iraqi Desert, Two Israeli Outposts Were Kept Secret for Months, New York Times, May 17, 2026). Somewhere on that drive, he saw something he was not supposed to see: soldiers, helicopters, and tents clustered around a landing strip.

He contacted Iraq’s regional military command to report what he had found.

A helicopter chased his truck, firing repeatedly until it jerked to a halt in the sand. His family searched for two days before finding his body, his head and fingers blackened, inside his charred pickup. They buried him beside the vehicle, beneath a simple grey tombstone.

What Awad al-Shammari had stumbled upon was an Israeli military base — one of at least two covert outposts operated intermittently by Israel in Iraqi territory for well over a year, used for air support, refuelling, and medical treatment during operations against Iran. The base predated the current war. It had been used during the 12-day war against Tehran in June 2025. Israeli forces had begun preparing the site as far back as late 2024.

The destroyed pickup truck in the Najaf desert on Tuesday.Credit...Qassem Al-Kaabi/Agence France-Presse — Getty Images

When Iraqi soldiers were dispatched to investigate, they were fired upon. One soldier was killed. Two were wounded. Two vehicles were destroyed. The unit retreated.

Iraq’s Joint Operations Command announced that “foreign” forces had attacked their soldiers. Iraq’s chief of staff called his American counterparts. “They confirmed the force is not an American force,” the regional commander told the New York Times. “So we understood it was Israeli.”

The implications are staggering. Washington almost certainly knew. The United States had compelled Iraq to shut down its radars to protect American aircraft, making Baghdad entirely reliant on U.S. forces to detect hostile activity on its own soil. Former top U.S. military commanders told the Times it was inconceivable that Central Command did not know about the Israeli presence.

A hostile state operated military bases inside a sovereign country — a country that has no diplomatic relations with Israel and whose population regards Israel as an enemy — and the country’s principal ally almost certainly facilitated it through silence.

“It shows a blatant disregard for Iraqi sovereignty, its government and its forces, as well as for the dignity of the Iraqi people,” said Waad al-Kadu, an Iraqi lawmaker who attended a confidential parliamentary briefing.

The war is expanding its footprint into countries that are not even parties to the conflict. And the cost is being measured not only in GDP forecasts and gas prices but in the body of a 29-year-old shepherd buried beside his truck in the Najaf desert, whose family says his killing has been ignored.

“They demand the government investigate this incident and why it happened,” his cousin said. “They want his rights respected.”

The president does not think about Americans’ financial situation. He does not think about Qatar’s liquefied natural gas industry or the vendors in Souq Waqif or the single spectator at the fountain show. He does not think about a drone striking a nuclear power plant in a country that is not even a party to the war. He does not think about Iraqi sovereignty or the radars Washington told Baghdad to shut down or the bases that appeared in the silence. He does not think about Awad al-Shammari.

He thinks about disarmament. That’s all.

Meanwhile, the economist who advised him on the campaign — Stephen Moore, a man not known for criticising Republican presidents — told the New York Times that “Republicans could face a tsunami election in November if inflation continues to stay high.” Barrett Marson, a Republican strategist in Arizona, put it more plainly: “You’re really not feeling our pain, you are adding to our pain, and on top of that, you don’t care about our pain.”

A conservative economist at the American Enterprise Institute observed that two presidents in a row have now watched consumer prices rise on their watch and dismissed those increases as temporary. “There have been an astonishing number of own goals in the last year and a half,” he said.

The White House Council of Economic Advisers predicted that prices would “go back down” once the war ended. Kevin Hassett mused on Fox News that GDP could top 6 percent this year. Most private forecasters expect less than half that.

And while the president was in China this week — attending a state banquet featuring lobster in tomato soup and roast duck, with his son Eric at his side raising questions about the lines between government business and private enterprise — he shared his principal takeaway from the trip on Truth Social:

“China has a Ballroom, and so should the U.S.A.!”

Gas is $4.52. Inflation is at a three-year high. Qatar is bleeding hundreds of millions a day. A drone hit a nuclear power plant. A shepherd is buried in the desert. And the president wants a ballroom.

The Senate parliamentarian, it should be noted, ruled this week against funding the White House ballroom in the budget bill. Even the accountants said no.

That’s a perfect statement. He’d make it again.
