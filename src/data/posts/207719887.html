Winston Churchill was, by the time he stood up in a college gymnasium in Fulton, Missouri, on March 5, 1946, no longer the man Scaramucci’s essay implicitly imagines delivering this speech from a position of settled postwar authority. He had led Britain to victory in Europe and then, in July 1945, been voted out of office by British voters less than two months after Germany’s surrender — a rejection so swift and so total that Churchill himself later described his wife’s private consolation, that the defeat might be a blessing in disguise, with the observation that at the moment it seemed quite effectively disguised. He arrived in Fulton as Leader of the Opposition, a private citizen with no government behind him, invited by a small Presbyterian college whose president, a Westminster alumnus with an unlikely personal connection to Harry Truman, had managed to secure a presidential endorsement for the invitation. Truman added a handwritten note to the college’s letter, telling Churchill that if he came, the president himself would introduce him. It is worth holding this detail against the version of Churchill’s authority Scaramucci’s essay implies — the confident architect of a durable Anglo-American order — because the man actually delivering the “special relationship” speech was, at that exact moment, a defeated politician borrowing a sitting American president’s institutional weight to give a private citizen’s remarks the standing of a state occasion.

It is worth asking, too, why an American president would want this particular argument delivered by someone else’s defeated former prime minister rather than in his own voice, because the answer says something about the political mechanics of alliance-building that the “term sheet” framing tends to skip. Truman’s own administration was, by early 1946, growing steadily more alarmed by Soviet conduct in Iran, Eastern Europe, and the emerging pattern of Moscow’s postwar behavior, but American public opinion, only months removed from a war fought in alliance with the Soviet Union against a common enemy, was not yet prepared to hear a sitting American president say so bluntly. Churchill, out of office, free of any formal diplomatic obligation, and possessed of a wartime reputation that gave his words weight without official consequence, was uniquely positioned to say in Missouri what Truman was not yet ready to say in Washington — and to let the American public’s reaction to a private citizen’s speech serve as a kind of test balloon for an argument the administration suspected it would eventually need to make itself. It is a very old diplomatic trick, dressing up a government’s actual thinking in the words of a friendly outsider so it can be disavowed if the reception goes badly and adopted if it goes well, and Churchill, whatever else can be said about his relationship with Washington across this series, was a willing and highly capable instrument for exactly that kind of test.

The speech itself, titled “The Sinews of Peace,” ran forty-six minutes and was heard by roughly fifteen hundred people in the college gymnasium, plus a considerably larger radio audience carried nationally and internationally. Its most quoted line arrived roughly two-thirds of the way through: “From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent.” Reporters had been given advance copies of the speech, but the passage containing that specific phrase had, whether by design or oversight, been omitted from the press advance, meaning much of the room heard the phrase for the first time as Churchill actually spoke it — a small accident of stagecraft that likely sharpened its impact considerably. But the speech’s other coinage, arriving earlier and delivered with rather less thunder, is the one this series has been building toward since Part I: “Neither the sure prevention of war, nor the continuous rise of world organisation will be gained without what I have called the fraternal association of the English-speaking peoples. This means a special relationship between the British Commonwealth and Empire and the United States.”

It is worth reading past that sentence to the next several, because Churchill did not leave “special relationship” as an abstraction — he specified, in the same breath, what he actually meant by it, and the specification is the detail nearly every subsequent invocation of the phrase, Scaramucci’s essay included, quietly drops. Churchill called for continued intimacy between British and American military advisers, common study of potential dangers, shared weapons standards, interchange of officers between the two countries’ military academies, and continued mutual use of naval and air bases — and then, explicitly, he named the working model he wanted extended more broadly: “the principle of the Permanent Defense Agreement between the United States and Canada,” in his own phrase, applied to the whole British Commonwealth rather than confined to the Anglo-American channel alone. This is not a minor footnote. In the single speech credited with naming the special relationship, Churchill pointed directly at the Permanent Joint Board on Defence — the institution established at Ogdensburg in August 1940, the institution Hugh Keenleyside served as Canadian secretary of from its founding — as the template he wanted the entire English-speaking world to adopt. The special relationship’s own coining moment cites the Canadian defense arrangement as its working example, and virtually no popular retelling of Fulton, including Scaramucci’s, ever mentions it.

The reception, in the moment, was considerably rougher than the speech’s later canonical status suggests, and it is worth recording plainly because Churchill’s own later vindication tends to erase how genuinely hostile the initial response was. American newspapers denounced the speech as needlessly provocative toward a wartime ally barely nine months removed from victory; the Chicago Sun called Churchill’s remarks “poisonous,” and when he passed through New York on his way back to Britain, protesters gathered outside his hotel chanting that the United Nations, not an Anglo-American bloc, was the future worth building. Stalin’s own response, delivered in a Pravda interview roughly ten days later, was sharper still: he compared Churchill’s call for a “fraternal association of English-speaking peoples” to Hitler’s own theories of racial supremacy, and characterized the speech as effectively a call to war against the Soviet Union. Even Truman, who had sat on the platform and introduced Churchill personally, found it politically useful afterward to distance himself publicly from the speech’s content, telling reporters he had not seen the text in advance — a claim that most historians treat with considerable skepticism, given how closely Truman had been involved in securing the invitation in the first place, but one that reflects how quickly the White House judged the speech’s reception had turned toxic enough to require some daylight between the president and his guest.

The reaction back home was, if anything, more awkward for Churchill personally, because it came from his own Parliament rather than from a wartime ally he could plausibly claim not to have fully understood him. Clement Attlee’s Labour government, only recently elected and still eager to test whether wartime cooperation with Moscow could be extended into the peace, took pains to distance itself from Churchill’s remarks, treating them as the private opinion of the Leader of the Opposition rather than any reflection of official British policy. A number of Labour backbenchers went further, attaching their names to a parliamentary motion criticizing Churchill directly for the speech — a formal rebuke, from the elected chamber of his own country, of a former prime minister for a speech delivered as a guest of a foreign government. Churchill himself recalled this episode ruefully in a Commons speech that October, noting that a speech which had drawn condemnation from his own colleagues eight months earlier would, by then, scarcely have attracted comment at all — an acknowledgment, in his own words, of just how quickly and how completely the argument he had been mocked for making in March had become the unremarkable consensus by autumn.

There is one further complication worth adding to the record, in keeping with this series’ running refusal to let Churchill’s own mythology stand in for the documented history underneath it: he did not coin “iron curtain” at Fulton, and by the time he used it there, he was not even the first person that year to have used it publicly. Churchill had already employed the phrase privately in a telegram to Truman on May 12, 1945, warning that an iron curtain had been “drawn down upon” the Soviet front and that nobody could say what was happening behind it — a full ten months before Fulton. But the phrase itself predates even that telegram by decades: it had circulated in European political discourse since at least the years following the First World War, describing the territorial divisions left by the collapse of the Russian and Austro-Hungarian empires, and closer to the war’s end, Nazi Germany’s own propaganda apparatus had used almost identical language to describe Soviet-occupied Europe — Joseph Goebbels employed the phrase in a February 1945 issue of Das Reich, and Germany’s finance minister used it again in a radio broadcast that May, both explicitly framing the coming Soviet occupation of Eastern Europe in the same “iron curtain” imagery Churchill would later make famous. It is a genuinely uncomfortable detail to sit with — that the phrase most closely associated with launching Western Cold War rhetoric had, within the preceding year, been deployed by the propaganda ministry of the regime the war had just been fought to defeat — and it is worth noting for exactly the reason this series exists: Churchill’s genius at Fulton was rhetorical delivery and political timing, not linguistic invention, and the distinction matters for anyone tempted to treat every memorable phrase in this family’s history as evidence of some singular, unrepeatable gift rather than a considerable talent for recognizing which existing words, spoken at the right moment to the right room, would stick.

It took roughly three years for the reception to invert itself. By 1949, with the Berlin Blockade broken, the Marshall Plan underway, and NATO in the process of formal establishment, Churchill was able to stand at MIT’s Mid-Century Conference and note, with evident and hard-earned satisfaction, that the same warnings that had once struck his audience as shocking now described the settled consensus of Western policy. This is the arc Scaramucci’s essay borrows when it treats Fulton as evidence of Churchill’s foresight — and the foresight was real, vindicated by events in a way genuinely few political speeches ever are. But the arc as usually told skips the three years in between, during which Churchill was, by the standards of his own government’s closest ally, in open and rather lonely disagreement with the prevailing consensus about how to handle the Soviet Union, delivering his most consequential postwar argument not from office but from the considerably weaker position of a defeated opposition leader borrowing an American president’s convening power to be heard at all.

There is a final detail worth sitting with, because it closes a loop this series opened seven parts ago. Churchill’s own text at Fulton makes clear that the special relationship, in his own conception of it, was never meant to be bilateral in the way the phrase gets used today. He wanted the Canada-US defense model extended to the whole Commonwealth — Canada was not an oversight in his vision, it was explicitly the working prototype. What happened instead, in the eighty years since, is that the phrase narrowed as it traveled: “special relationship” became shorthand specifically for London and Washington, the Commonwealth-wide extension Churchill actually proposed quietly dropped from the phrase’s popular meaning, and the country whose defense arrangement he’d held up as the model became, in nearly every subsequent invocation of the term, the unmentioned third party standing just outside the frame. Scaramucci’s essay, addressing “the English-speaking peoples” directly and imagining Churchill addressing Congress “one more time,” reproduces this narrowing faithfully — Congress gets the speech, Britain gets the audience, and Canada, whose defense board Churchill cited by name as his working template, does not appear in the room at all. It is not a new omission. It is the same omission Churchill’s own speech built into the phrase from the moment he coined it, simply repeating itself, eighty years on, in a Substack essay whose author almost certainly has no idea the omission has a history this specific.
