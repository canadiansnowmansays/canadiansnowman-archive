Every essay in this series so far has told some version of the same story: a Council built to require the concurrence of five great powers finds itself unable to act, because one of those five powers has an interest in the outcome and exercises the veto the Charter promised it. Korea is the exception that makes the pattern visible by breaking it, and the way it broke is almost accidental — a bureaucratic protest gone wrong, rather than any deliberate reform of the system this series has spent five essays describing.

A boycott over a chair, not a war

In January 1950, the Soviet Union stopped attending Security Council meetings entirely. The dispute had nothing to do with Korea, which was not yet at war; it concerned China’s seat. Since 1945, China’s permanent Council seat had been held by the Nationalist government under Chiang Kai-shek, which had lost the Chinese civil war to Mao Zedong’s Communists in October 1949 and retreated to Taiwan while still, in the UN’s eyes, representing all of China. The Soviet Union, which had recognised Mao’s People’s Republic immediately, demanded the Council seat be transferred to Beijing, and when the other members declined, the Soviet delegate Jacob Malik walked out and announced a boycott of Council proceedings until the matter was resolved. It was a protest tactic, not a strategic calculation about any future crisis — nobody in Moscow appears to have anticipated that the empty chair would matter for anything beyond the propaganda value of demonstrating Western intransigence on the China question.

The boycott held through the spring and into the summer, and on 25 June 1950, North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel in a full-scale invasion of South Korea. The Security Council convened that same day. With the Soviet seat empty and no delegate present to cast the veto every other essay in this series has treated as the Council’s defining structural feature, the Council passed Resolution 82, condemning the invasion and calling for North Korea’s withdrawal, without a single dissenting vote. Two days later, with North Korean forces continuing to advance, the Council passed Resolution 83, recommending that UN member states furnish South Korea with the assistance necessary to repel the attack and restore peace. On 7 July, Resolution 84 went further still, establishing a unified UN Command under a single member state’s military leadership — the United States, with General Douglas MacArthur in command — authorised to use the UN flag alongside the forces of the sixteen countries that eventually contributed troops, from Britain, Canada, Australia, and Turkey to the Philippines, Ethiopia, and Colombia. It remains, to this day, the only occasion in the UN’s history on which the organisation’s own flag has flown over a full-scale war fought under its direct legal authorisation, rather than over a peacekeeping or observer mission deployed after a ceasefire was already in place.

The “unified command” language did real work in practice, but it should not be mistaken for genuine UN control over how the war was actually fought. The Security Council resolutions recommended that member states place their forces under a single command and requested the United States to designate the commander; beyond that, the UN Command reported to Washington, not to the Council, through the entire course of the war. When President Truman relieved MacArthur of command in April 1951, over MacArthur’s public disagreements with the administration’s strategy for handling Chinese intervention, the decision was made and announced in Washington, with no Council vote, no Assembly debate, and no formal UN role in the process whatsoever — a vivid illustration that the UN’s authorisation had legitimised the war internationally without transferring any actual operational authority away from the government that already intended to fight it. The fighting itself killed an estimated two to three million people, soldiers and civilians combined, across three years, before an armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953 — an armistice, not a peace treaty, meaning that Korea has remained, in the strict legal sense the UN Command’s continued nominal existence still reflects, a war that was suspended rather than ended.

The Soviets return, and the door closes

None of this was legally uncontroversial at the time, and this series’ emphasis on the veto as the Council’s central mechanism should not obscure a genuine ambiguity the Korea episode exposed: nothing in the Charter’s text explicitly addresses what happens when a permanent member is simply absent rather than present and voting no. Article 27 requires “the concurring votes of the permanent members” for substantive decisions, and a reasonable lawyer could have argued in June 1950 — some did, at the time — that an absent permanent member had not concurred, and that the resolutions passed in its absence were therefore invalid on their face. The Council chose not to adopt that reading, then or since, treating an abstention or an absence as functionally different from an active negative vote, a precedent this series’ previous essay flagged as one of the quieter interpretive shortcuts that keeps the institution functioning day to day. Had the Soviet Union pressed the legal argument harder, or had any other Council member raised it formally, the entire Korean intervention’s UN legal basis might have unravelled in real time.

It is worth pausing on just how thoroughly this single episode taught every future permanent member the same lesson, because the evidence is negative rather than positive: no permanent member has ever repeated the Soviet Union’s 1950 mistake. In the seventy-five years since, through crises far more consequential to individual permanent members than a symbolic dispute over China’s seat, none of the five has walked out of the Council chamber and stayed away during a vote that mattered to its own interests, however furious the underlying dispute. China itself, whose seat question had triggered the entire episode, eventually resolved that dispute not through a boycott but through the ordinary mechanism of a General Assembly vote in October 1971, transferring the seat from Taipei to Beijing by a comfortable majority — a reminder that the Charter did, eventually, provide an orderly channel for exactly the representation question Malik had tried to force by walking out two decades earlier. The lesson of Korea, absorbed permanently into how every subsequent Soviet, Russian, American, British, French, and Chinese delegation has behaved at the Council table, is simple and has held without exception since: however strongly a permanent member objects to what the Council is doing, showing up and casting the veto costs nothing, while staying away costs everything.

The Soviets did not press it, for a straightforward reason: the boycott had backfired badly, and continuing it only compounded the damage. On 1 August 1950, the Soviet Union ended its boycott and returned to the Council, resuming its seat in time to take up the Council presidency for that month under the rotating alphabetical system — an almost comic detail, the delegation that had spent seven months boycotting the chamber now formally presiding over it. From that point forward, the loophole this essay is named for closed as completely and as quickly as it had opened. The Soviet Union, back in its seat, began vetoing further Council resolutions on Korea as a matter of course, and by autumn 1950 — with UN forces having pushed past the 38th parallel and Chinese forces entering the war on North Korea’s side that November — the Council itself was as paralysed on the question of how to proceed as it had been, in this series’ account, on Hungary, Suez, and every East-West crisis since.

The workaround built to survive the next boycott

With the Council re-deadlocked but American forces still fighting under the UN flag, Secretary of State Dean Acheson pushed through the General Assembly a resolution designed to ensure that no future Soviet veto — or future boycott, for that matter — could strand the organisation the way the Council’s paralysis now threatened to. The Uniting for Peace resolution, adopted by the Assembly on 3 November 1950 by a lopsided vote of 52 to 5, provided that whenever the Security Council failed to exercise its primary responsibility for peace and security because of a lack of unanimity among the permanent members, the General Assembly could take up the matter itself — convening in emergency special session within twenty-four hours if necessary — and recommend collective measures, including the use of armed force, to member states directly. It was, on its face, a fairly dramatic reallocation of authority, since the Charter as drafted at San Francisco had given the Council, not the Assembly, primary responsibility for exactly this kind of decision, and had built the veto specifically to keep the Assembly’s much larger and more unpredictable membership from being able to authorise action the great powers themselves opposed.

The resolution’s timing tells its own story about what it was actually designed to do. It did not, and legally could not, retroactively validate the Korean intervention, which had already proceeded under the accidental cover of the Soviet boycott months earlier. Its real purpose was prospective: to make sure that if the Soviet Union returned to its seat and began routinely vetoing action on Korea — which is exactly what happened within weeks — the United States would have an alternative venue already built and ready to use. Its architects were careful to frame the resolution not as an amendment to the Charter, which would have required the years-long ratification process by two-thirds of the membership and all five permanent members that makes formal Charter amendment all but impossible in practice, but as an interpretation of powers the Assembly already possessed under Articles 10, 11, and 14 — a framing that let the mechanism take effect immediately, by ordinary majority vote, rather than vanishing into the same ratification machinery that has kept the Charter’s actual text almost entirely unchanged since 1945. Uniting for Peace was less a general reform of the Charter’s architecture than a very specific insurance policy against one government’s veto, drafted and passed by the same government the veto would otherwise have been vetoing.

What the loophole has, and has not, since accomplished

The mechanism Acheson built has been invoked more than a dozen times in the seventy-five years since, and its record is genuinely mixed in ways worth stating plainly rather than folding into either an optimistic or a cynical reading of the UN’s capacity. It underwrote the Assembly’s authorisation of the UN Emergency Force at Suez in 1956, discussed in the previous essay in this series, after Britain and France vetoed Council action against their own invasion of Egypt — a case where the mechanism worked more or less as Acheson intended, producing the UN’s first large peacekeeping deployment. It was invoked again within days of the Soviet invasion of Hungary that same November, and the Assembly did pass a resolution condemning the intervention and calling for Soviet withdrawal — but no collective measures followed, no peacekeeping force deployed, and Hungary’s suppression proceeded regardless, because passing an Assembly resolution and mustering the political will and military capacity to enforce one turned out to be two very different things once the crisis in question involved a nuclear-armed permanent member acting within what it considered its own sphere. The pattern repeated almost exactly a quarter-century later: when the Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in December 1979, the Security Council predictably deadlocked on a Soviet veto within days, and the Assembly, convened under Uniting for Peace in its sixth emergency special session, passed a resolution in January 1980 by a margin of 104 to 18 demanding immediate Soviet withdrawal — a rebuke even more lopsided than the one Hungary had received, and, like Hungary’s, entirely without enforcement mechanism or practical consequence for the occupying power, which remained in Afghanistan for nearly a decade more. Most recently, Uniting for Peace’s Emergency Special Session mechanism has been the vehicle for the General Assembly’s repeated, lopsided condemnations of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine since 2022 — resolutions passed by margins exceeding 140 states, exactly the kind of overwhelming moral weight the mechanism was built to marshal, and exactly as legally non-binding on Russia as the Hungary resolution had been on the Soviet Union sixty-six years earlier.

The pattern across all of these invocations is consistent, and it is the pattern this entire series keeps circling: Uniting for Peace works, in the sense of actually producing enforceable collective action, precisely when the target is a state no permanent member is protecting — Egypt in 1956, notionally, though even there Britain and France’s own vetoes were what triggered the mechanism in the first place — and it produces moral condemnation without enforcement precisely when the target is a permanent member itself, or a client a permanent member is shielding. The loophole Korea opened by accident did not actually solve the structural problem this series has traced since the League’s own unanimity rule. It built a second chamber for the Assembly to voice its disapproval in, loudly and by overwhelming majority, whenever the Council’s veto-bound first chamber goes silent — a genuine achievement in a body starved of them, but not, on the evidence of seventy-five years, a substitute for the enforcement power the Charter’s framers deliberately withheld from anyone but the five states who insisted on keeping it for themselves.

The next essay in this series turns to the parts of the UN system that have never needed a Council resolution, an Assembly workaround, or a great power’s blessing to function at all — the technical, humanitarian, and development work that has quietly outlasted every political crisis this series has covered so far, including one Canadian diplomat with a personal stake in this project’s own family history.
