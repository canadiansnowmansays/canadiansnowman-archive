Every part of this series so far has traced a single monarchy or a small cluster of related cases across time. This part steps back for a single, deliberately compressed survey of every reigning royal house still standing in the world today, twenty-seven distinct dynasties, ranging from purely ceremonial figureheads with no formal governing power whatsoever to genuinely absolute rulers whose personal word remains, as this series traced through Saudi Arabia in Part XVII, close to the final word on matters of state.

Europe retains the largest single cluster, ten reigning houses in all, though their actual constitutional substance varies enormously. Britain’s own Charles III, examined at length in Part XVIII, presides over the most internationally prominent of these, a purely ceremonial constitutional monarchy whose head also serves, through a shared personal union this series has not needed to examine in detail elsewhere, as head of state for fourteen additional Commonwealth realms including Canada, Australia, Jamaica, and New Zealand, an arrangement under which each realm’s monarch is legally and constitutionally distinct despite being the same individual person, meaning Charles III is, in strict constitutional theory, King of Canada in a capacity entirely separate from his role as King of the United Kingdom, exercising that separate Canadian authority almost entirely through a Governor General acting on the advice of Canada’s own elected government rather than through any direct personal involvement whatsoever, a genuinely unusual legal architecture this series has not encountered anywhere else in its survey, and one this author, writing from Toronto, lives inside every day without ordinarily giving it a second thought. Several of these Commonwealth realms have, in recent decades, debated transitioning to a republican form of government entirely, most notably Australia, which held a national referendum on the question in 1999 that narrowly failed, and Barbados, which did complete the transition, formally removing the British monarch as head of state and becoming a republic in 2021, a reminder that this particular personal union, however durable it has proven so far, remains a live and ongoing political question in several of the fourteen realms rather than a permanently settled arrangement. Spain’s Felipe VI, Belgium’s Philippe, the Netherlands’ Willem-Alexander, Sweden’s Carl XVI Gustaf, Norway’s Harald V, and Denmark’s Frederik X, who acceded upon his mother Margrethe II’s own voluntary abdication in 2024, a decision this series notes as a considerably more modest echo of the voluntary self-limitation examined at length through Bhutan in Part XXI, all preside over similarly constrained constitutional monarchies, their actual governing power having been reduced, across the twentieth century, through broadly the same gradual accommodation strategy this series traced through Britain’s own three-and-a-half-century evolution. A further common thread among these European constitutional monarchies deserves separate attention, because it represents one of the more significant recent trends in how this ancient institution continues to quietly reform itself: the widespread abandonment, over the past four decades, of male-preference succession in favour of absolute primogeniture, granting the eldest child equal claim to the throne regardless of gender. Sweden led this reform in 1980, the Netherlands followed in 1983, Norway in 1990, Belgium in 1991, and Britain itself only in 2013, under the Succession to the Crown Act, which also removed longstanding provisions barring an heir from marrying a Roman Catholic, a change this series notes as a genuinely modern echo of the same accommodation strategy Britain traced across three centuries in Part XVIII, quietly modernizing the institution’s own internal rules well after its major constitutional battles had already been settled through other means.

Luxembourg’s Grand Duke Guillaume, who succeeded his father Henri on his abdication in October of 2025, and Liechtenstein’s Prince Hans-Adam II preside over two considerably smaller constitutional monarchies, the latter notably retaining somewhat greater residual reserve powers, including a limited royal veto over legislation, than most of its larger European neighbors. And Monaco’s Prince Albert II rules the smallest sovereign monarchy examined in this entire survey, a single city-state whose modern prosperity rests substantially on tourism, luxury real estate, and a longstanding tax-haven status rather than on any broader territorial or resource base.

The Middle East and Gulf region examined at length in Part XVII accounts for seven further reigning houses, and this series need not repeat that part’s detailed treatment here beyond noting the current rulers by name for completeness: Saudi Arabia’s King Salman, with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman exercising the great majority of actual day-to-day governing authority; Jordan’s King Abdullah II, whose Hashemite dynasty traces its own legitimacy to descent from the Prophet Muhammad and whose kingdom has functioned as a considerably more constitutionally constrained monarchy than most of its Gulf neighbors since reforms undertaken across the early 2010s; the United Arab Emirates’ President Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, ruling within the federal structure this series traced to its 1971 founding in Part XVII; Qatar’s Emir Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani; Kuwait’s Emir Mishal Al-Ahmad Al-Jaber Al-Sabah; Bahrain’s King Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa; and Oman’s Sultan Haitham bin Tariq, ruling the one Gulf monarchy this series has not examined in detail elsewhere, whose own historical legitimacy rests on a somewhat different Ibadi Islamic tradition distinct from the Sunni and Wahhabist frameworks underpinning its neighbors.

Asia contributes six further reigning monarchies, each resting on a distinct legitimating tradition this series has traced in detail across its earlier parts. Japan’s Emperor Naruhito, who acceded in 2019 following his father Akihito’s own voluntary abdication, the first in over two centuries of Japanese imperial history, occupies the purely symbolic constitutional role this series examined at length in Part XVI. Thailand’s King Vajiralongkorn, examined in Part XXII, presides over a monarchy whose actual legal protection remains considerably more absolute than its formal constitutional structure alone would suggest. Cambodia’s King Norodom Sihamoni occupies a similarly ceremonial constitutional role, the real power resting with the Hun family examined in Part XXIV, in a kingdom whose own monarchy, tracing its own lineage back through the medieval Khmer Empire this series has otherwise not had occasion to examine in detail, was formally abolished in 1970, endured the Khmer Rouge’s own genocidal rule through the mid-to-late 1970s under which the monarchy’s own institutions and much of the country’s educated class were violently destroyed, and was only restored, under United Nations-brokered peace accords ending decades of subsequent civil war, in 1993, with Sihamoni’s own father Norodom Sihanouk, who had already reigned once before his own country’s earlier 1970 upheaval, returning to the throne he had held decades earlier before eventually passing it to his son in 2004, a restoration this series notes as one of the more genuinely improbable survivals examined anywhere in this entire survey, a monarchy that outlived not merely political abolition but a regime that attempted, quite literally, to erase the society that had once supported it. Bhutan’s King Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck, examined at length in Part XXI, presides over the constitutional monarchy his own father voluntarily created. Brunei’s Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah, ruling since 1967, retains genuinely absolute personal authority over a small but immensely oil-and-gas-wealthy state on Borneo, a legitimacy model this series recognizes as directly comparable to the Gulf states’ own resource-funded bargain examined in Part XVII, though resting on personal sultanic authority considerably less constitutionally formalized than even Saudi Arabia’s own system. And Malaysia offers this series’ single most structurally unusual case anywhere in its entire survey: a rotating monarchy, in which the largely ceremonial role of Yang di-Pertuan Agong passes, once every five years, among the hereditary rulers of nine of Malaysia’s own constituent states, selected by a Conference of Rulers according to a fixed rotational order established at the country’s 1957 independence and generally followed by seniority of accession among the nine eligible sultans rather than by any competitive process, a genuinely unique mechanism this series has not encountered in any other case, designed specifically to prevent any single state’s own royal house from permanently dominating the federal symbolic office while still preserving each of the nine states’ own separate hereditary sultanate fully intact beneath the rotating national figurehead.

Africa and the Pacific together account for the remaining four houses this series has not already examined in dedicated parts. Morocco’s King Mohammed VI, whose Alaouite dynasty has ruled continuously since the seventeenth century and claims descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima, presides over a monarchy retaining substantially more formal executive authority than most of Europe’s own royal houses, including significant control over the country’s judiciary, military, and religious establishment, formalized in his own title as Commander of the Faithful, Amir al-Mu’minin, a designation this series recognizes as functionally comparable to the caliphal religious authority the Ottoman sultans claimed in Part XXIII, though considerably more narrowly scoped to Morocco’s own national religious establishment rather than any claim to leadership over the global Muslim community as a whole. Morocco did undertake a genuine, if still limited, constitutional reform in 2011, amid the same regional Arab Spring pressures this series traced testing the Gulf monarchies in Part XVII, formally requiring the king to appoint his prime minister from the largest party in an elected parliament rather than by unrestricted personal choice, a modest but real concession this series recognizes as continuing the same accommodation logic traced through Britain in Part XVIII, deployed here specifically to preserve the monarchy’s core authority by conceding a genuinely meaningful but still carefully bounded piece of it. Eswatini’s King Mswati III, ruling the last genuinely absolute monarchy remaining on the African continent, presides over a small landlocked kingdom, formerly known as Swaziland until his own 2018 decree renamed it, that has faced periodic and at times seriously violent pro-democracy protests over recent years, most significantly a wave of unrest in 2021 met with a harsh security crackdown resulting in dozens of deaths, without the same kind of decisive institutional response this series traced through Tupou V’s own reforms in Tonga in Part XX, and continues to operate under a political system that formally bans political parties from contesting national elections entirely, leaving Mswati III’s own personal authority over government appointments, land allocation, and national resources essentially unchecked by any of the constitutional mechanisms this series has traced constraining nearly every other monarchy examined across its final chapters. Lesotho’s King Letsie III occupies a more constitutionally constrained position within a kingdom entirely surrounded by South African territory, whose monarchy was itself briefly overthrown and restored across the late twentieth century amid a complex series of military coups, exiles, and South African-brokered diplomatic interventions, culminating in a 1993 constitution that established the current, considerably more constrained parliamentary system Letsie III has operated under since formally reascending the throne in 1996 following his own father’s earlier deposition and subsequent death in a car accident that remains, much like several of the other disputed royal deaths this series has traced across its earlier parts, a subject of persistent unofficial speculation within Lesotho itself. And Tonga’s King Tupou VI, examined at length in Part XX, completes this survey’s Pacific representation as the sole remaining reigning monarch across the entire Pacific island region, following Hawaii’s own stolen crown examined in Part XIX.

Considered together, this final tally of twenty-seven reigning houses reveals a genuinely uneven distribution of actual governing substance behind a formally similar title. At one extreme sit monarchs like Britain’s Charles III, Japan’s Naruhito, and Sweden’s Carl XVI Gustaf, whose formal constitutional power over their own governments’ actual decisions is close to zero, their continued relevance resting almost entirely on the kind of accumulated ceremonial and cultural authority this series traced through Bhumibol Adulyadej’s own moral standing in Part XXII. At the other extreme sit monarchs like Saudi Arabia’s Salman, Brunei’s Hassanal Bolkiah, and Eswatini’s Mswati III, whose personal word remains close to final law within their own kingdoms, resting on legitimacy models this series has traced through religious alliance, resource wealth, and simple unreformed tradition respectively. Between these two poles sit a considerable range of intermediate cases, from Morocco’s genuinely substantial but not absolute royal authority to Jordan’s more constrained constitutional arrangement, demonstrating that the single word monarchy, and the single image of a crowned figure it conjures, in fact conceals a spectrum of actual governing substance nearly as wide as this entire series has documented across its full historical survey from ancient Uruk to the present day.

It is worth noting, before closing this survey, that the very act of counting to twenty-seven required this series to make a handful of genuinely debatable classification choices, which is itself instructive about how blurred the category of monarchy can become at its edges even in the present day. The Vatican City State, ruled by a Pope elected for life by the College of Cardinals rather than through any hereditary mechanism, is sometimes informally described as an elective monarchy and excluded from this count specifically because its selection process bears no resemblance to any dynastic succession mechanism this series has traced elsewhere. Andorra presents an even stranger case, a co-principality jointly headed by the President of France and the Bishop of Urgell in Spain, an arrangement so far removed from any conventional single-monarch model that this series has likewise excluded it from the primary count of twenty-seven while acknowledging its own genuinely unique claim to some form of continued princely governance. These edge cases are a reminder that the category this entire series has spent twenty-five parts examining was never as cleanly bounded as the word king or queen alone might suggest.

What this final count of twenty-seven also reveals, considered against the far larger number of monarchies this series has documented falling across its previous twenty-five parts, whether to revolution in France and Russia, negotiated dissolution in India, colonial theft in Hawaii, or careful legislative dismantling in the Ottoman Empire, is the genuine rarity of institutional survival across the full sweep of recorded human history. Every one of these twenty-seven houses represents a legitimating story that succeeded, against very long odds, in outlasting the ancient civilizations, medieval kingdoms, and modern republics that surrounded and, in the overwhelming majority of documented cases, eventually overtook every other throne this series has examined.

Part XXVII turns from what survives to what this series has deliberately not covered, an honest accounting of the kingdoms, chiefdoms, and empires this survey could not reach, offered not as an apology but as its own kind of evidence for just how deeply and how universally this single idea, that one person’s rule over the rest of us needs some story to justify it, took root across every inhabited corner of the world.
