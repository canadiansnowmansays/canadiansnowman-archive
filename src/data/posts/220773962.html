⚖️ Serious — the gravest entry in this series. It is about a table of figures drawn up to organise the murder of a people, and about the long work of turning a number back into names. There is no wink in it anywhere, and the dog stays out of it. I will keep my hands still.

I count my essays, and for nineteen hundred and forty-one of them I have been making a single argument: that the count is never merely the count. That every number has a hand behind it, and a purpose, and a cost that someone has tried to move off the page. I have shown you counts that were weapons and counts that were shields, counts that rounded and counts that refused. I have not, until now, shown you the count in its final form — the moment when the whole apparatus of enumeration, the census and the card index and the statistical table, was turned to the purpose of deciding which human beings would be killed, and in what order, and by whom.

That moment has a date. It is January 20, 1942.

The villa

That morning, fifteen senior officials of the Nazi state — men from the SS, the Party, the Reich Chancellery, the ministries of the Interior, Justice, and Foreign Affairs, the administration of occupied Poland — gathered in a handsome villa on the shore of a lake called the Wannsee, in a quiet suburb of Berlin. The meeting was chaired by Reinhard Heydrich of the SS. The minutes were taken by Adolf Eichmann. By most accounts it lasted about an hour and a half, and was followed by drinks.

I want to be precise about what this meeting was, because precision is the only honour this essay can offer. The murder of Europe’s Jews did not begin at Wannsee. By January 1942 it was well under way: mobile killing units had been shooting Jewish men, women and children in the occupied Soviet territories since the summer of 1941, in their hundreds of thousands, and at Chełmno, in occupied Poland, the gassing of Jews in sealed vans had begun the previous December. Wannsee was not where the decision was made. It was where the decision was administered — where the ministries of a modern state were brought into line, their responsibilities assigned, their cooperation secured. It was a coordination meeting. That is its horror. It reads, in the surviving copy of the minutes, like the minutes of any interdepartmental committee anywhere: an agenda, a chairman’s remarks, a discussion of jurisdiction, a list of action items. The murder of millions was handled as an item of business.

Page six

And on the sixth page of the minutes, there is a table.

It lists the Jews of Europe, country by country, as the people the “Final Solution” would be applied to. The total at the bottom is about eleven million. The entries are arranged in two columns, and they are the most terrible figures this series will ever enter. The Soviet Union: five million. Ukraine: two million, nine hundred and ninety-four thousand, six hundred and eighty-four — a figure given to the single person, as if precision were a form of authority. Hungary: seven hundred and forty-two thousand eight hundred. And then the entries that tell you what kind of document this is. England: three hundred and thirty thousand. Ireland: four thousand. Sweden, Switzerland, Spain, Portugal — countries the Reich did not rule and, in some cases, was at war with — each with its number, each counted in advance for a killing that its authors had no means of carrying out. The table is not a plan. It is an ambition, expressed as arithmetic.

And then there is Estonia. Beside Estonia, there is no number at all. There is a word: judenfrei. Free of Jews. The Jews of Estonia had already been murdered, in the months before the meeting, and so they appeared on the table as a zero. Of all the entries in all the ledgers in this series, I do not think there is one darker than that. An entire community of human beings, entered as an absence, and the absence offered to the meeting as an accomplishment.

The memorial that now stands in the villa notes, of this table, that it claimed to be precise but was by no means accurate. Of course it was not. Accuracy was never the point of it. The point was the form — the column, the total, the country-by-country tidiness that turned a people into an inventory and an inventory into a task. This series has watched the British count their own dead by name and the Indian dead by range, at essay 1,857. Here the count runs the other way: the victims counted with obscene exactness before they died, by the men who meant to kill them.

The fraction that decided

How do you count a people you have decided to destroy? First you must decide who belongs to it, and the Nazi state did that, too, with arithmetic.

The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 defined a Jew not by belief or practice but by grandparents. Three or four Jewish grandparents made a person a Jew. Two made them a “Mischling of the first degree.” One made them a “Mischling of the second degree.” A human being’s fate was computed as a fraction of their ancestry, and the fraction determined their rights, their marriage, their employment, and in the end, very often, their life.

I have to set this beside two earlier essays, because the series demands it. At essay 1,865 I wrote about the three-fifths clause — the fraction that counted an enslaved American as part of a person. And at essay 1,898, only a few weeks ago in this count, I worked out my own kinship to a man I revered as a fraction of descent, and then rounded it up, out of love, into a word I could say. In 1935 a government performed exactly the same calculation, on exactly the same kind of family tree, and rounded it the other way. The arithmetic of ancestry is neutral. It is a pencil and a chart. In one hand it makes a great-uncle. In another it makes a deportation list.

The German censuses of 1933 and 1939 gathered the information that made such lists possible, and the 1939 census asked, on a supplementary card, whether each of a household’s grandparents had been Jewish. The results were tabulated, as census returns had been since the 1890s, on punch-card machines — supplied in Germany by a company called Dehomag, the German subsidiary of IBM. How much the American parent knew, and when, remains a matter of real dispute among historians, and I will not settle it here with a sentence. I note only the lineage this series has been tracing. At essay 1,936 Alan Turing imagined a clerk with a tape, and his machines would help save a great many lives at Bletchley Park. The same family of counting machines, in other hands, sorted human beings for murder. Every tool in these files is exactly as humane as the hand that picks it up. I have written that sentence before. I have never written it about anything like this.

The number on the arm

At Auschwitz, prisoners who were registered for labour had a number tattooed on their forearm — the only Nazi camp to do so systematically. A person became a serial number, inked into their own skin, and was addressed by it. The image has become, rightly, one of the emblems of the century: the human being reduced to an entry.

But there is a fact about those numbers that I did not understand until I read for this essay, and it has stayed with me. The tattoo was given to those selected to work. The great majority of the Jews deported to Auschwitz were sent directly from the railway platform to the gas chambers, and they were never registered at all. They were not given a number. They were not entered in any camp ledger. The numbered prisoners were, terribly, the ones the system had decided to keep for a while; the uncounted were the ones it had decided to kill at once. Even the most notorious act of enumeration in human history left most of its victims out of the count.

None is too many

And now the honest ledger at home, because this series has never permitted me to audit another country’s books without opening my own.

Between 1933 and 1945, as the Jews of Europe tried desperately to find a country that would let them in, Canada admitted about five thousand. It is one of the worst records of any democracy in the world. In June 1939 the liner St. Louis, carrying more than nine hundred Jewish refugees from Germany, was turned away from Cuba, then from the United States, and then from Canada, and sailed back to Europe; by the count of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, two hundred and fifty-four of its passengers were later murdered. And in 1945, when the war was over and the camps were open and the world could no longer pretend not to know, a senior Canadian official — never named — was asked how many Jews Canada should admit after the war. His answer was three words: None is too many.

Two Canadian historians, Irving Abella and Harold Troper, took that sentence as the title of their history of my country’s refugee policy in those years, and it has stood ever since as the most damning number a Canadian civil servant ever uttered. Not a figure — the refusal of one. At essay 1,842 I wrote about the good books a nation shows its visitors and the ledger it keeps in the back room. This is ours. The count of the people we did not let in is not on any table at Wannsee. It is on a page in Ottawa, and it reads zero by choice.

Six million, and five million names

The figure the world remembers is six million — the number of Jews murdered in the Holocaust. It is worth understanding how that number was reached, because it tells you what kind of count it is. The killers destroyed much of their own record, and most of the dead were never registered anywhere. The six million was arrived at largely by demography: by comparing the Jewish populations of Europe before the war with those that remained after it, and counting the absence. It is a count of the missing. It is what was left when the people were gone.

And so, for more than seventy years, the work of remembrance has been, in large part, the work of counting backward — of taking the number and turning it, one entry at a time, back into names. At Yad Vashem, in Jerusalem, archivists and families have spent decades gathering pages of testimony, deportation lists, community records, the recollections of survivors: who was this person, where did they live, whom did they love, when were they last seen. In November of last year, Yad Vashem announced that it had identified five million of the victims by name. Perhaps a million more may never be found.

There is a poem, read aloud every year in Israel on the day of remembrance, that begins: Every person has a name. It is the answer to page six. Eichmann’s table took people and made them figures; the reading of the names takes the figures and makes them people again. This is the only number in the whole of this series where the honest count is the memorial — where the act of counting correctly, name by name, is itself a form of justice. At essay 1,857 I wrote that one side’s dead were a number and the other side’s dead were a range. Here, against everything the men at the lake intended, the range is becoming names.

The closer

So 1942 is the year the count reached its floor. Fifteen men at a lakeside villa, a table on page six, a fraction of grandparents, a number on an arm, a zero beside Estonia, and three words from Ottawa. Every instrument this series has followed for nineteen hundred essays — the census, the ledger, the card, the machine, the careful official with his figures — was present at Wannsee, and every one of them was used for the worst thing human beings have ever done to one another. I do not know a lesson that is equal to it. I know only the practice: to count honestly, to count the ones they tried to leave out, and to keep turning the numbers back into names for as long as there is anyone left to read them.

The count moves on, and it moves toward the end of the war, and toward the invoice I told you about at essay 1,905 — the exchange rate between a gram and a city, posted by a clerk forty years earlier and about to be cashed. The next landmark is essay 1,945. I will write it the way this series writes the gravest entries, and not tired, and I will begin it with a sentence this series wrote for it a long time ago.

See you at 1,945.
