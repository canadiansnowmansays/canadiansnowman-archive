Five months ago, this dispatch noted that Donald Trump has never learned to hear the word no. Judges kept saying it. He kept not listening. It was, we said at the time, a two-letter word that has defeated him in business, marriage, and contract law for the better part of eighty years.

Today the Supreme Court solved the problem for him in the most Washington way imaginable: it found a word that isn’t yes, isn’t no, and gets him exactly what he wanted anyway. That word is standing.

Nobody Is Hurt Enough to Complain

The National Trust for Historic Preservation sued to stop the ballroom, arguing — reasonably, one might think — that a president cannot demolish a wing of the White House and put up a 90,000-square-foot party palace without so much as asking Congress. Their evidence that this was a bad idea included, among other things, a federal judge, an appeals court, and the plain text of several statutes.

Their evidence that they had a right to sue about it included a board member named Alison Hoagland, who likes to walk past the White House and would be sad to see it turned into Mar-a-Lago North. She described this as “aesthetic, cultural and historical” injury.

The Supreme Court’s majority considered this claim with the seriousness of a bouncer checking IDs at a club nobody wants to get into. Sorry, ma’am — feeling wistful about a demolished national landmark is not, technically, an injury the federal courts recognize. Try feeling something else.

Five justices agreed. The Trust, they ruled, “likely lacks standing.” Whether the ballroom itself is legal, the Court declined to say, in the same way a man declines to comment on his neighbour’s fence after helping him build it at midnight.

The Chief Justice Says the Quiet Part Loudly

Here is the detail that should have broken the internet and instead broke nothing, because we are all now the emotional equivalent of shatterproof glass: Chief Justice John Roberts dissented. He was joined by all three liberal justices, which made for the rare spectacle of the Chief Justice of the United States teaming up with Sonia Sotomayor to lose.

Roberts did not hedge. “That construction is likely unlawful,” he wrote, of a $400 million ballroom currently 65 percent complete and rising at the pace of a man who suspects, correctly, that the lawsuit will not catch him before the ribbon-cutting.

Let this sink in for a moment: the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States looked at the same facts as his five colleagues and concluded the President of the United States broke the law. And then everyone went home, and the concrete kept pouring. This is what “the rule of law” looks like when the law loses a vote 5–4 and nobody is legally required to care.

A Brief Statistical Interlude

For those keeping score — and we are, this being the Trump Accountability Project — the ballroom’s price tag has now travelled from $200 million to $400 million to a congressional ask of $1 billion, which is either a remarkable feat of inflation or the world’s most expensive game of Would You Rather.

Construction proceeds at 250 workers, two shifts, twenty hours a day, seven days a week — a pace normally associated with disaster relief, not interior design. Solicitor General D. John Sauer warned the Court that letting the Trust sue would “open the courthouse doors to any of the millions of people who visit government buildings, historic sites, or parks and happen to think that they dislike something they see,” a sentence that manages to describe both a legal slippery slope and the entire concept of democracy in a single breath.

The White House maintains the ballroom is funded entirely by private donors, a claim received by the American public with the same credulity generally reserved for timeshare pitches. Among the donors: several companies that happen to have billions of dollars in pending federal business, which is either a remarkable coincidence or the most transparent gift registry in presidential history.

The Man Who Never Needed to Hear Yes

What has actually been established since March is not that Donald Trump learned the word no. It’s that he discovered he never needed anyone to say yes, either. All he needed was a court patient enough, and technical enough, to spend eight pages explaining who isn’t allowed to ask the question, while the crew outside kept pouring concrete on the answer.

The East Wing did not survive the argument about whether it should exist. It simply outlasted the argument’s ability to conclude before the drywall went up. Ninety thousand square feet, forty-foot ceilings, room for a thousand guests — 999, technically, on account of the fire code, because even a ballroom built on a legal technicality needs one of its own — who will one day stand under the chandeliers and never wonder how the wing that used to house the First Lady’s staff simply stopped being there.

Nobody said yes. Nobody said no. The concrete does not require either.

The Trump Accountability Project documents the long arc of accountability for a man who has spent 80 years evading it. This is part of that record.
