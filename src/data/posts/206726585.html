Every monarchy examined so far in this series has been a single throne, rising or falling on its own particular story. India in the first half of the twentieth century presents this series with something entirely different in scale: not one kingdom’s collapse but the simultaneous dissolution of roughly five hundred and sixty-five of them, ruled by everyone from the reputed wealthiest man on earth to minor chieftains whose entire domain covered a few dozen square miles, absorbed into two newly independent republics within a matter of months in 1947 and 1948, mostly without the kind of prolonged violence this series has documented in nearly every other collapse it has examined. Understanding how and why this happened means first understanding a peculiar colonial arrangement that had nothing quite like it anywhere else in this series: an empire that ruled a third of its subcontinent not directly, but through hundreds of nominally sovereign kings kept in place specifically because doing so was cheaper and more stable than replacing them.

The British Raj’s relationship with India’s princely states rested on a legal fiction the British themselves had carefully constructed and just as carefully maintained for their own administrative convenience. Following the 1857 rebellion that this series examined at the close of Part IX — the uprising that ended the last remnants of Mughal authority and led the British Crown to take direct control of India away from the East India Company — British administrators drew a deliberate lesson from the experience: that ruling India entirely through direct annexation risked provoking exactly the kind of unified resistance the rebellion had briefly threatened, and that leaving a substantial portion of the subcontinent under the nominal rule of established local monarchies, bound to the British Crown through treaties of subordinate alliance rather than outright conquest, would prove both cheaper to administer and less likely to unite disparate populations against a single common enemy. The resulting system, formalized under what came to be called the doctrine of paramountcy, left princely rulers with genuine internal authority over their own territories — their own courts, their own tax systems, in some cases their own armies and even their own currencies — while reserving to the British Crown ultimate control over defense, foreign affairs, and communications, and, crucially, the power to depose any prince the British judged unfit to rule.

The scale and diversity of what this arrangement actually produced is genuinely difficult to convey in summary, and it’s worth dwelling on for a moment because no other case examined anywhere in this series involves anything like this degree of simultaneous variation. At one extreme stood Hyderabad, whose ruler, the Nizam, governed a population of some sixteen million people across a territory roughly the size of England, controlled his own currency, his own postal service, his own airline, and was reputed by contemporary accounts, including a 1937 Time magazine cover story, to be the wealthiest individual in the world, his personal treasury said to include diamonds the size of hen’s eggs kept, according to widely repeated legend, wrapped carelessly in old newspaper. At the other extreme stood states so small that their rulers governed little more than a fortified village and its surrounding farmland, holding princely titles and treaty relationships with the British Crown that were, in every practical sense, identical in form to Hyderabad’s own arrangement despite the almost comic disparity in scale. The British formalized this vast hierarchy of unequal monarchs through an oddly precise ceremonial device: the gun salute. Every princely ruler of any real standing was assigned a fixed number of cannon shots to be fired in his honor on state occasions, ranging as high as twenty-one guns for the very grandest rulers, including Hyderabad, down through nineteen, seventeen, fifteen, eleven, and nine guns for progressively less significant states, with hundreds of the smallest principalities entitled to no salute at all. The number was not a matter of local custom; it was formally assigned and periodically reviewed by British colonial authorities themselves, who used it as a precise instrument of imperial diplomacy, raising or lowering a prince’s salute to reward loyalty or punish friction with British administrators — a system that turned something as seemingly ceremonial as cannon fire into a running, numerically explicit scorecard of exactly how much sovereignty London was prepared to pretend each king actually possessed.

Between these extremes sat states as genuinely consequential as Kashmir, ruled by a Hindu maharaja over a majority-Muslim population in a combination that would prove catastrophically significant within a few short years, and Rajput states whose ruling houses, as this series noted in Part IX, had negotiated their own accommodation with Mughal power centuries earlier and simply transferred that same negotiated autonomy into a new treaty relationship with the British Crown once Mughal authority had faded.

This entire elaborate structure faced an existential deadline the moment Britain, exhausted by the Second World War and facing an increasingly unmanageable independence movement led by the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League, agreed to grant India independence by June of 1948 — a date that was, in the event, moved forward with startling speed to August of 1947 once Lord Louis Mountbatten, appointed as the last Viceroy specifically to oversee the transition, concluded that a faster timeline would reduce the risk of escalating communal violence rather than allow it to fester through a longer transition period. The Indian Independence Act, passed by the British Parliament in July of 1947, formally ended paramountcy itself as a legal doctrine, which created an immediate and genuinely alarming vacuum: every one of the roughly five hundred and sixty-five princely states was, as a matter of strict legal technicality, suddenly free to choose independence, accession to the new Dominion of India, or accession to the new Dominion of Pakistan, with no legal mechanism compelling any of them toward any particular choice.

The man most responsible for preventing this vacuum from fracturing the subcontinent into hundreds of competing micro-states was Vallabhbhai Patel, the Indian National Congress leader who became independent India’s first Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of States, working in close and highly effective partnership with a career civil servant named V. P. Menon. Patel and Menon’s approach combined patient persuasion with unmistakable underlying pressure: they offered princely rulers a document called the Instrument of Accession, a deliberately minimal legal agreement transferring only defense, foreign affairs, and communications to the new Indian government — precisely the same three subjects the British Crown had reserved under paramountcy — while leaving princes considerable internal autonomy and, crucially, a continued personal privy purse, a guaranteed annual payment from the state in exchange for their cooperation and the formal surrender of sovereign authority. This was, in effect, a negotiated buyout on a scale this series has not encountered anywhere else: hundreds of monarchs persuaded to give up their thrones’ actual sovereignty in exchange for money, ceremonial status, and the practical recognition that resistance against a newly independent nation of hundreds of millions of people, no longer restrained by any British-imposed limit on its own military action, was unlikely to end well for any individual prince who tried it.

Mountbatten himself understood that Patel and Menon’s careful legal drafting would only work if the princes could be brought to the table quickly and in a mood to cooperate rather than resist, and he lent his own considerable personal prestige to the effort in a manner none of the actual legal machinery could have achieved alone. In a pointed address to the Chamber of Princes — the consultative body representing the states’ rulers — in July of 1947, Mountbatten warned bluntly that paramountcy’s disappearance meant the princes would be entirely on their own the moment independence arrived, with no British protection to fall back on if their populations or neighboring states chose not to respect an independent status, and strongly encouraged accession on the generous terms Patel and Menon had already drawn up as the only realistic path to a secure and dignified future. Coming from the Crown’s own outgoing representative, someone many princes had trusted as an intermediary for exactly this kind of decision throughout the British era, the message landed with considerably more force than a similar argument from Congress politicians alone might have carried, and the overwhelming majority of princely rulers signed the Instrument of Accession within weeks, recognizing quickly that Patel’s offer, generous by the standards of what most conquered or absorbed monarchies examined elsewhere in this series ever received, was likely the best available outcome. Three holdouts, however, produced genuine crises that shaped the subcontinent’s subsequent history in ways still visible today. Junagadh, a small state on India’s western coast with an overwhelmingly Hindu population but a Muslim ruler who attempted to accede to Pakistan despite his state’s total geographic separation from Pakistani territory, saw its own population and administration effectively revolt against the ruler’s decision, and Indian troops moved in to secure accession after a hastily arranged plebiscite confirmed overwhelming local support for joining India instead. Hyderabad, the wealthiest and most consequential holdout, saw its Nizam attempt to negotiate an independent status entirely separate from both new dominions, a position India found unacceptable given Hyderabad’s location entirely within Indian territory and its substantial Hindu majority population governed by a Muslim ruling house. The Nizam’s government, during the year-long standoff that followed, increasingly relied on an irregular Muslim militia known as the Razakars to suppress internal dissent and project a show of independent strength, and reports of escalating violence against Hyderabad’s Hindu population became a central part of India’s public justification for intervention. When negotiations stalled, India launched a five-day military action in September of 1948, given the deliberately understated codename Operation Polo, that overwhelmed the Nizam’s modest defense forces within days, ended Hyderabad’s independent status by force, and folded the Nizam’s vast domain into the Indian union, allowing the Nizam himself to retain his personal wealth, his title, and a ceremonial role as the state’s governor for several more years despite having just lost, by force of arms, the sovereignty he had spent a year trying to preserve.

Kashmir proved, and remains to this day, the case this series’ framework of negotiated accommodation could least successfully resolve. Its Hindu ruler, Maharaja Hari Singh, initially attempted to remain independent of both new dominions entirely, a position that collapsed within weeks of independence when Pakistan-backed tribal militias invaded the state in October of 1947, prompting Hari Singh to sign the Instrument of Accession to India in exchange for military assistance against the invasion — a decision whose legitimacy Pakistan has disputed ever since, given the state’s Muslim-majority population, and which triggered the first of what would become several wars between India and Pakistan over the territory’s disputed status, a conflict that drew in the newly formed United Nations within months, produced a ceasefire line in 1949 that still functions, in modified form, as a de facto border between Indian- and Pakistani-administered territory today, and set the stage for further wars between the two countries in 1965 and 1971 that this series notes here without attempting to resolve, since Kashmir’s dispute remains, as of this writing, one of the world’s longest-running unsettled territorial conflicts, its roots running directly back to the specific and unrepeatable circumstances of a single monarch’s choice in October of 1947.

Even after the immediate crisis of accession had been resolved, the princely rulers who had signed away their sovereignty did not immediately lose everything the arrangement had promised them. India’s constitution, adopted in 1950, formally guaranteed the continuation of privy purses and certain ceremonial privileges to former princely rulers as a matter of ongoing constitutional right, a compromise that preserved a genuinely strange residual layer of quasi-royal privilege inside an otherwise thoroughly republican constitutional order for over two decades. This arrangement finally ended in 1971, when Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, facing an election in which she had campaigned explicitly against what she characterized as unearned aristocratic privilege surviving inside a modern socialist republic, pushed through a constitutional amendment abolishing privy purses and all remaining princely titles and privileges entirely. Her government’s first attempt to do this by executive order in 1970 had already been struck down by the Supreme Court as exceeding the President’s constitutional authority, forcing Gandhi to pursue the considerably more decisive route of a formal constitutional amendment instead — itself a small demonstration of how much more procedurally durable India’s new republican institutions had already become, within barely two decades of independence, than the princely thrones they were now dismantling. Former rulers argued, unsuccessfully, that the original 1947 accession agreements had constituted a binding and permanent contract the state could not unilaterally revoke, a position some of them carried into new careers in India’s own democratic politics, several former princes and maharanis winning parliamentary seats in the very system that had just formally stripped away their hereditary titles. The Supreme Court of India ultimately upheld Gandhi’s amendment, and with that ruling, the last formal legal vestige of India’s five hundred and sixty-five princely thrones was extinguished, nearly a quarter century after the thrones themselves had already surrendered their actual sovereign authority.

What this series draws from India’s particular version of monarchical collapse is a genuinely distinct lesson from anything examined in the preceding eight parts: that the end of monarchy need not arrive through regicide, revolution, or foreign conquest at all, but could instead be negotiated, state by state, through a combination of patient diplomacy, targeted financial incentive, and the unmistakable underlying reality that resistance against overwhelming force was pointless — a process closer to a corporate buyout than to any of the more dramatic collapses this series has traced so far. It is also a reminder of just how recently, and how casually by comparison with the ancient thrones examined earlier in this series, monarchy as an institution could simply be legislated out of existence by a determined and popular government, once the underlying military and political leverage genuinely had shifted away from the crowns themselves.

Part XI leaves the subcontinent for the Horn of Africa, and a single dynasty whose own founding legend claims a bloodline running not merely back centuries, as the Habsburgs or Mughals could claim, but directly back to King Solomon himself — a claim of continuity so audacious that this series has, without quite planning it, now arrived at the exact biblical figure Part II examined nearly a dozen essays ago, discovering that his legend traveled considerably further than Jerusalem.
