On the afternoon of 20 January 2025, a few hours after taking the oath for the second time, Donald Trump sat at the Resolute desk in front of the cameras and signed executive orders by the stack. Some were proclamations, some were memos, and some revoked orders his predecessor had signed on his own first afternoon four years earlier. The pen was handed to aides and the next folder was opened. It was government as theatre, and it was also government as it actually works at the top. A great deal of what an American president does is done with a signature, and whatever a signature does, another signature can undo.

That is the first tier of the Reversal Ledger. The ledger now holds 96 reversals, eight more than when the introduction to this series was written, and 34 of them were made by executive order, presidential memo, proclamation, withdrawal notice or an appointee’s administrative decision. A future president could reverse every one of them without asking Congress, without a year of rulemaking, and in most cases without waiting for a court. This is where the pendulum lives. It is also where the comforting version of American politics is most nearly true.

It is worth seeing exactly how true, and where it stops.

What the pen did

The first-tier reversals fall into a few families.

Identity and rights. Executive Order 14168, signed on inauguration day, directs every federal agency to recognise two sexes and to strip gender-identity questions from federal forms and surveys. It replaced a Biden order applying the Supreme Court’s 2020 Bostock ruling across the government. Under it, passports now show M or F by sex at birth, and the “X” marker the State Department introduced in 2021 and 2022 is gone; the Supreme Court let that policy take effect in November 2025 while the case goes on. Executive Order 14183 reimposed the ban on transgender people serving in the military. Executive Order 14151 closed federal diversity offices and cancelled equity plans across the government. Executive Order 14224 made English the official language and revoked a Clinton order, kept by Bush, Obama and Biden, that required agencies to serve people with limited English. The “Press 3” option on the 988 crisis line, which connected young LGBTQ callers to trained counsellors, ended on 17 July 2025.

The world. The second withdrawal from the Paris climate agreement took effect on 27 January 2026. The United States completed its withdrawal from the World Health Organization, which it helped found in 1948, on 22 January 2026. It left the UN Human Rights Council again by Executive Order 14199, and its departure from UNESCO takes effect on the last day of this year. On 28 September 2026 it gave notice that it will also leave GRECO, the Council of Europe’s anti-corruption monitoring body, which it joined under Clinton in 2000 and stayed in under Bush, Obama and Trump’s first term; only Russia and Belarus have left before. The Mexico City Policy, which bars American foreign aid to organisations that provide or promote abortion, was restored four days after the inauguration.

Borders. The refugee ceiling for 2026 was set at 7,500 and later raised to 17,500, with the extra 10,000 places reserved for white South Africans; on 25 September 2026 the ceiling for 2027 was set at 17,500 again, mainly for the same group. Temporary Protected Status has been terminated for Haitians, Syrians and Venezuelans, and on 25 June 2026, in Mullin v. Doe, the Supreme Court held that the courts have almost no power to review terminations of this kind. Humanitarian parole for Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans and Venezuelans was ended by a notice in March 2025. The guidance that kept immigration agents away from schools, churches and hospitals was rescinded on the first day, and survives now only for the 1,400-odd congregations that sued.

The machinery of government. Executive Order 14251 removed most federal workers from collective bargaining, a right President Kennedy granted by executive order in 1962. The commissioner of the Bureau of Labor Statistics was fired on 1 August 2025, the day a weak jobs report came out. The chairman of the Joint Chiefs, the chief of naval operations and the commandant of the Coast Guard were removed within the administration’s first month, along with the senior military lawyers of the Army and Air Force. Executive Order 14209 paused enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act for six months. The minimum wage for workers on federal contracts, set by Obama and raised by Biden, was rescinded.

Land and energy. By proclamation on 13 July 2026, Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante national monuments were each cut by about 90 per cent. The freeze on new federal coal leasing was lifted, and in June 2026 the Energy Department offered up to $850 million in support for coal, including two new plants, the first new coal plants in the United States since 2013. Under a rarely used emergency power, nine aging coal units at six plants have been ordered to keep running past their retirement dates.

Farms and schools. The Agriculture Department cancelled the $3.1 billion Partnerships for Climate-Smart Commodities, about 135 projects paying farmers to test lower-emission methods, and about $1 billion in 2025 funding for schools and food banks to buy from local farms.

Names. Denali is Mount McKinley again in federal usage. The Gulf of Mexico is the Gulf of America in the federal place-name database. All nine army bases renamed in 2023 have their old names back. The USNS Harvey Milk is now the USNS Oscar V. Peterson. The Department of Defense calls itself the Department of War. The Smithsonian is under review, and its secretary announced his resignation in September.

That is a long list, and every item on it rests on a signature.

The easy half

Some of these could be reversed so completely that a citizen would barely notice they had ever happened.

The Mexico City Policy is the clearest example. Since Ronald Reagan created it in 1984, it has been rescinded by every Democratic president and restored by every Republican one, a total of six reversals, each by memo, each within days of an inauguration. Nobody in Washington pretends this is anything other than a flag that changes with the party in power. The federal contractor minimum wage, the official-language order, the diversity offices and the language-access requirement are similar. A new order revokes the old one, the agencies are told to resume, and the regulations that carried them out are, in most cases, still on the books or easily reissued.

UNESCO offers a second calibration case. Reagan took the United States out in 1984; George W. Bush brought it back in 2003; Trump took it out again at the end of 2018; Biden rejoined in 2023; and the second departure takes effect on 31 December 2026. Five moves in forty-two years, each made by one president’s decision and each undone by another’s. A policy that changes with every administration is not being decided. It is being weathered.

Some of the other international withdrawals are nearly as simple. Rejoining the Paris Agreement takes thirty days from the date a president notifies the United Nations. Biden did it on his first day in 2021. Rejoining the Human Rights Council is a matter of standing for election. Even the names can be changed back. Barack Obama renamed Mount McKinley in 2015 at Alaska’s request, and a future president could do it again the same way.

Count the items that a new president could restore by signature with little lasting loss, and the number is respectable: perhaps a third of the first tier. On these, the pendulum really is a pendulum.

The hard half

Now look at the right-hand column of the ledger, the one headed “What can’t be put back.” For most of the first tier, it is not empty.

A pen can restore open military service to transgender Americans. It cannot restore the careers of the service members discharged in the meantime, or recruit the people who chose not to enlist. Since 2016 the policy has changed four times. Imagine a young officer who came out under Obama, watched the door close under Trump, saw it reopen under Biden and has now been pushed out again. She has learned something no new order will unteach: that her career depends on an election.

A pen can tell the State Department to issue “X” passports again. The documents reissued in the interval have already been printed, and the people whose papers no longer match their appearance have already travelled with them. A pen can restore gender-identity questions to federal surveys. It cannot fill the gap in the data series left by the years when the question was not asked. Statisticians have a phrase for this: a break in the series. It is permanent.

A pen can raise the refugee ceiling back to 125,000. It cannot reopen, overnight, the resettlement offices that closed when there were no refugees to resettle, or recall the caseworkers who found other jobs. A pen can redesignate Haiti for Temporary Protected Status. The families deported in the meantime cannot simply return.

A pen can restore the moratorium on coal leasing, and the next president may well do so. A coal plant built with federal support is designed to run for forty years. A pen can restore Bears Ears to its full size, as Biden did in 2021 after the first cut. Mining claims staked while the land was unprotected can survive the restoration, because a valid existing claim is a property right. A pen can rescind the emergency orders keeping coal plants open, but the carbon those plants emitted is in the atmosphere, and ratepayers have already paid hundreds of millions of dollars for it.

A pen can rejoin the World Health Organization. It cannot give back the time the United States spent outside the organisation’s disease surveillance and outbreak response, or guarantee its old seat at the table when it returns. A pen can rejoin Paris in thirty days, and that is precisely the problem. Allies who watched the United States join in 2016, leave in 2020, rejoin in 2021 and leave again in 2026 now assume, reasonably, that any American climate pledge lasts exactly as long as the president who made it.

And a pen can appoint a new commissioner of labour statistics. It cannot restore the assumption, held by markets and voters alike for 140 years, that the person publishing the monthly jobs number could not be fired for publishing a bad one.

The pen cuts both ways

There is a deeper problem with government by signature, and it applies to the restorers as much as to the people they would be replacing.

Everything a pen can do, a pen can undo. That is why the first tier is easy to reverse, and it is also why reversing it settles nothing. If a Democratic president restores open service, the “X” marker and the refugee programme by executive order in January 2029, a Republican president can remove them again by executive order in January 2033. The soldier, the passport holder and the refugee caseworker will have been through the same cycle twice. The Mexico City Policy is not an achievement of either party. It is a warning about what happens to any policy that lives only on paper signed by one person: it becomes a weather vane.

The administrations of the last two decades have relied on the pen more than any before them, largely because Congress has done so little. Obama’s legacy on immigration and climate was built substantially on executive action after Congress declined to legislate, and much of it was gone within a year of his leaving office. Biden’s was built the same way and lasted about as long. Trump’s second-term orders will meet the same fate if the next president chooses to reverse them. A policy made by signature is borrowed, not owned.

That is the lesson the first tier offers anyone planning a restoration, and later parts of this series will return to it. The pen is the right tool for the first afternoon. It is the wrong tool for anything meant to last.

The first swing

In Poe’s story, the prisoner’s first reaction to the pendulum is disbelief at how slowly it moves. He counts the swings. He has time. The first tier of the ledger is the part of the blade that is still high enough to see clearly and to measure, and most of it could be lifted by a single hand.

But even here, at the top of the arc, the blade has already cut something. Careers, data series, resettlement offices, families, mining claims, a coal plant’s forty-year life, an ally’s trust. None of those are on the pen’s side of the ledger. The next part of this series goes lower, into the reversals that no single signature can undo: the rules that took years to write and will take years to write again.
