There is a certain kind of truth that arrives not from scientists or activists, who can be dismissed as alarmists, nor from politicians, who can be dismissed as anything you like, but from the most coldly unsentimental institution in modern life: the insurance industry. Insurers do not march. They do not chant. They do not care, in any moral sense, whether the planet warms — they care only whether they can price the risk and still turn a profit. And across great stretches of North America, they have begun, quietly and without ideology, to conclude that they cannot. When the actuaries surrender, you should pay closer attention than when the protesters shout, because the actuaries have money riding on being right.

The numbers tell a story that no amount of political obfuscation can soften. A 2024 report from the United States Senate Budget Committee, drawing on 249 million policies, found that more than 1.9 million home insurance contracts had been dropped — non-renewed — since 2018, with climate change identified as the primary driver. Average home insurance premiums in America have risen more than thirty per cent since 2020, and far more than that in the places where the weather has turned dangerous. Insured losses from natural catastrophes in the United States averaged around a hundred billion dollars a year between 2023 and 2025, up from roughly fifteen billion a year a decade earlier. That is not a gradual trend. That is a near-sevenfold increase in a decade, and it is the sound of a business model breaking.

The two great laboratories of this collapse are California and Florida, and they are instructive precisely because they are political opposites that have arrived at the same cliff. In California, after the wildfires of recent years — culminating in the devastating Los Angeles fires of early 2025 — the state’s insurer of last resort, the FAIR Plan, has more than tripled, from 140,000 policies in 2018 to over 610,000 by mid-2025. State Farm, the largest home insurer in the state, stopped writing new policies, then secured emergency rate increases of seventeen per cent and more after the LA fires, and has signaled it intends to shed roughly a million policies. “The insurance crisis in the U.S. is the canary in the coal mine,” the former California insurance commissioner Dave Jones has said, “and the canary is dead.” In Florida, the mechanism differs — hurricanes rather than fire, a litigation environment rather than a regulatory one — but the destination is identical: insurers gone insolvent, homeowners dumped into a strained state-run insurer of last resort, premiums that consume a swelling share of every mortgage payment.

I want to dwell on why this is the most clarifying chapter in the whole climate story, because it cuts through something that politics is designed to obscure. A government can withdraw from the Paris Agreement and call it strength. A president can declare that coal is beautiful and clean. A prime minister can kill a carbon tax and call it relief. Language is infinitely flexible, and political language most of all. But an insurance company cannot lie to itself about risk, because if it does, it goes bankrupt, and bankruptcy is the one argument that admits no rhetoric. When State Farm declines to insure a house, it is not making a statement about climate change. It is making a bet with its own money about the future of that specific patch of ground, and it has decided the bet is bad. The actuaries have read the same science as everyone else, and unlike everyone else, they have to put a price on believing it.

And the price they have arrived at amounts to a verdict on the central conceit of this series. Recall the image we began with: the house built for winter, the infrastructure calibrated to a climate that has quietly departed. The insurance industry is the institution whose entire job is to know the difference between the climate a house was built for and the climate it now sits in — and it is the institution now, in effect, refusing to pretend that difference does not exist. Every non-renewal letter is a small, unsentimental confession that the old assumptions no longer hold. The homeowner built, bought, and borrowed on the premise that the past predicts the future. The insurer, doing the cold arithmetic, has concluded that it does not, and has walked away.

The consequences of this run far deeper than premiums, and this is the part that should worry even readers who live nowhere near a fire zone or a coast. Insurance is not a luxury bolted onto homeownership; it is load-bearing. You cannot get a mortgage without it. So when insurers withdraw from a region, they do not merely make life there more expensive — they make it, eventually, impossible to finance, and a place you cannot finance is a place whose property values begin to fall, whose tax base begins to erode, whose ability to fund the very schools and fire departments and sea walls it needs begins to fail. The chairman of the U.S. Federal Reserve, Jerome Powell — not a man given to climate rhetoric — has warned that in ten or fifteen years there will be regions of the country where you simply cannot get a mortgage, where the banks pull out, where the ATMs vanish, because the insurance that underpins everything is no longer available at any price.

Follow that chain one link further, because it leads somewhere that ought to alarm anyone who remembers 2008. A board member of Allianz, one of the largest insurers on Earth, laid out the sequence with a bluntness rare in his industry: a house that cannot be insured cannot be mortgaged; no bank will lend against uninsurable property; and so, region by region, credit markets freeze. In the United States, something like thirteen trillion dollars of outstanding mortgage debt rests, in part, on the assumption that the underlying homes can be insured. Homeowner’s insurance has risen from seven or eight per cent of a typical mortgage cost a decade ago to around twenty per cent today, and as it climbs, households buckle: the Federal Reserve’s own researchers have found that in places where climate-driven insurance costs are spiking, mortgage delinquencies and defaults rise in step. Where coverage does not vanish outright it quietly hollows out — the industry’s version of the supermarket trick the regulators have taken to calling “shrinkflation,” the same premium buying an ever-thinner slice of protection, more perils excluded, more risk shoved back onto the homeowner who often does not discover the gap until the house is already under water or ash.

Here is the distinction that makes this worse than a repeat of the last crisis, and it is the reason I keep returning to the image of the house built for winter. A United States senator has called the looming result “2008 all over again” — but that comparison, if anything, understates it. In 2008, house prices collapsed because of a financial bubble, and once the bubble cleared, the physical houses were still sound, still desirable, still where people wanted to live, and their values eventually recovered. The homes made worthless by climate risk will not recover, because nothing is wrong with the market; something is wrong with the place. The fire zone is still a fire zone. The floodplain still floods. A house that is uninsurable because it sits where the new climate has turned hostile does not bounce back when the market calms, because the market was right. This is not a bubble that will reinflate. It is a permanent revaluation of where it is safe to live, arriving through the unsentimental machinery of the insurance filing, and the wealth it erases — for many families their only real wealth, the equity in a home — is erased for good.

This is the quiet machinery by which a warming climate becomes an economic event rather than an environmental one, and it is happening now, in the present tense, in the policy filings and the non-renewal notices, well ahead of the dramatic future the doom-mongers like to invoke. The market is doing the math that the politics refuses to do out loud. And the market’s answer, delivered not in a manifesto but in a declined application, is that a great many of the houses we have built are no longer worth the risk of insuring — which is to say, no longer fully worth living in, financing, or counting on. The actuaries have surrendered. They were the last people in the room still paid to tell the truth about risk, and they have stopped pretending.

And before any Canadian reader files this under American problems, let me bring it home, because this is the essay where the comfortable distance collapses. We are not spectators to the insurance retreat; we are living through our own version of it, just a few years behind. The year 2024 was the costliest in Canadian history for weather-related insured losses — a record $9.4 billion, roughly twelve times the annual average of the previous decade. The Jasper wildfire alone destroyed much of that beloved mountain town and cost $1.1 billion; the summer floods in the Greater Toronto Area cost nearly a billion more; Alberta absorbed over four billion in damages in a single year. Two decades ago, Canadian insurers rarely paid out more than half a billion dollars a year for catastrophes; now a billion-dollar year is simply the norm, and the Insurance Bureau of Canada projects that annual insured losses could reach $100 billion by 2050.

And the Canadian industry is responding exactly as the American one did, by the same cold logic. Home insurance premiums rose 31 per cent nationally between 2021 and 2025 — against 15 per cent general inflation — and far more in the hard-hit provinces, with five-year increases estimated at 68 per cent in British Columbia and 58 per cent in Alberta. Insurers are quietly retreating: Aviva Canada announced it would phase out its direct-to-consumer home insurance in Alberta; Desjardins said it would not provide new mortgages for Quebec properties with more than a five per cent chance of flooding. Read that last one twice, because it is the Powell warning arriving on Canadian soil ahead of schedule — a major institution declining to finance homes because of where they sit on a flood map. The deductibles climb, the perils get excluded, the coverage thins, and in the highest-risk zones it begins, quietly, to disappear.

I told you, several essays ago, about a homeowner in Iqaluit whose house was cracking as the permafrost beneath it thawed, and who noted almost in passing that his insurance would not cover the damage, so he simply paid for the repairs himself. I asked you to hold onto that detail, and here is why. That man was living, years early, in the future the rest of us are now entering: a world in which the home — the single largest asset most families will ever own, the thing we are taught to regard as the very definition of security — quietly ceases to be insurable against the new weather, and the loss is privatized, shifted from the institution built to pool risk onto the individual least able to bear it. The North got there first because the North is warming fastest. But the logic is the same logic, rolling south, and it does not stop at the tree line.

I hold, as longtime readers know, a position in Tesla — a disclosure I make because the next stretch of this series will brush against electric vehicles and clean-energy markets, and you are entitled to know where my own modest interests lie before I opine on any of it. I mention it here because the insurance story is, in a sense, the mirror image of the question every climate investor eventually confronts: the same actuarial logic that is fleeing the wildfire zone is, elsewhere, quietly pricing in the energy transition. Money is not sentimental in either direction. It runs from the risk it can no longer ignore, and toward the change it has decided is coming. The trick, for a citizen and not merely an investor, is to read what the money is doing as information — and right now the money is telling us, in the plainest language it has, that the houses were built for a world that is leaving.
