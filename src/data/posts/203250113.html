Three accounts of a single handshake — capitulation, nothing, and a process already underway. They are describing the same afternoon at Bürgenstock. Choose the one that suits you; the men who signed it can't agree either.

There is a kind of agreement so successful that the two parties cannot agree on what it says. This is the agreement the United States and Iran now have.

In the last two installments I wrote about the document — the fourteen paragraphs, the all-capital-letters surrender demand that arrived as its own opposite, the long invoice the war will leave behind. Those essays assumed, as one does, that there was a text to argue about: a thing signed, with words in it, that meant something to the people who signed it.

This week I am no longer sure that assumption holds. Because the most striking feature of the talks that opened at a Qatari-owned resort above Lake Lucerne is not what the two sides agreed to. It is that, having sat together for the better part of eighteen hours, they emerged and told the world three entirely different stories about what had happened in the room — and have spent every day since insisting, with mounting irritation, that the other side is lying.

I want to be fair about the genuine progress first, because there was some, and a war diary that cannot acknowledge good news is not worth reading. The talks happened. After Friday’s postponement and Sunday’s eighty-minute walkout, the Americans and the Iranians actually sat down, with Pakistani and Qatari mediators in the room, and stayed there long enough to build something. They agreed on mechanisms — an oversight committee, a hotline for the Strait of Hormuz, a “de-confliction cell” for Lebanon. The mediators called it “encouraging progress.” The Swiss, who are professionally calm, called it “constructive.” Oil fell three percent on the news. After four months of war, men who wanted each other dead in February spent a June weekend in the same building working on the machinery of peace. That is not nothing. It deserves to be said plainly before the rest.

The rest is that they cannot agree on a single thing they decided.

I. Three Accounts of One Handshake

Begin with the nuclear inspectors, because it is the cleanest example and because it matters most.

On Monday, Vice President Vance stood at a microphone in Switzerland and announced that Iran had agreed to invite the inspectors of the International Atomic Energy Agency back into the country. He called it “a major milestone for the American people and the first step in permanently ending a nuclear weapons program in Iran.” The President went further, posting that “everybody is fully aware that Iran will agree to have Major Weapons Inspections in order to ensure ‘Nuclear Honesty’ long into the future.”

That is account number one: Iran has agreed to the inspectors.

Within hours, as the New York Times has reported, the spokesman for Iran’s foreign ministry, Esmail Baghaei, told Iranian state media that Iran had made “no new commitments” on inspections, and that any engagement would take place “under existing procedures.” By Tuesday he had sharpened it: “We had no detailed discussions on the nuclear issue.” Iran’s ambassador to the United Nations in Geneva added that it was “too early to discuss these things.” The nuclear question, both men said, was for later rounds.

That is account number two: nothing happened.

And then, cutting across both, came the inspectors themselves. Rafael Grossi, the director general of the I.A.E.A., told a Japanese broadcaster on Tuesday that inspections would commence — “the sooner the better.” He said his agency would seek access to Iran’s stockpiles of highly enriched uranium, even as he acknowledged the small practical difficulty that the relevant facilities had been bombed into partial rubble and that no one is quite sure how an inspector is meant to inspect a hole in a mountain.

That is account number three: it’s already on.

So we have an agreement that, depending on which capital you are standing in, represents either a total Iranian capitulation, or an event that did not occur, or a process already underway. The President’s response to the discrepancy was to escalate it. On Tuesday he posted that Iran had “fully and completely agreed to the highest level Nuclear inspections,” accused the Iranians of making false statements, and added the threat that gives the whole farce its edge: “If they did not agree to this, there would be no further negotiations!”

Read that sentence carefully, because it is genuinely remarkable. The President of the United States is threatening to collapse the negotiations unless the other side stops denying it agreed to something — a thing the other side says it did not agree to. He is not demanding that Iran do the thing. He is demanding that Iran stop saying it didn’t promise to. The dispute is no longer about the world. It is about the press release.

II. The Same Fog, Everywhere You Look

Vice President JD Vance after the United States and Iran held high-level talks at the Bürgenstock Resort in Switzerland on Monday.Credit...Pool photo by Nathan Howard

Once you notice the pattern, you cannot stop seeing it. It is on every issue, not just the nuclear one.

Take the frozen assets — the roughly twenty-four billion dollars of Iranian money sitting in foreign banks that Tehran wants released. On Monday, as the New York Times has reported, Vance described a clever arrangement, which he credited to the President’s son-in-law Jared Kushner: any unfrozen Iranian money would have to be spent on American goods — soy, corn, wheat — and the United States and Qatar would oversee how it was used. “If Iranian assets are ever unfrozen,” Vance said, “they’re going to go to make American farmers richer and to feed the Iranian people.” The President repeated it from the Oval Office: the food would be “bought exclusively through the United States from our farmers.”

It is worth pausing on the sheer creativity of this. The proposal is that Iran’s own money, frozen by America, be released by America on the condition that Iran spend it buying American crops, with America watching to make sure. There is a word for an arrangement in which you get your own money back only if you spend it at the company store, and it is not “milestone.”

But never mind the merits, because by Tuesday it had dissolved into the usual fog. Iran’s ambassador in Geneva confirmed that a first tranche of six billion dollars was being unfrozen — and then flatly rejected the rest of the American account. “Iran is the only country who will decide what to do with its assets,” he said. So: the money is moving, the conditions are not agreed, and the two sides have once again announced the same event in incompatible terms. Iranian officials have said all along the money is for rebuilding their own infrastructure, not for enriching Iowa.

The Strait of Hormuz tells the same story in a different key, and I will come back to the strait in a future installment because it has grown into its own grim subject — Iran has spent the week building a bureaucracy of toll booths, a “Strait Authority” and a dedicated insurance company that will sell ships protection against a danger Iran itself created. For now it is enough to note the headline contradiction. On Saturday Iran announced it was closing the strait. The American military said it was open and that “Iran does not control” it. On Monday the President called it “totally open,” “an oil gusher,” and claimed “we took in more oil yesterday than has ever gone through the strait.” A shipping executive named Harry Vafias, whose three vessels have sat trapped in the Gulf for three months, summed up the position of the actual human beings trying to move actual ships through actual water: “USA says it’s open while Iran says it’s closed.”

That is the war in a sentence. Two governments, two accounts, and a man with three ships and ninety crew waiting to find out which one is true.

III. The Frontman

Spare a thought, if you can, for the Vice President, who has been handed the job of selling a deal whose terms his own boss keeps rewriting in real time on the internet.

As Tyler Pager reported in the New York Times, as Vance entered the fifth hour of negotiations on Sunday, the President went on Fox News and threatened to start bombing again — telling a reporter that if Iran closed the strait, the negotiators in the room with Vance “won’t have a country” to return to. Picture the scene. The Vice President of the United States is across a table from the Iranian delegation, offering, in his words, an “outstretched hand” to the Iranian people. And while he is doing it, the President is on television threatening to erase their country. The outstretched hand and the clenched fist, working the same negotiation, in the same hour, on behalf of the same government.

How Vice President JD Vance handles the future of the negotiations will factor into Republicans’ performance in the midterm elections and his future as a potential successor to President Trump.Credit...Pool photo by Nathan Howard

Vance’s explanation for this on Monday was a small masterpiece of the genre. When the Iranians “engage in what us millennials might call trash talk,” he said, “you can’t expect the president of the United States not to respond.” This is the official position: the President of the United States threatened to annihilate a nation mid-negotiation because the other side had hurt his feelings, and we are to understand this as a generational quirk of communication style.

The Vice President’s predicament is not merely tonal. It is, as Karim Sadjadpour observed this week, genuinely precarious. Vance could emerge as the man who ended an unpopular war — or, Sadjadpour warned, he could be “viewed as the architect of an American humiliation and a deal that concedes billions of dollars to a committed U.S. adversary.” To succeed he must depend on the cooperation of the very Revolutionary Guard commanders who ran the war against him, which Sadjadpour drily called “not an auspicious position for any American politician, let alone an aspiring president.” And the President, characteristically, has set the terms of accountability in advance: “If it works out, I’m going to take the credit. If it doesn’t work out, I’m blaming JD.”

There is an entire theory of management in those two sentences, and it is not a generous one.

Sadjadpour offered one more observation that has stayed with me, because it reaches past this deal to the thing underneath it. “Americans dislike wars,” he said, “but they dislike defeats even more.” He was thinking of the last Vice President hauled down by a war’s ending — of how Joe Biden’s poll numbers cratered after the withdrawal from Afghanistan, the one in which thirteen American service members were killed. Thirteen. It is the same number who have died in this war. The symmetry is the kind a satirist would reject as too neat, and history has handed it over anyway.

IV. Why None of This Had to Happen

It would be comforting to think that all this confusion is the unavoidable fog of a hard peace. It is not. The fog is downstream of a decision, and the decision was a mistake of an old and well-documented kind.

Writing in the Atlantic today, Brynn Tannehill laid out the error with a clarity the war’s planners never managed. The American plan, she wrote, assumed that a war could be won by aerial bombing alone — that you could bomb Iran until either its people overthrew the government or the government capitulated. Neither happened. The Iranian people did not revolt. The Iranian government “hunkered down, closed the strait, and gambled that the U.S. would be unwilling to invade or strike at crucial infrastructure.” The gamble paid.

This is, Tannehill notes, the oldest false promise in modern war. Since the 1920s, theorists from Italy’s Giulio Douhet to the Royal Air Force’s Hugh Trenchard to America’s own Curtis LeMay have insisted that airpower alone could break a nation’s will. It has never once been true. The Blitz did not break Britain. The Allied bombing of Germany did not break the Nazis; the Red Army did. Japan did not surrender to firebombing but to the atomic bombs and the Soviet march into Manchuria. In Vietnam the United States dropped nearly three times the tonnage it had dropped in all of the Second World War, and lost. Even the apparent triumphs — the Gulf War, Serbia, Afghanistan — each required boots on the ground or the credible threat of them. A RAND study said as much in 1996. The lesson has been available, in writing, for thirty years.

Iran understood it from the first day. It grasped, as Tannehill puts it, that the outcome “would be determined by who could withstand the closure of the Strait of Hormuz,” and it “always had a plausible theory of victory and pursued it logically and consistently.” The United States, which had no credible threat of invasion and no appetite for the mass civilian casualties that might have forced a surrender, was left with only the bombs — and the bombs, as ever, were not enough.

A century of the same idea, dressed in newer silhouettes: that enough bombs, dropped from enough height, will break a nation's will. From Douhet to Vietnam to Iran, the planes got sleeker and the lesson stayed the same. The bombs keep falling. The ground keeps not surrendering.

There is a personnel dimension to this that Tannehill does not spare. A junior officer, she writes, might see a two-thousand-pound bomb dropped on a house as a self-contained solution to taking fire. A flag officer understands that the same bomb manufactures a hundred new enemies by next week. The Secretary of Defense, whose military experience was gained at the junior level, “seems to believe that technological might and physical strength, rather than carefully thought-out strategy, win wars.” The chairman of the Joint Chiefs, by one account, cautioned against the whole adventure. He was not heeded.

So the confusion of this week is not an accident of diplomacy. It is the natural condition of a government that won the bombing and lost the war, and must now narrate a defeat as though it were a triumph. Three accounts of one handshake is what it looks like when one of the parties cannot afford to say out loud what it actually signed.

V. The Reckoning in Jerusalem

There is one capital where the confusion has resolved into something clearer, and it is not a happy clarity.

Israel went into this war alongside the United States — the first joint military campaign the two nations have ever waged — convinced it would defang Iran for a generation. It has emerged sidelined from a deal it was not party to, a deal that constrains its freedom to fight in Lebanon, ignores Iran’s missiles, and leaves the nuclear question to a negotiation Israel does not control. Prime Minister Netanyahu has said he does not feel bound by it. His foreign minister says Israeli troops will not leave the “security zone” in southern Lebanon. And on Tuesday, a strike near Nabatieh killed two more people, three days after the ceasefire that was supposed to hold.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel during a news conference in Jerusalem this week.Credit...Pool photo by Ronen Zvulun

For Netanyahu the timing is merciless, as the New York Times has reported. Sixty days from now, when the President is meant to have finalized his peace, Israel will be weeks from a national election. The man who staked his career on stopping an Iranian bomb is heading to the polls having watched his great ally sign a deal that, in the words of one retired Israeli intelligence officer, amounts to “a collapse of all the strategy that we had regarding Iran.” The President, who once called Netanyahu the closest of allies, now says he “gets a little excited sometimes” and “will do whatever I want him to do,” and has confirmed calling him “crazy” in an expletive-laden phone call. As one former aide to the Prime Minister put it: “This is what you get when you put all your foreign policy eggs in Trump’s basket. Trump was viewed as the savior of Israel, and now Israelis feel he threw us under the nuclear-powered bus.”

It is the one account in this entire affair on which both sides agree. The Israelis believe they were abandoned, and the record does not contradict them.

VI. The Thing About a Document Nobody Reads the Same Way

I keep returning, in this diary, to a phrase a senior American official offered to CNN in the deal’s first hours — that people “shouldn’t read too much into the language” of the memorandum, because “it’s the understandings we have with them which is so much more important.” At the time it read as spin. This week it reads as prophecy. The language was never meant to be read closely, because the language was never the point. The point was that everyone could go home and tell their own people they had won.

This works, for a while. The President tells his supporters Iran capitulated. The Iranians tell their hardliners they conceded nothing. The mediators tell the markets there is progress. Everyone holds a different page and calls it the same book. But peace, unlike a press conference, eventually requires the two sides to do specific things at specific times, and a deal whose terms cannot survive a single news cycle without contradiction is not a foundation. It is a fog, and fogs lift.

Abbas Araghchi, Iran’s foreign minister and the most experienced negotiator in the room, wrote in his memoir last year — as profiled in the New York Times — that the Iranian bargaining style “means continuous and tireless bargaining. It requires a lot of time and energy, and he who gets tired and bored quickly will lose.” Across the table sits an administration whose own officials advised the press not to read the words, and whose President conducts his diplomacy in all-capital threats between television appearances. One side believes language is everything and patience is the weapon. The other believes words don’t matter. I would not bet on the side that stopped reading.

The war is paused. The strait is open, or closed. The inspectors are coming, or they are not. Iran has agreed to everything, or to nothing. Choose your account. They cannot all be true, and the people who signed the thing are no help, because they are the ones who disagree.

The Numbers

Days since the United States and Iran signed the memorandum of understanding: 8.

Separate, contradictory accounts the two governments have given of the nuclear-inspection agreement: 3 — Trump’s, Iran’s, and the inspectors’.

Hours the two delegations spent together in Switzerland: roughly 18.

Minutes into the first session before Iran walked out over a presidential social-media post: 80.

First tranche of frozen Iranian assets now being released: 6 billion dollars, of a total exceeding 24 billion.

Crops the Vice President proposed Iran buy with its own unfrozen money: soy, corn, and wheat.

Vessels that crossed the Strait of Hormuz over three days this week: 109 — against a prewar average above 130 every single day.

Ships still backed up in the Persian Gulf, waiting: 500 to 600.

Tonnage of bombs the United States dropped on Vietnam, the last time it believed airpower alone could win: 7.6 million — nearly three times what it dropped in all of the Second World War.

People killed near Nabatieh on Tuesday, three days into the ceasefire: 2.

American service members killed in the war: 13 — the same number killed in the Afghanistan withdrawal that ended one Vice President’s standing, a coincidence history declined to spare the current one.

Iranians reported killed: more than 3,000.

Children killed at the Shajarah Tayyebeh school in Minab in the war’s first hours: 175.

Senator Kennedy of Louisiana remains the only Republican who voted against the war.

Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison.
