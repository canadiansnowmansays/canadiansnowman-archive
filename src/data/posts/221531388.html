On Wednesday, October 21, in St. Petersburg, Steve Witkoff sat across from Vladimir Putin and thanked him. Russia had just released Stephen James Hubbard, a 74-year-old American teacher from Michigan. “You are a humanitarian also, sir,” Witkoff told the man wanted by the International Criminal Court for the deportation of Ukrainian children, “and I salute that.”

David Sanger, Kim Barker and Ivan Nechepurenko reported the exchange for the New York Times. The rest of this essay is about the week around that sentence, and about what the word “humanitarian” meant to the people who were not in the room.

The teacher from Izium

Hubbard was living in Izium, in the Kharkiv region, when Russian forces seized the town in the spring of 2022. They took him in April. He was tortured and starved, then held in the general prison population at IK-12, a penal colony in Mordovia. In October 2024 a Russian court convicted him as a “mercenary,” a charge that was implausible on its face. His son, Joseph Coleman, a Greek Orthodox priest in Cyprus, wrote to Putin himself. Father and son managed one phone call, in May 2025. Hubbard described himself as having “a slight lack of hope.”

His release is good news, and nobody should begrudge him a day of it. But it is worth being precise about what it cost.

President Trump said the United States was “not required to give anything in return,” and that “no ‘swap’ necessary.” Later the same day, as Valerie Hopkins noted in the New York Times, he said Russia would not have let Hubbard go if American sanctions had remained in place. So there was a price after all. It was paid in the sanctions relief described in Parts III and IV, which Russia is collecting in barrels.

And while Witkoff was in St. Petersburg, Russia arrested a German citizen on charges much like the ones it used against Hubbard. One prisoner leaves; another arrives. A hostage system does not shut down because it worked.

Witkoff also left something behind. He told Putin he had “brought some papers” on a peace deal, for the Russian president to “read at your leisure later.”

The other humanitarian question

Five days earlier, on Friday, October 16, Putin took a different call.

Pope Leo XIV had undergone surgery for lung cancer three days before. Surgeons had removed the lower lobe of his right lung. From his hospital in Rome, he telephoned the Kremlin to ask for one thing: a ceasefire long enough to get civilians out of Oleshky, as Elisabetta Povoledo and Siobhán O’Grady reported for the New York Times.

Oleshky is a small city in the Kherson region, on the Russian-held left bank of the Dnipro. Before the war, about 24,000 people lived there. Now about 2,000 remain, some 40 of them children, and Ukrainian officials say they are at risk of starvation. A mined riverbank lies on one side of the town and a mined road on the other. Vendors used to bring food to the markets. They stopped in May, after people trying to use the only road in and out were killed by mines and drones.

Escapees describe living on whatever their gardens produce. Ukrainian drones have dropped food packages, and residents say Russian soldiers stole some of them and intimidated the people who tried to collect them. The town’s exiled mayor, Tetiana Hasanenko, had not heard of the pope’s call. She said only that the situation grows “steadily more desperate.”

The Kremlin’s account of the conversation is a small masterpiece. Putin “thanked the Pope for his willingness to contribute to the resolution of the conflict.” He “supported the Vatican’s intention” to help. “Cooperation in this regard was agreed upon.” He also gave the pope “a comprehensive explanation of the Kyiv regime’s responsibility” for the hunger, and described Oleshky as a Russian city.

The Times added a sentence that every Ukrainian could have written: previous promises of humanitarian aid and evacuation along the front “have often yielded little.”

Then, on Friday, October 23, something moved. Melania Trump announced that she had brokered a “localized cease-fire” in Oleshky, to begin on Thursday, so that residents could leave “through a designated safe passageway,” as Katie Rogers reported for the New York Times. “It was abundantly clear that without my intervention, the possibility to bring humanitarian aid into the city and to evacuate the elderly would be impossible,” the first lady said. The Kremlin’s spokesman, Dmitri Peskov, said she had “made a significant contribution” and that the Russian military was “working on agreeing upon the timing.” In Kyiv, David Arakhamia, who leads Zelensky’s party in Parliament, offered “sincere gratitude” to both Mrs. Trump and the pope. The goal, he said, was “permanent humanitarian corridors for the safe evacuation of civilians and the delivery of aid.”

If it holds, it is the best news in this essay, and Ukrainians will take it from whoever delivers it. But some caution is earned. As this essay goes out, the ceasefire has not begun, and no one has said which way the evacuees will go. In the past, people fleeing Oleshky have had to travel deeper into occupied territory and out through Russia and Belarus to reach Ukrainian-held ground. The “impartial American media organization” the first lady is sending to document the mission is Newsmax. And her other humanitarian file shows how much remains undone. The White House says at least 34 children have been reunited with their families through her appeals to Putin. Ukraine accuses Russia of taking more than 19,000.

So here is the week’s ledger of humanitarianism. A pope recovering from cancer surgery asked for bread and a road for 2,000 people. A first lady announced she had secured them. The man who controls the road was thanked by both of them, and saluted by an American envoy for releasing one prisoner. If the corridor opens on Thursday, it will be the first good day Oleshky has had since May.

Who turned it off?

The same week, the question of who had throttled Ukraine’s army finally got an answer of sorts. It was not much of one.

Part IV described how Starshield, the military version of Starlink, began cutting out in mid-October for any drone flying faster than 100 miles an hour. Siobhán O’Grady, Carlotta Gall and Anton Troianovski went to the commanders who lived through it, for the New York Times. One, named only as Oleh, lost more than a million dollars’ worth of drones in one go and had to halt operations for three days. It was time, he said, to “take off the rose-colored glasses.”

Lt. Yuriy Butusov, a journalist turned soldier, called the outage “a vacation day” for Russia. “This day without Starlink saved many lives and many trucks for Russia,” he said. The cost would be “much more blood. Our blood.”

Then came the explanations, and they did not agree.

Elon Musk said last week that “Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the opposite: the United States “cannot direct a private company,” and neither Trump nor anyone “in our system ever requested that any changes to that system be made.”

On Thursday, October 22, Michael Crowley, Maggie Haberman and O’Grady reported in the New York Times that Trump had called Musk last week and asked him to restore Ukraine’s full access. The Wall Street Journal reported it first. Asked about it, Trump would not say whether he made the call. “Whether I called or whether somebody else called — doesn’t make any difference, I guess,” he said. “The bottom line — he put it back.”

Some Ukrainian officials said it was not yet fully back.

The satellites were not the only thing that flickered. During last week’s Russian missile barrage on Kyiv, Shane Harris reported in The Atlantic, disruptions to Starlink and to American early-warning systems left Ukraine blind to some incoming missiles, though there is also evidence that technical failures explain at least some of the misses. Officially, Washington has not turned off the intelligence. But Trump has threatened to, unless Ukraine stops striking Russian oil refineries, which it has been hitting, on average, once every three days this year.

Senator Thom Tillis, the North Carolina Republican, welcomed the reports. He then asked whoever had objected to restoring the service to “have the courage to raise your hand, and explain your justification for allowing Putin to continue to mow down toddlers in strollers in Ukrainian parks.” No hand has gone up.

Musk says the president decides. Rubio says nobody asked. The president says it doesn’t matter who called. For about a week, someone throttled the army of a country fighting for its survival, and no one will say who. Ukrainians are left with the result, which, as Butusov said, was paid in their blood.

The kill zone

What does the front look like from inside the restrictions? Constant Méheut and his colleagues Holder and Konovalova answered in the New York Times on October 17, in a piece called “This Is the Kill Zone.”

Henadii, 52, and Mykhailo, 51, of the 128th Brigade spent more than 200 days in a ruined house at Primorske. Their food was dropped to them by drone. The dead were dragged inside so that Russian drones would not see them. On the way in, they had walked about a mile. On the way out, the journey took six.

That is what the kill zone has become: a band of land, dozens of miles wide, where anything that moves is hunted. “Day by day, the kill zone is expanding, getting wider and deadlier,” Brigadier General Ihor Obolensky told Simon Shuster of The Atlantic this week. “So what’s the answer? You go underground.”

Underground

Shuster’s report, published on October 22, describes a front most of us could not have imagined a year ago.

Before the war, a web of steel pipes carried Russian natural gas into Ukraine and on to Europe. Ukraine ended that trade at the start of last year. Maps from Ukraine’s state energy company show at least 57 of these tubes running beneath the war zone, most of them one to two metres across. The Russians found them first. In the spring of 2025 their troops began crawling through them to surface behind Ukrainian lines near Kupiansk. “They kept climbing out of the earth like devils,” Obolensky said.

The Russians had learned the method at Sudzha, the Russian town Ukraine seized in August 2024, the first time since 1941 that an enemy had taken Russian land by force. They took it back, street by street, through sewers and tunnels, while North Korean troops helped retake the wider Kursk region. One Russian prisoner of war, a convicted murderer recruited from a labour camp, told a Ukrainian blogger that he had crawled through a pipe for two days, doubled over at the waist. When he asked by radio to retreat with an injured leg, he was told there was “no way back. Only forward.”

Ukraine’s soldiers now go down after them. Obolensky’s Khartiia Corps rides through the pipes on old coal-mining wagons fitted with electric-scooter motors, for deployments as long as 30 days. The tunnels reek of gas residue. When the two sides meet, they fire rifles and grenades at close range, and bullets and shrapnel ricochet off the steel. Explosions suck the air out of the pipe, so the men rely on gas masks and oxygen tanks. After his first trip down, in late July, Obolensky recorded himself on his phone, his face streaked with grime. “The guys working down in these pipes right now, they are heroes, unlike any other warriors I have seen,” he said.

In six months, in an operation named Veles after the Slavic god of the underworld, the Khartiia Corps has retaken about nine miles of pipeline. Two pipes are back in Ukrainian hands. Dozens are not.

There is a bitter symmetry in it. The pipes that once carried Russian gas, the trade that Washington is now trying to revive for Europe, have become a path for Russian soldiers into Ukraine. And Ukraine’s officers fear what comes next: a winter offensive to punch through the kill zone and take all of the east. Control of the pipe network, Obolensky said, could give Russia “a decisive advantage.”

Shuster also notes how much of Ukrainian life has gone below ground with the soldiers. Schools have built underground classrooms. Parts of the power grid have been buried. Weapons factories work inside bunkers. Part IV opened with a family in a metro station. A country this deep underground is still standing, but it is not living as it should.

In the air

Above ground, Ukraine is trying to hold the sky with what its allies have actually delivered. Lara Jakes reported for the New York Times on October 19 that Ukraine has about 50 F-16s, half of them grounded for maintenance at any given time. Belgium’s promised jets still have not arrived, three years on. Last month, Ukrainian forces shot down about 1,900 of the 3,500 jet-powered drones Russia launched. One pilot downed ten in a single flight.

The war has already taken pilots such as Lt. Col. Oleksiy Mes. Swedish Gripens are said to be coming “soon”; French Rafales are two or more years away. A trainee identified only as “S” summed up the arithmetic in four words: “We don’t have any choice.”

That is the gap the Starshield restriction cut into. As Part VI noted, the interceptors that rely on the connection have to outrun Russian drones flying faster than 100 miles an hour. A limit of 100 miles an hour is not a technical detail when the thing you are chasing goes faster.

“Everything is fine”

What about Russia? Ukraine’s strategy, which Washington spent October trying to restrain, is to make ordinary Russians feel the war. On Thursday, October 22, Ukraine sent 630 drones into the Moscow region. Valerie Hopkins went to see what Russians made of it, for the New York Times, in Sofino, a town about an hour from the capital, where Ukrainian drones had struck an industrial area and an apartment complex, killing two people.

At a Rosneft station with no diesel, the queue snaked past the exit lane. A young man named Danya had waited 30 minutes. “Everything is fine,” he said. The war “doesn’t affect my life at all.” In the damaged apartment building, a woman named Lusya was hauling rubbish down a rubble-strewn staircase. The strike had made her homeless, and the evening news had not mentioned it. “I support my president, even more so after all this,” she said. “I believe we are very lucky to have Putin.”

An independent Levada poll last month found that nearly 60 percent of Russians pay little or no attention to the fighting, the highest share ever recorded. Six in ten say the country is on the right track. “The pressure and the problems that are there are, in my view, not intense enough for him to reconsider,” said Janis Kluge of the German Institute for International and Security Affairs.

Not everyone shrugged. The station’s manager, Irina, has a brother who has been at the front since 2022. He expects the war to last until 2030. “We’ll just have to endure it,” she said. “If we die, we die.” At the headquarters of Yabloko, Russia’s main anti-war party, a 24-year-old named Maria answered Westerners who ask why Russians do not rise up. “You can’t demand that we go on strike or turn out for protests,” she said, “because all we’ll achieve is ending up in prison — or getting killed.” The banned rights group Memorial counts 1,856 political prisoners in Russia.

Hopkins draws out one difference that matters. Ukraine lacks the ballistic missiles and jet-powered drones that Russia fires at Ukrainian cities every day, and it aims mostly at economic and military targets. It cannot frighten Russians the way Russia frightens Ukrainians, and it does not try to. Kyiv’s ordinary Wednesday, described in Part VI, happens under ballistic missiles. Sofino’s “everything is fine” happens in a petrol queue.

The prize

Hopkins’s earlier report, on October 22, described what Putin has gained. It was exactly a year since Washington imposed sanctions on Rosneft and Lukoil. Now the Kremlin has agreed to low-level three-way talks next month, Putin has floated a fertilizer deal, and his envoy Kirill Dmitriev is talking up data centres and energy projects. Akhmedova, a pro-Kremlin journalist, put it plainly: “victory has already been achieved,” in the way the United States now engages with Russia.

Some analysts think the prize matters less than it once would have. Jeremy Shapiro of the European Council on Foreign Relations said, “Winning the Americans isn’t the prize it once was,” and noted that the Kremlin sees Trump as “very inconsistent.” Alexander Gabuev argued that normalisation helped Moscow “deactivate Trump.” Either way, Putin is collecting without giving.

David Sanger of the New York Times has suggested a comparison that Germans will recognise: Trump as the new Angela Merkel, building a relationship with Russia on energy and calling Moscow a “reliable supplier,” as Berlin did with Nord Stream 2, until the tanks rolled. Trump’s own summary of the balance sheet was this: “We’ve given a lot to Ukraine. They’ve given nothing to us.” Sanger also recalled Finland, which kept its independence after the Second World War by ceding about 12 percent of its territory.

The calendar is where it all leads. Jonathan Lemire and Simon Shuster of The Atlantic reported that Putin floated a great-power meeting in his birthday call with Trump. The APEC summit in Shenzhen comes in mid-November, and the G20 at Trump’s Doral resort in mid-December. The talk now is of “a new Yalta Conference.” As one official put it to them, “It looks like this is all happening without us.” Ukrainians have been saying that about their own country since February 2022.

On Radio Atlantic, Lemire put the moment in historical terms. It was, he said, the first time in American political history that a campaign’s October surprise was a U.S. president “throwing in his lot with the Kremlin.” The midterms are on November 3. Between then and the new Congress on January 3, there is a lame-duck window in which a great deal can be signed.

What Ukraine will not trade

Go back to the room in St. Petersburg. A man wanted for war crimes is told he is a humanitarian. A pile of papers is left for him to read at his leisure. Two months from now, he may be welcomed to a golf resort in Florida.

Now go to the places the papers are about. Two thousand people in Oleshky, eating from their gardens between a mined road and a mined riverbank, waiting to see whether Thursday’s corridor opens. Two men from the 128th Brigade who walked a mile in and six miles out. Soldiers of the Khartiia Corps coming up from a month in a gas pipe, black with grime. A pilot who shot down ten drones in one flight because there were not enough planes to share the work.

None of them was in the room. None of them was asked. And none of them, from what they tell the reporters who reach them, is ready to be traded.

“We don’t have any choice,” said the trainee pilot. He meant that Ukraine has to fight with what it has. It is also the plainest description of this series that I have heard. Ukraine did not choose this war. It has chosen, every day since, not to disappear.
