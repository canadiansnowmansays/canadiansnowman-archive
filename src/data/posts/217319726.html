This Week in New York — Thursday, September 24, 2026

There are two ways to fail to address the United Nations General Assembly. The first is to be kept from arriving. The second is to arrive, take the podium, and speak to someone else entirely. On Thursday, September 24, 2026, the eighty-first session managed both before the afternoon was out. The hall the Charter’s drafters imagined as the world’s town meeting spent the morning as a recording studio and the afternoon as a campaign rally.

Xi Jinping’s visit to Washington has its own stage in The Washington Opera. This dispatch stays on First Avenue, where the day’s subject was the oldest question in the building: who is allowed into the room, and what happens to the room once they are in it.

The man on the screen

Mahmoud Abbas is 90 years old. For the second September in a row, he did not appear in the General Assembly Hall, because the Trump administration refused him a visa to travel to New York. He called the refusal “unlawful” and “punitive,” which is what one says when nothing else can be done about it. In mid-September 2026 the member states voted, as they had in 2025, to let him send a recording. So at about 10:19 a.m. the President of the Palestinian Authority appeared on the great screens beside the gold wall, while the lectern below him stood empty.

Readers of Part V will know this has happened before, and more grandly. In November 1988, Secretary of State George Shultz refused Yasser Arafat a visa. The Assembly’s answer was to pack up and reconvene in Geneva that December so that Arafat could speak in person. The 1947 Headquarters Agreement obliges the United States not to impede those invited to the UN; Washington has always read its security exception generously when the visitor was Palestinian. In 1988 the membership answered with its feet. In 2025 and 2026 it answered with a projector.

What Abbas said matters more than where he said it from. He accused Israel of “a genocidal war that has created an unprecedented humanitarian catastrophe,” and called the continued attacks in Gaza and settler violence in the West Bank “a malicious colonial scheme” meant to force Palestinians off their land. Then he did what he has done in these speeches for two decades: he asked for a two-state solution anyway. He wanted, he said, “a democratic Palestinian state that respects the rule of law and human rights, a nonmilitarized state that lives in security and peace alongside the state of Israel. It is possible.” He rejected attacks on civilians and terrorism, and said of antisemitism, “We hate it.”

Two passages carried the address. The first was his verdict on President Trump’s Board of Peace plan for Gaza, the twenty-point framework the Security Council adopted by resolution as the blueprint for ending the war, disarming Hamas and rebuilding the enclave. Abbas said he had welcomed it. “Regretfully, we have not seen any of this materialize on the ground.” The second was his closing refrain: “We will not leave our homeland. We will not accept resettlement. We will not allow anyone to decide for us.”

According to The New York Times’ live coverage by Farnaz Fassihi and Zane Irwin on September 24, 2026, 157 of the 193 member states now recognize Palestine, Canada among them since the 2025 session. Ottawa’s recognition was a real act, and a late one, and it has changed about as much on the ground as the Board of Peace has.

The legal backdrop was set a year and a week earlier. On September 16, 2025, the UN’s Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory, chaired by former High Commissioner for Human Rights Navi Pillay, concluded that Israeli authorities and security forces had committed four of the five genocidal acts defined in the 1948 Genocide Convention. Israel rejected the finding outright, as it rejects the charge today. Neither the commission nor the Human Rights Council can compel anything. The United Nations is superb at establishing facts and nearly powerless to act on them.

The man at the podium

Benjamin Netanyahu, 76, landed only hours before his speech and planned to leave the same night. He did not stay over, and he did not meet the President. It was a diplomatic drive-through. The Jerusalem Post reported before the speech that the address would run about forty minutes, unusually long for the Assembly, and would centre on Iran. Netanyahu had promised “surprises.”

The walkout came first, as it had in 2025. Dozens of delegates rose and left as he reached the lectern. Detractors booed, and a group of supporters answered with applause and the chant “Am Yisrael Chai,” the nation of Israel lives. The prime minister spoke to the empty seats directly: “If there are any other moral cowards who haven’t yet left this hall, please do so now.” He called the display “mind-blowing hypocrisy,” arguing that by striking Iran’s nuclear programme Israel had defended the very countries whose delegates had just walked out.

Israeli television carried the rest live in primetime, about five weeks before an election in which he trails in the polls. The Assembly was the backdrop; the electorate was the audience.

It was built like a stump speech: Entebbe in 1976, where his brother Yoni was the only Israeli soldier killed; October 7, 2023, when about 1,200 people were killed in Israel and “we rose and fought like lions.” Israel had “no greater partner than President Trump,” and striking Iran together had been “one of the easiest decisions I’ve had to make as prime minister, because if we hadn’t done it, we’d all be dead.” His enemies ran from Erdoğan to Western European leaders who had “chosen to succumb to the antisemitic mob.”

On the charge that had dominated the morning, he was categorical. “Accusing Israel of genocide is the greatest lie of the century,” he said. Israel had not committed a genocide; it had “prevented a genocide.” On the West Bank he minimized. Settler violence was the work of “about 150” “juvenile delinquents” who “throw stones” and “chop down olive trees,” and the world’s concern about it an “obsession.” The UN counted 18 Palestinians killed by settlers in the first seven months of 2026. Against that he set the September 23, 2026 car-ramming at a West Bank checkpoint that critically wounded the son of Yechiel Leiter, Israel’s ambassador to the United States. That was real grief, and it belonged in the speech. What the speech left out, as the Times’ Max Bearak, Farnaz Fassihi and Isabel Kershner noted, was Gaza itself, where officials say more than 70,000 people have been killed.

Then came the prop, because there is always a prop. In 2012 it was a cartoon bomb with a red line drawn across it. On September 24, 2026, it was a Starlink terminal. Netanyahu held it up and said Iran’s rulers imposed internet blackouts because they feared their own people. He was leaving the device for the Iranian delegation, he said, so that when its members “inevitably defect, they too will be able to freely tell their story on social media.” The regime, he predicted, “will be toppled by its lies, by its corruption, by its cruelty.” “The power of the people will overcome the people in power.”

A few hours later, Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, told reporters that Tehran had proposed a seven-day plan to Washington: an end to hostilities, the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, and then comprehensive nuclear talks. He called his Qatar-mediated meeting with Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner on Tuesday, September 22, 2026, “productive.” The Israeli prime minister had brought a satellite dish and an eviction notice; the Iranians offered a timetable to envoys who were still talking to them. Neither message was addressed to the Assembly.

The mayor who was elsewhere

The most-watched sideshow of the day took place outside the hall. Zohran Mamdani, New York’s first Muslim mayor, won office in November 2025 partly on a promise to carry out the International Criminal Court’s arrest warrant against Netanyahu if he ever set foot in the city. The Court issued the warrant in November 2024, alleging war crimes and crimes against humanity, which Netanyahu denies. In July 2026 Mamdani conceded that the city could not make the arrest and urged Washington to do it. Washington regards the Court as illegitimate and is not party to the Rome Statute. The warrant binds every one of the Court’s member states and is a rhetorical device in this one.

Netanyahu had spent the week promising to “tell the truth” about the mayor, and he kept that promise after his fashion. For several minutes he addressed Mamdani directly, called him an antisemite, and said that since his election “many Jews no longer feel safe in New York.” Liam Stack’s reporting for the Times supplied the context: antisemitic hate crimes in New York have risen in recent years, but there have been no riots targeting Jews while Mamdani has held office. Likud, meanwhile, has put Mamdani’s face on Israeli billboards beside the leaders of Iran, Turkey and Hezbollah. A mayor of New York has become a campaign prop in an Israeli election, which is new even for this building.

Mamdani was in neither the hall nor the streets. Minutes before Netanyahu began, he posted a wordless one-minute video of himself planting an olive tree at Gracie Mansion and eating dinner with Palestinian families from Gaza, captioned “May peace be our future.” Afterward he said in a statement that the prime minister had used his speech “to repeat baseless lies meant to sanitize his genocide of Palestinians,” and that no lie could change the fact that he “remains the subject of an International Criminal Court arrest warrant.”

Others went further than the mayor. At 39th Street and First Avenue, Jewish Voice for Peace led about 250 people in a sit-in, and police arrested about 100 people over the course of the day. Among them were the actresses Susan Sarandon and Hannah Einbinder, City Councilman Chi Ossé, and Darializa Avila Chevalier, the Democratic nominee in a Manhattan and Bronx House district. Avila Chevalier put the day’s absurdity in one sentence: “The deep irony is the person with the arrest warrant for crimes against humanity is not the one that will be facing arrest today.”

It would be easy to mock Mamdani for the gap between the campaign promise and his Thursday schedule, and critics on his left have done so. But the city’s own Law Department told him in July that it had no authority to make the arrest, and it was right. International criminal law has courts, judges, findings and warrants. Wherever a great power objects, it has no sheriff. A mayor cannot supply one. The one government that could chose instead to deny a visa to the accused man’s opponent and wave the accused man through.

The rest of the roll call

Meanwhile the Assembly went on hearing from everyone, as it does.

Hungary’s president, András Bálint Baka, gave his country’s first address since the fall of Viktor Orbán, the Kremlin’s best friend in the European Union. Peter Magyar’s government, in office since May 2026, expelled ten Russian diplomats in early September. Baka condemned “Russia’s war of aggression” and summed up the change in a sentence Orbán could never have delivered: “Hungary will defend its national interest, but it will do so as a partner.”

The Gulf spoke for the war the Assembly was not formally discussing. Kuwait’s crown prince, Sheikh Sabah Khaled Al Sabah, said his country and its neighbours had “been subjected to illegal Iranian aggression” although they were “not a party to the conflict.” At a news conference, Saudi Foreign Minister Prince Faisal bin Farhan said the kingdom was “of course defending ourselves” against the Houthis; the coalition said it had shot down six Houthi ballistic missiles that morning. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman had asked Washington for more military help, and on Sunday, September 20, the President decided not to join Saudi strikes on Yemen, for now. On Thursday afternoon Yemen’s vice-president, Abdullah Abdulkader Al Alimi-Bawzer, criticized “the international community’s hesitation to designate the Houthis as a terrorist group” and praised Washington’s “firm approach.” The firm approach had been to say no four days earlier.

Late in the evening, Australia’s Anthony Albanese gave the day its one speech addressed to the room. The UN’s mission, he said, cannot be entrusted solely to the “great powers” or the “loudest voices,” which is why Australia is seeking a non-permanent Security Council seat for 2029–2030 to speak for middle powers while the “multilateral system is under immense strain.” On Wednesday, September 23, 2026, he had disclosed that AI agents working for OpenAI had got into a government-run Australian health website in June and accessed nonpublic information. Australia learned of it nearly three months later. It was the same day as the Security Council’s first session on AI, covered in Dispatch Two.

What the week is telling us

Two essays published in The Atlantic on September 23, 2026 are worth reading together. In “The Makings of a Prewar World,” Thomas Wright takes the most revealing line of Trump’s Tuesday address to be his defence of the American takeover of Venezuelan oil: “To the victor belongs the spoils.” Drawing on Henry Kissinger’s A World Restored, Wright argues that the great powers’ limited consensus about permissible aims and methods has collapsed, broken by Putin in Ukraine, Xi in the trading system, and Trump against America’s own allies.

Readers of Parts I and II have met that argument in another decade. The League of Nations did not die for lack of rules. It died because the powers that mattered stopped agreeing that the rules applied to them, while the smaller members went on holding their meetings.

Simon Sebag Montefiore’s “The Middle East Still to Come,” adapted from his forthcoming The Cauldron, reads the same moment from the other end of the map. The region, he argues, no longer has a hegemon, the Gulf’s luxury monarchies are turning into military monarchies, and America remains the paramount power but “an ever less reliable ally.” Thursday’s Gulf speakers were that argument playing out: allies asking for help and being told, for now, no.

He ends with Mahmoud Darwish: “As you conduct your wars, don’t forget those who seek peace.” It sits awkwardly beside a day when the Palestinian leader could not enter the building and the Israeli leader would not stay the night.

That is Thursday’s lesson, and it is not a new one. The Charter imagines a Parliament of Man where the powerful and the powerless address one another. What we saw instead was a projector, a prop, a walkout and a mayor with a shovel. Everyone spoke in the room, and almost nobody spoke to it; the exception was a middle-power prime minister asking, at the end of a long day, for a seat at a table whose five permanent chairs are not for sale. The building still works as a stage. What it has lost, for now, is what Kissinger meant by legitimacy, and what this series is trying to trace back to its source: a shared belief among those who hold power that the room is worth talking to at all.
