The Supreme Court in session during the Citizens United era. The 2010 decision opened the door; SpeechNow.org v. FEC (64 days later) walked through it and created the super PAC

Citizens United was decided on January 21, 2010. The super PAC was born on March 26, 2010. The interval between those two dates — sixty-four days — is the amount of time it took for the legal logic of one Supreme Court decision to produce, through the application of a lower court, an entirely new category of political organization that had never previously existed and that has, in the sixteen years since, spent billions of dollars reshaping American elections.

Sixty-four days. The political money system moves faster than most people appreciate.

The case that created the super PAC was SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. SpeechNow.org was an unincorporated nonprofit association — a loose organization of individuals who wanted to make independent expenditures in federal elections, meaning they wanted to spend money supporting or opposing candidates without donating directly to campaigns. Under existing law, groups that made independent expenditures were still subject to contribution limits — individuals could give no more than $5,000 per year to such a group, the same limit that applied to traditional PACs.

SpeechNow argued that this was unconstitutional. Its reasoning followed directly from Citizens United: the Supreme Court had just held that independent expenditures cannot corrupt. If independent expenditures cannot corrupt, then contributions to groups that make only independent expenditures cannot corrupt either — because the contributions go entirely toward the non-corrupting activity. If the contributions cannot corrupt, the government has no anti-corruption interest in limiting them. If the government has no anti-corruption interest, the contribution limits fail First Amendment scrutiny.

The D.C. Circuit agreed. Unanimously. The contribution limits for groups making only independent expenditures were struck down. Any individual, corporation, union, or other entity could now contribute unlimited amounts to such a group, provided the group spent only on independent expenditures rather than direct contributions to candidates.

The super PAC — officially known as an “independent expenditure-only political action committee” — had been born. Not by the Supreme Court. Not by Congress. By the mathematical extension of one premise to its logical conclusion, conducted by a lower court that had no choice but to follow where Citizens United led.

The name “super PAC” was not official. It was coined by journalists and political operatives to distinguish these new unlimited-contribution vehicles from the traditional PACs that remained subject to the old limits. The name stuck because it was accurate: super PACs were PACs with the restrictions removed. They could raise unlimited amounts. They could spend unlimited amounts. The only thing they could not do — technically — was coordinate with the candidates they supported.

The significance of that technical prohibition became clear almost immediately, and the clarity was not reassuring.

The Federal Election Commission’s coordination rules, as written, prohibited super PACs from working directly with candidates’ campaigns — sharing polling data, jointly developing strategy, explicitly discussing advertising plans. What the rules did not prohibit — because prohibiting it would have raised its own First Amendment problems — was a wide range of activities that produced the same practical result as coordination while maintaining technical independence.

Candidates began uploading high-resolution campaign footage to YouTube with no sound, making it publicly available so that their super PACs could use it in advertisements. This was not coordination because the footage was public. The super PAC’s use of it was not a transaction with the campaign. The resulting advertisement was, of course, indistinguishable from one produced with the campaign’s direct involvement, because it had been produced with the campaign’s direct involvement, just through a technically public channel.

Former campaign staff launched single-candidate super PACs devoted entirely to electing their former employer, relying on years of professional intimacy with the candidate’s strategic priorities, messaging preferences, and target audiences — intimacy acquired through years of direct employment — to ensure that the super PAC’s spending was perfectly aligned with the campaign’s needs. This was not coordination because the employment had ended before the super PAC was established.

The FEC, as we will examine in the next essay, was structurally incapable of pursuing these cases. The prohibition on coordination existed. The enforcement of it did not.

The first election cycle in which super PACs operated was 2010 — the congressional midterms, held less than eight months after SpeechNow was decided. Super PACs spent approximately $65 million in that cycle. The number sounds large until you see what came next.

Super PAC spending explosion, 2012–2022. From $609 million in the first full cycle to over $2 billion in 2020. A vehicle that didn’t exist on January 1, 2010, became central to American elections in just over a decade

These are not soft money numbers laundered through party committees. These are direct, reported super PAC expenditures — the portion of the dark money iceberg that is visible above the surface. The portion below the surface, routed through the nonprofit organizations we will examine in Part IX, is additional and largely unmeasured.

The trajectory is the argument. From zero in 2009 to $2.7 billion in 2020 in eleven years. A vehicle that did not exist before March 2010 became, within a decade, one of the primary mechanisms through which American elections are financed.

The single-candidate super PAC deserves particular attention, because it is the mechanism that most clearly demonstrates the gap between the theory and the practice of Citizens United.

The theory: independent spending cannot corrupt because there is no transaction between spender and candidate.

The practice: a super PAC created by a candidate’s former chief of staff, funded by the candidate’s major donors, staffed by the candidate’s former colleagues, spending tens of millions of dollars on advertising produced from footage the candidate provided, in coordination with a strategic plan developed through years of professional relationship, is not independent in any meaningful sense. It is the candidate’s campaign with a different letterhead.

The 2012 presidential election provided the first large-scale demonstration of this dynamic. Mitt Romney’s campaign was supported by Restore Our Future, a super PAC run by a former Romney political director. Barack Obama’s campaign was supported by Priorities USA Action, run by two former White House staff members. Both super PACs raised and spent hundreds of millions of dollars. Both maintained technical independence from their associated campaigns. Neither was independent in any practical sense.

This was not a secret. It was reported extensively. The FEC was aware of it. The FEC did nothing, for reasons that will occupy the next essay. The candidates did nothing, because doing nothing was the rational response to a situation in which your opponent was doing the same thing and the enforcement mechanism was broken.

The theory of Citizens United required good faith independence. The reality of Citizens United produced strategic coordination with extra steps.

There is a secondary effect of the super PAC era that receives less attention than the spending numbers but is, in some ways, more corrosive to democratic function. It is the effect on candidates themselves — specifically, on how they spend their time.

Before Citizens United, a congressional candidate spent a significant portion of their campaign time raising hard money from a large number of donors subject to contribution limits. This was inefficient and exhausting, but it had a democratic side effect: it required candidates to interact with a broad range of constituents, to make their case to people of ordinary means, to be responsive to a donor base that was, relative to the super PAC era, relatively diverse.

After Citizens United, a candidate who has secured the support of a small number of very wealthy super PAC donors has, in practical terms, outsourced a substantial portion of their campaign financing to people who require no further cultivation. The incentive to engage with small donors diminishes. The incentive to maintain relationships with large donors — to attend the dinners, return the calls, be available for the conversations that wealthy people expect in exchange for their support — increases.

The result, documented by political scientists who study how members of Congress allocate their time, is that elected officials in the super PAC era spend more of their time with wealthy donors and less of their time with constituents of ordinary means than their predecessors did. This is not corruption in the legal sense. It is corruption in the civic sense — the gradual reorientation of elected officials’ attention and responsiveness away from the people they represent and toward the people who finance their representation.

No one designed this outcome. It followed from the incentives. The incentives followed from the rules. The rules followed from the Court.

Dark money scale in 2020 — the visible super PAC portion was only part of a larger system that reshaped campaign finance.

A note on what super PACs are not, because precision matters here.

Super PACs are not the only or even the primary vehicle through which dark money moves in the post-Citizens United landscape. They are the visible portion — the organizations required to disclose their donors and their spending to the FEC. What they have in common with the darker organizations we will examine in Part IX is the fundamental premise: that unlimited money can be deployed to influence elections, and that this deployment is constitutionally protected speech.

The super PAC made that premise operational at scale. The dark money nonprofits made it invisible. Together they constitute the infrastructure of a campaign finance system that bears no meaningful resemblance to what the reformers of 1974 built, or to what the architects of the Constitution had in mind when they wrote the document that the Roberts Court used to dismantle it.

Sidney Hillman collected voluntary contributions from union members. He built a committee. He spent $1.5 million on a presidential election.

The super PACs of 2020 spent $2.7 billion.

The principle is the same. The scale is not.
