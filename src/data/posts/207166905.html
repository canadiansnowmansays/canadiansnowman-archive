I. The Bill Nobody Sent

Alex Reisner, writing in the Atlantic on July 14, 2026, opens with a receipt: hard drives he bought for $350 two years ago now cost $800, when they can be found at all. Laptop prices are up as much as 50 percent. Affordable entry-level computers may, per one forecast, disappear entirely by 2028. The cause isn’t a chip shortage in the traditional sense — it’s that AI companies are reportedly buying up 70 percent of the world’s high-end computer memory to keep pace with systems that, according to Reisner’s reporting, are not merely large but structurally unable to get proportionally cheaper as they grow.

This is not a footnote to the labor story this series told in Part 63. It’s the same story’s other ledger.

II. Quadratic, Not Logarithmic

Every computer-science undergraduate learns the distinction early: a program that handles a bigger workload gracefully scales logarithmically — more input costs proportionally less over time. Large language models, per Reisner’s reporting, do the opposite. They scale quadratically. Give one more words to process and it doesn’t just get slower — it gets slower and hungrier for memory at a compounding rate. Epoch AI’s published cost curves, cited in the piece, show this isn’t a rounding error; it’s the operating shape of the technology.

That distinction matters for this series because Part 63 asked who gets to design the parachute for AI-driven labor disruption, and on what authority. Part 64’s answer is blunter: the same industry positioning itself as capable of engineering a soft landing for the labor market has not, three years into deployment, engineered a system that gets cheaper as it gets bigger. Ilya Sutskever, OpenAI’s former chief scientist, told an interviewer in November that the brute-force approach persists because it’s “a very low-risk way of investing your resources” — an admission, in effect, that scale is being chosen not because it’s efficient but because it’s easy to fund.

III. The Altman Arithmetic

Sam Altman’s own framing, quoted in Reisner’s piece, is worth setting directly against his posture in Part 63. There, Altman was floating public ownership stakes and meeting with Bernie Sanders about a sovereign wealth fund to cushion the labor transition his industry is accelerating. Here, on the engineering side of the ledger, his stated ambition is “maybe with 10 gigawatts of compute, AI can figure out how to cure cancer” — a claim that treats scale itself as the solution to problems scale has not yet solved, made by the same person positioning his company as a responsible steward of the transition it’s causing.

Both postures can be sincere. Both can also be, at the same time, a bet that bigness buys forgiveness — for the labor market and for the balance sheet.

IV. The Silence on the Record

When Reisner asked Anthropic directly whether its “compute multiplier” claims — Dario Amodei’s own term for efficiency breakthroughs — reflected any actual resolution of the quadratic-scaling problem, the company declined to comment on the record. Readers of this series will recognize the shape of that silence. It’s the same shape as Claude Corps: real money, a real fellowship, a real 400 nonprofits — but no independent party gets to verify the premise the whole program rests on, which is that deploying more Claude, faster, is straightforwardly good for the workers Claude Corps is meant to help.

Efficiency and accountability turn out to be the same argument wearing different clothes. A company that won’t show its work on whether the technology gets cheaper to run is not obviously the company that should be trusted to show its work on whether the technology is safe to keep displacing labor around.

V. The Road Not Funded

Reisner’s piece isn’t only a complaint; it names an alternative. Alexia Jolicoeur-Martineau, a Microsoft researcher, won a $50,000 prize for a “tiny recursive model” that solves hard logic problems without the resource footprint of a frontier LLM. Her own words on the industry’s brute-force consensus: “it’s a bit insane.” Her paper goes further, calling the assumption that only massive, corporately funded foundation models can succeed at hard tasks “a trap.”

That’s the missing chapter in every transition-fund press release this series has tracked. Raise Us has half a billion dollars for wage insurance pilots. Claude Corps has $85,000 salaries for a year of nonprofit deployment work. None of that money, as far as the public record shows, is chasing the researchers arguing the underlying technology could be built to need less of everything — less memory, less electricity, less capital, and by extension, less labor market disruption per unit of capability delivered. The industry has decided which problem is worth solving at scale, and it is not the one Reisner is describing.

VI. TACO, Applied to the Arithmetic

Run the industry’s response to Reisner’s reporting through the framework this series has used since Part 1. Threaten doesn’t quite fit here — there’s no overt warning being issued, though the implicit one (”don’t slow us down or you’ll miss the cure for cancer”) runs under Altman’s 10-gigawatts framing. Accommodate is closer to what Raise Us and Claude Corps represent: real concessions, real dollars, aimed at softening a problem the companies concede exists. Capitulate — a genuine change in the underlying practice — is nowhere in evidence; nothing in Reisner’s reporting suggests any major lab is redirecting meaningful funding toward efficiency research like Jolicoeur-Martineau’s, or abandoning the scale-first bet Sutskever described.

That leaves Obfuscate, and it’s the dominant mode. Amodei’s “compute multipliers” are, per Reisner, “described in vague terms,” with no evidence attached that the core problem has been solved. Anthropic’s decision to decline comment on the record, when asked directly whether that term reflects real progress, is obfuscation by omission rather than by statement — arguably the cleanest kind, because it generates no quote to be held against later. The industry is not denying the inefficiency. It is simply declining to quantify it, in the same breath it asks the public to trust its stewardship of a labor transition it has not yet balanced its own books on.

VII. The Gap, Restated Once More

This series has argued for sixty-three parts that the Asimov Gap is what happens when deployment outruns law, and that the companies doing the deploying have appointed themselves the interim government of the gap. Part 63 showed that government funding its own transition apparatus. Part 64 shows the same government has not balanced its own books — the engineering underneath the empire remains, by its own researchers’ account, inefficient in a way with no known precedent in software history. Yann LeCun, one of the field’s own “godfathers,” told the New York Times recently that large language models are “not a path to superintelligence or even human-level intelligence” — a verdict from inside the family, not outside it.

A parachute built on unverified arithmetic is not a parachute. It’s a claim about a parachute, made by the people who also sold you the plane.
