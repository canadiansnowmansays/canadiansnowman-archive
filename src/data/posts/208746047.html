If you had asked any of the merchants, generals, or emperors this series has discussed so far what the Silk Road was fundamentally for, the answer would have been some version of “moving goods, and generating tax revenue on the goods being moved.” None of them would have said “spreading Buddhism into China” or “carrying Nestorian Christianity as far east as the Tang capital” or “seeding Manichaean communities from Persia to the Chinese coast,” and yet a plausible argument can be made that the religious transformation the Silk Road enabled outlasted, and mattered more than, almost any single dynasty or trade good that traveled the same routes. Religions do not require armies to spread, and they do not require anyone’s permission. They only require a route that people are already using for other reasons, and a population willing to listen when a traveling merchant, monk, or refugee happens to mention something new about the nature of suffering, salvation, or the divine.

Buddhism’s journey into China is the largest and best-documented case, and it began modestly, riding along on the same trade networks the Han Dynasty had built for entirely secular reasons. The religion originated in India in the sixth or fifth century BCE, and by the first century CE — the exact date is contested, with traditional Chinese accounts offering a semi-legendary story about an imperial dream prompting a mission to India, and modern historians preferring a more gradual, undramatic diffusion via Central Asian merchant communities — Buddhist ideas and eventually Buddhist monks themselves had begun moving into China along the same Hexi Corridor route this series discussed in Part I. Buddhism’s arrival was, crucially, not a single dramatic conversion event but a centuries-long process of gradual, low-visibility transmission through exactly the kind of intermediary populations that made the Silk Road function commercially in the first place: Central Asian and Sogdian traders, many of them Buddhist themselves by the time they were operating merchant colonies inside China, who brought their religious practices with them the way any diaspora community does, and who gradually attracted Chinese converts through ordinary social contact rather than missionary campaigns.

The oasis kingdoms along the Silk Road’s Central Asian stretch became, in this process, crucial waystations not just for goods but for Buddhist scholarship itself. Khotan, on the southern rim of the Taklamakan, is the clearest case: a wealthy oasis kingdom built on jade, agriculture, and its position on the route, it became one of the most important Buddhist centres in Central Asia by the third or fourth century CE, and Chinese pilgrims travelling toward India routinely stopped there for extended periods to study and to acquire texts. The Chinese monk Faxian, who travelled to India between 399 and 412 CE and left the earliest surviving pilgrim’s account of the journey, spent three months at Khotan and described a kingdom in which Buddhism was so thoroughly established that the population numbered monasteries in the thousands and staged elaborate annual processions with image-carrying carriages moving through the streets. Khotan mattered not merely as a place where Buddhism flourished but as a translation and transmission node: Khotanese Buddhist scholars produced texts in their own Middle Iranian language, and a substantial number of the Sanskrit works that reached China arrived via Khotanese intermediaries rather than directly from India, which meant that Chinese Buddhism absorbed a certain amount of Central Asian interpretation along with the original doctrine.

Dunhuang, further east at the mouth of the Hexi Corridor, performed the same function on an even larger scale, and its cave temple complex — begun in 366 CE and worked on continuously for roughly a millennium — preserves the fullest surviving physical record of what Silk Road Buddhism actually looked like, along with a sealed manuscript cache whose contents document the religious life of the town in extraordinary detail. Dunhuang has an entire installment of this series to itself, so the details can wait; what matters here is the pattern it shares with Khotan. Both were oasis towns whose commercial prosperity funded religious institutions on a scale far out of proportion to their population, because caravan wealth was concentrated, because merchants were reliable donors, and because a monastery in a town everyone had to pass through received a steady stream of both travellers and endowments.

Buddhism’s high-water mark in China arrived under the Tang Dynasty, beginning in the seventh century, when the religion enjoyed sustained imperial patronage, enormous monastery-building programs, and a level of genuine intellectual exchange with Indian Buddhist centers that produced, among other things, one of the more remarkable individual journeys in the entire history of the Silk Road: the monk Xuanzang, who departed China without imperial permission in 629 CE — leaving illegally, in defiance of Tang border restrictions on travel — and spent roughly seventeen years traveling overland to India, studying at the great Buddhist university of Nalanda, and returning in 645 CE with hundreds of Sanskrit texts that he spent the remainder of his life translating into Chinese, a body of translation work so significant that it reshaped Chinese Buddhist scholarship for centuries afterward. Xuanzang’s own account of his journey, along with the substantially embellished sixteenth-century novel Journey to the West it eventually inspired, made him one of the most famous travelers in Chinese cultural memory — a genuine parallel case, worth noting, to Marco Polo’s later fame in Europe: a real journey along established Silk Road infrastructure, later mythologized into something considerably larger and stranger than the actual historical trip.

Buddhism was never the only religion moving along these routes, and Tang China’s relative openness to foreign religious communities, at least until a series of persecutions in the ninth century reversed the trend, meant that Dunhuang’s multi-religious manuscript cache was not an anomaly but a fair representation of Silk Road religious traffic more broadly. Nestorian Christianity — a branch of Christianity centered on a Christological position condemned as heretical by the mainstream church at the Council of Ephesus in 431 CE, and consequently pushed east into Persia and Central Asia by its rivals within the Roman and Byzantine church — established missions and communities stretching remarkably far into China, a presence documented by the famous Nestorian Stele, a stone monument erected in the Tang capital of Chang’an in 781 CE, inscribed in both Chinese and Syriac, describing over a century of Nestorian Christian activity in China by that point and even naming the religion’s founder in China as a Persian monk who had arrived generations earlier. Manichaeism, a dualistic religion founded by the Persian prophet Mani in the third century CE, blending elements of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, and Buddhism into a distinct cosmological system centered on a cosmic struggle between light and darkness, spread along the same routes and found particular institutional success among the Uyghur Turkic khanate, which adopted Manichaeism as a state religion in the eighth century — a genuinely unusual case of a minor, persecuted Persian religious movement becoming the official faith of a major Central Asian steppe empire, entirely as a byproduct of the trade and travel routes that had carried its missionaries there in the first place. Zoroastrianism itself, Persia’s ancient state religion, traveled alongside all of these, carried by Sogdian merchant communities who maintained fire-temple worship in their Chinese trading colonies for generations, a practice documented in some of the same archaeological finds that reveal the ordinary lives of Silk Road traders discussed in the previous installment on Samarkand.

What arrived in China, though, was not what had left India, and the process of transformation is arguably more interesting than the transmission itself. Buddhism reached a civilization with an ancient, sophisticated, and thoroughly established philosophical tradition of its own, and the fit was poor in several fundamental respects. Buddhist monasticism required celibacy and withdrawal from family obligation, which collided directly with Confucian filial duty and the imperative to produce descendants who could maintain ancestral rites; early Chinese critics of Buddhism attacked it precisely on these grounds, characterizing it as a foreign doctrine that dissolved the family. Buddhist cosmology, with its vast cycles of rebirth across innumerable worlds, had no ready Chinese equivalent. And the vocabulary problem was acute: Sanskrit philosophical terminology had no Chinese counterparts, so early translators resorted to geyi, or “matching concepts,” borrowing existing Daoist vocabulary to approximate Buddhist ideas — rendering nirvana with terms carrying Daoist connotations of non-action, for instance — which made the texts comprehensible at the cost of making them, in places, subtly wrong.

Later translators, Xuanzang prominent among them, worked hard to correct this, and the seriousness of the effort is itself notable: an enormous, state-sponsored, centuries-long programme of translation, revision, and philosophical argument conducted to get a foreign religion’s terminology right in Chinese. The eventual result was not imported Indian Buddhism but a set of distinctly Chinese schools, of which Chan — better known in the West by its Japanese name, Zen — is the most consequential export in its own right, having subsequently travelled onward to Korea, Japan, and eventually to a twentieth-century Western audience that generally has no idea it is engaging with the far end of a transmission chain that began with merchants on the Hexi Corridor. Pure Land Buddhism, with its emphasis on devotional practice accessible to laypeople who had neither the leisure nor the literacy for monastic study, spread even more widely within China and remains the most popular form of Buddhist practice in East Asia today. Neither school would have been recognizable to a fifth-century Indian monk.

The reverse case is equally instructive. Buddhism transformed and took root; Zoroastrianism, travelling the same routes at the same time with equally established merchant communities behind it, essentially did not. Sogdian traders maintained fire temples in Chinese cities for generations, and Chinese authorities registered and permitted them — but the religion attracted virtually no Chinese converts and vanished from China entirely once the Sogdian communities themselves assimilated. The difference is not mysterious. Zoroastrianism in this period functioned largely as an ethnic religion, bound up with Persian and Sogdian identity, priestly lineage, and ritual purity requirements that were difficult to detach from a specific community; it made no serious attempt at universal appeal and had no mechanism for incorporating outsiders. Buddhism, by contrast, had been a proselytizing religion from very early on, possessed a monastic institution that could be replicated anywhere, and offered a doctrine that made claims about the universal human condition rather than about the fate of a particular people. A religion travels the trade routes as easily as any other cargo. Whether it takes root at the far end depends on whether it was built to be adopted by strangers.

Judaism traveled these routes as well, in smaller and less institutionally documented numbers than Buddhism, Nestorian Christianity, or Manichaeism, but with a longevity that rivals any of them. A Jewish merchant community, likely arriving via a combination of overland Silk Road routes and maritime trade through India, established itself in the Chinese city of Kaifeng by at least the Song Dynasty period, roughly the tenth or eleventh century CE, and maintained a functioning synagogue, its own Hebrew scriptures, and distinct communal practices for close to a thousand years afterward, gradually assimilating into the broader Chinese population over the following centuries in a process that accelerated markedly after the synagogue was destroyed by flooding in the nineteenth century and never rebuilt. Jesuit missionaries encountering the Kaifeng community in the seventeenth century were reportedly startled to find, deep in the Chinese interior, a group practicing what was recognizably rabbinic Judaism, having maintained the tradition across a journey and a settlement period longer than the distance between the founding of Rome and the present day — a small, quiet case study in just how durable a religious community can prove once it finds a stable niche at the end of a very long trade route, even with no ongoing institutional connection back to its place of origin.

What all of these religious movements share, and what makes them worth treating as a connected phenomenon rather than a series of unrelated coincidences, is that none of them spread primarily through conquest or state-sponsored missionary campaigns, at least not in their initial phase. They spread because merchants, refugees, and itinerant monks were already moving along routes built and maintained for entirely different reasons — tax revenue, military security, the horse trade — and religious ideas travel remarkably efficiently along any infrastructure built to move people and goods, requiring no separate investment of their own. A Sogdian silk merchant setting up a trading colony in a Chinese city brought his Zoroastrian or Buddhist practices with him as a simple extension of his own household life, not as a deliberate missionary project, and Chinese neighbors who found those practices interesting had centuries of ordinary, low-pressure contact in which to gradually absorb them. This is, in miniature, the same lesson this series drew from Zhang Qian’s accidental founding of the entire route: the infrastructure gets built for one purpose, by people solving an entirely different problem, and then everything else — trade goods, technologies, and evidently entire belief systems capable of reshaping empires — travels along afterward, essentially for free, riding on rails nobody laid down with them in mind.

The last of the great religious transmissions along these routes was also the most complete, and it worked somewhat differently from the others. Islam arrived in Central Asia initially by conquest, in the Arab campaigns across Transoxiana in the early eighth century — a subject this series takes up in a later installment — but conquest by itself converts remarkably few people, and the region’s actual Islamization was a slower, more commercial process that took centuries. Merchants converted because trading within the vast Islamic commercial world was easier for a Muslim, with access to shared legal frameworks, credit networks, and communities of coreligionists at every major node from Córdoba to the Indus. Turkic steppe populations converted through contact with Muslim traders and holy men rather than through military defeat, and when a ruler converted — Satuq Bughra Khan of the Kara-Khanids in the tenth century being the pivotal case for the eastern routes — the population generally followed over subsequent generations. By the time the process had run its course, the Buddhist civilization of the Tarim Basin oasis kingdoms, which had flourished for the better part of a thousand years and had served as the conduit through which Buddhism reached China in the first place, had essentially ceased to exist.

That last point deserves to be sat with, because it complicates any tidy narrative about the Silk Road as an engine of pluralism. The routes did carry an extraordinary diversity of belief across an extraordinary distance for an extraordinarily long time. They also carried the religion that eventually displaced most of the others along their central stretch, and they did it by exactly the same mechanism — merchants, contact, gradual adoption — that had carried Buddhism east eight centuries earlier. Transmission has no preferences. The infrastructure that let Buddhism reach Chang’an let Islam reach Kashgar, and the second transmission erased most of the physical and institutional traces of the first across a thousand kilometres of Central Asia. What survives of Silk Road Buddhism survives largely where the sand buried it before anyone could dismantle it, which is a grim way for a religion to be remembered and the only reason we know as much as we do.
