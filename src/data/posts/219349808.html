Sunday Satire. The almanac is invented. The rant, the phone number, the senator and the sunrise times are not.

THE OLD FARMER’S ALMANAC, PRESIDENTIAL EDITION
Calculated for the Meridian of Mar-a-Lago, but Applicable, More or Less, to the Whole Country
Being the Year of Our Lord 2027, and the Second Year of the Second Term
Fitted for the Several States, Excepting Arizona and Hawaii, Which Have Wisely Declined to Participate

Price: one hour, payable twice yearly.

To the Patrons

Dear Reader,

Your editors have published this almanac without interruption since the Washington administration. We have weathered the Panic of 1837, the Spanish flu and the invention of the leaf blower. We did not anticipate having to recalculate every sunrise in the book because of a social media post sent from an aeroplane at a quarter past midnight.

Yet here we are.

At 12:12 a.m. on 2 October 2026, as the Daily Beast reported, the President posted 385 words from Air Force One about the clocks. He wished to make daylight saving time permanent. The House of Representatives had already passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July. The President had urged the Senate to follow on 5 August, as the Indiana Capital Chronicle reported. And now, he wrote, the bill was being held up by Senator Tom Cotton of Arkansas, “for reasons unknown.” He added that the measure enjoyed a 100 per cent approval rating.

Reuters reported that the President, warming to his theme, also shared the senator’s telephone number.

Your editors would observe two things. First, Senator Cotton’s reasons are not unknown. He has stated them repeatedly and in plain English. He believes permanent daylight time would make winter mornings dark and dismal for millions of Americans, and that children would walk to school in the dark. Second, a 100 per cent approval rating is a figure this almanac has never recorded for anything, including sunshine, pie or the month of May.

We have nonetheless done our duty and prepared the tables. They follow.

Yours in perpetual twilight,
The Editors

Sunrise Tables, Winter 2027

Calculated under the President’s proposal. All times local. Lanterns not included.

Editors’ note on Little Rock. In the senator’s own state capital, the sun would rise after eight o’clock in the depths of winter. A child catching a 7:15 bus would make the entire journey to school by starlight. The senator, it appears, has done this arithmetic. The President, it appears, has done the arithmetic on the senator’s phone number instead.

Editors’ note on Toronto. Our Canadian readers will wonder why their city appears in an American table. It is because the Province of Ontario, in a feat of legislative courtesy rare in any age, passed a law in 2020 promising to adopt permanent daylight time on the condition that Quebec and the State of New York do so too. The mornings of fifteen million Canadians therefore depend, in part, on whether a senator from Arkansas answers his phone. Your editors find this a fitting summary of the Canadian condition.

Weather Prognostications for the Coming Season

Your editors have long based their forecasts on a secret formula involving sunspots, tides and the behaviour of woolly bear caterpillars. This year, at the request of the White House, we have switched to a new method based on Truth Social posting times.

October. Unsettled. Storms after midnight, chiefly from the direction of Air Force One. A cold front over Arkansas.

November. Clocks fall back on Sunday 1 November. A general election follows on Tuesday 3 November. Voters will therefore walk to their polling stations in the first dark evenings of winter, which seems, on reflection, appropriate.

December. Long nights. Sharp frosts. A brief thaw in the Senate, followed by a refreeze.

January. Dark. Darker in the morning than you remember. Children at bus stops resembling small, well-wrapped lighthouses.

February. Groundhog Day falls on a Tuesday. The groundhog, emerging at what his body tells him is 7:00 a.m. and the clock insists is 8:00, will see nothing at all. Six more weeks of whatever this is.

Lessons of History

From the Almanac’s archive, for the benefit of those who believe nothing has ever been tried before.

The United States has, in fact, done this. In January 1974, during the oil crisis, President Nixon signed a law placing the country on year-round daylight saving time. It was popular in the autumn when it was proposed. It was much less popular in January, when children went to school in the dark and parents discovered what eight o’clock sunrises feel like. Before the year was out, Congress had voted to put the clocks back, and President Ford signed the repeal.

The Senate itself passed the Sunshine Protection Act by unanimous consent in March 2022. The House let it die. Four years later the House has passed it and the Senate is the obstacle. Your editors note that the bill has now been rejected by both chambers in turn, which in Washington is regarded as bipartisanship.

We further note, without comment, that in December 2024 the President announced his party would work to eliminate daylight saving time, describing it as inconvenient and costly. By the spring of 2025 he was calling the whole question a 50-50 issue. By the autumn of 2026 he wanted daylight saving time made permanent, and was cross with a senator for disagreeing with a position the President himself had held less than two years earlier.

In the Almanac’s experience, only the weather changes its mind this often, and the weather at least has the excuse of the jet stream.

Planting Guide

Plant by the Moon. Your editors continue to recommend the old method.

Plant by the Post. For readers who prefer the new method, the following guidance applies:

  • Sow seeds when the President is asleep. These periods are brief and unpredictable.

  • Transplant seedlings at the moment a senator is publicly accused of holding something up “for reasons unknown.” This is when the soil of public opinion is most thoroughly turned over.

  • Harvest after the midterms. Most crops planted in October will look very different by 4 November.

  • Do not attempt to grow anything in Arkansas in January before 8:17 a.m.

Best Days to Telephone Your Senator

The Almanac has traditionally offered a table of best days to cut hair, wean animals, go fishing and ask for a raise. This year, given recent developments, we have added a new column.

Household Hints

To keep children safe on a dark winter morning, dress them in reflective tape. A child wrapped thoroughly enough will also glow faintly in the headlights of passing debate.

To settle an argument about the time, consult the sun. It has kept accurate time for roughly four and a half billion years and has never once posted about it.

To remove a stubborn grudge from a senior senator, apply flattery liberally and wait. Results vary. In Washington, results may take years.

Old Sayings, Revised for the Season

Red sky at night, shepherd’s delight.
Red sky at midnight, President’s in flight.

Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise.
Early to rise under permanent daylight time makes a man stand at a bus stop in the dark.

The early bird catches the worm.
The late-night post catches the senator.

Closing Remarks from the Editors

Your editors take no position on daylight saving time. We have printed tables under every system Congress has devised, and we expect to print tables under several more before we retire. People of good faith disagree about whether they would rather have light at breakfast or at supper, and there is no shame in either preference.

What we would gently observe is that a question of this kind, which turns on children, farmers, shift workers and the arithmetic of the sky, is precisely the sort of thing that ought to be argued out in daylight. Not at 12:12 a.m. Not by attaching a man’s phone number. Not by announcing that his reasons are unknown when he has published them.

The sun, after all, will rise whenever it rises. It has never once checked with the White House. And it will be up there, punctual as ever, long after everyone in this almanac has been reduced to a footnote in somebody else’s tables.

The Old Farmer’s Almanac, Presidential Edition, will return next year, unless the clocks have changed again, in which case it will return an hour earlier, or later, depending.
