Part V ended on an absence. Moscow and Beijing each published their edition of the Shenzhen joint statement within the hour. Of the Washington edition, Part V could only say: “There is none, at least not yet.”

On Friday, November 20, 2026, the missing edition arrived. It came in two forms: a pair of handout slides passed around the White House briefing room, and the president himself, at the podium, fourteen minutes late.

It was his first formal news conference since July, at the NATO summit in Turkey, as Erica L. Green noted in the live coverage of The New York Times. Foreign trips customarily close with a news conference abroad, and Trump had skipped it in Asia. So the curtain call was taken at home, in a room Green described as jam-packed. Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio flanked the podium. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth sat in the chairs beside it. Stephen Miller, the newly appointed chief of staff, was in the room.

What follows is the Washington edition, read the way Part V read the other two: for what it says, what it leaves out, and who it is really written for.

The programme notes

Aides distributed two slides before the president appeared. The first claimed “$17 billion in deals and investments” from Thailand and the Philippines. The second presented a “declaration toward peace” from the meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, stressing consensus on the role of the United Nations, the International Criminal Court, energy production and the reduction of nuclear risks.

Readers of Part V will recognise the second slide. It is the joint statement with the verbs warmed up. What Moscow and Beijing presented as an agreement to keep talking, Washington repackaged as a declaration.

The trade figures were treated the same way from the podium. Ana Swanson, who covers trade for the Times, noted that the new agreements with Thailand and the Philippines will lower tariffs and encourage American exports, but that the details remained unclear. Trump’s claim to have persuaded the Philippines to buy 100 per cent of its agricultural products and energy from the United States, she wrote, “seems highly unrealistic.” Her colleague Luke Broadwater added that independent analyses have found Trump’s claims about earlier trade deals inflated, at times to nearly double the figures posted on the White House’s own website. At the podium, Trump spoke of deals bringing in tens of trillions of dollars.

The overture was the aeroplane

Trump did not open with the summit. He opened with the plane: the new Air Force One, the Boeing 747-8 that Qatar gave him, and its long nonstop flight. “It’s an amazing plane,” he said. “China was very impressed. And Russia was very impressed. And I’m very impressed.”

Michael Crowley, the Times’ State Department reporter, put it plainly as the monologue rolled on. Back from a major foreign trip and meetings with a string of world leaders, Trump had no clear story to tell about it. What he seemed to prize most was the plane’s performance and the symbolic honour of Xi greeting him in person at the airport.

Before the trilateral had even begun, he told Fox News that the most significant moment was already behind him: “the greeting yesterday, which in the history of China has never happened.” It was, he said, “a big win for us. It’s a big win for China too.” Anne Applebaum, writing in The Atlantic, judged the second half of that sentence the accurate one. Katie Rogers and David E. Sanger, reporting from the city for The New York Times, filled in the rest of the welcome: a military exercise and a troupe of flag-waving dancers in pink, blue and white, Xi’s return of the courtesy Trump had paid him at Joint Base Andrews in September. They also noted what Trump did not do in Shenzhen, the heart of China’s robotics, telecommunications, semiconductor and software industries. He visited none of the Chinese firms that compete most directly with American ones. A president who complained in Shenzhen that the media “couldn’t see some of it” had seen rather little of it himself. The media had been kept well away by design. Simon Shuster, one of nearly 1,000 journalists accredited, described in The Atlantic a vast media centre several miles from the summit, with robot baristas, massage chairs and an endless buffet, and no access to any of the leaders.

What was sung about the bomb

Nuclear weapons were the trilateral’s official subject, and on Friday Trump talked about them at length, in the register of a man musing aloud rather than negotiating. “It’s very, very difficult to say, let’s get rid of our nuclear weapons,” he said. On the steady growth of China’s arsenal, which worries military strategists a great deal: “They’re going at it hot and heavy, but they have a lot.”

Crowley noticed that Trump consistently called nuclear weapons a “deterrent.” For all his bellicosity, Crowley wrote, there is no evidence he has seriously considered using them against Iran. That is reassuring as far as it goes. It is not an arms-control policy. Shuster reported that in a pre-summit call, a senior U.S. official had complained that China needed to “step up to the adults’ table” on nuclear arms. By Friday, the president’s own summary of China’s arsenal was that “they have a lot.”

Rogers and Sanger had already measured the gap. The three leaders had not agreed to return to anything like the arms-control talks that anchored Cold War diplomacy, and Trump had brought no detailed plan for reductions, nor a path to one. China, never a party to any reduction treaty, is building towards Russian and American levels. Xi called nuclear weapons a “sword of Damocles hanging over humanity” that should be eliminated, then, as Beijing always does, left the first step to Washington. On Friday, Trump all but confirmed that nobody intends to take it.

The friend nobody could vouch for

Kaitlan Collins of CNN asked the question that framed the Putin portion of the afternoon. Russia, she noted, is Iran's biggest ally. Did Trump consider Putin a friend to America? "I can't speak to America," the president of the United States replied. He could speak to his own experience, and did. "I've been dealing with Putin during the fake Russia, Russia, Russia hoax, and to be honest, he was very nice about it," he said, adding that the hoax "probably helped me win the election—re-win the election." For ten years, as Jonathan Chait observed in The Atlantic, Trump had insisted that every warm word for Putin was cold calculation on America's behalf. On Friday he said he could not tell whether Putin was good for America, only that Putin had been good to him.

Asked whether Russia had accepted NATO-style security guarantees for Ukraine, as Jared Kushner claimed on Thursday and the Kremlin now disputes, Trump sidestepped. “Look, our side put out things because it’s also a public relations war, and we’ll say things, and they’ll say things,” he said. He could vouch only that Putin “would like to get this settled, and that’s with surety.” Putin, for his part, had told reporters after the trilateral that he would not be negotiating with Ukraine, Rogers and Sanger reported. Green noted that Russia launched huge airstrikes on Ukraine less than 24 hours after the meeting at which Trump said he had urged Putin to end the war by diplomatic means.

The Iran war came up in the same key. Iran wants a deal, Trump said, “but they don’t want to make a deal that’s acceptable to me.” Crowley called that true, and noted that it reflects Tehran’s belief that it holds the upper hand over a president rebuked at the midterms partly because of that war. Part V recorded his pre-summit answer on whether he would ask Beijing or Moscow for help: “We don’t need any help.” Phillips Payson O’Brien, writing in The Atlantic on November 20, set out how much help Iran has had from those same two capitals. By early March, U.S. intelligence assessed that Russia was passing Iran targeting information on American installations, along with drone components and tactical lessons from Ukraine. Press reports soon found Chinese materials, including vital chemicals, feeding Iran’s missile attacks. The strikes on American bases in Kuwait, Bahrain and Saudi Arabia in the opening weeks of the war killed nearly two dozen Americans and wounded more than 800. In July, an Iranian attack broke through the defences of a base in Jordan and killed three more, and O’Brien points to strong indications that Chinese satellite imagery and Russian tactical advice lay behind it.

Trump’s answer has never varied. In March he called a question about Russia helping Iran target Americans “stupid.” In April he said China had agreed not to send Iran weapons, and that Xi would give him “a big, fat hug” when he arrived. When Hegseth told Congress in July that China and Russia were helping Iran, Trump contradicted his own defence secretary, saying both leaders had personally assured him otherwise. Friday’s news conference added nothing to that record. The man Collins asked about remains “my friend” to Trump, and a friend to America that the president would not vouch for. On Thursday, the White House had posted video of the three leaders together under the caption “Peace through strength.”

The director’s notes on Xi

If Putin was the friend nobody could vouch for, Xi was the critic whose praise Trump read aloud. Asked about Jensen Huang, the ousted Nvidia chief executive, Trump said he had raised Huang with Xi. “And he said, ‘Oh, brilliant, brilliant.’ He’s a brilliant man. He is. And I think the company, if they were smart, they’d take that brilliant man, accept his apology and let him go back to work.” Julian E. Barnes noted that Nvidia’s board chair, Harvey C. Jones, had said on Tuesday’s earnings call that the board had no plan to reinstate him.

Consider what that exchange was. The president of the United States sought a character reference for an American executive from the leader of the state whose top civilian spy agency, according to U.S. officials, ran the woman at the centre of the scandal. In Shenzhen, Part V noted, Trump would not answer a Bloomberg reporter who asked whether Huang had been disloyal, and she was barred from the trilateral for asking. Back home, he answered a different question nobody had put to him: Xi thinks Huang is brilliant.

The ground had been prepared before he left. Jonathan Swan, Maggie Haberman, Ana Swanson and Shawn McCreesh reported in The New York Times on November 16 that Trump departed with his cabinet openly split over China. Bessent, Rubio and much of the National Security Council staff wanted broad limits on Chinese access to advanced chips; Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick resisted. The licensing rule for foreign data centres, which Part III covered, went out on the morning Trump left, even as he told reporters: “I don’t think you want to insult China just before you get there.” Part V quoted Xi’s reminder that stable relations depend on “concrete actions to implement the consensus.” Nobody in the briefing room on Friday learned whether the rule had been offered up to that consensus.

The stage abroad and the house at home

Peter Baker, the chief White House correspondent, wrote from Shenzhen for The New York Times that this was the picture Trump had wanted for years: three titans at a round table, just the three of them. Baker set it against what awaited Trump at home, where he will soon share power with ascendant Democrats. Part V quoted the line Trump gave Fox News in Shenzhen about the new Congress, “veto, veto, veto,” and Baker’s note that Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries had yet to be invited to the White House.

On Friday the tone shifted, at least on the surface, and the invitation seemed nearer. “We could end up better than dealing with the Republicans,” Trump said, calling Jeffries and Schumer smart leaders. He reminisced about meeting Schumer in New York decades ago, when Schumer was “running for assembly or whatever.” “And I gave him $500,” Trump said. “That was a big contribution.” He would use his veto, he added, both to block legislation and as leverage in negotiations on issues like health care.

In Shenzhen, Baker wrote, the autocrats got the hugs, arm pats and broad smiles, while at home his administration threatened criminal investigations of Democrats who pursue aggressive oversight. On Friday the Democrats got a hug too, with the veto held behind the president’s back.

Rubio, to his credit, made the strongest case for the trip that anyone made all afternoon. “If these leaders can’t get along and can’t engage with one another and don’t have a positive relationship, it is impossible to get anything good done for the country and the safety of the world,” he said. Crowley called it probably the strongest retort to critics of the summit, delivered in the manner of a classic foreign-policy practitioner. It is a respectable argument. It is also an argument for a process, and Shenzhen produced only the opening scene of one.

Curtain call

The rest of the afternoon wandered a long way from Shenzhen. Past the 25-minute mark, McCreesh wrote, the focus of the news conference was difficult to discern. Trump moved from potholes and asphalt to his late uncle at M.I.T. (”I guess maybe genetically I’m blessed”), from the Federal Reserve to the midterms (”I wasn’t on the ballot. I hope you remember that”). Vance came to the podium after 40 minutes to say he had felt like the child in Home Alone while the president was away. On the digital outages and the slide in the stock market at home, Trump had already delivered his verdict while abroad: “It’s life. When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It’s fine.”

That is the Washington edition. Moscow’s was about civilizational values and a court that must not overreach. Beijing’s was about consensus, Taiwan and concrete actions. Washington’s, a day late, was about an aeroplane that impressed everyone, an airport greeting never given to anyone else, a Chinese leader who thinks a disgraced American chief executive is brilliant, and a friend whose friendship with America the president could not speak to. The summit itself, the nuclear arithmetic, the war in Ukraine, the Chinese and Russian help behind Iranian strikes on American bases, appears in it mainly as things the president was asked about.

Applebaum called Shenzhen an attempt to cosplay Yalta: brief, nearly substance-free and choreographed for the benefit of the hosts. Yalta decided the fate of nations whose leaders were not in the room. Shenzhen decided nothing, and Applebaum found the good news in that: none of the three men any longer has the power to settle Ukraine’s fate over its head.

An edition published a day late, by the one performer who had the most to say and said the least about the performance itself, is not really a libretto. It is a press release about the touring company. The production moves next to Miami, where Trump hosts the G20 in December and holds the director’s chair for the first time in this cycle. There, at least, Washington will have no excuse for leaving the record to its partners. Before then comes an intermission, and the Marx Brothers.
