Of all the erasures in this series, this is the one I find hardest to describe without sounding as though I am making it up, so let me state the mechanism plainly at the top and then show you it is real.

Congress, by law, renamed nine army bases that had honored Confederate generals — men who took up arms against the United States — replacing them with names honoring American soldiers of other kinds. The current administration wanted the old names back. But changing a base’s name back to honor a Confederate would require an act of Congress, which it did not have. So the Defense Department found a workaround of genuine, almost admirable perversity: it restored each old name while attaching it to a different person who happened to share the same surname. Fort Bragg returns — but officially in honor of a World War II paratrooper named Roland Bragg, not the Confederate general Braxton Bragg. Fort Benning returns — but officially for a World War I corporal named Fred Benning, not the Confederate general Henry Benning.

The base gets to wear the Confederate’s name again. The paperwork insists it is honoring somebody else. Everyone understands that the point is to bring back “Bragg” and “Benning,” the names that were removed precisely because of who they honored. And the mechanism by which this is accomplished is the discovery, each time, of an obscure same-named soldier to serve as an official alibi. This is not erasure by deletion. It is erasure by substitution — and it is, as it happens, exactly the operation Winston Smith performed at his desk.

What Winston Actually Did

I keep returning to Winston Smith’s job, because this administration keeps re-enacting its specific tasks, and this one is the most precise match yet.

In 1984, one of Winston’s assignments is to fix a problem: Big Brother had, in an old published speech, praised a Party official named Comrade Withers, who has since been purged and unpersoned. The speech cannot simply be deleted — it existed, it was on the record. So Winston is instructed to rewrite it, replacing the praise of the now-nonexistent Withers with praise of an invented, entirely fictional war hero named Comrade Ogilvy. Ogilvy never lived. But once Winston has written him into the record — a childhood, a spotless Party life, a heroic death — Ogilvy exists, and Withers is gone, and the speech reads seamlessly. “Comrade Ogilvy, unimagined an hour ago, was now a fact.”

Read the base renamings against that and the resemblance is not loose; it is exact. You have a name you wish to restore to honor — but the man it honored is inconvenient, has been, in effect, officially purged by act of Congress. You cannot simply reinstate him. So you locate a substitute who can carry the same name — Roland Bragg, Fred Benning — and you write him into the record as the “real” honoree. The name comes back. The substitute provides the cover. And the man everyone knows the base is actually named for, the Confederate, is honored precisely by the pretense that he is not. Ogilvy, unimagined an hour ago, is now a fact — and Withers, or rather the renaming that unpersoned Withers, is undone.

The difference is only that Winston had to invent his substitute, while the Pentagon’s researchers had to go find theirs. In a way, that is worse. Roland Bragg and Fred Benning were real men who served their country honorably, and they have been conscripted, posthumously, into a shell game — their genuine service used as camouflage for the restoration of names that were removed to stop honoring traitors. The decorated dead have been drafted as alibis.

The Man Who Said the Quiet Part

I do not have to infer the intent behind this, because the Secretary of Defense described it himself, in advance, in blunter terms than any critic could have supplied.

Before taking office, Pete Hegseth had called the replacement names — Fort Liberty, Fort Moore — “garbage.” He said that changing the Confederate names had broken, in his word, a “generational link.” On his first official day at the Pentagon, before any memo was signed, he pointedly referred to the bases by their old names, Bragg and Benning, signaling what was coming. When the restoration came, he announced it with satisfaction: “Bragg is back.” He posted a photograph of the restored Fort Bragg sign with a single word: “Always.”

Always. Consider what that word is doing. The base was named Fort Bragg for roughly a century, in honor of a Confederate; renamed Fort Liberty in 2023; and restored to Bragg in 2025. “Always” is not a description of that history — that history contains a conspicuous interruption, a period during which the base honored the abstract value of liberty rather than a slaveholding general. “Always” is an erasure of the interruption. It insists that the name was never truly gone, that the two years as Fort Liberty were an aberration to be forgotten, a mistake now corrected and best treated as if it had never occurred. The generational link, Hegseth said, had been broken; “always” is the claim that it has now been repaired so completely that the break itself should vanish from memory. This is the Smithsonian’s “restored to 2008” in four fewer letters. Restore the name, and erase the years in which it was questioned.

And note the word link — the generational link. It is the honest word, and it gives the game away. The value of the old name, to those restoring it, is precisely its continuity with the century in which the base honored Braxton Bragg. The renaming to Fort Liberty broke that link on purpose, because the link ran back to the Confederacy. To call its restoration the repair of a “generational link” is to admit that what is being restored is the connection to the original, Confederate namesake — the very connection the substitute honoree is officially there to deny. Fred Benning, Distinguished Service Cross, World War I, is the fig leaf. The generational link is the body.

The Family That Objected

There is a human cost to this that is easy to miss in the shell game, and one family named it out loud.

Fort Benning had been renamed, in 2023, Fort Moore — in honor of Lieutenant General Hal Moore, the hero of the Battle of Ia Drang, and his wife Julia Moore, who transformed the way the Army notifies the families of soldiers killed in action. It was, unusually, a base named for a couple, and for a particular kind of service: his in combat, hers in caring for the bereaved. When the name was stripped back to Benning, one of the Moores’ sons wrote publicly against it. The administration allowed that the Moores’ legacy might be honored in some other, lesser manner — a plaque, a gesture, “in a manner that celebrates their significant contributions to the local community.” But the base itself, the honor that mattered, was taken from them and returned to a Confederate’s surname, held in trust by a World War I corporal who had nothing to do with any of it.

That is the tell of an erasure, as opposed to a mere renaming: someone is un-honored in the process, and it is done quietly, with the offer of a smaller consolation elsewhere. Julia Moore’s innovation in how the Army treats grieving families is exactly the kind of memory a country should want carved onto something permanent. It was, for two years. Then it was traded back for a name whose entire value, by the Secretary’s own account, is its “generational link” to the era before.

Why a Name Is the Purest Case

I write a separate series, The Name Game, about this administration’s compulsion to write the president’s own name onto the surfaces of the republic. That series watches what gets added. This one watches what gets erased — and the base renamings sit precisely on the seam, which is why they belong here rather than there. Because a name, more than almost anything, is memory in its most compressed form. A name on a building is a decision, renewed every day it stands, about who a community agrees to remember and honor. To change it is not to relabel a box. It is to change the answer to the question whom do we choose to keep?

When Congress removed the Confederate names, it was making a deliberate act of memory: these men waged war against the United States to preserve slavery, and we will no longer carve their names over the gates where American soldiers train. When the administration brought the names back through the surname dodge, it reversed that act of memory while pretending not to — restoring the honor to the Confederate by the transparent device of formally assigning it to someone else. The “someone else” is the whole trick. It is what lets everyone involved deny that any Confederate is being honored while the sign, the letterhead, the century-old association, and the Secretary’s own “always” all say otherwise.

Orwell understood that the most durable lie is the one built into a name, because a name is repeated a thousand times a day without examination. Every soldier who mails a letter from Fort Bragg, every family that drives through the gate, re-inscribes the name a little deeper, and the substitute honoree fades further into the fine print until only the surname remains — Bragg, Benning — carrying, as it always did, its original freight. Unimagined an hour ago, Roland Bragg is now a fact. And Braxton Bragg, whom the base is once again functionally named for, has been restored to honor by the one method Congress had tried to make impossible.

The generational link, the Secretary assures us, is repaired. He is right. That is exactly the problem.

This is Part 7 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory. Where the sister series The Name Game watches what this administration writes onto the republic, this essay sits on the seam — a case where restoring an old name is itself the erasure, undoing a deliberate act of national memory through a surname loophole that honors a Confederate by formally pretending to honor someone else. It is Winston Smith’s Comrade Ogilvy, rebuilt from real men’s names. The next essay turns to the library shelves being quietly emptied.
