Early in 2026, the man in charge of the agency that supports America’s most important preventive-care panel had an idea for saving time. Dr. Roger D. Klein, a molecular pathologist and lawyer whom the Trump administration had put at the head of the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, wondered aloud why the research centres that review the evidence for the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force were needed at all. His assistant, he said, could do that work with artificial intelligence. Two officials heard him say it, as Maggie Astor reported for The New York Times on November 18, 2026. One of them was Tess Miller, then the agency’s acting scientific director for the task force.

The evidence reviews are the task force. Its sixteen volunteer members grade what years of systematic review by research teams show. Hand that work to a political appointee’s assistant and a chatbot and you have not streamlined the task force. You have abolished it and kept the name.

As it happens, nobody needed the chatbot. By then Robert F. Kennedy Jr. had not let the task force meet for nearly a year, and it would not sit down to do its actual work again until November 19, 2026, twenty months after its last working meeting. Fifteen former members and agency employees have described how it came to this: a public-health institution taken apart without a single dramatic act, just eighteen months of unsigned forms.

The panel nobody sees

Most Americans have never heard of the task force, and most have benefited from it. Set up in 1984 to judge screenings, counselling and medicines that catch disease early in people who feel fine, it grades services, and under the Affordable Care Act most insurance plans must cover anything graded “A” or “B” at no cost. A colonoscopy without a bill, a pregnant woman screened for high blood pressure, a smoker offered medication to quit: that is the task force.

In a normal year it meets in person three times and publishes around a dozen recommendations. It has published none since June 2025. Guidance on at least eighteen topics has stalled, including cardiovascular disease, HIV screening and food allergies, which means doctors and patients are working from advice that grows older by the month.

The ruling that saved it, and handed over the keys

The irony of the task force’s collapse is that it began with a court victory for preventive care.

On June 27, 2025, the Supreme Court decided Kennedy v. Braidwood Management. A group of Texas employers had argued that the task force’s members were wielding federal power without being properly appointed, and that its coverage mandates were therefore unconstitutional. Had they won, free preventive care for 150 million insured Americans would have been thrown into doubt. By a vote of six to three, the court disagreed. The members were constitutional, it held, because they were “inferior officers” answerable to the health secretary, who could remove them at will and review their recommendations before they took effect.

Former members say it was the turning point. The ruling saved the mandate by confirming that the secretary was in charge, and the secretary was Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

Agency employees were told that a political appointee had to approve every public action the task force took: research plans, drafts posted for comment, final recommendations. The documents went up the chain. According to Dr. Justin Mills, then a medical officer supporting the task force, Dr. Miller and several others, they never came back down signed.

The Department of Health and Human Services has said that work was paused until it understood how best to proceed after the ruling. Seventeen months is a long time to read a judgment.

Maybe now

The panel did not stop working. It stopped being allowed to finish. Members kept meeting virtually and even wrote their annual report to Congress, but without an in-person meeting they could not vote, and without a signature they could not publish or send anything.

The clearest casualty was cervical cancer screening. In 2025 the task force finalised a recommendation endorsing self-collection, a test a woman can take herself, as an alternative to the Pap smear, exactly the kind of change that brings in women who put off screening. Agency staff told the journal due to publish it to hold off, again and again, because the political sign-off never came. Dr. Miller said they moved the date more times than she could count. Another agency within the department eventually issued similar guidance. The task force’s own version has still not been published.

In December 2025, Dr. Michael Silverstein, then the task force’s chair, wrote to Dr. Klein that the cervical cancer guidance was ready, and asked for permission to meet and vote and for departing members’ terms to be extended. He followed up in January. He received no reply to either message, Astor reported in The New York Times on May 20, 2026. A legally mandated report to Congress missed its deadline.

The man with the pen

Dr. Klein was appointed in June 2025, the same month as the ruling. Task force leaders and staff describe an administrator who mostly did not answer, neither the chair’s emails nor the employees pressing him to clear the backlog.

When he did engage, it was to argue with the science. He called a draft recommendation on preventing alcohol misuse “worthless,” according to Dr. Miller. Dr. Mills and another employee recall him asking for references to race to be cut from other documents. In written comments on a draft about risk assessment for cancers linked to the BRCA genes, which Dr. Silverstein shared with The Times, Dr. Klein disputed the task force’s conclusions and appeared to confuse genetic-testing guidance for people who already have breast cancer with guidance for people who do not. Dr. Silverstein said the comments showed no grasp of how the task force weighs a service’s benefits against its harms.

The department’s replies are the best case for Dr. Klein. A spokeswoman said he did not recall the alcohol conversation “as described,” but had thought resources might be better spent on other topics. On race, she said, he had wanted precise descriptions of patient populations and of why the findings mattered clinically. She also said that secretarial oversight of the task force predates this administration.

That last point is technically true and practically misleading. Former members and staff say that before 2025, interference by political appointees was unheard of. Oversight meant administration, not an appointee editing conclusions. This series has a name for answering a specific charge with a general truth. In the TACO framework (Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate) it is the fourth letter.

“Prematurely” appointed

In July 2025, Kennedy’s office called off the panel’s in-person meeting at the last minute, the first of four cancellations in a row. On July 25, The Wall Street Journal reported that Kennedy planned to oust the panel’s members altogether, as he had purged the vaccine advisers of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices the month before. Dr. Silverstein said the threat changed the work: what if the panel read the data in a way that made the administration angry?

The purge, when it came, was a slow one. Terms expired in December 2025 and March 2026 and nobody was appointed to replace the departing members. By late March the panel was down from sixteen to ten. Meanwhile DOGE cuts and frustrated resignations emptied the agency behind it. The medical officers who reviewed evidence and drafted recommendations are all gone, and a panel of part-time volunteers cannot function without them.

Then, in May 2026, the two remaining leaders, Dr. John B. Wong of Tufts and Dr. Esa M. Davis of the University of Maryland, were invited to meet Dr. Klein for the first time, expecting to discuss what the task force needed. Instead they found Dr. Klein sitting with Benjamin Robles, the department’s deputy general counsel, who fired them. Kennedy’s letters, dated May 11, said the firings were not about performance but about avoiding uncertainty over the validity of future task force actions. At the meeting, Dr. Wong said, they were told that they had been “prematurely” appointed under the Biden administration, and that a Trump appointee should have named the chairs.

Kennedy’s letters invited both doctors to reapply. Both did, were interviewed and were rejected. In Dr. Wong’s interview, he said, Dr. Klein pressed him to defend evidence-based medicine itself, describing it as “socialized medicine” designed to control costs. Dr. Mills was in the room and confirmed the account. The department says Dr. Klein does not believe evidence-based medicine is socialised medicine, and that his point was that rigid application of its techniques, which are often biased against taking action, was embraced early by socialised systems.

As a Canadian, I can only note that being biased against taking action is the whole point. Evidence-based medicine exists because doctors once took plenty of confident action that turned out to hurt people.

Dr. Silverstein, speaking in May, said the administration was doing to the task force what it had done to the vaccine committee, only in slow motion.

The new members

On September 17, 2026, Kennedy appointed eight new members, as this series noted in “Friends in All the Wrong Places.” How they were chosen came out only because a newspaper went to court.

Selection used to belong to the task force’s scientific director, a career employee, working with the panel’s chairs. This time Dr. Klein and other political appointees ran it. A Federal Register notice promised that nomination materials would be publicly available. Agency officials refused to show them to a Times reporter, and The Times sued. It obtained the names of 247 nominees and of the 40 who were interviewed.

Former task force leaders who reviewed the list for The Times said Kennedy’s picks did not meet the usual standard of expertise in primary care and research. Most are specialists. Only two work in primary care, and neither has the depth of experience in systematic evidence review that was typical of past members. The new chair, Dr. Seth J. Corey, is a paediatric haematologist-oncologist whose publications show little experience with systematic reviews. Dr. Klein says the appointees were chosen for expertise, experience and diversity of settings and geography, and that Dr. Corey’s master’s degree in public health gives him formal training in evidence analysis.

One of sixteen

Then there is the money.

Four of the eight new members own stock in companies that make drugs and devices directly affected by the task force’s decisions, The New York Times reported on November 5, 2026, based on conflict-of-interest waivers it obtained through a public records request. They are Dr. Patrick K. Hunter, a paediatrician; Dr. Ronald P. Karlsberg and Dr. Venkatesh L. Murthy, cardiovascular specialists; and Dr. Dennis W. Wulfeck, a radiologist. All four hold shares in Eli Lilly and Johnson & Johnson. Between them they also hold Merck, Pfizer, Novo Nordisk, Cigna, CVS Health and dozens more.

Historically, said Dr. Quyen Ngo-Metzger, who assessed conflicts of interest as the task force’s scientific director from 2012 to 2019, any candidate with holdings like these would have been asked to sell. Anyone who refused would not have been appointed. Dr. Silverstein said the panel used to take conflicts as small as $1,000 seriously.

Dr. Klein signed off on the waivers anyway. His reasoning, set out in the documents, was that each member is only one of sixteen and has no power to decide anything on behalf of the United States. He noted that the medical companies made up no more than 15 per cent of Dr. Karlsberg’s portfolio, 20 per cent of Dr. Murthy’s and Dr. Wulfeck’s, and 45 per cent of Dr. Hunter’s.

Forty-five per cent. Dr. Silverstein said the percentage thresholds were something the task force had never even considered, and that the one-of-sixteen argument did not pass the smell test in a body that works by discussion, persuasion and a vote. That is the right instinct. A deliberative panel is precisely the place where one confident voice can move a room.

The department says the members are now “special government employees,” a status the ruling created, for whom a properly issued waiver is a lawful remedy. It promised a recusal process without setting out in public how it would work. Dr. Silverstein’s point is that a company the size of Johnson & Johnson makes something relevant to almost every topic the task force will take up.

The bill comes later

The damage will not arrive as a headline. Karina Davidson, a former chair, told The Times that nobody will be able to point to a single moment when it went wrong. It will show up years from now, she said, in later-stage diagnoses, in missed interventions and in preventable deaths, when it is too late to undo.

That is the particular cruelty of attacking prevention. A guideline two years late kills no one this week. It means a woman never hears about a self-collection kit, and a cancer is found at stage three instead of stage one: real, deferred and invisible damage, ideal for a department that prefers not to be held to account.

Thursday

The panel finally met on Thursday, November 19, and Friday, November 20, in person for the first time since March 2025. Dr. Klein had sworn in all sixteen members at the agency’s offices on September 30, after a two-day orientation for the newcomers, according to the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, but this was the first time they sat down to do the work. It was also the first time the public could watch. Dr. Klein presented that as a decision by Kennedy and President Trump to bring transparency to the panel, as Lauren Clason reported for Bloomberg Law on November 19, 2026.

Transparency is an odd boast from a department that kept the panel’s work in a drawer for a year and a half. The first day’s agenda was an inventory of that drawer: presentations to ratify final recommendations on cervical cancer screening, the self-collection guidance the journal had been asked to hold again and again; on unhealthy alcohol use, the subject of the draft Dr. Klein had dismissed as “worthless”; and on perinatal depression. Then came an evidence presentation on diet, exercise and weight loss to prevent heart disease. That was the topic the panel had been due to discuss on July 10, 2025, before Kennedy’s office called that meeting off at the last minute, as Axios reported at the time. Friday turned to kidney disease, colorectal cancer and lung cancer screening.

Even the work that survived the drawer has aged in it. Carrie Patnode, a behavioural epidemiologist at Kaiser Permanente, presented her team’s evidence review on lifestyle changes and heart disease and noted that it had been finished last year and left out everything published since. Many of the stalled reviews will be just as out of date.

It soon became clear what kind of panel Kennedy had built. Some of the new members did not seem to know how the task force works. Dr. Louis Wilson asked what its work groups normally did in the virtual meetings they hold between the three in-person sessions each year, as Maggie Astor reported for The New York Times on November 20, 2026. In the discussion of screening teenagers for unhealthy drinking, Dr. Karlsberg and Dr. Corey, the new chair, made comments suggesting they thought the question was whether teen drinking was healthy, rather than whether there was enough evidence that screening for it does any good. The eight original members and the eight new ones argued openly over whether to keep weighing evidence the way the task force always has. And on Friday morning, in a break with past practice, the panel listened to presentations from outside experts with financial conflicts of interest.

The conflicts reached the table too. Bloomberg Law counted five new members and one earlier appointee who have disclosed conflicts, including medical industry stocks, and reported unexpected changes to the way recommendations are made. Three of the new members, all among those granted waivers to keep their drug stocks, had to recuse themselves from the cervical cancer discussion. Dr. Silverstein, who chaired the panel until March, could recall a recusal only twice in about a decade, and then for a narrow conflict on a single topic. Three on one topic, he told The Times, point to a body of members “for whom conflicts of interest are going to be a very big problem.” Every recusal is the system working as designed. Every recusal is also one fewer expert voice on the question at hand. That is the trade Dr. Klein’s waivers made.

The tensest moment came over Dr. Klein’s own pen. He had asked for edits to the cervical cancer recommendation, among them the removal of its references to racial disparities in the disease, the same kind of cut agency staff had heard him ask for before. Dr. Sei Lee, a member appointed before Kennedy took office, said he wanted to “call out the elephant in the room.” Edits from political leadership to the top line, he warned, could make it look as though the task force’s recommendations were no longer scientifically independent.

The panel went into private session to vote on the three recommendations. Its press office said the tallies would be made public but did not say when. According to a person who was in the room, who spoke to The Times on condition of anonymity, Dr. Klein heatedly defended cutting the racial-disparity language, and the task force approved the cervical cancer recommendation with its original wording anyway. It is a small victory, and it may not hold. Under the Braidwood ruling, Kennedy can overrule anything the panel decides.

Earlier in November, seven former chairs asked congressional committees to subpoena Kennedy and Dr. Klein, and Senator Angus King and Representative Maxine Dexter have proposed a bill writing the old membership standards and the panel’s independence into law. With Democrats taking both chambers in January, it has a better chance than it had before the November 3 elections.

Another problem is already on the calendar. Three members are due to leave at the end of the year. Replacements are normally named months in advance. None has been.

The task force has met, with a quorum, a chair and an agenda. Whether it is still the institution that told Americans, on the evidence alone, which tests were worth their time is a question two days of meetings could not answer. The answer will come slowly, in the recommendations it publishes and the ones it doesn’t, and in who gains from each.

Slow motion, after all, is still motion.
