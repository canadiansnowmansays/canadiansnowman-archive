👤 Personal — the year the count reaches its own counter. Warm, mostly, with a wink or two, because I was not present for any of it in a capacity I can recall, and the family records are what they are. Dior appears, for scale.

I count my essays, and for nineteen hundred and fifty of them the year on the door has belonged to somebody else: a conqueror, a clerk, a queen, a great-uncle I rounded up out of love. Essay 1951 is the first one that belongs to me. I was born in it. On the fifth of October, in London, Ontario, the count that would one day keep this series acquired the person who would keep it.

I am drafting this in the week I turn seventy-five, which is the kind of coincidence this series exists to notice and is not permitted to take credit for. Let me tell you what the records say about my arrival, and — more interestingly — what they do not.

The census I missed

Begin with the count I was not in.

On the first of June, 1951, Canada took its ninth national census. It was a landmark count: the first ever to include Newfoundland, which had joined Confederation only two years before, which makes it the first census of the country as I have always known it — ten provinces, coast to coast to coast, the whole of the addition begun at essay 1,867 finally summed. The enumerators went door to door and counted fourteen million and nine thousand, four hundred and twenty-nine people.

I was not one of them. I arrived on the fifth of October — four months after the enumerators had folded their forms and gone home. And because the country did not count itself again nationally until 1956, I spent the first four years and eight months of my life entirely outside the official ledger. As far as the Dominion Bureau of Statistics was concerned, I did not exist until I was nearly five years old.

There is a further joke in this, and I have saved it for the end of the paragraph because the family would want it there. I was born a month premature. I was, in other words, early — the only time in my life, my brothers would tell you, that I arrived anywhere ahead of schedule — and I was still four months too late for the count. A man who has written nineteen hundred essays about who gets counted and who gets left out turns out to have begun his own life as a rounding error in the national tally. I cannot say I mind. It explains a good deal.

Measured in sinks

What do I know about my own beginning? Less than I know about the transit of Venus.

I know the city: London, Ontario. I know the hospital — St. Joseph’s, in the north end of town — because it was the same one for all four of us: my three brothers and me, four boys delivered under one roof, which may be the single most consistent statistic my family ever produced. I was number two of the four. In a series that has spent nineteen hundred essays on who gets counted first, I can report that I was born a month ahead of schedule and still came second. The ordinal, at least, was never in dispute. I know I came a month early. And I know, because it was told to me more than once, that I was so small I could be bathed in the bathroom sink.

That is very nearly the whole record.

Here is what strikes me, as a man who has spent a retirement auditing other people’s numbers. I do not know my birth weight. It was surely written down — a nurse weighed me, a figure went on a card, the card went somewhere. It was the first number anyone ever recorded about me, and it is the one number about me I do not have. The family did not keep the figure. The family kept the sink.

And I think the family was right. “Five pounds, some ounces” would tell you almost nothing; it is a figure, and figures, as this series has been arguing since 1066, are easy to state and hard to feel. “Small enough to bathe in the bathroom sink” is a unit of measure a person can hold in their hands. It tells you the size of the baby and the size of the worry and the size of the house, all at once. It is the same choice the chroniclers made at essay 1,337, and the same choice I made with Hugh at 1,898 — the true figure set aside in favour of a measure that can be carried. The weight is lost. The sink is not.

Nine days old, and a princess on York Street

Now the coincidence I did not know about until I went looking for this essay, and it has rearranged the whole series in my head.

On the fourteenth of October, 1951 — nine days after I was born — Princess Elizabeth and Prince Philip came to London, Ontario. It was her first visit to Canada, a five-week tour by air and rail and motorcade that had begun in Montreal on the eighth. In London, a crowd of some twenty thousand people gathered outside the Canadian National station on York Street to see the royal train. She was twenty-five. Within four months her father would be dead and she would be Queen.

Somewhere across town, I was nine days old and the size of a sink. I cannot tell you whether I was still at St. Joseph’s — premature babies often stayed on, in those years — or whether my mother had me home. I can tell you I was not on York Street. London counted twenty thousand people at the station that day. It did not count me. Nobody was counting me yet, except my mother.

But consider the lattice this series has been building. At essay 1897 I wrote that I have lived my whole life between the only two sixty-year reigns in British history. At essay 1898 I told you my mother’s favourite story — that Hugh, who helped prepare the royal tour of 1939, sat years later beside the woman who had been the girl left at home, and told her every detail of her parents’ journey. And now it turns out that the second of those two queens, on her very first visit to the country she would reign over for seventy years, came to my hometown in the second week of my life. She was counted by twenty thousand. I was counted by no one. Both of us were, as it happens, at the beginning of a very long count.

The year counting was handed to the machines

There is one more 1951 I need to enter, because it belongs to the series as much as to me.

On the thirty-first of March, 1951, the United States Census Bureau formally accepted delivery of a machine called UNIVAC — the first electronic computer built and sold as a commercial product in America. The first customer for the first commercial computer was a census. The most ancient job in this whole series — counting the people — was the first job the new machines were bought to do. In Manchester that same winter, the Ferranti company delivered the first computer sold in Britain, and Alan Turing, whose imaginary clerk with a paper tape I wrote about at essay 1,936, was helping write the manual for programming it.

So I was born, four months late for my own country’s census, in the very year counting itself was handed from clerks to machines. And seventy-five years later, I keep this series in open collaboration with a direct descendant of those machines. The counter who was not counted grew up to count with the counters’ grandchildren. I don’t know what to make of that, except that the series appears to have been planning it longer than I have.

Dior, for scale

The husky, for the record, would not fit in the bathroom sink. She would not fit in the bathtub, if consulted, and on her more expansive evenings she does not entirely fit in the bathroom. I mention this only so the reader can calibrate. The family’s unit of measure, applied to the current household, produces the following ledger: one man, formerly sink-sized; one dog, approximately one bathroom. We have both grown into our units. She, unlike me, was counted from the start — registered, licensed, vaccinated, microchipped, a number on a tag at her throat within weeks of her arrival. The dog has never once been missing from an official count. I find this mildly humbling.

The name

And now the ending, which my mother wrote for me seventy-five years ago, though neither of us knew it.

My full name is Stephen Gordon Lawton. When I began this project in retirement, I chose to publish as Stephen Gordon — my first name and my middle name — and dropped my surname, for reasons that seemed practical at the time. The Gordon is my mother’s father: Gordon Keenleyside, born in 1882, who died in 1951, the year I was born. He was the grandson of Anthony Keenleyside through that first, brief marriage that ended when Hannah Place died bringing a son named James into the world. Hugh, born in 1898, was Anthony’s grandson through the second marriage. Gordon and Hugh were half-first-cousins: two grandsons of one man, by two different wives.

Which means this. Every essay in this series — all nineteen hundred and fifty-one of them so far, the medieval ones and the comic ones and the ones I could not make funny — has carried at its very top, beneath the title, the name of the man who links me to Hugh. I did not plan it. I did not notice it until essay 1898, when I was drawing the family tree in pencil and saw the name I sign every morning sitting on the branch between the two of us. My byline has been a genealogy the whole time.

So here is the honest ledger of my own year. The census missed me. The hospital weighed me and the family lost the figure. The princess came to town and I wasn’t in the crowd. But my mother, who counted me before anyone else did, gave me her father’s name in the year she lost him — and I have been signing it to the count ever since.

I was not counted in 1951. I was named.

The closer

The count moves on, and it moves only two years, to a spring in which the princess from York Street was crowned — on the second of June, 1953, in the first coronation the world could watch on television. That same spring, two young men in Cambridge announced that they had worked out the structure of the molecule that carries the instructions for every living thing: a code written in four letters, the alphabet that had counted me into existence, sink-sized, in London, Ontario. The next landmark is essay 1,953, and it is the year the code of life was read — and the year the scientist whose photograph made the reading possible was left out of the count.

See you at 1953, where the code is cracked and the woman who held the key is filed under someone else’s name.
