One morning in October, in an underground bunker in eastern Ukraine, the room got loud. Soldiers called across one another over banks of screens showing live video from the battlefield, until their commander, Yuriy Horobets, cut through it. “Guys, let’s fight the war first and deal with our stuff later,” he shouted. Then he pointed at a tiny pale shape moving across one of the feeds. “Our FPV is coming in now.”

Horobets is 45, a battalion commander in the 66th Mechanized Brigade, and like many of his infantry he is closer to fifty than to twenty. A year ago his brigade was losing ground. Its supply routes were under fire, and it could neither bring in ammunition nor get the wounded out. This autumn it was storming tree lines, as Carlotta Gall and Kamila Hrabchuk reported for The New York Times after two weeks embedded with the units involved.

In Abu Dhabi on Wednesday, November 11, Vladimir Medinsky, the man Vladimir Putin sent to lead Russia’s delegation, spelled out what Moscow still wants. “Every inch of our land,” he said, “will be returned to Russia.”

Those two scenes belong in the same essay. Russia is demanding at the table the very land it is losing on the battlefield.

Vivaldi

The operation had a composer’s name and, for months, no publicity at all. Operation Vivaldi was run by Brigadier General Andriy Biletsky, who took over Ukraine’s Third Army Corps in the summer of 2025. Its main thrust began in May. In October, Biletsky gathered journalists in an underground bunker to announce that it was over and to tally the results.

He claimed his brigades had wiped out Russia’s 144th Motor Rifle Division and reversed a whole year of Russian gains. More to the point, Vivaldi stopped Russia from encircling the last cities of Donetsk Province, the cities Moscow says it must have before the war can end. “What we are seeing now looks more like desperate attempts to establish a defensive line,” Biletsky said of the Russians, “an agony, rather than organised, systematic military operations.”

Commanders say such things. What makes this claim harder to dismiss is who agrees with it. Western analysts and Russian military bloggers alike confirmed that Russia lost a key salient north of Lyman, and an equal stretch of ground on the Zaporizhzhia front. According to the Institute for the Study of War, October was the second month in a row in which Ukraine gained more territory than Russia. It was also the month in which Russian forces suffered their worst losses of the war: more than 54,000 killed or seriously wounded.

How the 66th Brigade turned its fortunes around is a story of small decisions taken by people fed up with dying. Several battalion commanders, tired of being pounded from a hill above a village, took the high ground on their own initiative and knocked out the Russian communications there. The Russians below could no longer see. “At that point, we had superiority over the enemy in terms of both strike drones and aerial reconnaissance,” said Nazar Kinash, 25, an acting battalion commander. The Russians “had effectively been blinded.”

The brigade infiltrated the village and set ambushes. It took the village within a month, and a second one six weeks later. Biletsky’s reforms made the rest possible. Decisions moved down to the commanders on the ground. Six brigades began working in sync for the first time, and they closed the gaps between units through which Russian infantry had been slipping for two years.

Then came the drones that reach behind the lines. The Third Army Corps was striking up to 50 Russian vehicles a day, some as far as 100 miles behind the front. “It became normal for the enemy to unload 40 to 50 kilometres from the front line and then continue on foot,” Biletsky said. Some Russian soldiers carried a single artillery shell twenty miles. Some held up cardboard signs outside their positions saying they were hungry and thirsty.

On the Ukrainian side, the robots kept coming. Remote-controlled ground vehicles trundled through the kill zone carrying food, ammunition, batteries and sometimes the dead. Many broke down or were hit, but enough got through that Biletsky’s brigades were delivering three times as much cargo to the front as brigades that had only recently come under his command. A 27-year-old former assault trooper who goes by the call sign Makar now runs a battalion of armed robots. Ukraine cannot outnumber the Russians, he said. “But we can outperform them technologically, in terms of quality and scale, if we consistently stay several steps ahead of them.”

Part VII of this series described the kill zone as the thing that made this war a grinding stalemate. Vivaldi is what it looks like when one side learns to use the kill zone against the other.

The table

While the 66th Brigade hunted stragglers in the tree lines, Washington was preparing to talk.

On Thursday, November 5, Melania Trump hosted Kirill Dmitriev, Putin’s envoy and the head of Russia’s sovereign wealth fund, at the White House, along with Ukrainian officials, to mark the Oleshky truce she had helped to broker. Afterwards, Donald Trump told reporters in the Oval Office that he had met “the head person of Russia”. He had met only the Russian, not the Ukrainians, as Anton Troianovski reported for the Times.

“I can’t tell you what I discussed,” Trump said, “but I’m just telling you, there’s going to be an agreement, it’s going to be a big agreement, and it’s going to involve more than Ukraine. It might involve all of Europe. It’s going to be a big, beautiful agreement, and everyone is going to be thrilled.” Asked about Russia’s attacks on Ukrainian civilians, he answered, “The other way, they kill, too.”

That was three days after Russian glide bombs killed more than forty people collecting food at a school in Komyshuvakha.

The phrase “all of Europe” was not loose talk. Jared Kushner said the same day that American officials had discussed “security guarantees for Ukraine and a broader European security architecture” with Europeans and Ukrainians in Brussels. The idea, as Andrew E. Kramer explained for the Times on November 9, was to widen the frame. Talks had stalled in February over Russia’s demand that Ukraine hand over about 2,000 square miles of Donetsk, roughly the fifth of the province that the Russian army had failed to take, and then the war with Iran had swallowed Washington’s attention. Perhaps, the thinking went, Putin would soften on the land if he were offered a seat at the table where Europe’s future security was being drawn up.

Not everyone in Kyiv saw that as a way out. “Putin’s goal is to hold a new Yalta,” said Volodymyr Ariev, an opposition member of parliament, referring to the 1945 conference at which the victors carved Europe into spheres of influence. The EU’s foreign-policy chief, Kaja Kallas, said that “any agreement would require E.U. participation to be implemented.” The Kremlin said no Europeans would be in the room.

A ceasefire was not on the agenda. Ukraine’s offer of an immediate, unconditional ceasefire along the current front line, first made in March 2025, still stands. Russia has never accepted it. The night before the delegations flew to Abu Dhabi, Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles at Ukraine and knocked out power for about 600,000 people.

Abu Dhabi

The talks ran for about four and a half hours on Tuesday, November 10, and about two hours on Wednesday. Rustem Umerov led Ukraine’s delegation, with David Arakhamia. Medinsky led Russia’s. Steve Witkoff and Kushner ran the room.

There was one concrete result: a prisoner-of-war exchange, agreed in principle and possibly the largest of the war, as Meduza reported. Kushner spoke of “significant progress” and, to ABC News, of a “positive step”. He was also candid about what had not moved. Territory, he said, was “the hardest” issue. The American negotiators, Meduza reported, saw no trust at all between the two sides.

Medinsky made sure no one misunderstood. Besides “every inch”, he offered a view of the continent: Europe’s security system, he said, was “essentially sick”, and “a band-aid won’t fix this.” That is the language of a man who wants to redesign the house, not repair the room he broke into.

Umerov was more careful but no less clear. “Significant differences remain,” he said, as Interfax-Ukraine reported. “The first step toward this must be an end to the war and security guarantees for Ukraine.” Volodymyr Zelensky put it more bluntly: “Real tools are needed to block any recurrence of the war.”

The war did not pause for the talks. While the delegations met, Russian missiles and drones hit six major power substations in four regions and caused outages in eight, according to the Kyiv Independent. In one overnight attack, Russia launched 163 drones, 91 of them jet-powered. Russia’s defence ministry claimed to have downed 627 Ukrainian drones, 150 of them over the Baltic oil port of Ust-Luga. Each side talked in Abu Dhabi and struck where it hurts the other.

Moscow

On Thursday, November 12, Witkoff and Kushner flew from the Gulf to the Kremlin. It was Witkoff’s tenth face-to-face meeting with Putin. Medinsky, Dmitriev and Putin’s foreign-policy aide, Yuri Ushakov, sat in.

Putin began with thanks. He asked the envoys to thank Trump for arranging contacts between Russia and Ukraine, and to pass his gratitude to Melania Trump for Oleshky, according to Russian media accounts relayed by UNN. Then he spoke of wanting a “lasting, permanent peace”, and in the next breath warned that “any foreign forces deployed there will be considered legitimate targets for the Russian Armed Forces,” as the Kyiv Post reported. A permanent peace, in other words, in which Ukraine can have no one to guarantee it.

Ushakov called the meeting “useful”. He said Russia had told the Americans that NATO could not expand any further, that the trilateral talks would continue, and that Putin and Trump would take up the whole range of issues at the APEC summit in China later this month, according to Reuters, as published by The Moscow Times. Asked whether peace was closer, Witkoff said, “I hope so.” He added that Europeans would join the next round. That evening he and Kushner were due to fly on to Dublin to brief Zelensky.

The striking proposal was a new humanitarian mission, modelled on Oleshky, this time in Kostiantynivka, a ruined city in Donetsk about twenty kilometres south of Kramatorsk. Before the war it was home to about 70,000 people. Putin said 400 to 500 civilians remain and offered to clear mines for five days so the two countries’ human-rights commissioners could lead them out, with foreign journalists watching. He claimed Russia controls the city. “If Kyiv believes it controls the city,” he added, “let it ensure security on its side.” The Americans backed the idea, Ushakov said.

The trouble is that Kostiantynivka is contested. Russia holds most of it, but Ukrainian units still hang on at the northern and western edges, supplied by drone, according to Meduza. Ushakov said openly that the mission’s purpose was to show “who really controls Kostiantynivka.” A truce with foreign cameras rolling would make Russian control look complete. It would also give Russian troops a pause in which to find the Ukrainian positions on the outskirts. Dmytro Lytvyn, an adviser to Zelensky, recalled that in Oleshky Russia had brought in equipment and rotated troops under cover of the evacuation. It was clear, he said, what Moscow had in mind.

Part IX asked what six days of mercy buy when the side granting them is the side doing the killing. Putin’s answer, it seems, is that they buy a photograph.

Every inch

Strip away the hotel suites and the thank-yous, and the shape of the week is plain.

On the ground, Ukraine is doing something that seemed impossible a year ago. It is taking territory back, killing Russian soldiers faster than Russia can replace them, and starving Russian positions until their occupants hold up cardboard signs. Its soldiers are middle-aged men and twenty-year-olds steering robots, and they are winning, slowly, in the tree lines of Donetsk.

At the table, Russia’s demands have not shrunk by a single square mile. The 2,000 square miles it could not take in February it still cannot take, and it still demands them. Medinsky promises “every inch”. Putin offers peace on condition that no one may defend it, and proposes a humanitarian corridor through a city whose ownership he wants the world to certify for him. Russia’s answer to a European security system it calls sick is to set the terms of the cure.

William Taylor, a former American ambassador to Ukraine, put the real question before the talks began. “Has Putin recognised he has a problem?” Taylor asked, as Kramer reported. So far, he said, there was no sign of it. “There’s no indication of any interest.”

There is a prisoner exchange coming, and it will bring hundreds of men home, which is not nothing. There is another round of talks, this time with Europeans in it, which is better than the alternative. But a Kremlin that still believes time is on its side has no reason to give up anything, and a president in Washington who says “the other way, they kill, too” is not going to persuade it otherwise.

The persuading is being done somewhere else. Back in his bunker, as Vivaldi drew to a close, Horobets watched a live feed of an open field. A soldier with a backpack and a rifle came out of the trees and crouched low in the tall grass, almost invisible.

“He’s Russian,” Horobets said. “We have work to do.”
