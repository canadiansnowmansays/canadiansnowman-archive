On 17 August 1992, in the Houston Astrodome, Patrick J. Buchanan stood before the Republican National Convention and told the delegates that the country was at war with itself.

“There is a religious war going on in our country for the soul of America,” he said. “It is a cultural war, as critical to the kind of nation we will one day be as was the Cold War itself.” He closed by calling on his audience to “take back our cities, and take back our culture, and take back our country.” The full text is preserved by the University of Maryland’s Voices of Democracy project, and it reads today less like a convention speech than a table of contents.

The columnist Molly Ivins remarked that the speech “probably sounded better in the original German.” The Bush campaign winced. George H.W. Bush lost that November to Bill Clinton, and the Republican establishment concluded, reasonably enough, that Buchanan had been a liability. They filed him under “fringe” and went back to the business of tax cuts and free trade.

That was the first time the party managed the grievance instead of answering it. It would not be the last.

The man before the man

Buchanan had already done something more alarming than give a speech. In the New Hampshire primary of 18 February 1992 he challenged a sitting Republican president, a man who had just won the Gulf War, and took roughly 37 per cent of the vote. He ran on what he called “America First,” a slogan he revived knowingly from the isolationist movement of 1940. He opposed the North American Free Trade Agreement, then being negotiated with Canada and Mexico. He wanted a wall, or at least a fence, on the southern border. He attacked the “globalists” in both parties and the press that covered them.

If that list sounds familiar, it should. Almost every plank of the 2016 Trump platform was on the table in 1992. Only the delivery was different. Buchanan was a Catholic intellectual and former Nixon speechwriter who read Burke and quoted Macaulay. Trump would reach the same audience with none of that baggage.

That same autumn, Ross Perot, a Texas billionaire with no party, no experience and a talent for charts, ran as an independent against both parties and took nearly 19 per cent of the popular vote. In the presidential debate of 15 October 1992 he warned of the “giant sucking sound” of American jobs heading south if NAFTA passed. Perot showed that a rich outsider attacking trade and the political class could pull a fifth of the country. Buchanan showed that the same message could split the Republican base from within. Neither man won. Together they drew the map.

Buchanan came back in 1996 and won the New Hampshire primary outright, urging his supporters forward as “peasants with pitchforks.” The party closed ranks behind Bob Dole and beat him again. In 2000 he ran as the Reform Party nominee, and here the story acquires its first good joke. One of the men briefly seeking that Reform Party nomination was a New York property developer named Donald Trump, who withdrew and, on NBC’s Meet the Press in October 1999, dismissed Buchanan as “a Hitler lover.” The future inheritor of Buchanan’s platform began by insulting its author. Stephen Leacock would have recognised the pattern from any small-town council in Ontario: the newcomer who denounces the old crank and then, a few years later, runs on the old crank’s ideas.

The theorist in the back room

Behind Buchanan stood a quieter figure, Samuel Francis, a columnist and adviser whose name almost nobody outside the movement remembers. Francis borrowed a term from the sociologist Donald Warren’s 1976 book The Radical Center: “Middle American Radicals.” These were white, working- and lower-middle-class voters who felt squeezed between an elite above them that despised their values and a dependent class below them that the elite seemed to favour. They were not conservatives in the country-club sense. They liked Social Security and Medicare. They distrusted big business almost as much as big government. What they wanted was a champion.

Francis argued that the Republican Party would never give them one, because its donors wanted cheap labour and free trade, and its intellectuals wanted to be respected in New York and Washington. He said, in effect, that the base would one day find a candidate who did not care about either. Francis was eventually pushed out of mainstream conservative journalism over his views on race, and he died in 2005. He did not live to see the candidate. But the description of the voter was precise enough that, read today, it is less like a theory than a prophecy.

Learning to talk like a war

Grievance needs a language, and the 1990s supplied one.

In 1990 Newt Gingrich’s political action committee, GOPAC, circulated a memo to Republican candidates titled “Language: A Key Mechanism of Control.” It advised them to describe their opponents with words such as “sick,” “pathetic,” “traitors” and “betray.” The memo was a training manual in exactly what Stephanie Slade, in an exchange with John Guida first published by the New York Times on 5 October 2026 and republished by the Salt Lake Tribune, calls “will-to-power politics,” in which “hatred toward the opposition is treated as a virtue.” Gingrich did not invent partisan contempt. He professionalised it. In 1994 he led the Republicans to their first House majority in forty years, and the lesson that contempt wins elections was absorbed by a generation of operatives.

The media followed. Rush Limbaugh’s radio programme had gone into national syndication in 1988, and by the mid-1990s he was reaching an audience of millions every weekday afternoon, teaching them that the mainstream press was the enemy. Fox News launched on 7 October 1996. Neither invented the audience. Both discovered that it was enormous, loyal and lucrative, and that the most profitable thing to sell it was the conviction that it was under siege.

That word, siege, is Slade’s. In her exchange with John Guida she argues that the cultural left and Democratic identity politics fed “a siege mentality” on the right that made voters want a fighter rather than a gentleman. It is a fair point and worth stating plainly: the soil was not fertilised by the right alone. But the right’s own institutions spent two decades telling their audience the walls were being breached, and an audience told that for long enough will eventually go looking for someone willing to man the walls by any means.

The Tea Party, and the first takeover

On 19 February 2009, a month into Barack Obama’s presidency, the CNBC commentator Rick Santelli stood on the floor of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange and erupted over the new administration’s plan to help homeowners facing foreclosure. “How many of you people want to pay for your neighbor’s mortgage that has an extra bathroom and can’t pay their bills?” he shouted, and he proposed “a Chicago Tea Party in July.” The clip went viral, and as Mediaite later noted, Santelli was widely credited with naming a movement that Ron Paul’s supporters had been building for some time.

Within weeks there were Tea Party rallies across the country, with especially large crowds on Tax Day, 15 April 2009. The movement presented itself as being about deficits and bailouts, and many of its members sincerely believed that. But researchers who tracked it, including the Institute for Research and Education on Human Rights in its report on the origins of the Tea Parties, found something else running through it: anxiety about immigration, about the country’s changing face, and about the man in the White House himself.

The Tea Party did not stay in the streets. It went into the primaries. In the midterm elections of November 2010 the Republicans gained 63 House seats, the largest swing since 1948, and many of the new members owed their seats to Tea Party energy rather than to the party leadership. The establishment congratulated itself on the majority and assumed it could domesticate the new arrivals. It could not. The arrivals came with a veto, and the veto was the primary challenge.

The clearest proof came on 10 June 2014, when Eric Cantor, the House majority leader and heir apparent to Speaker John Boehner, lost his Virginia primary by about ten points to Dave Brat, an economics professor who ran almost entirely against immigration reform. No sitting majority leader had lost a primary since the post was created. Every Republican member of Congress drew the same conclusion: the danger was no longer the Democrat in November. It was the challenger from your own side in the spring.

The birther rehearsal

Into this atmosphere, in the spring of 2011, stepped Donald Trump. He did not arrive as a Tea Party ideologue. He arrived as a television celebrity asking, again and again, on any programme that would book him, whether Barack Obama had really been born in the United States. The question was false, and Trump knew enough to frame it as a question rather than a claim. But it did something no policy argument could: it told a slice of the Republican base that their sense that the president was illegitimate, foreign and somehow not one of them was not a fringe suspicion but something a famous man would say on national television.

On 27 April 2011 the White House released Obama’s long-form birth certificate. Three days later, at the White House Correspondents’ Dinner, Obama mocked Trump to his face while the room laughed. Trump sat stone-faced. Whatever that evening did to him, it did not end his interest in politics. He had learned that grievance, delivered with a showman’s confidence, could command an audience the party’s official leadership could not reach.

The autopsy nobody read

The Republican leadership drew a very different lesson from the same years. After Mitt Romney lost to Obama in November 2012, a defeat sharpened by the leaked recording in which Romney dismissed “47 percent” of Americans as dependent on government, the Republican National Committee commissioned a review. The Growth and Opportunity Project report of March 2013, quickly nicknamed “the autopsy,” concluded that the party was losing because it was alienating women, young people and Hispanic voters, and it urged Republicans to “embrace and champion comprehensive immigration reform.”

That June, the Senate passed the bipartisan “Gang of Eight” immigration bill by 68 votes to 32. It died in the Republican House, where members looking over their shoulders at Tea Party challengers would not touch it. A year later Cantor fell. The autopsy had diagnosed the patient correctly in one sense: the party did have a demographic problem. But it had misread the patient’s wishes entirely. The base did not want to be broadened. It wanted to be defended.

This is the gap in which Trumpism grew. The party’s donors and strategists wanted lower taxes, freer trade and a modest immigration deal to keep the economy supplied with workers. Its voters, or a decisive share of them, wanted less trade, less immigration and someone to say out loud what they believed about the country’s elites. For twenty years the leadership managed that gap with rhetoric at election time and quiet betrayal in between. Buchanan, Perot, Francis and the Tea Party each tried to close it from below. Each failed.

What the soil was ready for

By the spring of 2015, then, the ground was ready. A large bloc of Republican primary voters believed the party leadership despised them. They had been told for two decades by their own media that the country was under siege. They had learned through the Tea Party that they could break incumbents. And they had watched a celebrity tell them, on television, that the president was not really American.

What they lacked was a candidate who owed nothing to the donors, cared nothing for the intellectuals’ good opinion, and had the fame to bypass the party machinery entirely. Samuel Francis had predicted such a figure. Pat Buchanan had tried to be him. On 16 June 2015 he came down an escalator in a building on Fifth Avenue with his own name on it.

It is tempting to tell the story of Trumpism as the story of one man’s appetite. It is more accurate, and more unsettling, to tell it as the story of a party that spent twenty-three years deciding its voters’ anger was a messaging problem. The anger was real. The establishment’s response was to manage it. When someone finally came along who would feed it, the managers discovered that they had never been in charge of it at all.

That is the subject of Part II.
