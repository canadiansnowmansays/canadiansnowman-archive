When I published Code Blue in December 2025, I asked Congress to take its numbers seriously. That obliges me to take them seriously too.

This autumn I went back through all ten parts and the Epilogue, checking every figure, quote and name against its source. The argument of the series holds: Americans pay far more for healthcare than people in comparable countries, cover fewer of their citizens, and have failed for 80 years to fix it. But too many of the specifics supporting that argument were wrong. Some were outdated, some were mismatched, and some could not be traced to any source at all.

As my About page explains, I write this newsletter in open collaboration with Claude, an AI model. The Code Blue drafts were produced with an earlier model, and I did not check them closely enough before publishing. That responsibility is mine. Every essay has now been corrected and marked with the date of revision, and the most important changes are listed below. The rewritten bill is set out section by section in the Code Blue Ledger.

The bill

Part 10 presented the American Health Security Act and called it “10 titles, 52 sections, fully drafted, fully scored.” It had five titles and 22 sections and had never been scored. Its arithmetic did not add up, and it counted savings to employers and families as savings to the federal government. It cited Swiss and German statutes by the wrong article numbers. It listed senators as sponsors and co-sponsors who had never agreed to anything, including one who had already left the Senate.

The bill has been rebuilt as a smaller, honestly costed proposal: twelve sections, each drawn from ideas that have already won support from members of both parties, paid for with savings the Congressional Budget Office has scored, and roughly deficit-neutral by design. The larger reforms the original promised are still there, under “Going further”, with their real costs. Senator Hawley

The series opened with a quotation attributed to Senator Josh Hawley, “Why don’t we make health care free in America?”, and later with a letter he supposedly sent in November 2024. Neither can be found. What Senator Hawley actually proposed, on December 3, 2025, was the No Taxes on Healthcare Act: a deduction of up to $25,000 per person for medical costs, available whether or not people itemize. Parts 1 and 10 now say so. I regret attributing words to him that he did not say.

Named people

Part 6, on lobbying, gave incorrect career histories for several former congressional staff members. Liz Fowler, for example, did not become an Anthem executive in 2021; she led the CMS Innovation Center. Several others were described in roles they never held. Every named person in Part 6 has been checked; the errors are corrected or the names removed. Where the essay needs examples of the revolving door, it now uses documented cases from ProPublica and the Sunlight Foundation.

The main factual corrections

  • Part 1:

    • “Healthcare costs have increased 2100% since 1960” now says medical-care prices rose about 2,500% since 1960 (Bureau of Labor Statistics).

    • Lyndon Johnson called Truman “the real daddy of Medicare”, not “of healthcare”.

    • I have not lived under universal healthcare “for 74 years”; Ontario’s hospital insurance began in 1959.

  • Part 2: The Congressional Budget Office estimated the 2017 House repeal bill would leave 24 million more people uninsured by 2026, not 53 million. The administration’s Supreme Court brief against the ACA was filed in June 2020, not December.

  • Part 3:

    • The title said American healthcare “costs 2100% more”. It costs about twice as much as in comparable countries, and the title now says so.

    • Several statistics had no traceable source and have been replaced or removed, including the Cleveland Clinic’s “1,400 billing clerks” and the per-country administrative costs.

    • The comparison of doctor-visit lengths had the United States and Germany reversed.

  • Part 4:

    • Other countries’ spending figures were several years older than the U.S. figure, exaggerating the gap. All now use the same year (OECD, 2024).

    • Germany’s statutory insurance is compulsory for most workers, not chosen voluntarily.

    • Canada’s wait times were understated; the corrected essay uses the Fraser Institute’s 2025 figures.

  • Part 5: A set of “Kaiser Family Foundation 2024” poll figures could not be found and have been replaced with verified KFF results.

  • Part 6: The industry spends more than $700 million on lobbying every year, not $700 million over eight years. Insurers publicly opposed repealing the individual mandate in 2017; they did not quietly support it.

  • Part 7:

    • More than half of Medicare enrollees are in private Medicare Advantage plans; traditional Medicare covers about 34 million people, not 67 million.

    • Traditional Medicare has no cap on out-of-pocket costs.

    • Paul Ryan’s 2011 Medicare plan passed the House.

    • Medicare covers about 20% of Americans, not 8%.

  • Part 8:

    • Oklahoma, West Virginia, Louisiana and Arkansas have all expanded Medicaid, though the essay listed them as states that refused.

    • Two of the ten states that have not expanded have Democratic governors.

    • Maternal mortality figures, hospital closure dates and the Bristol, Virginia–Tennessee comparison were wrong and have been corrected or removed.

    • Florida does allow citizen ballot initiatives.

    • The 2025 reconciliation law’s Medicaid changes, the largest in a decade, are now covered.

  • Part 9:

    • Several “executive actions” in fact require Congress.

    • The “copy-paste” quotations from Swiss and German law were not real statutory text and have been replaced with accurate descriptions.

    • Two senators listed as possible votes had already left office.

  • Epilogue: ACA sign-ups were running about 800,000 behind the previous year; that was not 800,000 fewer people insured. The House passed its subsidy extension on January 8, 2026, not the day before the Epilogue.

What hasn’t changed

The core of Code Blue stands. Americans pay roughly twice as much per person as comparable countries. Some 27 million remain uninsured. Congress let the enhanced ACA subsidies expire. Switzerland and Germany do achieve universal coverage with private and non-profit insurers and regulated prices. Medicare is popular, and the 2027 Medicare Advantage exits I wrote about in the Postscript show why the public program matters.

An argument this important deserves accurate evidence. I have tried to supply it.

If you spot anything still wrong, please tell me in the comments.
