The last instalment of this series ended on a warning. The White House counsel, Will Scharf, had written to Representative Robert Garcia, the incoming chairman of the House Oversight Committee, to suggest that aggressive oversight might be a crime. As law, I argued, the letter would almost certainly fail. Whether it worked as a warning depended on everyone who read it except the man it was addressed to.

That was the position on Monday, November 9, 2026. By the end of the week, the warning had stopped being a letter and become a programme.

On Wednesday, November 11, a sitting United States attorney wrote to a sitting United States senator to caution him about “significant legal consequences” if he kept investigating the donors to President Trump’s White House ballroom. On Thursday, November 12, the attorney general stood at a podium and explained, in a sentence that deserves to be framed, the point at which criticism of the government turns into a crime. The same day, Scharf said in an interview that he meant every word of his letter. And on Friday night, November 13, a court filing in Washington described what this Justice Department actually does to people who get in its way, beginning with two workers at a New York apartment building.

As Luke Broadwater, Zolan Kanno-Youngs and Devlin Barrett reported for The New York Times on November 14, 2026, the administration has spent months preparing an all-out effort to block Democratic oversight for the next two years. Alan Feuer, writing for The New York Times the same day, laid out the filing. Read side by side, the two stories describe one department facing in two directions: shield for the president’s friends, sword for everyone asking about them.

The letter from Houston

Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut spent twenty years as his state’s attorney general before he went to the Senate. He knows what a prosecutor’s letter is for. The one he received on November 11 came from Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, and it concerned an inquiry into who has been paying for the ballroom.

The next day, Blumenthal, Senator Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts and Garcia announced that they were investigating Meredith O’Rourke, a top Trump fund-raiser, after reports that she had coordinated more than $800 million in contributions to the president’s favourite construction projects. Their letter to her said the fund-raising operation suggested the possibility of quid pro quo politics. A spokesman for O’Rourke replied that the president could not be bought and that the accusations were baseless. That is how this is supposed to go: a committee asks, the subject denies, and the documents decide.

What is not supposed to happen is a federal prosecutor weighing in before the documents are requested. Attorney General Todd Blanche confirmed on November 12 that senior Justice Department leaders had been working with Reitz on the matter. Nobody has explained why the office that handles federal crime in Houston has an interest in the investigative plans of a senator from Connecticut. Part XXVI of this series listed handpicked prosecutors and venues among the marks of a manufactured case. A Texas prosecutor policing a New England senator’s oversight is about as handpicked as venues get.

The escalation matters more than the venue. Scharf is the president’s lawyer. He can write letters, and he can brief reporters, but he cannot convene a grand jury. Reitz can. A warning from the White House counsel is a political document. A warning from a U.S. attorney is a reminder of what his office has the power to do.

Threats, which can be allowed

At his news conference on November 12, Blanche said there were no ongoing criminal investigations into statements by incoming members of Congress. Then he explained why there might be. Officials were concerned, he said, about some of what had been said since the election. “There can come a point when a criticism becomes a threat,” he said, and then he added the sentence that ought to be quoted back to him for the rest of his tenure: “There are threats, which can be allowed as well, but at some point it does become a crime.”

Take him at his word, because the law does draw a line here, and it is not where he implied. Threatening to kill or injure a federal official is a crime. Since Counterman v. Colorado in 2023, the Supreme Court has required prosecutors in true-threat cases to show that the speaker at least recklessly disregarded the chance that his words would be taken as a threat of violence. A promise to issue subpoenas is not a threat of violence. “Lawyer up” is not a threat of violence. A letter asking a fund-raiser to account for $800 million is not a threat of violence. Every one of them belongs in Blanche’s own category of threats that “can be allowed,” which is to say, ordinary politics.

The only communications in this whole story that carry the explicit promise of state force are the ones going the other way. Reitz did not tell Blumenthal he disagreed with him. He told him there could be legal consequences.

Scharf, asked on November 12 whether his letter meant the White House was prepared to prosecute lawmakers, said the letter “stands for itself” and that he meant everything he said. He would not say how any of it squared with the Speech or Debate Clause, which bars the executive from prosecuting legislators for their legislative work. Part XXXVI set out why that silence is the whole argument. Nothing in the past week has filled it.

The machine behind the letters

It is now clear that the letters are not improvisation. The pushback will be run in part by Stephen Miller, whom the president named White House chief of staff after the Democratic sweep. Supporters describe him as a born brawler with long experience on Capitol Hill. At the Justice Department, responses to subpoenas, records requests and accusations will be coordinated by Rachel Jag, a former staff member to Representative Jim Jordan of Ohio, who has just been chosen to lead House Republicans. Jag took the job on an interim basis after the department’s head of legislative affairs, Patrick Davis, quit shortly before the midterms for reasons nobody has explained. The White House Counsel’s Office has hired two more lawyers to handle oversight.

Part of the plan is ordinary and, on its own terms, legitimate. Scharf and his colleagues are collecting statements by Democrats that, they say, show the coming investigations have no legislative purpose. Donald F. McGahn II, White House counsel in Trump’s first term, called that smart, and he is right. Under Trump v. Mazars, a subpoena without a valid legislative purpose can be quashed, and a chairman who boasts about humiliating his targets is handing the other side its exhibit. If that were the whole strategy, this would be a hard but fair fight, refereed by judges.

It is not the whole strategy. The Office of Legal Counsel’s summer opinion, discussed in Part XXXVI, asserts that executive privilege can cover communications by people outside government if they concern the president and his top aides. Put that beside the reporting that Trump has grown worried about his biggest donors to his construction projects becoming targets, and the design comes into focus. The privilege theory is the shield, and it is drawn wide enough to cover a fund-raiser. The criminal warnings are the sword, and they are pointed at the people asking her questions.

What the department does when someone says no

If the warnings to Congress were the only evidence, one could argue that they are bluster: letters that sound fierce and lead nowhere. The Friday night filing is a reminder of what happens when this department decides to lead somewhere.

The filer was Daniel C. Richman, the Columbia law professor and longtime friend of James Comey who served as his aide at the FBI. Readers of this series will remember him from Part XXIX, when he was subpoenaed to the “grand conspiracy” grand jury in Florida. His papers were filed in federal court in Washington, nominally in support of CNN’s effort to unseal secret Justice Department filings in a matter connected to the Comey prosecutions. In substance they are a bill of particulars against the government.

The filing says that this summer, an FBI agent turned up at Richman’s apartment building in New York to serve the subpoena, and two building workers declined to let him in. Building staff turn away unannounced visitors every day; that is a large part of what they are paid to do. For this, according to the filing, prosecutors hauled both workers before a grand jury in Florida. Prosecutors also threatened to charge Richman himself with obstruction of justice for supposedly dodging the subpoena, although he says his lawyers had already agreed to accept it for him.

It gets less competent from there. Richman’s lawyers describe a series of unsettling and irregular communications in which three different prosecutors gave three different answers to the most basic question a witness can ask: am I a target of this investigation, or merely a subject? The prosecutors could not agree whether to give him immunity before he testified. In the end Richman invoked his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination. His lawyers argued that the government’s conduct suggested its interest was not a viable investigation but “part of a harassment campaign.”

The Justice Department did not respond to the Times’ request for comment.

None of this is out of character. In the three cases brought against Comey, the department has already been caught reusing a search warrant obtained in a different case to seize his messages with Richman, botching its grand jury presentation, and installing a prosecutor, Lindsey Halligan, whose improper appointment got the first indictment thrown out. In the second case, over a beach photograph of seashells spelling “86 47,” investigators interviewed Salvatore Gravano, the former mafia hit man, in the hope he could explain the slang to a jury. In the third, filed as a criminal information exactly ten years after a 2016 Times article about a surveillance technique on Yahoo’s email platform, prosecutors have floated adding defendants, Richman or possibly The New York Times itself, in a way that could stretch the statute of limitations past October. Part XXXV called that case a race against the calendar. It is now a race the government proposes to win by enlarging the list of people it accuses.

Blanche’s test, applied to Blanche

So let us apply the attorney general’s own standard, the point at which criticism “becomes a threat,” to his own department’s week.

A senator announces a document request. A U.S. attorney writes to warn him of significant legal consequences. Two apartment-building employees refuse entry to a stranger, which is their job. They are summoned to testify before a grand jury in another state. A witness’s lawyers agree to accept a subpoena. Prosecutors threaten the witness with obstruction anyway. The witness asks whether he is a target. He gets three answers.

If any Democrat had done one of these things to a Trump official, Blanche would have called it intimidation, and he would have had a point. In the TACO framework this series uses, this is the Threaten stage carried out at industrial scale, and the threat does its work whether or not a single charge is filed. The building workers are the clearest lesson. They have no politics in this story at all. Their summons is a message to every concierge, receptionist, records clerk and junior lawyer who might one day be asked to cooperate with an oversight committee, or to stand between an FBI agent and a door: the cost of saying no is a trip to Florida.

Who enforces the rules

Stanley M. Brand, the House’s top lawyer under Speaker Tip O’Neill, put the structural problem plainly to the Times. The White House threats against lawmakers, he said, carry little legal force. But the House will struggle to enforce its own subpoenas, because a criminal contempt referral goes to the Justice Department, and the attorney general reports to Trump. Although Democrats control Congress, Brand noted, the administration still controls the department, and he does not expect contempt referrals to go anywhere.

That is the asymmetry this series has been tracking since Part I, and the past week laid it bare. The same department receives Congress’s referrals and writes Congress’s warnings. It decides whether a witness who defies a subpoena faces any consequence, and it decides whether a senator who issues one does. A contempt citation from the House can be left in a drawer. A letter from a U.S. attorney cannot.

There is one tool that does not run through the Justice Department. Each chamber retains its “inherent contempt” power, the authority to direct its own sergeant-at-arms to arrest an official who defies it. The Supreme Court upheld the power in McGrain v. Daugherty in 1927 and again in Jurney v. MacCracken in 1935, and it has gone essentially unused since. Reviving it would be an extraordinary escalation, and a dangerous one; the picture of congressional police arresting a cabinet officer is not one any Democrat should want. But the administration has spent the week establishing that every ordinary channel for enforcing a subpoena runs through officials who have already threatened the people issuing them. It should not be surprised if the extraordinary channels start to look less unthinkable.

The scorecard

  • Sen. Richard Blumenthal (new). Method: Letter from Aaron Reitz, U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, warning of “significant legal consequences” over his inquiry into ballroom donors, coordinated with Justice Department leadership. Status: Blumenthal, Warren and Garcia opened an investigation into fund-raiser Meredith O’Rourke the next day; no charges.

  • Rep. Robert Garcia. Method: White House counsel letter suggesting oversight may be criminal. Status: Scharf says he “meant everything”; Blanche says no criminal investigation is under way.

  • Daniel C. Richman. Method: Threatened with obstruction over a subpoena his lawyers say they had agreed to accept; contradictory target/subject answers; possible defendant in the Comey leak information. Status: Invoked the Fifth before the Florida grand jury; filing alleges a “harassment campaign.”

  • Two New York building workers (new). Method: Summoned before a Florida grand jury after declining to admit an FBI agent. Status: No wrongdoing alleged against either.

  • James B. Comey. Method: Three cases (Alexandria, dismissed; North Carolina “86 47”; Washington leak information). Status: Motion to dismiss the leak case pending; prosecutors float adding defendants to beat the statute of limitations.

  • The New York Times. Method: Raised as a possible co-defendant in the leak case. Status: Not charged.

A week ago, the Enemies List gained an entry that was a job description: the chairman of a committee that did not yet exist. This week it gained two building workers who simply did their jobs. That is the trajectory worth watching. A department that will summon building staff to a grand jury in Florida to make a point about a law professor is telling the new Congress exactly how it intends to treat everyone between it and the documents. The new majority takes office in January. The warnings arrived first.
