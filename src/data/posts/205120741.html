Postmark: A state-backed facility, somewhere in China. One hundred and twenty humanoids in neat rows. Each one sorting, scooping, diapering — beside a human holding the controller.

In June, the Chinese government did something no government has ever done. Through two agencies with formidable names — the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, the body that oversees the country’s vast constellation of state-owned enterprises — Beijing issued a joint directive ordering more than ten thousand humanoid robots into regular commercial deployment by the end of 2026. Not built. Not demonstrated. Deployed — put to work in factories, logistics centres, hospitals, retail floors and emergency-response operations, across more than a hundred distinct application scenarios, with local governments and state firms required to file implementation plans by the end of June and progress reports by November.

Read that timeline again, because it is the part that ought to stop you. As the South China Morning Post reported, one senior industry figure noted with some understatement that six months is not a long time — but that a concentrated national effort, compressed into months, could force an entire industry to converge on the technology paths that actually work, and in the longer term determine whether embodied AI reaches large-scale commercial deployment at all. A ministry does not usually set the clock for an industry. In China, this June, a ministry set the clock for an industry, and the clock said Christmas.

This is the factory-floor postcard’s sequel — Part IV of this series watched robots arrive on the assembly line one machine at a time, through the ordinary gravity of cost and productivity. This postcard is about what happens when a state decides that ordinary gravity is too slow, and reaches for the throttle. It is the most ambitious act of industrial will in the robotics century so far. And the most honest thing I can tell you about it is that whether it succeeds depends entirely on a detail you will not find in the directive: the man standing next to the robot, holding the controller.

The number that keeps doubling

Start with the money, because the money is doing something remarkable — it keeps revising itself upward, and the revisions are the real news.

As CNBC reported in late June, Morgan Stanley raised its forecast for Chinese humanoid robot shipments in 2026 to fifty thousand units. That is not the striking part. The striking part is the trajectory: the bank began the year forecasting fourteen thousand, doubled that to twenty-eight thousand in the winter, and has now nearly doubled it again — a more than threefold increase in a single professional forecast in less than six months. When a Wall Street bank has to rewrite the same number twice in half a year, and each rewrite is a doubling, the number has stopped being a forecast and become a confession: nobody actually knows how fast this is moving, and every guess has been too low.

The longer arc the bank draws is steeper still — a Chinese humanoid market growing from around two billion dollars this year to fifteen billion by 2030, with annual shipments reaching four hundred and forty-six thousand units, a compound growth rate the analysts put at over a hundred per cent a year. And the deployments underneath the forecast are real, not notional. As industry analysts noted, the State Grid Corporation of China — the national electricity utility, one of the largest companies on Earth — placed a single procurement order worth roughly a billion US dollars for five hundred humanoids, three thousand dual-arm robots and five thousand quadrupeds. Robotera’s machines are sorting parcels in more than ten China Post and SF Express logistics centres. This is not a trade-show fantasy. Somewhere in China tonight, a state-owned utility’s robots are being unpacked.

Here is the geopolitical fact that Part VIII first raised and that this news drives home like a nail. In 2025, of the roughly thirteen to sixteen thousand humanoid robots shipped worldwide, Chinese manufacturers accounted for more than eighty per cent — by some counts closer to ninety. The two largest, AgiBot and Unitree, each shipped over five thousand units. Their principal American rivals — Tesla, Figure, Agility — shipped, by the same industry count, on the order of a hundred and fifty units each. Let that disparity sit. The country that this series’ companion publication spends its days worrying about, in the context of AI software and model weights, has already won the hardware war so decisively that the word “war” flatters the competition. The Americans lead in the brains. The Chinese have built the bodies, and are now building them by the tens of thousands, on government order.

The man with the controller

And now the sentence that saves this postcard from becoming Chinese state advertising, because it comes from inside China’s own press.

As CNN reported at the end of June, in the finest piece of humanoid-robot journalism I have read this year, there is a state-backed facility — one of dozens like it across the country — where more than one hundred and twenty humanoid robots stand in neat rows, each performing a single task over and over: sorting packages, changing diapers, scooping popcorn. Beside each robot stands a human trainer with a handheld controller. The machines are not, in the sense a factory manager would recognize, working. They are being taught, laboriously, one repetition at a time, and the teaching requires a person for every machine.

This is the detail the directive cannot legislate away, and it is the hinge of the entire story. A short walk from that training facility, CNN found the showcase version — robotic dogs performing a lion dance, a humanoid in a Michael Jordan jersey sinking free throws, the choreographed spectacle Part IX of this series examined. The distance between those two rooms — the dazzling demo and the hundred and twenty machines with their hundred and twenty human minders — is the distance between the announcement and the achievement. And it is not a short walk. It is the whole problem.

The reporting is candid about what the viral videos conceal: the heavily promoted technology is still years from replacing human labour, whether on a factory floor or in a home. A humanoid can be choreographed to flip and dance flawlessly, because a dance is a fixed sequence performed in a known space. Real work is the opposite — it is endless small deviations, the parcel that arrives at the wrong angle, the diaper that does not sit right, the unfamiliar object on the belt. Unitree’s own founder, Wang Xingxing, put it with a candour his government’s directive does not share: the industry, he said, is one to three years before its ChatGPT moment, and that moment arrives only when a robot dropped into an unfamiliar setting can complete perhaps eighty per cent of its tasks from a plain voice instruction. It cannot yet. Not close.

The rental economy of the almost-useful

What China has built instead, in the gap between the promise and the capability, is one of the strangest and most instructive economies of the machine age: a rental market for robots that cannot quite work yet.

As CNN reported, an e-commerce livestreamer in Hangzhou spent thirty thousand dollars on his first android after watching the Spring Festival Gala, and now rents it out at four hundred-odd dollars a day — to draw crowds at exhibitions, to perform at events, even to stage a marriage proposal. He is not alone. There are, by state-media count, more than a hundred and fifty thousand robot-rental businesses in China. AgiBot launched a rental subsidiary called SHAREBOT, which logged thousands of orders in its first months, renting humanoids by the day complete with a human operator to control and program the machine.

Sit with the shape of that business. You rent a humanoid robot, and it arrives with a human being whose job is to make it work. The robot is, functionally, a very expensive costume for the operator’s skill — a way to sell the appearance of autonomous labour while a person off to the side supplies the actual intelligence. Readers of The Asimov Gap will recognize this instantly as the reverse-centaur that publication has documented in the white-collar world, now rendered in steel and running down a Chinese street: the human has not been replaced by the machine; the human has been hidden behind it, holding the controller, so the machine can take the credit.

I find this genuinely poignant, and also genuinely clarifying. The rental economy is not a sign that the robots have arrived. It is a sign that they have almost arrived — close enough to be worth renting as spectacle, not yet close enough to be worth buying as labour. It is the exact economic signature of a technology in the last mile before it works, and the last mile, as every engineer knows, is the one that takes the longest.

The standard-setter’s advantage

There is a dimension to Beijing’s directive that has nothing to do with whether the robots work well and everything to do with who writes the rules — and it is the part that ought to concern Western capitals most.

In February, before the deployment order, China had already released its first national standard system for humanoid robots and embodied intelligence, covering the entire industry chain. This is the quiet move beneath the loud one. Whoever deploys first, at scale, sets the de facto standards — the connectors, the safety protocols, the data formats, the testing regimes — that everyone else must then either adopt or argue with. It is how the shipping-container industry works, how the mobile-phone-charger wars were fought, how a hundred invisible technical committees have shaped the modern world. A senior American foreign-policy analyst put the danger precisely, as reported in coverage of the directive: if Washington treats the contest solely as a race to hit new capability benchmarks, it could lead in invention but fall behind in influencing where and how the technology is actually used worldwide. The Americans, in other words, could win every lab and lose the plug socket.

This is the TACO framework’s opposite number, and it is worth naming for the readers of this series’ parent publication who have followed that lens through the trade war. Where the American approach to strategic dominance has often been to threaten and then accommodate, the Chinese approach here is patient and structural: build the supply chain, deploy at scale, write the standards, and let the rest of the world discover, a few years hence, that the humanoid century runs on Chinese fixtures because those were the only fixtures in the room when the concrete set. Canada, which has spent two years relearning the cost of depending on infrastructure it does not control, should read this directive as a documentary about its own near future.

What the order can and cannot buy

So what, from the balcony, do we make of a government ordering a technology into existence on a deadline?

The bull case is formidable and I will state it at full strength. Those hundred and twenty machines with their hundred and twenty human minders are not a failure — they are a data-collection operation of a scale no market has ever produced. Every repetition, every corrected error, every awkward parcel is training data, and China’s state-directed deployment is about to generate more real-world humanoid operating data than the rest of the planet combined. The directive’s true purpose may not be ten thousand working robots by Christmas at all. It may be ten thousand teaching robots by Christmas — a nationwide apprenticeship that compresses the learning curve by brute force, so that the machines of 2028 arrive already knowing what the machines of 2026 are being painfully taught. A senior McKinsey figure put the point plainly: walk into any Chinese factory now and you will find more automation deployed than anywhere else on Earth, and it is a below-the-radar story precisely because it is not the dancing robots — it is the boring, grinding, real-world accumulation of capability. That is how you win a decade.

The bear case is equally real, and the honest analysts state it too. A forecast revised twice in six months signals uncertainty as much as momentum. Battery life still constrains everything — a humanoid that runs a few hours before recharging is of limited use where uptime is the entire point. The gap between demo performance and real reliability remains wide. And a government-mandated target across state-owned enterprises invites exactly the failure mode that command economies always invite: headline compliance. If any deployment counts as a deployment, ten thousand robots will absolutely be “deployed” by December — standing in rows, minders alongside, technically working, actually rehearsing. The number will be hit. Whether the number means anything is a separate question, and it is not one a progress report filed in November is designed to answer honestly.

Here is where a Canadian’s particular vantage earns its place. We have watched, through the long file of the trade war, what happens when a state decides that strategic industries are too important to leave to the ordinary gravity of the market — and we have learned to hold two thoughts at once. State direction genuinely can compress timelines and force an industry into being; the Chinese EV industry, which this series keeps returning to because the robot supply chain grew directly out of it, is the proof. And state direction genuinely can manufacture the appearance of success while the substance lags, because the people filing the reports answer to the people who set the target. Both are true of the humanoid directive. Which one dominates by Christmas, none of us — not Morgan Stanley, not the ministry, not Wang Xingxing — actually knows.

What I keep returning to is that room. One hundred and twenty machines, patient in their rows, and one hundred and twenty humans beside them, holding the controllers, teaching the future to sort a parcel. It is the most important room in the robotics century, and it is the exact opposite of the room the videos show you. The videos show you a robot that has mastered a dance. The real room shows you a robot being taught to work, by a human, one repetition at a time, on a government deadline. Somewhere between those two rooms is the answer to whether ten thousand robots will actually be working by Christmas — or merely, magnificently, standing by.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one was mailed on a deadline.
