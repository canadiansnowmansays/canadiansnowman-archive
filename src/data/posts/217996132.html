In 149 BC, a Roman consular army landed at Utica, a few kilometres up the coast from Carthage, and the Carthaginians did what a frightened small power does. They sent envoys, and they complied.

Carthage was no longer the Carthage of Hannibal. It had lost two wars to Rome, surrendered its fleet, paid an indemnity spread over fifty years, and agreed by treaty not to make war without Roman permission. It had finished paying that indemnity early. When its neighbour, the Numidian king Masinissa, a Roman ally, spent decades nibbling at Carthaginian territory, Carthage appealed to Rome for arbitration again and again, and Rome’s commissioners kept finding reasons to side with Masinissa. In 151 BC Carthage finally fought back without asking, and lost. That was the pretext.

So when the legions arrived, the envoys asked what Rome wanted. Rome wanted three hundred children of the leading families as hostages. Carthage sent them. Rome then wanted the city’s weapons. Carthage handed over, by the ancient accounts, some two hundred thousand sets of armour and two thousand catapults. Only then, with the city disarmed and its children gone, did the consuls deliver the real demand: the Carthaginians were to abandon their city entirely and resettle at least ten Roman miles from the sea, which for a trading city was a death sentence in slower form.

Carthage refused. It rearmed with whatever it could make from temple metal and women’s hair for bowstrings, and held out for three years. In 146 BC Scipio Aemilianus broke in, fought street by street for six days, sold the survivors into slavery, and levelled the city. (The story that Rome salted the earth afterwards is a nineteenth-century embellishment; the destruction needed no garnish.) The same year, on the other side of the Mediterranean, a Roman army destroyed Corinth, one of the great cities of Greece, for leading a revolt of the Achaean League. Its men were killed, its women and children sold, its art shipped to Italy.

Two cities erased in a single year, one in Africa and one in Greece, both of them visible to every other client, ally and neighbour Rome had. That was not an accident of timing. It was a message.

Why Rome comes first

The Introduction to this series said Rome opens it not because Rome invented the pattern but because it perfected the combination. Carthage is the cleanest demonstration of the five-point rubric in the whole ancient record, but it is also, in a sense, the least interesting part of the Roman story. Any strong army can destroy a disarmed city. What Rome built that was genuinely new was a system for getting everything it wanted without destroying most places at all, because it had destroyed a few so thoroughly.

That system had three working parts: the client king, the tribute collector, and the language that made both sound like a favour.

The client king

Rome’s preferred instrument in its first centuries of expansion was not the province but the friend. A ruler who submitted could be recognised by the Senate as amicus et socius populi Romani, a friend and ally of the Roman people, and keep his throne, his court and his title. What he lost was everything that made the title mean something: an independent foreign policy, the right to make war, often his own heir, who would be raised in Rome as an honoured guest and a hostage at the same time.

The arrangement was a bargain only in the sense that the alternative was Carthage. Herod the Great is the best known example. He was named King of Judaea by a vote of the Roman Senate in 40 BC, at a moment when he did not control Judaea, and he spent three years and a Roman army taking the kingdom Rome had given him. For the next thirty years he built on a colossal scale, rebuilt the Temple in Jerusalem, and named his new harbour city Caesarea after his patron. He was, by any practical measure, an extremely effective Roman administrator who happened to wear a crown.

The client system also had a convenient ending built in. Kings died, and Rome decided what happened next. Attalus III of Pergamon left his entire kingdom to Rome in his will in 133 BC, which became the province of Asia. Bithynia came the same way in 74 BC. When a client kingdom was bequeathed rather than conquered, Rome could present annexation as the honouring of a friend’s last wishes. When no helpful will turned up, as in Judaea after Herod’s son Archelaus proved unsatisfactory in AD 6, the territory was simply converted to direct rule, and a Roman governor ordered a census so the new province could be taxed. That census, under Quirinius, set off a revolt. It would not be the last.

The tribute collector

Once a territory became a province, it paid. Land taxes, poll taxes, customs duties and requisitions flowed to Rome, and for most of the Republic’s history the collection was contracted out to private companies of publicani, who bid for the right to collect a province’s taxes and kept whatever they could squeeze above their bid. The system was designed to extract, and it did. Cicero, prosecuting the governor Verres in 70 BC for his plunder of Sicily, described a man who had stripped the island of grain, cash and art over three years. Verres fled into exile before the trial ended. He was prosecuted not because extraction was unusual but because he had been unusually greedy about it.

Resistance to the collectors was the most common reason Roman provinces rebelled, and the answer was always the same. In 88 BC Mithridates VI of Pontus, exploiting hatred of the tax companies in Asia, organised the killing of perhaps eighty thousand Romans and Italians in a single coordinated massacre. Rome spent the next quarter-century destroying him. In AD 60 Boudica’s revolt in Britain began when a Roman procurator treated the kingdom of the Iceni, whose king had tried to protect it by leaving half to the emperor in his will, as simple property: the estate was seized, Boudica was flogged, her daughters were raped. Her rising burned London. The reprisal, under Suetonius Paulinus, was savage enough that Rome eventually replaced him to calm the province. In AD 70, after the Jewish revolt, the Temple was destroyed and its treasures carried through Rome in triumph, as the relief on the Arch of Titus shown in the Introduction records.

The language that made it sound like a favour

This is where Rome earns its place at the head of the table, and where the best witness is a Roman.

Tacitus, writing about his father-in-law Agricola, governor of Britain in the AD 70s and 80s, describes how Agricola pacified the island not only with legions but with amenities. He encouraged the Britons to build temples, forums and townhouses, had the sons of chieftains educated in Latin, and watched with satisfaction as the toga came into fashion. Then Tacitus adds the line that makes his book one of the most honest imperial documents ever written. The Britons, he says, gradually drifted into the pleasures of colonnades, baths and elegant dinners, and in their innocence called it civilisation, when it was really a part of their subjection.

Tacitus understood exactly what the civilising mission was for. In the same book he gives a speech to a Caledonian chieftain named Calgacus, addressing his warriors before the battle of Mons Graupius. Whether Calgacus ever said anything like it is doubtful; the speech is Tacitus’s own composition, which makes it more remarkable, not less. In it the chieftain describes the Romans as plunderers of the world who, having exhausted the land, now search the sea. To robbery, slaughter and theft they give the false name of empire, he says, and where they make a desert, they call it peace.

That line has been quoted against every empire since, and it was written by a Roman senator about Rome. The empire’s own elite knew what the rhetoric was covering. It deployed the rhetoric anyway, because the rhetoric worked: it gave client kings a dignified story to tell their subjects, gave provincial elites a reason to adopt Roman ways, and gave Rome itself a way of describing conquest as the spread of order.

Augustus made the claim official. His Res Gestae, the account of his achievements he had inscribed on bronze outside his mausoleum and copied onto temples across the empire, is a long list of provinces added, kings received as suppliants, and peoples brought under the rule of the Roman people, presented throughout as the extension of peace. The best surviving copy is on the walls of the Temple of Augustus and Rome in Ankara, in what was then the province of Galatia. The provincials were expected to read their own subjugation described as a blessing, on a temple they had built to the man who did it.

Scoring Rome against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. Not always. Rome fought genuinely close wars against Carthage in the third century BC, against Pyrrhus, against Mithridates at his height, against Parthia, which Rome never subdued. Those were contests, and they do not belong in this series. By 149 BC, though, Carthage had been disarmed by treaty and then disarmed again on demand. Judaea, Pergamon, Bithynia and the client kingdoms of the east dealt with Rome from positions of total dependence. For the cases scored here, the gap was real and uncontested. Satisfied.

Coercion as first resort. The Third Punic War is almost a textbook example: each concession Carthage made was followed by a harsher demand, and the final demand was designed to be refused. Rome’s standard opening move with a new client was a demand for submission backed by a legion within marching distance. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Tribute, land, slaves, grain, art and strategic position all flowed one way, and the stated justifications (Carthage’s treaty violation, the protection of allies, the bequests of kings) diverge from the operative goal in ways Roman writers themselves documented. Cato the Elder ended his speeches with the demand that Carthage be destroyed for years before the pretext arrived; the rich farmland of North Africa became the province of Africa and one of Rome’s granaries. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Peace, order, civilisation, friendship: the Roman vocabulary set the pattern for everything that follows in this series, and the Res Gestae is the first great example of an empire publishing its own conquests as a public service. Satisfied, and then some.

Resistance gets punished. Carthage and Corinth in the same year. Jerusalem in AD 70. The suppression of Boudica. The message was aimed as much at the watching provinces as at the rebels. Satisfied.

Five for five. Rome sets the standard every later case has to be measured against.

What Rome tells us about the rest of the series

Three things are worth carrying forward.

The first is that the most effective bullying is the kind that rarely needs to be repeated. Rome destroyed a handful of cities completely and governed dozens of client kingdoms with a letter from the Senate. The spectacle was an investment. Every later empire in this series learns the same lesson, and the Opium Wars in Part III and Suez in Part VI are, in different ways, stories about what happens when the spectacle stops frightening people.

The second is that the empire’s own people often know exactly what is going on. Tacitus is not a dissident; he was a consul and a governor. He simply wrote down what the civilising mission was for. British parliamentarians will do the same in Parts III and V, and Americans in the chapters after that. The pattern survives its critics because the critics tend to be admired and then ignored.

The third is the one that brings us back to this week. When President Trump told the United Nations General Assembly on 22 September 2026 that “to the victor belong the spoils” of Venezuela’s oil, the phrase itself was not Roman. It comes from an American senator, William Marcy of New York, defending Andrew Jackson’s patronage appointments in 1832, and it was about government jobs rather than foreign oil fields. But the idea it expressed is exactly the one carved on the Arch of Titus and the temple walls at Ankara: that what is taken by strength is rightfully held, and that saying so openly is a sign of confidence rather than shame. Rome at least had the discipline to wrap the claim in the language of peace. Part XIII will have to consider what it means that the current version mostly doesn’t bother.
