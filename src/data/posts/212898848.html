Readers of my essays will already know the broad shape of the story from The Great Canadian Trade War, the companion series that has tracked, in granular real time, the collapse of talks between Ottawa and Washington this August: the walked-away negotiations, the Section 338 tariffs, the tit-for-tat retaliation lists, the legal exposure now hanging over an obscure ninety-year-old statute that has never once been used to impose tariffs. That series is concerned with the diplomacy and the mechanics of the fight itself — who said no to whom, and why, and what it means for the two countries’ relationship going forward.

This instalment of The Household Ledger asks a narrower and more literal question: what does all of that actually cost, in dollars, at a cash register, for a household on either side of the border that has never read a communiqué and does not especially care who blinked first.

The list itself is a grocery list

It is worth simply reading Canada’s retaliation list for what it is, because it makes the point better than any summary can. Announced on August 25 and effective September 8, Canada’s tiered tariffs of 50, 25, and 15 percent apply to roughly seven hundred American products worth about twenty billion dollars in imports — a list that runs, in the words of the reporting that catalogued it, from household aluminum foil to railway locomotives and steel bridges. It mirrors the American list in clothing, forestry products, and tools, but adds an unmistakably domestic, kitchen-and-basement flavour: dishwashers, washing machines, stoves, and a wide array of fresh and frozen fish. This is not an abstraction dreamed up by trade lawyers. It is, functionally, an appliance showroom and a hardware store, itemized and taxed.

The American tariffs Canada is responding to are, if anything, more consequential for ordinary construction and manufacturing costs. Steel and aluminum tariffs sit at fifty percent, the statutory maximum under the Section 338 authority the administration invoked. Lumber has faced a ten percent tariff since last autumn, rising toward thirty percent on January 1, 2026, with wood furniture, cabinets, and vanities facing a parallel climb from twenty-five toward fifty percent. Autos and auto parts have been targeted at points in the dispute for tariffs as high as fifty percent effective January 2027, a threat the president made explicit on Truth Social this Monday even as steel and aluminum quotas were still being negotiated down in other channels.

How a tariff schedule becomes a receipt

The mechanism connecting a tariff rate to a store price is not mysterious, and the research on it is unusually consistent for an economics question. A National Bureau of Economic Research paper examining tariff incidence in 2026 found that pass-through to US import prices runs close to one hundred percent — meaning that when a tariff rate rises, importers and exporters absorb very little of the increase, and almost the entire cost eventually reaches the price a consumer pays. A separate Federal Reserve analysis reached a similar conclusion specifically for goods most exposed to the 2025 tariff regime, estimating that tariffs implemented through November 2025 had raised core goods prices by roughly 3.1 percent by February 2026 — effectively the entirety of the excess inflation the US economy experienced in that category relative to pre-pandemic norms.

The National Association of Home Builders has put a specific number on what this means for the cost of a house: builders surveyed in the spring estimated a typical cost effect from tariff actions at roughly $10,900 per home, with more than sixty percent of builders reporting they had already seen higher costs as a direct result. The mechanism is straightforward and worth spelling out once, because it repeats at every stage of a supply chain: a washing machine that would otherwise cost $500 and carries a 25 percent effective tariff burden costs the consumer roughly $625 once that cost is passed through — an extra $125 for the identical appliance. Multiply that arithmetic across framing lumber, steel rebar, aluminum siding, wiring, and every appliance in a new kitchen, and a fairly modest-sounding tariff schedule compounds into a five-figure addition to the cost of a home, exactly as the builders’ own estimate suggests.

A Joint Economic Committee analysis, tallying the cumulative effect across the whole tariff regime rather than any one category, put the average American household’s tariff-related cost at roughly $1,745 between February 2025 and January 2026, with a further average increase of about $700 projected for 2026 alone. None of these figures come from an anti-tariff advocacy group. They come from the same kind of government and academic economists who measure inflation, GDP, and every other statistic a Federal Reserve chairman studies before deciding what to do about interest rates — the subject of the first instalment of this series.

It is also worth noting how quickly this pass-through happens, because it undercuts a common assumption that tariff costs take years to filter through an economy. Research tracking retail prices in near-real time found that within days of a major tariff announcement, prices of imported goods most exposed to the new rate rose by roughly two percent — a striking speed that suggests retailers and manufacturers, far from absorbing new costs while they wait to see whether a tariff sticks, tend to pass the increase along almost immediately, on the reasonable assumption that a rate is more likely to rise further than to be reversed.

Who actually absorbs the hit, on each side of the border

The distribution of pain is not symmetrical, and it is worth being precise about that rather than treating “a trade war” as a single undifferentiated cost shared equally by everyone involved. Joseph Steinberg, an economist at the University of Toronto, has estimated that retaliatory tariffs hurt Canada roughly ten times more than they hurt the United States, in either direction — a function of the simple size mismatch between the two economies, with Canada’s roughly a twelfth the size of its neighbour’s. Canadian economists describe the country’s retaliation, candidly, as “a pea shooter in a gun battle”: necessary as a matter of political and economic self-respect, but not remotely capable of inflicting proportional damage on the larger partner.

Within Canada, the burden is also unevenly spread by sector and by province. The forestry sector — plywood, laminated wood, fiberboard, paper, packaging — has been identified as the hardest-hit industry in the most recent tariff tranche, even as the sector’s own trade association continues to support the government’s decision to walk away from a deal it judged inadequate. Geographically, Ontario and Quebec have absorbed the largest share of the damage to date, while Alberta has been largely spared, thanks to the tariffs’ pointed exclusion of oil, gas, electricity, and potash — Canada’s largest exports to the US by value, and the ones with no readily available substitute for American buyers.

Within the United States, the picture inverts in a familiar way: the costs are smaller as a share of the overall economy, but they land hardest on exactly the households and sectors already most exposed to rising prices elsewhere — new-home buyers already squeezed by the mortgage-rate dynamics traced in Part I of this series, and manufacturers of appliances, machinery, and vehicles who must either absorb the higher input costs themselves or pass them along, and who, as the trade press covering the steel and aluminum tariffs has noted, generally choose the latter. It is a useful reminder that tariffs, whatever their stated purpose, function domestically much like a sales tax that falls unevenly across categories of spending — regressive in effect, since lower-income households spend a larger share of every dollar on exactly the tariffed goods (appliances, clothing, furniture, food) that better-off households can more easily defer or substitute away from.

Vehicles deserve a brief note of their own, since the auto sector sits at the intersection of nearly every tariff category discussed above. A modern vehicle is assembled from components sourced across dozens of countries, so tariffs on steel, aluminum, electronics, and finished auto parts all compound in the same finished product. Manufacturers facing that compounding cost have consistently added it to the sticker price rather than absorbing it in profit margins, and repair shops have reported higher bills for replacement parts for the same reason — meaning the tariff cost shows up twice for a car-owning household: once at purchase, and again at every subsequent repair.

The number that both sides actually agree on

There is one figure in this dispute that both the Canadian government’s own economists and the US-side estimates broadly agree on, and it is a useful corrective to language on both sides that tends toward the apocalyptic. Trevor Tombe, an economist at the University of Calgary, has calculated that the September 8 retaliation tranche raises Canada’s average effective tariff rate on the tariffed goods to roughly 6.5 percent — notably still well below the roughly 15 percent rate the United States has negotiated with allies who accepted a comprehensive deal, and a small fraction of the rate applied to China at the height of that dispute. RBC’s own modelling puts the GDP effect of the entire episode, to date, at roughly 0.4 percent of Canadian output — real money, concentrated painfully in specific sectors and provinces, but not the economy-ending event the most heated rhetoric on either side of the border sometimes suggests.

That is worth holding in mind precisely because it complicates a tidy narrative in either direction. It does not mean the tariffs are costless — the receipts above make clear they are not, for a household in Windsor, Ontario buying Canadian-made appliances at a markup, or a family in Michigan pricing out a kitchen renovation with tariffed steel appliances and tariffed lumber. But it also means the household-level costs traced in this instalment are the product of a dispute that, on the numbers even its participants largely accept, remains a genuinely bounded one rather than an unlimited one — which makes the question of whether either government continues to escalate it, rather than the dispute’s current scale, the thing actually worth watching in the weeks ahead.
