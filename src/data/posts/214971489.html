There is a particular exhaustion familiar to anyone who has stood behind a building inspector and a contractor who does not believe the word “no” actually applies to him. The contractor argues the permit should be granted. The inspector says no. The contractor files an appeal. The inspector issues a preliminary injunction. The contractor files the appeal again, this time with more urgency and a different hat. The inspector stamps the permit DENIED a second time. And the contractor, having used up the standard moves in the playbook, discovers that he is actually out of options, which is not the same thing as having accepted that he is out of options.

This, in microcosm, is the last week of the Trump administration’s legal campaign against mail-in voting.

The Calendar vs. The Court On Thursday, September 3rd, 2026, the Justice Department filed an emergency application with the U.S. Supreme Court seeking immediate implementation of the U.S. Postal Service’s new mail-ballot restrictions. The filing was framed, as these things always are, with a sense of urgency: states are about to start mailing ballots, the portal won’t be ready until next week, time is running out, the Court needs to act now. The administration’s Solicitor General, D. John Sauer, argued that the rule “simply imposes reasonable preparation requirements for certain election-related mail” and that its “regulation of the U.S. mail — not federal elections — is plainly constitutional.”

Twenty-four hours later, on Friday, September 4th, Judge Indira Talwani issued a preliminary injunction — the stronger, longer-lasting version of her earlier temporary restraining order — blocking the rule from taking effect. Her reasoning landed with the weight of a gavel on concrete: the rushed time frame being pushed by the administration will make voter disenfranchisement “practically inevitable” if the rule is allowed to stay in place. She noted, with the precision that marks every one of her rulings in this case, that the administration had acknowledged in its own filings that the portal it intends to use for states to upload voter lists won’t be ready until sometime next week — and yet was asking the Court for emergency relief to enforce a rule it currently lacked the technological capacity to administer.

The states challenging the rule, in their response to the Supreme Court (filed Sept. 9 on SCOTUS docket 26A305), phrased it even more directly: “The Trump administration makes ‘no effort to explain how they could possibly be entitled to emergency relief to effectuate a Rule that they currently lack the technological capacity to administer.’”

On Sunday, September 6th, the administration filed again.

What You Do When “No” Doesn’t Take

The third filing — this one a brief to the Supreme Court seeking to override Talwani’s preliminary injunction while the litigation continues — arrived without any material change to the underlying facts. The rule still wasn’t ready. The timeline was still impossible. The evidence of mail-ballot fraud was still absent from the record. The only new element was urgency itself: the temporary restraining order was set to expire on September 10th, and the administration wanted the Supreme Court to lift Talwani’s block before that happened, on the theory that asking again, quickly, might produce a different answer than asking urgently on Thursday.

This is not irrational in the sense that it lacks internal logic. It is irrational in the sense that it mistakes repetition for argument. A judge who says “this cannot be done in time” does not become persuaded by the observation that time has now passed and the case is more urgent. An appellate court does not overturn a preliminary injunction because the party seeking the overturn has filed the motion twice in four days. Courts operate according to a set of procedural rules that account for exactly this scenario — the repeated emergency application from a party that has already lost the same motion — and those rules exist precisely to prevent the courthouse from becoming a place where whoever files the most motions wins by sheer attrition.

And yet: Solicitor General Sauer told the justices, in this third filing, that “the longer [the Court] waits to grant the administration’s request the more likely it is that some states may not be able to comply with the new rules before having their mail ballots printed for the fall election and, in turn, might deprive some voters of being able to receive a ballot delivered by USPS.” He was correct that time was passing. He was not correct that this fact, by itself, was reason to overturn what a district court judge had just decided.

This is the moment at which the difference between losing in court and accepting a loss in court becomes most visible. The administration has not accepted that Talwani’s preliminary injunction is the answer to this question. It has instead decided that the answer is “not yet” — that the Supreme Court will eventually intervene, that something will break the logjam, that the sheer weight of urgency will eventually bend the law’s arc toward what it wants. And it is filing as though that belief were true.

The Portal Problem

One detail from Talwani’s preliminary injunction deserves to sit in the centre of all of this, because it explains why the administration’s repeated filings are not just losing, but are losing for reasons that have nothing to do with jurisprudence or constitutional law. The USPS portal — the system into which states are supposed to upload their voter lists and receive approval before mailing ballots — was not going to be ready, according to the administration’s own admission in court, until sometime the week after the Supreme Court heard the emergency application on Thursday, September 3rd.

This is not a procedural delay. It is not a matter of filing the right paperwork or finding the right legal theory. The technology does not exist yet. An election official in a county that is supposed to start mailing ballots on September 19th cannot upload voter information to a system that has not been built. That is not an obstacle the Court can overcome with a favorable ruling. That is an obstacle that requires time, and time is the one thing this entire exercise has already run out of.

As Judge Talwani wrote: “Through the Final Rule, the USPS is refusing to send mail ballots to eligible voters unless States or local election officials submit their mail ballot envelopes and return envelopes and receive advance approval from the USPS without regard to impending deadlines for the November 3, 2026 election. It also requires elections officials to create accounts for a new electronic portal, certify that they have received approval regarding the mail ballot design, and thereafter upload to the USPS portal each voter’s information, including name, address, and a unique intelligent mail barcode.”

And then the judge asked the question that cuts through every legal filing the administration has submitted: “How?” Not as rhetorical flourish, but as a genuine procedural puzzle. How does a county that has sixty days until it is supposed to mail ballots create accounts on a system that does not yet exist, certify that it has received approval from an agency that has not yet built the system, and upload voter information to a portal that is still, as of the day the administration filed its emergency application, somewhere between one week and “sometime next week” away from existence?

The answer, which the administration has never satisfactorily provided, is: it doesn’t. And no Supreme Court ruling can change that fact.

The Constitutional Weakness

Beyond the logistics problem sits a problem that matters more: the law. Judge Talwani found that the administration sought to “avoid” the separation-of-powers issue by “denying that the final rule is an election rule” — a denial she wrote “rings hollow.” The Constitution gives Congress, not the executive, responsibility over the “Times, Places, and Manner” of federal elections. The USPS, acting on the President’s executive order, was attempting to exercise that power. Talwani found that “throughout its body of election legislation, Congress has never delegated its Elections Clause power to the USPS,” and that the rule “clashes with Congress’s statutory scheme, and is unconstitutional where it intrudes not only on Congress’s elections clause powers but also that power left to the states.” This is not a procedural objection or a timeline complaint. This is a judge saying the administration does not have the authority to do what it is asking to do, full stop.

The Pattern Recognition Problem

What emerges from the last week is not a legal strategy but a pattern — the shape of an administration that has concluded that the correct response to losing is to file the same motion again, with more urgency and a different framing, on the assumption that something will eventually give. Part XLV, in an earlier iteration of this series, called this pattern TACO: Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The threat phase was the executive order itself, signed in March and defended through the summer. The accommodation phase was the pivot to the courts, the argument that yes, the administration understands the law, here is why the law supports us. The capitulation phase would be accepting the preliminary injunction, the temporary restraining order, the district court ruling, and moving on. The obfuscation phase is what is happening now: filing the same motion again, hoping that urgency and repetition will somehow transmute defeat into delay, and delay into eventual implementation.

The difference between capitulation and obfuscation matters, because obfuscation does not require winning. It only requires that the question remain unsettled long enough for the election to arrive anyway. An election official in North Carolina, watching this cascade of filings and preliminary injunctions and emergency appeals, does not need a final Supreme Court ruling to understand that the risk of some ballot not clearing a scanner on some barcode check has now been taken seriously by the federal government, even if the courts keep saying that rule is not allowed. The ambient uncertainty — the signal that this might still happen, that the administration is not done trying, that there is still time for something to break in the administration’s favour — is itself the product being manufactured here.

By this logic, the administration does not need to win the Supreme Court case. It only needs the case to stay in front of the Supreme Court long enough for the November elections to arrive, for the confusion to settle in, for a percentage of voters to decide that the safest thing is simply not to vote by mail, regardless of what the courts have said. The preliminary injunction is not a defeat, from this angle, so much as an inconvenience that the administration will simply file away and re-litigate the moment the law allows.

The Question the Supreme Court Cannot Avoid

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, the justice designated to handle emergency appeals from the 1st Circuit, ordered the plaintiff states to respond to the Trump administration’s emergency application. A decision from the full Court could come at any time. But the question the Court faces now is not the one Solicitor General Sauer posed in the original filing. It is not: “Does the administration have the authority to restrict mail-in voting?” That question has already been litigated. Judges appointed by presidents of both parties have concluded that the answer is no, or at least “not in the time frame you are proposing, not with the technological infrastructure you currently possess, not in a way that will not risk massive disenfranchisement.”

The question the Court faces now is: “What do we do with a party that has lost and is asking us to let it lose again, on the theory that asking twice is worth the effort?” That is not a constitutional question. It is a question about the nature of finality, about what it means for a court to issue an order, about whether an administration that has exhausted its emergency appeals is entitled to file them again because new hours have passed and urgency has increased.

The answer, under ordinary appellate procedure, is no. An emergency application that has been denied does not become a new emergency application simply because the applicant believes the emergency has worsened. But we do not live in ordinary appellate times, and the Supreme Court’s recent posture toward the Trump administration’s election-related requests has not exactly been one of strict adherence to ordinary procedure. The Court allowed the administration to implement this rule on August 24th, 2026, on the narrowest of grounds — that the implementing rule had not yet been finalized. Two weeks later, the rule was finalized and a judge blocked it. The administration went back to the Supreme Court. The Court’s response to that second request is what the next few days will answer.

The Chaos of Waiting By Wednesday evening, with the Supreme Court’s response deadline passed and no ruling forthcoming, election officials across the country were operating in real time in the world the friction strategy had already created. As Sullivan and Sella reported for The Times, officials in Madison had mailed 18,000 ballots more than a week early just to get them out the door before any surprise ruling changed the rules mid-process. In Weber County, Utah — a deeply red state that votes almost exclusively by mail — officials were considering abandoning mail voting entirely and setting up backup distribution centers instead. In Philadelphia, where ballots were scheduled to go out the following week, the Democratic vice chair of the city commissioners called the situation “just chaos.” As she put it: “Quite frankly, we don’t even really have a way to prepare because we don’t know when that ruling is going to come down, and then we’re going to get state guidance, and then we are crunching up closer and closer to Election Day.”

North Carolina had already mailed ballots with no idea whether the Postal Service would somehow verify them retroactively if the new rule took effect. An election official in Mecklenburg County simply said: “We have not been provided any further information regarding their process for verifying the ballots.” In Texas, a county clerk said she had “no confidence” that an untested federal portal could even connect to the state’s own election system, which was still buggy months after launch. The administration had not won the mail-voting case. But it had already won the conversation in the sense that the conversation now existed at all — and election officials were living in it, paralyzed and unable to plan because the question of what the rules would be remained unsettled.

What We Know About “No”

One of the facts this series has established in two installments is that “no” from a judge is not the same thing as “no” from a voter. A court order blocking a rule is not the same thing as a court order preventing the ambient uncertainty that the rule has already generated. By the time Judge Talwani issued her preliminary injunction, millions of voters in dozens of states had already spent weeks wondering whether their ballot would clear a scanner, whether the barcode on their envelope was going to be the one out of ten thousand that got flagged, whether the federal government had won a battle that the courts kept saying it had not. And by Wednesday evening, election officials were spending their time the same way: trying to figure out how to run an election when nobody knew what the rules would be.

The preliminary injunction can block the rule. It cannot un-ring the bell of those weeks of uncertainty, or un-generate the voter suppression by friction that already exists in the minds of voters who have spent September watching this litigation cascade through the courts. What it can do is sit on the Court’s docket while the justices decide whether five votes exist for granting emergency relief to an administration that has now asked for it repeatedly, within hours of being told no, on the theory that urgency and repetition are arguments in law. The Court’s answer — whether it comes by Thursday, Friday, or simply by the passage of time — will itself be an answer. Sometimes the silence of a judge is the loudest ruling of all.
