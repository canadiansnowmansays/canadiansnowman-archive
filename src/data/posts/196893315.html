Ronald V. Dellums Federal Courthouse, Oakland, California

A companion series to The Asimov Gap, CanadianSnowmanSays

The line to enter the courthouse Thursday was the shortest since the trial began. The crowd had come for Musk. With Musk gone, so had the crowd. Inside, the jury heard from three witnesses who collectively described not a company that had strayed from its mission by accident, but one that had systematically dismantled the structures designed to enforce it.

The day’s testimony produced what may be the trial’s most significant governance disclosure — not a dramatic confrontation, not a text message, not a journal entry about billions. A quiet statement from a former safety employee about two teams that no longer exist.

On the same day the courthouse heard this testimony, OpenAI announced a new Trusted Contact feature for ChatGPT — allowing users to designate someone to receive automated alerts if their conversations suggest self-harm risk. The timing was not commented on by either side.

Rosie Campbell: The Teams That Were Dissolved

Rosie Campbell arrives at the Ronald V. Dellums Federal Courthouse, Oakland, May 7, 2026. A former member of OpenAI's AGI Readiness Team, Campbell testified that both the Readiness Team and the Superalignment Team were dissolved at the end of 2024. "It would be difficult to achieve the mission without that kind of work being done." She left OpenAI shortly after.

Rosie Campbell joined OpenAI’s AGI Readiness Team in 2021. The team was charged with thinking about the risks of AGI and how to mitigate them. It was particularly concerned, she said, about “concentrations of power” — a small number of companies controlling disproportionate amounts of AI capability. OpenAI also had a Superalignment Team, focused on ensuring that AI is developed in alignment with human values and remains under human control.

When Campbell joined, she said, the company was “very research-focused and common for people to talk about AGI and safety issues.” Over time, it became more like a product-focused organization.

She was there in November 2023 when the board fired and then reinstated Altman. After the reinstatement, two independent board members resigned and were replaced. She was concerned their replacements did not have “the same kind of safety experience” as the previous members.

Then, at the end of 2024, she was told that the AGI Readiness Team was going to be dissolved. The Superalignment Team would also be disbanded. Members were given the opportunity to find other roles within OpenAI. About 50 percent of them stayed — some in roles with safety elements, some without. Campbell looked for opportunities to continue the work she had come to do. She did not find them. She left OpenAI.

“It would be difficult to achieve the mission without that kind of work being done,” she said from the stand.

She also pointed to a specific incident: Microsoft deployed a version of GPT-4 in India through its Bing search engine before the model had been evaluated by OpenAI’s Deployment Safety Board. The model itself did not present a huge risk, Campbell said, but the company needed “to set strong precedents as the technology gets more powerful. We want to have good safety processes in place we know are being followed reliably.”

Under cross-examination, Campbell held the distinction that defined her testimony: “The mission itself is to ensure that AGI benefits all of humanity, which doesn’t necessarily imply that you have to build it if you can’t ensure it’s gonna be safe.” Computing power was needed for commercial products, she acknowledged, but it was “not necessarily needed to achieve the mission.”

Campbell also explained why she signed the letter supporting Altman’s return after the firing — a detail OpenAI’s lawyers clearly wanted on the record. Her reason was not endorsement of Altman’s leadership. It was a calculation about the alternative. “It was my understanding at the time that the best way for OpenAI to not disintegrate and fall apart would be for Sam to return,” she said. She feared that without him, OpenAI employees would end up at Microsoft — which she believed would be even less devoted to AI safety. She chose the lesser of two safety evils. She still testified that both safety teams were subsequently dissolved and that she left because she could not find safety work to do.

One final moment from cross-examination belongs in the record. Campbell was asked her speculative opinion on whether OpenAI’s approach to safety was better than xAI’s — Musk’s AI company. She said she believed OpenAI’s was better. The plaintiff’s own witness testified that the defendant has a superior safety culture to the plaintiff’s competing company.

Natasha McCauley: The Evil Coup

Natasha McCauley, former OpenAI board member, testified by video deposition on May 7, 2026. She joined the nonprofit board in 2018, voted to fire Sam Altman in November 2023, and resigned after his reinstatement. "If it all comes down to one CEO making those decisions, and we have the public good at stake, that's very suboptimal." She is now calling for government regulation of advanced AI.

Natasha McCauley joined OpenAI’s nonprofit board of directors in November 2018. She resigned in November 2023, after Altman was reinstated as CEO. Her testimony was delivered via video deposition.

She described joining the board as “cautiously optimistic.” The organization, she believed, was “oriented around its mission” and had been “structured to resist pressure from investors.” That structure depended on leadership keeping the board informed. It did not work that way.

The board had “buckets of concerns” about Altman’s leadership. Resistance to oversight. Lies. Concerns from OpenAI’s senior leadership. There were “repeated crisis events stemming from Sam’s behavior.” Altman had fostered, she said, a “culture of lying and culture of deceit” that had trickled down to other members of OpenAI’s leadership.

“We had real doubts that we could trust what the CEO was telling us,” McCauley said. “We are a nonprofit board and our mandate was to be able to oversee the for-profit underneath us. Our primary way to do that was being called into question. We did not have a high degree of confidence at all to trust that the information being conveyed to us allowed us to make decisions in an informed way.”

McCauley testified to specific deceptions. Altman lied to another board member about McCauley’s intention to remove Helen Toner — a third board member who had published a white paper with implied criticism of OpenAI’s safety policy. He failed to inform the board about the decision to launch ChatGPT publicly. He wrongly told the board that OpenAI’s legal department had cleared GPT-4 Turbo from requiring internal safety board review before its launch in India. He failed to disclose potential conflicts of interest.

Ilya Sutskever — OpenAI’s former chief scientist, whose 70-page memos to the board were documented in the Farrow-Marantz investigation — sent McCauley an email describing “dozens of pages of examples of different chaotic events that had occurred from Sam’s behavior or lies that he had told.” Those memos are now doubly documented: in the investigation and in the trial record.

After the board fired Altman, McCauley and other board members received reports that Altman and Brockman were calling employees saying: “There’s been an evil coup.” When staff sided with Altman and Microsoft worked to restore the status quo, the board reversed course. The governance mechanism worked exactly once and was then dismantled from the outside using the company’s own employees as leverage.

McCauley also testified about a conflict of interest that had not previously been publicly documented. The board discussed whether companies in Altman’s Startup Fund were receiving early access to OpenAI features before general release — potentially giving them commercial advantages. The board was concerned the fund may have been diverting returns from OpenAI investors. McCauley said she remembered discussion of the concern but could not recall specifically whether it had happened. Her deposition then turned to a question whose answer has never been made public: on November 16, 2023 — the day the board voted to remove Altman — did he recuse himself from his position as board member and CEO? The trial record now contains the question. The answer has not yet been reported.

McCauley’s closing statement is the trial’s most important line from any witness who is not Musk: “If it all comes down to one CEO making those decisions, and we have the public good at stake, that’s very suboptimal.” She said the failures of internal governance at OpenAI should be a reason to embrace stronger government regulation of advanced AI. The board member who voted to fire Altman, who watched the governance mechanism be dismantled from the outside, has concluded that internal governance of transformative AI cannot work.

David Schizer: The Expert’s Conclusions

David Schizer, a former Columbia Law School dean, testified for Elon Musk as an expert witness on nonprofit governance. Tom Williams/CQ-Roll Call, Inc via Getty Images

David Schizer, former dean of Columbia Law School, was paid $1,500 per hour — compare to Stuart Russell’s $5,000 — and estimated his total compensation at between $300,000 and $375,000. He has never testified as an expert before. He clerked for Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg.

His testimony was technical by design but its conclusions were sharp. A nonprofit’s fundamental purpose is to pursue its mission, not profits. Nonprofit corporations do not have owners. The board’s responsibility is to oversee officers and employees to ensure the mission is being fulfilled — and if a CEO is not advancing the mission, it is appropriate to discharge them.

Schizer then applied those standards to OpenAI. Assuming Musk’s lawyers prove the facts they used in their questions, he said, OpenAI has not followed the customary practices of nonprofit corporations in a number of key areas.

On the ChatGPT-4 Turbo release: Altman released the model without a safety review and falsely told others that no review was required. “Part of that is taking safety rules seriously,” Schizer said. “If something needs to be subject to safety review, it needs to happen. What matters is the process issue.” A board cannot accept that conduct even if the model turned out to be safe.

On the Microsoft transactions: both the 2023 $10 billion investment and the 2025 recapitalization gave up significant nonprofit value without adequate evaluation of what the nonprofit received in return.

On cross-examination, Microsoft’s lawyer Randall Jackson pressed the counter-argument: the nonprofit’s stake in the for-profit is currently estimated to be worth $200 billion. “Is there any aspect of its mission that OpenAI cannot pursue because it doesn’t have enough money?” he asked.

Schizer’s reply — reported by The Information under the headline “OpenAI Foundation Should Have ‘A Lot More’ Than $200 Billion” — was quiet and precise: if the board had fulfilled its responsibility, OpenAI could have had more money. The question is not whether OpenAI is rich. The question is whether it became rich by honouring its founding promise.

What Earlier Witnesses Added

Two disclosures from earlier in the week deserve the record they have not fully received.

Mira Murati’s deposition described Altman as someone who would frequently tell one person one thing and the complete opposite to another, leading executives to pit themselves against each other and putting OpenAI at “catastrophic risk” of falling apart. She originally wanted Altman to remain CEO despite this — not because she trusted him, but because she believed his removal would cause the implosion she feared.

Shivon Zilis testified that the board felt “extreme concern” about the launch of ChatGPT — done without proper communication with the board. McCauley’s deposition confirms and extends this: Altman actively hid information about the launch from board members.

The Wired reporting surfaced one additional Zilis detail from cross-examination on Day 7 that belongs in the record. In the months before Musk left OpenAI’s board in February 2018, he tried to recruit Altman to lead a “world-class AI lab” within Tesla — offering him a Tesla board seat. Emails and testimony presented during Zilis’s cross-examination document this. The man who is suing Altman for betraying the nonprofit mission first tried to bring Altman with him when he left.

The Brockman detail from Part 4 also belongs in the permanent record. During the August 2017 Seattle meeting, Musk tore a painting of a Tesla Model 3 off the wall before issuing his funding ultimatum and storming out. The founding feud has a physical choreography: confetti on the floor, Sutskever’s handmade Tesla painting as a gift, Musk tearing a different Tesla painting from the wall, Brockman standing there wondering if he was about to be hit.

What This Confirms

Cross-references to The Asimov Gap

The dissolution of the safety teams is the trial’s most significant governance disclosure. The AGI Readiness Team and the Superalignment Team were OpenAI’s institutional answer to the governance question this series has been asking for thirty-five parts. They no longer exist. They were dissolved quietly, at the end of 2024, more than a year after the crisis that was supposed to have resolved the governance problem. The pattern examined in Part 28 — setting up guardrails and navigating around them — extended to the safety infrastructure itself.

Campbell’s distinction between means and ends is the clearest articulation of the safety argument in eight days of testimony. The mission is to ensure AGI benefits humanity. That does not necessarily imply you have to build it if you cannot ensure it will be safe. That sentence belongs in the trial record’s permanent summary.

McCauley’s “culture of lying and culture of deceit” testimony confirms the Farrow-Marantz pattern under oath from someone inside the governance structure. She is not Musk’s partisan. She signed the letter that brought Altman back. She still described a CEO who lied to her, lied to colleagues, fostered a culture that replicated that behavior, and may have participated in his own removal vote without recusing himself. The Sutskever email — “dozens of pages of examples” — is now doubly documented.

McCauley’s call for external regulation is the governance argument of this series stated by someone who tried internal governance and watched it fail. The board voted to fire Altman. The mechanism was dismantled from the outside. Her conclusion: stronger government regulation of advanced AI is necessary. This is Parts 28, 30, and 31 confirmed from inside the boardroom.

The Schizer reply to the $200 billion argument is the dispatch’s closing thought. If the board had fulfilled its responsibility, OpenAI could have had more money. The governance failure was not a necessary cost of commercial success. It was a choice.
