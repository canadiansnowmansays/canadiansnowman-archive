Explosions and smoke rise over Tehran during the opening hours of the US-Israeli strikes, February 28, 2026. This is what the end of the multilateral order looks like up close: not abstract legitimacy debates, but the immediate human cost on the ground.

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched coordinated air strikes against Iran.

There was no UN Security Council authorization. There had been no consultation with NATO allies — or with any allies other than Israel. Congress had not been asked for a vote. The American public had not been informed in advance. The day before the strikes, the United States was ostensibly engaged in diplomatic discussions with Iran through Pakistani intermediaries.

“Iran was attacked in the middle of a diplomatic process,” Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman said on March 31, 2026. “And this has happened for the second time within nine months.”

He was right. The June 2025 strikes — twelve days of American and Israeli bombing of Iranian nuclear facilities, after which Benjamin Netanyahu declared “a historic victory” — had also been conducted outside any multilateral framework, also without the arms control process reaching a definitive failure, also as a unilateral assertion of American and Israeli military judgment against the constraints of the diplomatic process.

The second round, launched in February 2026, was the conclusion that the first round had made inevitable. Iran had used the nine months between the two assaults to rebuild its nuclear program — faster than before, with better understanding of what it needed to protect, with the demonstrated lesson that diplomatic engagement provided no protection against American military action. The IRGC had spent those nine months expanding its missile inventory, dispersing its assets, hardening its underground “missile cities,” and developing the drone and missile tactics that have now produced a hit rate that doubles approximately every three weeks.

Satellite view of damage at Iran’s Natanz nuclear facility after the strikes. The war aimed to stop nuclear proliferation but set a precedent that may accelerate it: nuclear-armed states can strike preventively outside UN/NPT frameworks.

The Iran war of 2026 is, in this sense, the culmination of everything this series has documented. It is the credibility trap from Part IV, applied to a new theater. It is the failure to learn from the self-inflicted wounds of Part VII, repeated at larger scale. It is the consequence of the JCPOA withdrawal from Part IX, now presenting its bill. It is the product of the rogue superpower dynamic that Robert Kagan identified in The Atlantic — a nation simultaneously dominant and destabilizing, acting outside the order it built, generating the very chaos it claimed to be preventing.

The War’s Character

The Iran war of 2026 is unlike any previous American military engagement in one fundamental respect: it is a war against a country that has successfully denied the United States the conventional victory that American military doctrine assumes.

Previous American wars — Korea, Vietnam, the Gulf War, Iraq, Afghanistan — were fought against adversaries whose military capacity was either roughly comparable to American forces or dramatically inferior. In Korea and Vietnam, the adversary had sufficient conventional capability to impose real costs. In the Gulf War and Iraq 2003, the adversary was destroyed in weeks by American air and ground power. In Afghanistan, the adversary was so inferior in conventional terms that it simply dispersed and waited.

Iran is different. Iran’s military strategy was designed, over four decades, specifically for this scenario — a war against American air power that it cannot match. The IRGC’s doctrine is not to win conventional military engagements. It is to impose costs sufficient to make the American public and the American political system conclude that the war is not worth its price. The Strait of Hormuz blockade is the strategic instrument of this doctrine — the lever that translates military action into economic pain into political pressure.

The doctrine is working. Gas is above $4 a gallon in the United States for the first time since 2022. The stock market has lost $12 trillion in value since the war began. The S&P 500 is in correction territory. Eurozone inflation has risen to its fastest pace in a year. The UNDP projects that one month of war has cost the Arab region $194 billion and pushed 4 million more people into poverty. Australian states are making public transit free to cushion the energy price spike. Canadian gas prices have risen 40 percent.

The war is generating the economic pain that Iran’s doctrine requires to be generated. And the American political response — Trump looking for an exit ramp, markets bouncing on reports that he is willing to wind down without securing a full Hormuz opening, Hegseth’s concession that Iran retains the ability to launch missiles — is exactly the political response that Iran’s doctrine is designed to produce.

This is what the post-war order’s absence looks like in strategic terms. An adversary that understands the mechanism by which economic pain translates into political pressure in a democratic system, and that has designed its military strategy around exploiting that mechanism, while the United States conducts a war without the multilateral legitimacy that would have broadened the cost-sharing and the political coalition necessary to sustain the effort.

The Order’s Verdict on Itself

The international response to the Iran war is the post-war order’s verdict on what the United States has become.

Spain refused American use of its air and naval bases for combat operations — the first time a NATO member had explicitly denied the United States basing rights for offensive operations against a third country. The Iberian Peninsula, from which the United States has historically staged air operations in the Middle East, was unavailable. The American bombers are flying from RAF Fairford in England — bases that Britain officially maintains are being used only for “defensive” purposes, while quietly acknowledging that the distinction has been blurred beyond recognition.

Germany, France, and the rest of continental Europe have withheld support. Not because they support Iran or are indifferent to nuclear proliferation — but because the war was conducted without consultation, without authorization, and in violation of the diplomatic process that had been underway when the bombs fell. The precedent that nuclear-armed states can launch wars of choice against diplomatic partners, without multilateral authorization, whenever they judge the security threat sufficient, is a precedent that no European democracy can endorse without undermining its own claim to live within a rules-based international order.

Japan and South Korea — the Asian allies whose oil dependence makes them among the war’s most direct economic victims — were “not receiving any communication from the Trump administration” in the war’s first weeks, according to their own diplomats. The countries whose energy security depends most directly on Hormuz were informed of the war’s outbreak by news reports rather than allied consultation.

The United Nations Security Council has been irrelevant. Russia and China — both with veto power — have not even needed to use it. The United States has not sought authorization. The war is being conducted outside the multilateral framework that the United States helped build after the Second World War precisely to prevent wars of this kind.

Robert Kagan’s epitaph is accurate and worth repeating: “One would be hard-pressed to find any nation in the world that has been reassured by the Israeli and American war against Iran, other than Israel itself.”

The Thread From Keenleyside to Hegseth

This series began with Hugh Keenleyside in the conference rooms of the 1940s, watching the post-war order being built. It ends with Pete Hegseth at the Pentagon podium on March 31, 2026, saying “we negotiate with bombs.”

The thread connecting them runs through everything this series has documented: the architecture that was built and why it worked; the stresses it absorbed and the lessons that were drawn; the failures that damaged it and the recoveries that seemed to restore it; the rivals who filled the vacuum American overextension created; the domestic fractures that eroded the political foundation on which international commitments depend; and the accumulating consequences of treating multilateral institutions as obstacles rather than assets.

What Keenleyside understood — what his generation understood, from lived experience of what unconstrained power produces — was that the architecture of restraint is not an obstacle to American effectiveness. It is its precondition. An America embedded in legitimate multilateral institutions is an America whose power is amplified by the cooperation of others. An America that has repudiated those institutions is an America that must pay every cost alone, bear every burden alone, and justify every action alone — and that therefore reaches the limits of its power faster, pays higher prices, and achieves less durable outcomes.

The Iran war is illustrating this principle in real time. The United States has struck more than 11,000 targets. It has killed Iran’s supreme leader, its top military commanders, and most of its experienced leadership. It has reduced Iran’s conventional military capacity dramatically. And it has not achieved its stated objectives. The Strait of Hormuz is closed. Iran’s nuclear program’s enriched uranium remains at Isfahan. The IRGC runs Iran. And the new supreme leader — Mojtaba Khamenei, more hardline than his father — is a product of the war that was supposed to produce regime change.

Hegseth’s “we negotiate with bombs” is not just a rhetorical failure. It is a strategic failure — the reduction of diplomacy to coercion, of international order to force, of legitimacy to capability. It is the final repudiation of everything that Keenleyside’s generation built.

What Has Been Lost

Let me try to be specific about what the order’s end means, concretely, for the people who will live in the world it leaves behind.

Freedom of navigation has been suspended in the Strait of Hormuz — Iran’s Parliament has legislated the toll system, and the claim to sovereignty over the waterway has been institutionalized. The precedent that nuclear-armed states can launch preventive wars without UN authorization, without exhausting diplomatic alternatives, without the NPT framework — has been established, and every country currently calculating whether nuclear deterrence is worth the cost is drawing the obvious conclusion. The alliance system has been publicly threatened: at a Saudi sovereign wealth fund conference in Miami, Trump said that because NATO nations had not joined the Iran war, “I guess we don’t have to be there for them.” Article 5, invoked once in seventy-seven years, is now contingent on European participation in American wars of choice. And the dollar’s reserve currency status — the petrodollar system built on Gulf oil — is under pressure at exactly the chokepoint the system requires to stay open, accelerating the diversification toward Chinese alternative architecture that has been underway for a decade.

None of these losses is irreversible, in theory. International orders have been rebuilt after catastrophic failures. The post-war order itself was built from the rubble of the worst catastrophe in human history — and it required extraordinary leadership, sustained commitment, genuine sacrifice, and the wisdom to construct frameworks that constrained the builders as well as everyone else. Whether that wisdom can be found in the current moment — or in the moment that follows the Iran war’s end — is the question the conclusion of this series addresses.

The Last Word on Keenleyside

Hugh Keenleyside, when he joined the Canadian Department of External Affairs in 1928, was twenty-nine years old. The world he entered was the world of the interwar period — the years of fragile peace between two catastrophes, the years when the League of Nations seemed to offer the possibility of a different kind of international order and then demonstrated, in Manchuria and Abyssinia and the Rhineland, that the possibility was insufficient without the commitment to enforce it.

He spent the next four decades trying to build the commitment that would make the possibility real. He succeeded, in the ways that matter — the institutions he helped construct outlasted him, outlasted the Cold War, outlasted even the first Trump term. They held in Ukraine in 2022, when it seemed they might not.

They are not holding in Iran in 2026.

I do not know if the order can be rebuilt. I do not know if the damage being done in the spring of 2026 — to alliances, to institutions, to norms, to the basic principle that power requires legitimacy to be sustainable — can be repaired in a generation, or two, or three. I do not know if the world that emerges from this catastrophe will find its way to the kind of legitimate multilateral architecture that Keenleyside’s generation built, or whether it will find something worse, or something different, or nothing at all.

What I know is that the attempt to build it was not naive. It was not idealism. It was the most hard-headed, clear-eyed, strategically sophisticated project in the history of international relations — the recognition, forged in the experience of the worst catastrophe in human history, that power without legitimacy is unstable, that dominance without consent is temporary, and that the most durable form of security is the kind that benefits its recipients enough that they defend it as their own.

That is what has been lost.

That is what will need to be rebuilt.

And the builders of whatever comes next — in whatever language they speak, from whatever country they come, carrying whatever weight of history they inherit — will need to understand what was built before them and why it mattered.

This series has been one attempt to help them understand.

From the 1945 conference rooms where Hugh Keenleyside’s generation built the architecture of restraint, to the spring of 2026 where that architecture is being dismantled from within. The question for the next generation is whether the wisdom to rebuild it can still be found.
