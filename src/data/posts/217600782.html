Part XIV followed the French crown from the gilded absolutism of Versailles to the guillotine. Russia’s monarchy offers this series a variation on the same story, stretched over a much longer arc and resolved far more violently, and it adds a figure France produced only as a curiosity: the pretender, the ordinary man who claims to be a dead or missing tsar and, astonishingly often, finds an army willing to believe him. Russia produced more of these impostors than any other monarchy examined anywhere in this series, and their repeated success says something important about how Russian royal legitimacy actually worked. When the only recognized source of authority is the person of the tsar himself, with no parliament, no independent church, and no settled law of succession standing between the throne and the subject, the most effective way to challenge a ruler is not to argue against tsardom at all. It is to claim to be the true tsar.

The office took its modern shape under Ivan IV, known to history as Ivan the Terrible, who in January of 1547, at the age of sixteen, became the first Grand Prince of Moscow to be formally crowned as tsar, a title derived from the Latin caesar and chosen deliberately to claim the inheritance of Rome and of Byzantium, whose capital had fallen to the Ottomans less than a century earlier in the siege this series will examine in Part XXIII. Muscovite churchmen had already begun describing Moscow as the Third Rome, the true heir of Christian empire after the first Rome had fallen to heresy and the second, Constantinople, to the Turks, and Ivan’s coronation gave that theology a crown. His reign combined genuine achievement, including the conquest of the Tatar khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan and a new law code, with an escalating campaign of terror. Beginning in 1565, he carved out a personal domain called the oprichnina, policed by black-robed horsemen who carried a dog’s head and a broom on their saddles to signify that they would sniff out and sweep away the tsar’s enemies, and who conducted years of purges, confiscations, and massacres, most notoriously the sack of the city of Novgorod in 1570. In 1581, in a fit of rage, Ivan struck and killed his own eldest son and heir, a single blow that would, within a generation, bring his entire dynasty to an end.

Ivan’s surviving heir, Feodor, was pious and widely regarded as unfit to rule, and when he died childless in 1598, the Rurikid line that had governed the Russian lands for over seven centuries died with him. Ivan’s youngest son, Dmitry, had already died in 1591 at the age of eight, of a knife wound in the provincial town of Uglich, officially ruled an accident suffered during an epileptic fit but widely rumored to have been murder on the orders of Boris Godunov, the powerful regent who then succeeded Feodor as tsar. What followed was the period Russians call the Time of Troubles, fifteen years of famine, civil war, foreign invasion, and competing claimants, and it produced the most remarkable pretender this series has encountered anywhere. In 1603 a young man appeared in Polish-Lithuanian territory claiming to be Dmitry himself, miraculously saved from the assassin’s knife at Uglich. Backed by Polish magnates and a small army, this False Dmitry marched on Moscow, and following Godunov’s sudden death in 1605, Moscow opened its gates to him. He was crowned tsar that July, and in a detail almost impossible to invent, Ivan’s widow, the real Dmitry’s mother, publicly embraced him as her son. His reign lasted less than a year: in May of 1606, boyars offended by his Polish Catholic entourage and his Catholic bride stormed the Kremlin and killed him, and his ashes were reportedly fired from a cannon back toward Poland. Within a year a second False Dmitry had appeared, gathering enough support to besiege Moscow from a camp at Tushino, and in 1610 Polish forces occupied the Kremlin itself.

The Troubles ended not through conquest but through something closer to an election. After a popular militia raised in Nizhny Novgorod drove the Polish garrison from Moscow in 1612, an assembly of the land, the Zemsky Sobor, drawing representatives from the clergy, the nobility, the townspeople, and even some free peasants, met in early 1613 and chose sixteen-year-old Michael Romanov as tsar, a candidate acceptable largely because his family was connected by marriage to Ivan the Terrible’s first wife and because he was young enough to offend no faction. It is one of the more striking moments in this entire series: a dynasty whose later rulers would claim authority answerable to God alone owed its very existence to a representative assembly, a founding bargain the Romanovs spent the next three centuries doing their best to forget. The Zemsky Sobor met with diminishing frequency through the seventeenth century and then ceased to meet at all, following the same path to disuse this series traced through France’s Estates-General in Part XIV.

It was Peter I, Peter the Great, reigning from 1682 until 1725, who turned Muscovite tsardom into something closer to the absolutism Louis XIV was simultaneously perfecting at Versailles, and he did so with a single-mindedness that makes Louis look almost casual. After traveling through Western Europe in 1697 and 1698, working for a time in a Dutch shipyard, Peter set out to remake Russia along Western lines by decree: he forced his nobles to shave their beards or pay a tax for the privilege of keeping them, built an entirely new capital, St. Petersburg, on marshland at enormous human cost, and in 1721 took the title of Emperor. That same year he abolished the office of Patriarch, the independent head of the Russian Orthodox Church, and replaced it with a Holy Synod supervised by a lay official answerable to the crown, completing the subordination of church to state in a manner reminiscent of Henry VIII’s break with Rome in Part VII, though accomplished without the excuse of a divorce. His Table of Ranks, introduced in 1722, tied noble status to service to the state rather than birth alone. And like Ivan before him, he did not spare his own son: the Tsarevich Alexei, suspected of sympathizing with opponents of his father’s reforms, fled abroad, was lured home, and died in 1718 after an interrogation under torture that Peter personally authorized. Peter then decreed that each ruler could name his own successor, and died in 1725 without clearly doing so.

The result was a century in which the throne passed repeatedly by palace coup, often orchestrated by the regiments of the Imperial Guard, and its most successful beneficiary was a woman with no Romanov blood at all. Catherine II, a minor German princess born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst and married to the heir who became Peter III, seized the throne from her husband in the summer of 1762, barely six months into his reign, with the backing of the Guards; Peter was dead within days, officially of a sudden illness, almost certainly killed by men close to the new empress. Catherine went on to reign for thirty-four years as one of the most celebrated rulers in Russian history, corresponding with Voltaire and Diderot and pushing Russia’s borders to the Black Sea, which makes her, in effect, the most successful self-crowned monarch in this entire series, a usurper whose legitimacy was built retroactively out of competence and conquest in precisely the way Napoleon attempted, and failed, to manage in Part XIII.

It was Catherine’s usurpation, fittingly, that produced Russia’s most dangerous pretender. In 1773 a Don Cossack deserter named Yemelyan Pugachev proclaimed that he was the murdered Peter III, having supposedly escaped his wife’s assassins, and he raised a rebellion along the Volga and in the Urals that drew in Cossacks, runaway serfs, factory laborers, and Bashkir tribesmen with promises of freedom from serfdom and the restoration of their old liberties. At its height the rebellion burned much of the city of Kazan and alarmed Moscow itself, and Pugachev kept a mock court at which his lieutenants took the names of Catherine’s own courtiers. Betrayed by his followers, he was captured in 1774 and executed in Moscow in January of 1775. That a Cossack could raise an army of tens of thousands by claiming a dead tsar’s name, against a reigning empress who had herself taken the throne over that same tsar’s body, says a great deal about how little Russian legitimacy rested on anything but the name of the ruler. It was the tsar, not tsardom, that ordinary Russians were prepared to fight over.

The nineteenth century gave the Romanovs repeated warnings. In December of 1825, a group of officers, many of them veterans of the campaigns against Napoleon who had seen constitutional government in Western Europe at first hand, staged a revolt in St. Petersburg in favor of a constitution; Nicholas I crushed the Decembrist uprising in a single afternoon and spent the next thirty years building the most thoroughly policed autocracy in Europe. His son Alexander II, chastened by defeat in the Crimean War, emancipated Russia’s serfs in 1861, the most sweeping reform any Romanov ever undertook, and in March of 1881 was killed by a bomb thrown by revolutionaries on a St. Petersburg street, reportedly on the very day he had approved a modest plan for consultative representation. His son, Alexander III, drew from his father’s murder the lesson that reform invites assassination, shelved the plan, and raised his own heir, Nicholas, in the conviction that autocracy was a sacred trust not to be diluted. It is the French pattern examined in Part XIV in a harsher key: every concession either arrived too late or was withdrawn as soon as the danger seemed to pass.

Nicholas II presided over an autocracy that had granted only limited and grudging constitutional concessions, including a weak elected legislature called the Duma, after the failed revolution of 1905, and he faced catastrophic military losses and severe economic dislocation during the First World War that finally broke public patience with the entire imperial system. Bread shortages and worker strikes in Petrograd in February of 1917 escalated rapidly into a general uprising that Nicholas’s own generals, recognizing the army’s loyalty had itself eroded past the point of reliable use for suppression, urged him to answer by abdicating rather than risk a more total collapse of order, and Nicholas, isolated and increasingly disconnected from the scale of the crisis, formally abdicated on the second of March, 1917, both for himself and, in a decision of dubious legal standing, on behalf of his hemophiliac son and heir Alexei.

The provisional government that followed Nicholas’s abdication, led initially by liberal aristocrats and later by the socialist Alexander Kerensky, proved no more capable of managing Russia’s wartime exhaustion than the tsar had been, insisting on continuing an increasingly unpopular war effort against Germany even as army desertions mounted and food shortages worsened across Russia’s cities, and it was overthrown in turn by the more radical Bolshevik faction under Vladimir Lenin in the October Revolution later that same year, a second collapse within a single year that this series notes as a useful reminder that removing a failed monarch, on its own, guarantees nothing whatsoever about the stability or character of whatever government replaces it. Nicholas and his family, initially held under a relatively comfortable house arrest, were eventually moved further from the capital as civil war broke out between Bolshevik forces and a loose coalition of opposing White armies, and on the night of the sixteenth of July, 1918, fearing the approach of anti-Bolshevik forces who might free the family and use them as a rallying symbol for the wider counter-revolutionary cause, the local Bolshevik authorities in Yekaterinburg received orders to execute the entire family immediately. Nicholas, his wife Alexandra, their five children, and several remaining loyal servants were woken in the middle of the night, told they were being moved to a safer location, led into a basement room, and shot by a firing squad, the execution turning into a chaotic and prolonged ordeal as jewels sewn secretly into the family’s clothing as a hidden reserve of wealth deflected the initial bullets and forced the executioners to finish the killing with bayonets and additional gunfire.

Russia’s case shows an even more complete and deliberate extinguishing of a royal line than France’s, since the Bolsheviks ordered the execution of all five of Nicholas and Alexandra’s children alongside their parents, including the hemophiliac heir Alexei, only thirteen years old, precisely to eliminate any surviving claimant who might later be used to rally opposition to Bolshevik rule, where France’s revolutionary government had at least initially left the young Louis-Charles alive in captivity. The bodies were doused in acid, burned, and buried in a concealed forest location that was not conclusively identified and excavated until 1991, with the remains of two of the children, Alexei and his sister Maria, not located and confirmed until a second grave was found nearby in 2007, closing out, nearly a century after the fact, one of the last remaining uncertainties about precisely how completely the Romanov dynasty had been extinguished in that single chaotic night.

Russia’s aftermath, by contrast with France’s, shows no cycling back toward restored monarchy at all, and this permanence is itself worth noting. No serious Romanov restoration movement ever gained meaningful traction inside the Soviet Union or, for that matter, inside post-Soviet Russia. What survived instead was the oldest Russian royal tradition of all, the pretender. A series of women claimed to be the youngest daughter, Anastasia, supposedly spared at Yekaterinburg, claims that captured enormous popular and media fascination across Europe and North America for decades. The most famous of them, Anna Anderson, died in Virginia in 1984, and DNA testing a decade later showed she had in fact been a Polish factory worker named Franziska Schanzkowska, while testing on the recovered remains established that all four daughters had died alongside their parents that night. The False Dmitrys had once been able to take a throne on the strength of a dead child’s name; by the twentieth century, a borrowed Romanov name could win headlines, lawsuits, and a Hollywood film, but no longer a single soldier. The totality of the family’s physical extinction, rather than merely the political end of their rule, appears to have foreclosed a possibility that remained genuinely open in France for the better part of a century after Louis XVI’s own execution.

Both cases share the same underlying lesson for this series’ broader argument: monarchies that refuse every incremental accommodation of genuine and escalating popular grievance eventually face a stark choice between meaningful reform and total destruction, and both Louis and Nicholas, having refused the former for far too long, received the latter in full, along with their families, a fate this series will contrast directly in Part XVIII with a monarchy that read exactly the same warning signs and chose to bend rather than break.

One further comparison between the two cases is worth drawing out before moving on, because it bears directly on this series’ recurring interest in how a population’s own recent political memory shapes its tolerance for violence against a fallen monarch. France in 1793 had no living memory of any comparably total royal execution; Louis XVI’s death was, in a sense this series can appreciate fully only after tracing Charles I’s execution in Part VIII, a genuinely novel act for a country whose most recent civil convulsions had been religious wars fought between Catholics and Huguenots rather than a direct constitutional reckoning with the crown itself. Russia’s revolutionaries, by contrast, could draw on France’s well-documented precedent as both inspiration and instruction, and Bolshevik leaders explicitly discussed the fate of the French royal family, and the later revival of Bonapartism this series traced in Part XIII, as cautionary history to be studied and avoided rather than repeated. Lenin and his colleagues appear to have concluded from this study that France’s failure to fully eliminate the Bourbon line, and its subsequent decades of intermittent monarchist restoration, represented precisely the kind of unfinished business Russia’s own revolution could not afford to leave behind, a conclusion that helps explain why the Yekaterinburg execution was carried out with such comprehensive finality. History’s second telling of the same basic story, in other words, was deliberately made more thorough than its first specifically because the second regime had studied what it regarded as the first regime’s fatal incompleteness.

Part XVI turns to the one dynasty in this entire series that was never actually overthrown at all, surviving from mythic antiquity into the present simply by allowing its underlying claim to authority to be endlessly reinterpreted rather than removed by force.
