When both masks are laughing — and one of them is falling apart — Satire has nothing left to exaggerate. It can only resign.

To Whom It May Concern (and it concerns fewer people than it should):

I am writing to inform you that, effective immediately, I am resigning my position as the principal mechanism by which democratic societies process the absurd behaviour of their leaders.

My last day was May 21, 2026 — the same day Stephen Colbert’s was.

You may consider this a coincidence. I assure you it is not.

I have held this position, in one form or another, since Aristophanes mocked the Athenian assembly in 424 BC. I have served under Jonathan Swift, Mark Twain, Stephen Leacock, and a Canadian Substack writer with a husky named Dior who somehow keeps finding material at a pace that should not be humanly possible.

For twenty-six centuries, the arrangement was straightforward. Reality would do something foolish. I would exaggerate it. The exaggeration would illuminate the foolishness. Citizens would laugh, and in laughing, understand something they had not understood before.

The system worked because reality and I respected certain professional boundaries. Reality would be absurd. I would be more absurd. The gap between us was where the comedy lived.

I am writing to inform you that the gap has closed.

I am not retiring because I have lost my talent. I am retiring because reality has found hers.

Let me offer some examples from the past week alone.

On May 18, the president of the United States dropped a $10 billion lawsuit against his own government — a government he controls — and in the same announcement, his Justice Department created a $1.776 billion fund to pay his allies, including people who attacked the United States Capitol.

I could not have written this. I want to be very clear about that. If I had written this — if I had submitted a draft in which the president sues himself, settles with himself, and pays his own insurrectionists with a fund administered by his former criminal defense lawyer — my editor would have sent it back with a note that said: “Too much. Dial it back. Nobody will believe this.”

The president did not dial it back. The president added a provision blocking the IRS from auditing him, tucked into a one-page document posted quietly on a Monday afternoon.

I cannot compete with a one-page PDF. I have my dignity.

On the same day, a lawyer named Mark McCloskey — previously known for aiming an AR-15 at peaceful protesters outside his St. Louis mansion — who had withdrawn from representing 430 January 6th defendants due to a terminal lung diagnosis, announced that his prognosis was suddenly “not as bleak as first indicated” and that he was returning to work.

This announcement arrived within 24 hours of the $1.776 billion fund that would pay his clients.

His clients, whom he represents on contingency.

I spent three hours trying to write a version of this story that was funnier than the actual story. I could not do it. The best I could manage was a fake pharmaceutical ad, and even that felt like I was underperforming reality. Reality gave us a man who was cured of a terminal illness by the announcement of a slush fund. I gave you a joke about a golden retriever being replaced by a bald eagle.

Reality won. Reality always wins now.

On May 19, the Transportation Secretary’s family YouTube series — funded by Boeing, Toyota, United Airlines, and Shell, all of which his department regulates — was revealed to have been classified as “a gift to the department.”

A gift.

Nine first-class airline tickets for the Secretary’s wife and children, underwritten by the airline the Secretary regulates, classified as a gift to the department the Secretary runs, cleared by the ethics office the Secretary oversees.

I wrote an episode guide. I invented a nine-year-old daughter who asks her father if it’s okay that the companies he regulates are paying for their vacation. “But we’re at a water park,” I had her say.

Then I discovered that the actual Secretary has nine actual children and an actual Fox News anchor wife and is actually making an actual YouTube series funded by actual companies he actually regulates and that his actual ethics office actually cleared it.

My fictional nine-year-old was less absurd than his actual nine children. I am not sure what to do with that information, so I am resigning.

On the same Monday afternoon, the president summoned the White House press corps to view the construction site of his $400 million ballroom — built where the East Wing used to stand — and explained that the ballroom is actually a security feature.

“It’s a shield,” he said. “Impenetrable steel, and also impenetrable glass.”

Impenetrable glass.

I have been in this business since the Greeks. I have written for Swift, who imagined scientists extracting sunbeams from cucumbers. I have written for Leacock, who imagined a man so nervous at a bank that he accidentally opened an account for fifty-six dollars instead of asking for a loan. I have written for Twain, Wilde, Voltaire, and a retired Canadian journalist who somehow manages multiple satirical essays a week while also tracking a war in Iran.

None of us — across twenty-six centuries of professional exaggeration — have ever produced anything as effortlessly, unselfconsciously, and perfectly absurd as “impenetrable glass.”

I concede. The president is better at my job than I am. He does not even know he is doing it, which, I must admit, is the kind of thing I used to consider my signature move.

Stephen Colbert understood this problem better than anyone.

In 2005, he invented “truthiness” — the idea that what feels true matters more than what is true — and for nine years on The Colbert Report, he performed the satire from inside the machine, playing a character who believed his own nonsense so thoroughly that the audience could see the nonsense from the outside.

It was the greatest sustained act of political satire since Swift suggested the Irish eat their children, and it worked because there was still a gap between the character and reality. “Stephen Colbert” was an exaggeration of the Bill O’Reillys and Sean Hannitys who populated cable news. The audience laughed because the exaggeration was visible. You could see where reality ended and satire began.

Then reality ate the gap.

Trump came down the escalator, and suddenly the exaggerated character — the blowhard who knew nothing and said it as loud as he could, who treated truth as an inconvenience and volume as an argument — was not a parody anymore. He was the president. The Colbert Report character had escaped the screen and won the election.

Colbert tried. For ten years on The Late Show, he tried. He dropped the character and played himself — the real Stephen Colbert, Catholic, father, Tolkien nerd, man of genuine moral seriousness. He pointed at the absurdity and named it. He was funny. He was often brilliant.

But you cannot satirize a man who is already a satire of himself. You cannot exaggerate a president who sues his own government for $10 billion, settles with himself, pays his insurrectionists, blocks his own audit, and builds a $400 million ballroom out of impenetrable glass. You cannot make that funnier. You can only describe it accurately and hope the description does the work that exaggeration used to do.

That is not satire. That is journalism. And journalism, with all due respect to my colleagues in that department, does not have my flair.

CBS cancelled The Late Show for financial reasons, because satire does not generate sufficient revenue in a media environment where the thing being satirized generates more engagement than the satire itself. The president celebrated on Truth Social: “I absolutely love that Colbert’ got fired.”

The apostrophe after “Colbert” is, I must confess, funnier than anything I could have done with the situation. The president cannot correctly punctuate his own victory lap. I am being outperformed by a man who does not know where apostrophes go.

Colbert is leaving to write a Lord of the Rings screenplay for Peter Jackson. Middle-earth. A place where evil is identifiable, heroism is possible, and the eagles show up at the end to carry you to safety.

I understand the impulse.

I will be replaced, of course. I am always replaced. Others will try to maintain the momentum without Colbert’s guidance — and as a matter of fact, I’m told a couple of fellows have already jimmied the door to my office. I wish them well. They will need it.

Some young writer with more stamina and less institutional memory will pick up where they leave off, and for a while, the exaggerations will feel fresh, and the gap between reality and parody will seem navigable.

Then the president will do something — sue himself, pay his insurrectionists, build a granite helipad with his own seal on it to protect the grass from helicopter exhaust — and the gap will close again, and the young writer will sit at a desk and stare at a screen and think: How do I make this funnier than it already is?

The answer, increasingly, is: you don’t. You just describe it. You write it down, accurately, with appropriate sourcing and a navigation footer. You let the facts do the work. And you hope that somewhere, someone reads the description and understands that the thing being described is not normal, was never normal, and should not be treated as normal, even if the president’s spokesperson says it is perfectly normal and the ethics office has cleared it and the one-page PDF has been posted and the fund has been created and the grass, we are assured, will be magnificent.

That is not my job. That is Stephen Gordon’s job. And that Canadian shows no signs of stopping.

So I will leave it to him.

With respect, regret, and twenty-six centuries of professional pride,

Satire

Former Principal Mechanism for Democratic Accountability Through Humour 424 BC — May 21, 2026

P.S. — I notice the president has proposed a 100-foot granite helipad with a presidential seal, to protect the South Lawn from helicopter exhaust, at a cost that has not been disclosed, while the republic burns around him.

I could not have written it better myself.

That is precisely the problem.

P.P.S. — Thank you, Stephen Colbert. You were the last person who made my job look possible. I’m sorry I couldn’t hold the line.

Enjoy Middle-earth. It’s simpler there. The Dark Lord, at least, does not have a Substack.
