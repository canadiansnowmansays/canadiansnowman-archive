Stephen Gordon August 31, 2026

On Monday, August 24, the president announced that the last of the Iranian mines had been removed from the Strait of Hormuz. Admiral Brad Cooper, who commands Central Command, confirmed as much: as of last week, American forces had cleared the mines Iran had planted in the waterway.

On Sunday, August 30, Iranian Revolutionary Guards were observed on Larak Island preparing to fire rockets loaded with sea mines into that same strait.

As Eric Schmitt reported for the New York Times, American forces destroyed two rocket launchers before they could disperse their cargo. Captain Tim Hawkins of Central Command described the operation. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said the attack would be answered with punishment on the aggressor. The Iranian news agency Tasnim reported two people killed, which could not be confirmed, and the Pentagon did not immediately respond to questions.

It was the first American strike on Iran in a month. The last was July 29, when Central Command hit dozens of targets — command centres, missile and drone facilities, coastal surveillance sites.

Iran answered overnight. As Aaron Boxerman reported for the Times on Monday morning, the Revolutionary Guards fired on bases hosting American personnel in Jordan and the United Arab Emirates. Jordan said it intercepted eight missiles that entered its airspace; the Emirati military said it had responded to a drone coming from Iran. No damage or casualties were reported on either. Iran’s foreign ministry said several people had been killed and wounded on Larak Island, without specifics.

So the monthlong calm ended inside twenty-four hours, in both directions.

Six days elapsed between the declaration that the mines were gone and the discovery that more were coming. That interval is the war in miniature, and it arrived on the day the conflict turned six months old.

I. Pretending to Fight

Two days earlier, Steven Erlanger published a six-month assessment for the Times in which a former State Department official supplied the sentence this diary has been circling for weeks.

Jeremy Shapiro, now at the European Council on Foreign Relations, said he could not understand why the president had taken none of the available off-ramps from a war so unpopular at home. By pushing more sanctions, he said, Trump officials are pretending they are still fighting instead of pretending they have won — or admitting they have lost.

On Sunday night they stopped pretending. Two rocket launchers on an island in the strait is not a resumption of the war; the reporting is careful to say the strike appeared limited rather than the start of large-scale bombing. But it is a return to the thing the economic campaign was supposed to replace, six days after the campaign was launched with the language of D-Day and the fall of the Berlin Wall.

Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group had already explained why this was coming. Iran’s leaders regard economic strangulation as another form of warfare, he told Erlanger, which gives the American strategy two failure modes and no success mode. If the sanctions fail, the only remaining option is military escalation. If they work, Iran escalates to break the naval blockade. Either way it does not produce what the administration wants — sanctions as an alternative to war. It is more likely a prelude to another round of it.

He wrote that before Larak Island. It took less than a week.

II. The Verdict at Six Months

The anniversary produced a set of assessments from people not given to hyperbole.

Sanam Vakil of Chatham House: the war has failed to deliver American objectives. Suzanne Maloney of Brookings, who judged it a failure outright: a great deal of damage was done to Iran, and Iran has come out stronger than before. Iran’s neighbours, she added, have concluded they must find a way to live with an Islamic Republic that is not going away — including countries that Iranian missiles were falling on a few months ago, now working to repair relations with Tehran.

And Maloney’s summary of the domestic cost: this war has really overtaken the Trump presidency.

The verdict from the region is blunter still. Anwar Gargash, a senior Emirati official, wrote on Monday that the June ceasefire had failed to outline a practical and acceptable road map for ending the conflict, and called for a new agreement returning the strait to its prewar status without saying what that would look like. His summary deserves preserving: the state of neither war nor peace cannot be a sustainable solution.

That is a senior official of a country that has absorbed thousands of Iranian missiles and drones on America’s behalf, saying six months in that the arrangement his ally has produced is not one that can hold.

Erlanger’s own accounting is unsparing. The Islamic Republic has survived. It effectively controls the Strait of Hormuz. It has strengthened its regional influence. American credibility among allies has suffered, along with the president’s reputation for avoiding interminable Middle Eastern wars and for delivering affordability at home. Several thousand Iranians have died, including children, in numbers no one can verify. Eighteen American service members have died. The promise made on the first day of the war — that the hour of Iranian freedom was at hand — now reads as an artefact.

III. The Campaign With No Stated Goal

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced Operation Economic Outcast on Monday in the following terms: a sustained campaign to collapse every last option for Iran, gathering force with every day that follows, not ending until the regime stands alone.

He did not say how long it would last, what instruments it would use, or what it was meant to achieve.

Esfandyar Batmanghelidj of the Bourse & Bazaar Foundation put the resulting problem in one sentence. There is no clear articulation of America’s goals, he said — and if you do not define the behaviour change you want, sanctions as coercion fail. Sina Toossi of the Center for International Policy said the same thing from the other end: the capacity to hurt Iran is clear, and the path from pain to capitulation or collapse is not.

Vaez adds that the confusion is not confined to Washington. Iran’s leaders are genuinely unsure whether the United States wants collapse, capitulation or compromise. A government cannot concede to a demand that has not been made.

Worth noting what the campaign’s centrepiece turned out to be. The major financial institution Bessent promised by week’s end is Bank Melli, Iran’s largest — a bank that has been sanctioned repeatedly over the past twenty years, now sanctioned again, with the aim of eliminating its ability to operate abroad.

And on the same day the American Treasury Secretary hosted the G20 finance ministers in Asheville, North Carolina, and asked them to join Operation Economic Outcast, President Pezeshkian was in Bishkek.

Paul Sonne and David Pierson report for the Times that the Iranian and Russian presidents attended the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation summit in Kyrgyzstan alongside Xi Jinping and the leaders of India and Pakistan — a ten-nation grouping that has expanded steadily into a counterpoint to Western institutions, and which now includes Iran. China is the largest importer of both Russian and Iranian crude. Central Asia has become an alternative import route for Iran under the American blockade. Xi, they note, was able last month to brush off the threatened economic D-Day against countries providing Tehran a lifeline.

Bessent’s own framing of the limits, offered last week, was a rhetorical question: why would he want to blow up the global financial system? It is a fair question, and it is also the answer to his campaign. An isolation programme that cannot afford to sanction the buyer is a programme with a customer list rather than a blockade. While it was being pitched to finance ministers in a North Carolina mountain resort, its target was being photographed with the leaders of the two most populous countries on earth.

IV. Eighty-Four Percent

Leily Nikounazar and Yeganeh Torbati spoke to Iranians for the Times by voice memo and text message, all of them withholding their surnames for fear of the government. What they describe is the war’s actual product.

Iran’s central bank put inflation at eighty-four percent this week. The rial has hit a record low of roughly two million to the dollar. Eggs are beyond the reach of many middle- and working-class households. Fear that petrol subsidies will be cut has produced long queues at filling stations — and the precedent everyone remembers is 2019, when a fuel price increase brought enormous demonstrations.

Marzieh, forty-four, a teacher in Bandar Abbas, described neighbours stockpiling food, especially Pakistani rice. Chickpeas and beans have become expensive. She has given up cafes and stopped having people to the house. What frightens her, she said, is that salaries are running out before the new month begins.

The change in outlook over six months is the part that should be recorded carefully, because it is the reverse of what the war intended. Negar, a thirty-five-year-old lawyer, said that on the first day she felt anxiety and worry but no grief — she was not sad. Six months later she cannot begin to describe her anxiety about the future. Fahimeh, forty-three, said hopelessness has taken over, that she is not waiting for anything anymore, that she has stopped trying to move forward and switched to trying to survive.

Behzad, a public relations executive, said the Islamic Republic is now convinced it has defeated the world and that everyone is afraid of it. Ali, an engineer, said he has much less hope than he did that the government will fall. Mostafa, a technology worker, said he had not expected such a display of contradictory and childish positions from Washington.

And Esmaeil, thirty-seven, an artist in Tehran, offered the assessment the sanctions campaign will have to answer to. You cannot stop an economic uprising with anything, he said — war will not stop it and violence will not stop it. Iranians, he said, have fallen into a black hole: hungry, confused, unable to find a way out, alone in a catastrophic situation of chaos and abandonment.

Meanwhile the authorities have executed dozens of political prisoners and have grown less tolerant of dissent than before the war began.

V. The Waterway

The number that has changed most since this diary last counted is the one nobody is counting.

There have been more than seventy-two attacks on ships since the war began, and nineteen sailors have been killed. Two of those deaths came in the past fortnight, and another mariner is missing. On Saturday a tanker was struck by an unknown projectile while transiting inbound near Oman, without casualties.

About fifteen ships a day made the crossing over the past week, against more than a hundred and thirty before the war. The American energy secretary said this month that eight million barrels a day are leaving the Persian Gulf; Tanker Trackers puts crude through the strait at about 5.2 million barrels a day over the past four weeks, against nearly fifteen million before February.

Set against that: for three days in June, after the memorandum was signed, more than sixty ships a day passed through the strait. That was the agreement’s entire achievement, and it lasted seventy-two hours.

Iran now charges for passage through a waterway that was free. The Navy shepherds commercial vessels up the Omani side while Air Force jets and Army helicopters watch for launchers. Shipping companies that can avoid the strait do. Saudi Arabia has moved more crude to the Red Sea, where Vivian Nereim reports for the Times that the Houthis have brought the kingdom to the edge of a new war — one analyst there sees no political off-ramp and says simply that war is coming, and the United Nations envoy told the Security Council on August 13 that Yemen faces the gravest risk of large-scale conflict since the 2022 truce.

So the detour from the war has its own war forming across it.

VI. Hanging On

Why an economic campaign, and why now? Jonathan Lemire and Nancy Youssef reported for the Atlantic that the answer is partly electoral, and their sourcing is unusually direct.

Part of the plan, advisers and allies told them, is to push Iran into the background — to let the White House say it is trying something and then stop talking about the war, in the hope that petrol prices ease a little and attention moves to more congenial subjects before November. Bessent’s own phrase for the approach, quoted by them, is economic asphyxiation. The political virtue of asphyxiation is that it is quiet.

One adviser put it without ornament: we are just trying to hang on until the midterms.

The same reporting contains something the president has not said publicly. Gone are the promises about regime change and the nuclear programme; he has told political allies privately that once the strait reopens he is inclined to declare victory and move on. Six days ago he said in public that sanctions would collapse the regime.

And the closing observation of that piece now reads as a forecast. Iran, too, is watching the American calendar. Some Iranian officials fear the president will become more aggressive once the ballots are counted — which gives Tehran an incentive to escalate first, and put the war back in front of voters precisely when the White House needs it out of sight.

Larak Island may or may not be the beginning of that. But it is difficult to look at a set of rocket launchers loading mines into a strait the president had just declared clear and conclude that anyone in Tehran is trying to keep things quiet until November.

VII. By the Numbers

  • 6 days between the president’s announcement that the last mines were cleared and Iranian forces preparing to fire more.

  • 1 month since the previous American strike on Iran, on July 29.

  • 71 attacks on ships since the war began; 19 sailors killed, two of them in the past fortnight, with one more missing.

  • 3 days in June when more than 60 ships a day transited the strait — the entire achievement of the memorandum.

  • 84% inflation, per Iran’s central bank; the rial at roughly 2,000,000 to the dollar.

  • 18 American service members killed; 653 wounded, still recorded across two Pentagon webpages.

  • Several thousand Iranians killed, in numbers nobody can verify.

  • 0 of the war’s three original objectives achieved, at six months.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, in a country whose government has executed dozens of political prisoners since the war began.

VIII.

There is a temptation, at six months, to write that nothing has happened. The bombing stopped, the negotiations stalled, the sanctions arrived, and the maps look much as they did in July.

But something did happen, and Sunday night made it legible. A president announced that a waterway had been cleared of mines. Within a week the same waterway had more mines coming, and American aircraft were destroying the launchers, and Iran was promising punishment, and Tasnim was reporting two dead that nobody could confirm.

That is not stalemate. It is the war’s basic transaction, running at low volume, with the economic campaign layered over the top of it like a tarpaulin over a fire.

Shapiro’s formulation was that the administration is pretending to fight rather than pretending to have won. Six days into Operation Economic Outcast, it turned out not to be pretending. And the people who will pay for whatever comes next are the ones already stockpiling rice in Bandar Abbas, and the nineteen sailors, and a lawyer in Tehran who felt something like hope on the first morning of the war and cannot now describe her fear of what comes after it.
