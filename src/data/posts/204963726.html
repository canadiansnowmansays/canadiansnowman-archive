by Stephen Gordon

I count my essays. The number climbs, it lands on a year that already means something, and I stop and tell you what. This is essay 1,485, and after the fall of an empire I promised you something lighter, and I am a man of my word: today we bury a king under a parking lot.

We don’t do it on purpose. That is the whole joke, and it takes five hundred years to arrive at the punchline, but I promise it is worth the wait.

On the twenty-second of August, 1485, on a field called Bosworth in the English Midlands, Richard III — King of England, last of the Plantagenets, the dynasty that had ruled England for over three hundred years — was killed in battle. He was the last English king to die in combat, which is itself a distinction, and he lost not only his life but his crown, which came off his head on that field and, according to legend, was found in a hawthorn bush and set upon the head of the man who beat him: Henry Tudor, thereafter Henry VII.

That battle is usually marked as the end of the Wars of the Roses — the long, grinding, dynastic bloodletting between the houses of Lancaster and York, two branches of the same royal family who spent three decades killing each other over which of them got to sit in the chair. Thirty years of civil war, and it ends at Bosworth, and it ends in the tidiest way imaginable: Henry, a Lancastrian by a rather creative reading of the family tree, marries Elizabeth of York, fuses the red rose and the white into a single Tudor rose, and declares the whole thing settled by combining the two logos into one.

I am being a little glib. But only a little. A generation of slaughter, resolved by a wedding and a redesigned flower. Sometimes history really does hand you the metaphor for free.

Here is the part I actually want to tell you, though, and it is the part that makes this a Numbers Game essay, because it involves a number so absurd it took me three readings to believe it.

Richard III died in 1485. And then he was lost.

His body was buried, without much ceremony, in a church of the Greyfriars in the city of Leicester. And over the following centuries, the church was dissolved, demolished, built over, forgotten. The city grew up on top of it. The exact location of the last Plantagenet king’s grave slipped out of human knowledge entirely — a king of England, the subject of one of Shakespeare’s most famous plays, simply misplaced, his bones somewhere under a city that had paved over the memory of where.

For five hundred and twenty-seven years, nobody knew where Richard III was.

And then, in 2012, a team of archaeologists — acting on historical detective work and a good deal of hope — dug an exploratory trench in a spot in central Leicester, and found a skeleton. A man, killed by battlefield wounds, with a curved spine, buried where the lost Greyfriars church had once stood. They ran the DNA against known descendants of Richard’s family line. It matched. It was him. They had found the King of England.

He was under a parking lot.

Not near a parking lot. Not beside one. Under one. A municipal car park belonging to the Leicester City Council, a flat expanse of tarmac where people left their cars while they ran errands, and the whole time, a few feet down, lay a King of England who had been missing for five centuries. And here is the detail that I could not have invented, that no satirist would dare invent, that history simply provided, free of charge, as though it knew this series would one day need it: the spot where they found him, the exact parking space above his grave, was marked with a painted letter.

The letter was R.

Reserved, presumably. It stood for reserved. But it stood, for five hundred and twenty-seven years, over Richard, and no one knew, and I would like whoever painted that R to understand that they achieved, by pure accident, the single finest piece of unintentional poetry in the history of municipal parking.

Run the count, because the count is the comedy here.

He ruled for two years. He was lost for five hundred and twenty-seven. He was found under a letter that happened to be his own initial. And the last of the Plantagenets, a dynasty that ruled England for three centuries, the man Shakespeare turned into the most famous villain in the English language, spent more than half a millennium as the least-marked grave in Britain — a king reduced to a parking stall, a monarch under the tarmac, waiting for someone to think to look down.

There is something in this that I find genuinely consoling, in a way I did not expect when I started writing it. Because the previous few essays in this series have been heavy — a burned girl, a fallen empire, a plague I could not make funny. And here is history, in 1485, quietly demonstrating its other mode: the one where it does not crush you with significance but simply misplaces a king for five centuries and then reveals him under a car park with his own initial painted on top, as if to say, don’t take all of this too seriously; even the kings end up under the parking lot eventually, and the universe has a sense of humour about where it leaves the bodies.

Richard got a second funeral, by the way. In 2015, five hundred and thirty years after Bosworth, they reburied him properly, in Leicester Cathedral, with the ceremony a king is owed. It took five centuries and a parking lot, but he got there in the end. History is slow, but occasionally it circles back and finishes the job.

Now, before I close, a glance across the Channel — because while England was sorting out its roses, something rather larger was gathering elsewhere, and this series is about to sail straight into it.

In 1485, England was absorbed in itself: which cousin got the crown, which rose won, whose bones would eventually go under whose parking lot. It was, in the grand scheme, a domestic squabble with excellent costumes. But down in Spain, in these same years, Ferdinand and Isabella were finishing a very different project — the long reconquest of the Iberian peninsula, the consolidation of a unified Christian Spain, and the building of an apparatus of enforcement whose name still carries a chill five centuries on. Spain, in 1485, was not counting roses. It was inquiring, elsewhere, into other matters, with an intensity I am not going to make light of here and will have cause to speak of more soberly when the count reaches the year it all came together.

Because it all came together in one year, and that year is coming. In 1492, Spain would expel its Jews, complete its reconquest at Granada, and — needing that new road east that opened when Constantinople fell, remember, back at 1453 — send a Genoese sailor west across the Atlantic on a theory and a prayer. Three things in one year, one of them a discovery, one of them a triumph, and one of them a cruelty. But that is essay 1,492, and it deserves its own full telling.

For now, in 1485, England has its Tudor and its combined rose and its freshly lost king, and does not yet know that the center of gravity of the whole world is about to shift westward and leave its domestic rose-squabbles looking very small indeed.

So this is essay 1,485, and it is the light one, the palate cleanser, the essay where history declines to be solemn and instead misplaces a monarch for five hundred and twenty-seven years and files him under R.

Two years a king. Five centuries lost. One parking space, one painted letter, one improbable exhumation. And a dynasty of three hundred years ending on a field, resolved by a wedding, redesigned into a single flower, and eventually reburied with honor once somebody remembered to move the cars.

I will see you at essay 1,492 — the big one, the one this whole window has been sailing toward, where a fallen empire’s closed road and a unified Spain’s terrible year and one stubborn sailor’s bad math all arrive at once. Bring your whole attention for that one. It changed everything, and not all of it for the better.

But today? Today a king was under a car park, and his space was marked R, and sometimes that is all history wants to tell you.
