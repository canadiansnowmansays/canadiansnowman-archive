ANNOUNCER: Live, from a soundstage that may or may not also be a federal building — it’s the show where the house always wins, because the house pardoned itself before the game began! Say hello to your host, and to tonight’s lucky contestants, who qualify for today’s episode by having, at some point, stormed something, threatened someone, or simply been born into the right family!

[Applause sign lights up. Nobody in the studio audience actually claps, because the studio audience is also contestants.]

HOST: Welcome back, everybody, to Who Wants to Be a MAGA Millionaire. Tonight’s jackpot, as always, is entirely real for some of you, and completely theoretical for others — and no, we won’t be telling you in advance which category you’re in. Quick refresher on the rules, for anyone joining us mid-season: contestants compete for cash, clemency, and continued relevance, while one very special family sits backstage, permanently exempt from ever having to play at all. Let’s meet tonight’s board.

ROUND ONE: INSURRECTION OR JUST ENTHUSIASM?

HOST: Our first category, worth up to $500,000 depending on how photogenic your assault charge was. Contestant number one, you stormed a building on January 6th, assaulted a police officer, served part of a sentence, and received a full pardon. For the bonus round: was that insurrection, or just enthusiasm?

CONTESTANT ONE: [beaming, no hesitation] Enthusiasm! I’d do it again tomorrow, actually — is that a bonus question too?

HOST: [buzzer sounds, then immediately overridden] Incorrect, but also correct, because the correct answer changes depending on who’s asking and what week it is! Here’s your pardon anyway!

[Confetti cannon. A single eagle screech plays over the PA system.]

HOST: Let’s go to contestant number two. You didn’t storm anything personally — you just organized the buses. Middle management insurrection. How do you feel about your prospects tonight?

CONTESTANT TWO: Honestly? Optimistic. I hear organizing counts for a lot more than participating.

HOST: It certainly used to be that way in every white-collar prosecution in this country, and we see no reason for that trend to stop now! Moving on.

ROUND TWO: NAME THAT MILITIA

HOST: Our second category is a personal favorite of mine. Contestants, I’m going to describe an armed private group with no formal government authority, and you tell me whether it’s (A) an alarming symptom of democratic backsliding, or (B) a valued private-sector security partner. Ready?

CONTESTANT THREE: [glancing around nervously, like he wandered in from a different show] Ready. I think. Nobody told me there’d be a written portion.

HOST: Group organizes independently, arms itself, claims to be defending the country from an enemy only they can see, and receives a friendly nod from senior officials rather than a raid.

CONTESTANT THREE: [buzzes in] B! Valued private-sector security partner!

HOST: [ding ding ding] Correct! And for the bonus: what happens to a group that does the exact same thing, but picks the wrong administration to admire?

CONTESTANT THREE: ...Raid?

HOST: Raid! Give this contestant the full five hundred, and also, disturbingly, give them a follow-up meeting.

ROUND THREE: THE FAMILY IMMUNITY LIGHTNING ROUND

HOST: And now, ladies and gentlemen, the round none of tonight’s actual contestants are allowed to play, because it isn’t a competition — it’s a permanent condition. Let’s go backstage.

[Camera cuts to a comfortable greenroom. Nobody is buzzing in. Nobody needs to.]

HOST (V.O.): While our contestants sweat over their own individual legal exposure, one family enjoys a standing exemption from the entire premise of the show — civil, criminal, tax-related, for offenses committed up to and including tonight’s broadcast. No lightning round, because there’s no clock. No buzzer, because there’s no wrong answer. It’s not a game. It’s a bye week that never ends.

HOST: Back to the studio floor! Contestants, how does that make you feel, knowing the biggest jackpot on this stage was never actually in play?

CONTESTANT ONE: Inspired, honestly. Gives you something to aspire to.

CONTESTANT TWO: Grateful, mostly, that they let the rest of us play at all.

HOST: Quick turnaround here, folks — sixty seconds on the clock. I’ll name a legal dispute, you tell me whether it ends in a trial, a settlement, or a fund with a patriotic-sounding name that nobody outside government has actually seen the paperwork for.

CONTESTANT FOUR: Go.

HOST: Lawsuit against a federal agency, filed by a sitting president, over his own leaked tax information.

CONTESTANT FOUR: Fund with a patriotic name!

HOST: Correct! “Anti-Weaponization Fund” — the actual proposed name, if you can believe it, which sounds like something you’d find bolted to the side of a tank rather than sitting in a settlement agreement. Thirty seconds left — final question. Federal agency accused of running an insurance racket in an international waterway.

CONTESTANT FOUR: Sanctions! Sanctions on the company, nothing about the waterway!

HOST: [ding] And that’s time! A clean sweep on the speed round — somebody get this contestant a novelty check and a nondisclosure agreement, in that order.

ROUND FIVE: WHOSE TURN IS IT ANYWAY?

HOST: Our final competitive round tonight borrows its scoring system from a much older game — recess. Contestants, I’m going to describe an escalating international dispute, and you tell me, using only the information the administration itself has provided, whose turn it currently is to escalate.

CONTESTANT FIVE: I’ve been studying for this one specifically.

HOST: Good, because it changes weekly. Missiles intercepted, president says “our turn” on television. Whose turn was it, according to the transcript?

CONTESTANT FIVE: Ours! He said so himself, no elaboration needed.

HOST: Correct — and for the bonus, does the other side ever get confirmed as having a turn, or does the turn just sort of... relocate, permanently, to us?

CONTESTANT FIVE: It relocates. Turns only go one direction on this show.

HOST: [wipes away a single, theatrical tear] That’s the whole format in one sentence, folks. Give this contestant the grand prize: a several-months’ supply of “temporarily withdrawn, to be reinstated later” — the same trick currently being used on an actual attorney general nomination, redeemable at any federal appointment of your choosing.

FINAL ROUND: THE LIFELINES

HOST: Before we wrap up tonight’s episode, let’s review the lifelines available to any contestant who gets stuck.

“Phone a Friend (Who Also Needs a Pardon)” — a mutual-aid lifeline, in which the friend you call is statistically likely to need the same favor you do, meaning the call functions less as assistance and more as a referral network.

“Ask the Audience” — historically the most reliable lifeline in game-show history, now complicated by the fact that a meaningful share of the audience is out on pardon themselves and may have a personal stake in the answer.

“50/50” — eliminates two of the four wrong answers, though on this show, “wrong” has proven to be a temporary and revisable designation, subject to further executive review.

HOST: And that’s tonight’s episode of Who Wants to Be a MAGA Millionaire. Remember, contestants: the house always wins, mostly because the house wrote the rules, owns the studio, appointed the judges who’d otherwise review the rules, and has already pardoned itself for whatever happens after the credits roll. Tune in next week, when we’ll be taking your calls — assuming the phone lines haven’t also been reclassified as a national security asset.

A NOTE ON THE FORMAT

Credit where it’s due: the premise for this one comes from a sharp bit Representative Jamie Raskin posted on his Substack, riffing on the idea of a game show built around pardoned insurrectionists competing for cash while the president’s own family enjoys standing immunity from prosecution. It’s a good bit because the joke requires almost no exaggeration to land — the actual shape of the last several years already resembles a rigged game show closely enough that turning it into one is less satire than transcription.

What makes the format worth returning to, rather than treating as a one-off, is how cleanly it maps onto the actual mechanics of selective accountability: contestants who face real risk, real trials, real prison time, competing for a prize that one family never had to enter the game to collect. A pardon is dramatic and visible — cameras, a signature, a press release. A standing immunity nobody has to formally invoke is quieter and, in its way, more total. The contestants on this show, at least, know they’re playing. The house doesn’t have to.
