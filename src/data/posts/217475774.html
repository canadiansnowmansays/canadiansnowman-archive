By Stephen Gordon

Part LV of this series told Missouri’s map fight through the people living inside it — a pastor, a challenger, a postcard writer — and touched only briefly on the ruling that reopened the fight. This essay is about that ruling, and about the week it lasted. On Monday, September 21st, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit — all three nominated by Republican presidents — ruled that Missouri’s plan to use the 2022 congressional map for the November general election “violates the Constitution.” It is worth sitting with the strangeness of that sentence, because the plan the court found unconstitutional was the plan every Missouri election official had been executing for weeks, under direct order from the state’s own Supreme Court, with ballots already in the mail. By Friday, the U.S. Supreme Court had set the ruling aside.

A Different Argument, and a Different Result

Part LV reported that Michael Wolff, a former Missouri Supreme Court judge, considered the fight essentially settled regardless of what the Eighth Circuit did, because the Missouri Supreme Court’s ruling that the 2025 map “is not the law and never was” was final as a matter of state law, and no federal ruling on the map’s validity could revive it. That reasoning held, as far as it went. The problem is that it was answering a different question than the one the Eighth Circuit actually decided.

The panel did not rule that the 2025 map was lawfully enacted, or that the Missouri Supreme Court was wrong about the referendum. It ruled on a claim brought by Representative Bob Onder and state Senator Rick Brattin that rested on a separate constitutional theory entirely: that conducting a primary under one set of district lines and a general election under a different set, after candidates have already campaigned, run television advertisements, and been nominated by voters under the first map, violates the constitutional rights of both the candidates and the voters who nominated them — independent of which map is ultimately the “correct” one under Missouri law. The judges wrote that using the 2025 map for November, rather than switching back to 2022 lines only for the final round, would cause “the least amount of confusion for voters subjected to a string of changes this month,” noting that “Missourians filed to run for Congress, met candidates, watched campaign advertisements, received campaign mail and voted under the 2025 map.”

There is a real argument buried in that reasoning, and it deserves to be stated on its own terms rather than dismissed as partisan cover: an electorate that nominates candidates in August under one geography and is then asked to elect different neighbours’ representatives in November under a different geography has, in a meaningful sense, been asked to participate in two different elections that happen to share a ballot. The Eighth Circuit’s answer to that problem was not “hold the primary again.” It was “keep the map the primary was run under.” Whether that is the right fix for a problem Missouri’s own secretary of state created by waiting until the last legal hour to reject a referendum petition is a separate question from whether the problem is real, and this series has tried, from Part L onward, to keep those two questions distinct rather than collapsing the court’s remedy into an endorsement of how the mess was made.

A Ruling With Its Own Brakes

What made Monday’s decision harder to categorize as either a Republican victory or a continuation of the chaos is what the panel did immediately after issuing it: it stayed its own order and instructed the federal district court to give the U.S. Supreme Court a full week to weigh in before anything actually changed on the ground. Practically, that meant the 2022 map remained the operative map, exactly as it had been since the Supreme Court’s second intervention. Ballots carrying 2022 district lines had already been mailed to military and overseas voters, as required by law, and absentee voting, by mail and in person, began the following day. The Eighth Circuit found the current plan unconstitutional and then, in the same ruling, allowed that unconstitutional plan to keep operating for another week.

This is not a contradiction so much as it is the honest shape of emergency election litigation at this late a stage: a court can conclude a plan is legally wrong without being able to unwind it instantly, because unwinding it instantly carries its own constitutional and practical costs — ballots recalled, voters renotified, local election offices given even less runway than the three weeks Part L already described them losing once. The panel’s own language acknowledged this bind directly, framing its remedy explicitly in terms of which outcome would produce the “least amount of confusion,” not which map is correct in some abstract sense.

The Third Trip to Washington

Richard von Glahn, the leader of People Not Politicians Missouri — the group behind the original referendum petition, and the same voice this series quoted in Part L urging officials to “stop wasting taxpayers’ money on pointless legal action” — filed an emergency appeal with the Supreme Court late on Tuesday, September 22nd. It was the third time this month that Missouri’s congressional map had landed in front of the justices, following Justice Brett Kavanaugh’s order on September 8th and the full Court’s order on September 10th, both of which had kept the 2022 lines in place. The group’s argument, as the Washington Examiner reported, was simple: “A lower court cannot demand the same relief this Court has stayed by cloaking it in a new order.”

Brianna Lennon, the Boone County clerk whose refreshed court dockets opened this series’ original Missouri coverage in Part L, offered the closest thing to a consensus view available from anyone actually running the election: “I expect that the Supreme Court will have a say in this before it is over.” She confirmed Monday evening that her office had received no new guidance from the secretary of state, that ballots under the 2022 map had already gone out to overseas voters, and that more absentee ballots under that map were set to be mailed Tuesday regardless of what the Eighth Circuit had just ruled. Once again, Missouri’s clerks were printing and mailing ballots faster than the litigation above them could resolve.

The state answered on Wednesday afternoon. Missouri’s filing, as Amy Howe reported for SCOTUSblog on September 23rd, warned that pausing the Eighth Circuit would mean “a fifth court-ordered change to its map in September,” and said Secretary of State Denny Hoskins believed that leaving the 2025 map in place “is the best way to promote stability.” Missouri’s attorney general, Catherine Hanaway, went further, in a sentence that may prove the most accurate thing anyone filed all week: “The honest truth is that — regardless of what this court does — Missouri will not be able to run an orderly 2026 general election.” No option, she wrote, was ideal.

Friday: “It Has Already Started”

The answer came on Friday, September 25th, three days before the Eighth Circuit’s stay would have run out, as Ann E. Marimow and Mitch Smith reported for the New York Times. In an unsigned five-page order with no noted dissents, the Supreme Court blocked the 2025 map for the third time this month.

This time, the Court explained itself. Its first two interventions had been bare orders, with no reasons given, and it was into that silence that the Eighth Circuit had read an invitation. At oral argument, the panel’s presiding judge, James Loken, said he took the Court’s silence as an instruction: “Time is of the essence... they told us, ‘Get to work.’” Friday’s order was the reply: “Given this court’s two prior orders in this litigation, our conclusion that this was error should come as no surprise.”

The Court’s reasoning ran on timing. “The election is not merely upcoming; it has already started,” the justices wrote. “Ballots using the 2022 map have been printed and mailed. Absentee voting has already begun, as has military and overseas voting.” Changing course now, they warned, would invite “electoral chaos.” The Eighth Circuit had chosen its remedy as the one causing “the least amount of confusion.” The Supreme Court concluded that at this stage, the least confusing map is the one voters are already holding.

Then the Court did something neither of its earlier orders had done. It assigned responsibility. “In the several-month period before the August primary election, the Missouri secretary of state was well aware that conducting the August primary election with the 2025 map carried significant risks,” the order said. “The secretary nonetheless chose to proceed with the 2025 map for the primary.” Every Missouri essay in this series since Part L has traced the mess back to one decision: Hoskins sitting on a referendum petition carrying more than 300,000 signatures from December until primary day in August. On Friday, the Supreme Court traced it to the same place. The official who asked the justices for “stability” on Wednesday was named by them on Friday as the reason there was none.

It is worth staying fair to the Eighth Circuit’s premise, as this essay tried to be above. The problem the panel identified is real: close to a million Missourians nominated candidates in one district and will vote in another. The Supreme Court did not say that problem was imaginary. It said the fix had come too late, and that the official who created the problem had been warned. Those are different findings, and the second is the one that should outlast this election.

Richard von Glahn’s response was short: “We are done with corrupt politicians trying to force their map into effect outside the process outlined in the Missouri Constitution.”

The Number the Litigation Was Actually About

One figure from the week’s coverage is worth holding onto now that the map is settled: roughly 970,000 voters, spread across six of Missouri’s eight congressional districts, are assigned to a different district for the general election than they were for the primary. That is close to a quarter of the state’s registered voters. Some of those voters nominated a candidate in August who will not appear on their November ballot at all, because that candidate is now running in what is, for purposes of the general election, someone else’s district. Officials in both parties had told reporters they expected the losing side to challenge the results in court after the fact, and nothing in Friday’s order rules that out. Under the 2022 lines, Democrats are favoured to hold two of Missouri’s eight seats, including Emanuel Cleaver’s Kansas City district, the seat the 2025 map was built to break.

That is the sentence this series keeps arriving at from different directions, in different states, about different mechanisms. The Missouri Supreme Court ruled. The U.S. Supreme Court backed it, three times. A secretary of state was held in contempt and reversed himself. A federal appeals court ruled the opposite way on a different question, stayed its own answer, and lasted four days. The institutions held — but they held the way a dam holds, by absorbing the pressure rather than removing it. Close to a million Missourians will cast ballots this November for a district in which they did not nominate anyone. And on the same ballot, Missouri’s voters will decide Proposition A, the referendum on the 2025 map itself, which is where this fight started and where, for once, the people rather than the courts will have the last word.
