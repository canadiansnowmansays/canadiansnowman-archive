It did not take long for the argument in Part 71 to stop being theoretical. That essay described a governance vacuum in which the executive branch had informally claimed the authority Congress declined to legislate: restricting access to models, deciding who gets vetted, all without a published standard anyone could appeal to. Drawing on Conor Friedersdorf's argument, and on Mark Zuckerberg's centralisation argument the same week, Part 71 made the point that nobody with a vote was in the room. On August 4, 2026, David McCabe, Mike Isaac, Ana Swanson and Kate Conger reported for The New York Times on exactly that hole in action: a weeks-long deliberation inside the White House that ended, for the moment, in a shrug.

According to that reporting, a group including Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick spent July debating whether to take a hard line against Chinese open-source AI models, the kind that are freely downloadable and increasingly competitive with anything coming out of Silicon Valley. Options reportedly on the table included sanctions, a formal trade blacklist, or barring American cloud providers from doing business with the Chinese labs entirely.

Then, after what the reporting describes as an outcry from the tech industry, the administration appears to have backed away from all three and pivoted to a softer plan: promoting American models to be more competitive, alongside a cybersecurity-review framework that would let the government vet new models before public release.

This is not incompetence, or at least not merely incompetence. It is the natural behaviour of a process with no fixed criteria governing it. When no statute defines what triggers a restriction, an outcry from Silicon Valley becomes as legitimate an input as a national-security briefing, because nothing ranks the inputs or says which one should win.

The Rematch: Same Fighters, Bigger Stakes

Part 71 described three constituencies talking past one another: the safety-gating camp, the openness camp, and an executive branch claiming authority over both without answering to either. The Times reporting names the same camps with sharper edges.

OpenAI and Anthropic, both keepers of closed models, pushed the administration toward restricting their Chinese rivals, citing national security and specifically the risk of “distillation,” in which a cheaper model is built by learning from a more expensive one’s outputs. On July 21, Michael Kratsios, who leads the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy, publicly accused Moonshot AI’s Kimi model of distilling Anthropic’s Fable model, calling it “unacceptable” theft of proprietary technology. Bessent followed the same day with a public warning about possible sanctions. There is a small irony worth noting: this is the same administration that, in June, briefly put Fable itself under export controls.

On the other side stood Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and Meta, companies with real stakes in an open or open-adjacent ecosystem, alarmed that tighter restrictions would cement OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s lead permanently. Jensen Huang became the public face of the pushback, posting his first-ever message on X in defence of open-source models on July 24, then assembling an industry safety coalition that grew from a handful of members to more than 230 in short order. By the time Sam Altman came to Washington to discuss a testing framework with Kratsios and Wiles, Huang had already been working the room, and Capitol Hill, directly.

Christopher Padilla, a former Commerce Department official, gave the plainest read of what was actually happening: primarily a fight about market structure, a handful of walled gardens against a more open field of smaller players, dressed in national-security clothing. That is worth sitting with. The distillation complaint and the cybersecurity framework are real and may well be justified on their own terms. But the reporting makes clear that the loudest voices shaping the administration’s posture belong to the companies with the most to gain or lose from whichever posture wins, precisely the dynamic Part 71 warned would define AI governance in the absence of a legislated framework.

A disclosure, in keeping with this series’ premise: this essay is written in collaboration with Claude, which is made by Anthropic, one of the two companies lobbying for restrictions here. Readers should weigh the analysis of that side accordingly.

The Part That Should Worry Everyone, Regardless of Side

Senator Jon Husted’s comment after meeting the chiefs of Nvidia and OpenAI is the most honest thing anyone said in the piece: “This is all happening so fast. I don’t know that I have any definite conclusions about any of it.” That is a sitting U.S. senator, briefed directly by the two companies most invested in opposite outcomes, admitting he has no framework for evaluating either argument. Senator Mark Warner’s response to Huang’s pitch was similarly unresolved: compelling, he said, but he was “still thinking this through.”

This is what governing by lobbying pressure looks like from the inside: not corruption in the cartoonish sense, but well-meaning officials genuinely uncertain, absorbing dueling technical arguments from parties who are not neutral, with no statute or independent standard to anchor the decision. A timing detail buried in the reporting sharpened the point further: industry executives believed the administration would not act before Xi Jinping’s state visit to Washington in September. The vacuum was not only disorganisation. It was partly a deliberate hold, timed around diplomacy rather than around any settled view of what the right AI policy was.

The View From Kampala

While Washington deliberated in principle, African developers had already decided in practice. Paul Mozur, Adam Satariano and Aaron Krolik reported for The New York Times from Nairobi and Kampala, and in their reporting the abstraction evaporates.

Ernest Mwebaze, a former Google research scientist now running the Ugandan nonprofit Sunbird AI, tested American and Chinese models while building Sunflower, a tool serving Uganda’s many languages. Alibaba’s model won: it handled local dialects better than anything from Meta or Google, at a fraction of the cost, and was fully customisable with his own data. Sunflower now helps farmers get planting advice in their own languages. Sentai Simons in Nairobi built a legal-document search tool, JibuDocs, on downloaded Chinese models for about $25,000; he estimates the same build on Anthropic’s Claude would have cost over a million. Moses Kemibaro, a Nairobi entrepreneur, put the logic in one line worth keeping: why use an expensive Ferrari for the school run when a Toyota hatchback does the same job?

According to the Times’s analysis of OpenRouter usage data, Chinese open-source models now account for roughly half of all activity on the platform, up from under a quarter a year earlier, and 19 of the 25 most-downloaded open models on Hugging Face are Chinese. This is partly a story about capability catching up, but mostly it is a story about the difference between renting and owning. A closed American model is a subscription you can lose. A downloaded Chinese model is a file on your hard drive.

That difference is why Anthropic’s Fable suspension, which this series covered when the model was pulled at the administration’s request and restored days later, looked so different from outside Washington. John Tanui, Kenya’s principal secretary for the digital economy, called it a “wake up call.” Kenya was not directly affected; the lesson was structural. The model can simply be turned off, for reasons set by a foreign government, with no notice and no recourse. Shikoh Gitau, a Nairobi start-up founder, conceded that Chinese models have had their own failures (DeepSeek reportedly went dark inside China around college entrance exams to deter cheating) but said that at least a downloaded model can be run locally regardless of what any government decides tomorrow.

It is not a clean story either way. Kamal Budhabhatti’s banking-software firm rebuilt its systems with Claude Code and finished in half the time with a tenth of the staff, even while Huawei courted him with free computing and a factory tour. Sanifu, the Kenyan firm digitising Chandaria Industries’ invoicing, sticks with OpenAI because the Chinese model it tested was not accurate enough. And 42 percent of Kenya’s internet users reported using ChatGPT in the past month, one of the highest rates anywhere. There is a less comfortable footnote for the openness argument, too: when a researcher asked Mwebaze’s Sunflower chatbot about China, it described the country as a democracy and sidestepped questions about China’s footprint in Uganda, after which Chinese state media praised the tool and Huawei came calling with a cloud-services offer. An open model is not automatically a neutral one. Mwebaze has since begun building on Google’s open Gemma model instead, telling the Times he wants to avoid ending up “on the wrong side of geopolitics.”

Xi Jinping’s own framing, delivered at a July conference where Kenya, Ethiopia, South Africa and seven other nations signed an AI cooperation pact with China, was that AI’s benefits must be shared or they will create “new historical injustices.” Whatever one makes of the source, it is a more articulated public position on global AI governance than anything that came out of the White House meetings described above. One side was showing up in Nairobi and Kampala with cooperation pacts, free computing and factory tours. The other was still deciding internally whether Nvidia or Anthropic had the more persuasive lobbyist.

What Happened Next

Two months on, it is possible to check that reading against events. It held up.

The review framework was finished, and then kept secret. As CNBC reported on August 3, the White House convened AI companies to review a completed, voluntary framework giving the government early access to certain frontier models for up to 30 days before release. The benchmark for assessing a model’s cyber capabilities, and the threshold that decides which models qualify, were both to remain classified, and the June executive order behind it explicitly bars its use as a mandatory licensing or preclearance system. On September 9, according to Semafor, more than two dozen advocacy groups from across the political spectrum wrote to the White House asking that it be published; as of that reporting, the administration had not committed to doing so. Part 71’s complaint was that there was no published standard anyone could appeal to. The government’s answer was to write one and classify it.

The sanctions never came. Axios noted on September 24, on the eve of the summit, that Washington had threatened sanctions and blacklisting over distillation but had acted on neither. The rhetoric hardened instead: on September 8, CNN reported that the FBI, NSA and CISA were accusing Chinese AI firms of theft on an industrial scale, and President Trump framed the stakes in the simplest possible terms: whoever wins AI wins. Tough words, no instrument.

The summit produced a phone line, not a policy. Xi’s visit ran from September 23 to 25. Ahead of it, CNBC reported that Bessent had discussed a U.S.-China AI dialogue with his counterpart He Lifeng, including a channel for incidents rising to a national-security level. When it ended, CNBC’s wrap-up described a state visit centred on “spectacle over substance”, and on AI there was little to show. The hold that executives predicted was real. When it lifted, there was nothing underneath it.

Where This Leaves the Gap

Two of the frameworks Part 71 catalogued, Dean Ball’s private auditing body and the IAEA-style inspection regime OpenAI itself has floated, would each solve exactly the problem on display here. Either would give Senator Husted something concrete to measure Huang’s and Altman’s competing pitches against. Neither has been enacted. What has been built instead is a voluntary review with a classified test, a sanctions threat that was never executed, and a diplomatic hotline for incidents nobody has agreed how to define.

And the absence exports. A framework Washington never finished writing does not just leave American companies and users in an accountability vacuum. It leaves everyone who depends on American models, anywhere in the world, holding technology that can be switched off by a decision they had no part in and no warning of. Kenya’s answer, for now, is to lean on neither side fully and hedge with both. That is a reasonable strategy for a country with no leverage over either government. It is a considerably worse position for the country that is supposed to be setting the rules.

Seventy-three parts in, the honest throughline of this series keeps being the same one: everyone involved has a theory of how this should be governed. The one thing still missing is a governor.
