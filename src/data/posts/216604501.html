by Stephen Gordon

Three years ago in this series, Britain lost eleven days and complained about it. The Empire had spent a hundred and seventy years refusing a correction, accepted it at last in September 1752, and then discovered that arithmetic performed on a calendar feels, to the person standing on the calendar, exactly like theft.

Now we go three years further, to a garret above a house just off Fleet Street, where a large, twitching, half-blind man with a terrible temper and worse finances was conducting a different experiment on the English language. He was trying to count it.

He is remembered for the number he arrived at. I am going to argue that the number he is remembered for is the wrong one — that it is not even his best number, and that on the count everyone assumes he won, he actually came second.

The estimate

In June of 1746, a consortium of London booksellers signed Samuel Johnson to produce a dictionary of the English language. They paid him fifteen hundred guineas — one thousand five hundred and seventy-five pounds — which sounds magnificent until you learn it had to cover nine years of work, six paid assistants, rent on a house large enough to hold them, paper, ink, and the modest human requirement of eating.

Johnson estimated three years.

This is the first number in the story and it is wrong, which is a very good sign, because the Numbers Game is at its happiest when the opening figure turns out to be an opinion wearing a numeral’s coat. He would take nine. He would also, partway through, throw out an entire manuscript representing three years of work and begin again on a broader method — which means that the three-year estimate was correct, in a sense. It simply described a dictionary he decided not to write.

When a friend pointed out that the French Academy had needed forty scholars working forty years to produce theirs, Johnson performed the calculation that has followed him ever since. Forty times forty is sixteen hundred. As three is to sixteen hundred, so is the proportion of an Englishman to a Frenchman.

I want to be precise about what I admire here, because it is not the patriotism. It is the form. Johnson did not say I am better than they are. He said it with a ratio. He took a national prejudice and gave it a denominator. That is either the most English sentence ever spoken or the most Numbers Game one, and I have decided I do not need to choose.

For the honest ledger: the Academy took fifty-five years, not forty. The insult undercounted its target. This happens more often than you would think.

The count he is remembered for

Nine years later, on 15 April 1755, the thing appeared: two folio volumes, pages nearly a foot and a half tall, some two thousand three hundred pages of double columns, four pounds and ten shillings to buy, which put it beyond the reach of almost everyone whose language it described. Johnson called it vasta mole superbus — proud in its great bulk. He was not a modest man and he had just done a very large thing.

The number on the door is 42,773. Forty-two thousand seven hundred and seventy-three words, defined.

And here is where I must spoil the party, because that is the number every account leads with and it is the least interesting number in the room.

Twenty-five years earlier, a Stepney schoolmaster named Nathan Bailey had published the Dictionarium Britannicum, which contained roughly forty-eight thousand entries. More words than Johnson. Bailey’s earlier dictionary outsold Johnson’s for the rest of the century. And Johnson’s own working copy of Bailey sat on his desk throughout the nine years as the quarry he mined.

So: Johnson was not first, was not largest, and was not even the best-seller. On the headline count — the one carved into every plaque — he lost to a schoolmaster.

Which raises the only question worth asking about 1755. If he lost the count, why do we remember him and not Bailey?

The count that actually mattered

Because of a second number, which almost nobody quotes: 114,000.

That is the number of illustrative quotations Johnson packed in beside the definitions — passages from Shakespeare, Milton, Dryden, Bacon, Locke, the Bible, and several hundred others, marked up in his own books and copied out by his assistants onto slips, to be filed under the words they demonstrated.

Roughly two and a half receipts per word.

Earlier dictionaries told you what a word meant. Johnson’s showed you where he got it. Bailey asserted; Johnson cited. The difference between the two is the difference between a man saying “trust me” and a man saying “here is the evidence, and here is who wrote it, and you may check me if you like.”

This is the entry I would like on the record, because it is the one the series has been building toward since Magna Carta. The number that makes a count trustworthy is not the size of the count. It is the number of receipts behind it. Forty-eight thousand asserted words are worth less than forty-two thousand demonstrated ones, and the whole apparatus of modern reference — the footnote, the citation, the source note, the thing your teacher meant when she wrote show your work in the margin — descends more from Gough Square than from anywhere else in English.

It is a strange kind of monument. Johnson is remembered for the smaller number because of the larger one nobody mentions.

The garret

The work happened in a rented house at 17 Gough Square, in an attic fitted out like a countinghouse. Six amanuenses at a long deal table, copying slips. Five of the six were Scots, which is worth knowing before you read Johnson’s definition of oats: a grain which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.

He wrote that with five Scotsmen in the room, and they stayed, and one of them was still working for him decades later, which tells you something about Johnson that the definition does not.

He put himself in the book, repeatedly and without apology. Lexicographer: a writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge. Dull: not exhilarating; not delightful; as, to make dictionaries is dull work. Excise: a hateful tax. Network: any thing reticulated or decussated, at equal distances, with interstices between the intersections — a definition that has never once helped anybody understand a net, and which I love for exactly that reason.

And when a lady asked him how he came to define pastern as the knee of a horse, he did not manufacture an explanation. Ignorance, madam. Pure ignorance.

Two words. It is the single most honest entry in the whole ledger, and it cost him nothing but the thing most men will pay anything to keep.

The definition as counter-punch

There is one more definition that belongs to the arithmetic.

Years earlier, Johnson had addressed the Plan of the dictionary to Lord Chesterfield, in the hope of patronage. Chesterfield sent ten pounds and then, for seven years, nothing: no encouragement, no interest, no reply. Then, in the winter of 1754, with publication approaching and the book clearly about to be an event, Chesterfield published two glowing puffs in The World, positioning himself beside the achievement.

Johnson’s reply, dated 7 February 1755, is the most famous letter in English literature that nobody was meant to read. Is not a patron, he asked, one who watches a man struggling in the water, and, when he has reached ground, encumbers him with help?

But the letter is not the revenge. The revenge is in the book, alphabetically, where it will outlast them both.

Patron: commonly a wretch who supports with insolence, and is paid with flattery.

A man who has spent nine years building the official inventory of a language has one weapon available to him that no lord can answer, and Johnson used it precisely once, on precisely the right target. Whoever controls the definitions controls a very quiet kind of permanence.

The cost column

Now the sentence I said I would not soften.

Elizabeth Johnson — Tetty — died on 17 March 1752, a little more than halfway through. She had been ill through most of the work; the copyists and the noise and the slips had taken over the house she lived in; and the book she had watched consume her husband’s forties was still three years from existing when she stopped being there to see it.

Johnson finished it anyway, in the way that men of that construction do, which is by not stopping.

And when he came to write the Preface, he did not pretend. He wrote that he had protracted his work until most of those he had wished to please had sunk into the grave, and that success and miscarriage had become empty sounds.

There it is: the entry the plaque leaves off. Nine years is not a duration. It is a withdrawal, made from an account nobody tells you the balance of.

The surrender

He had one more correction to post, and it was against himself.

Johnson took the commission believing, as his backers did, that a dictionary could fix the language — settle the spellings, stabilise the meanings, stop the drift. The booksellers wanted English nailed down the way the Académie had tried to nail down French.

Nine years inside the language cured him. In the Preface he admitted it outright: sounds are too volatile for legal restraint, and to chain syllables, or to whip the wind, are equally the undertakings of pride.

Which is the second great number-idea of 1755, and the one that ties it to a ship that came home in 1522 with a perfect log and the wrong date. Johnson’s count was accurate. His frame was moving. He measured English exactly as it stood between 1746 and 1755, and the moment the ink dried, the thing he had measured went on without him — as it had every year before, and has every year since. He did not fail. He produced a photograph and was asked for a cage.

A small postscript in the same spirit, because it delights me. Look up the number of entries today and you will find 42,773 in most places and 42,733 in others — a transposed pair of digits, sitting in respectable reference works at this moment, in the count of the book that taught English how to cite its sources. The man who filed a hundred and fourteen thousand receipts is commemorated by a figure the record still cannot hold steady.

He would have enjoyed that enormously, and then corrected it.

The other door

One honest note before I close, because this year has a second entrance and I am not walking past it without knocking.

On 1 November 1755, All Saints’ Day, an earthquake destroyed Lisbon — the shocks, then the fire, then the sea coming back in over the people who had run from both. Tens of thousands dead; the figure has never been settled and by now cannot be. It is the count that broke the eighteenth century’s confidence in a well-ordered universe, and Voltaire spent the rest of his life arguing with it.

That is a grave essay, and this is not it. I mention it because the convention of this series is that the year is a door, and some years have two — and the one I chose to open today happens to be the one with a man at a desk, counting. The other door is still there. I am simply not the man to open it in a wry register, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of false accounting.

The auditor at my feet

Dior is, I am obliged to report, a working lexicographer of the strict descriptive school.

Her dictionary runs to perhaps nine entries, all of them verbs, and she did not arrive at any of them by consulting an authority. She built the whole thing from citations. Walk is not defined by what I mean when I say it; walk is defined by the hundred and some occasions on which the word was followed by a leash, minus the six on which it was followed by nothing at all, which she has filed as irregular usage and holds against me.

She has never once asked what a word is supposed to mean. She only asks what has reliably happened afterwards, and updates.

Johnson took nine years and a broken heart to reach that position. She was born holding it.

The closer

So we leave him in April 1755 with two enormous volumes, a reputation that will outlive the language he tried to freeze, no money, no wife, and a hundred and fourteen thousand receipts filed against forty-two thousand words — a man who set out to build a fence and accidentally built the first honest audit in English.

The Académie went on with their French. Johnson had his English. Two great inventories of two great languages, each compiled by people convinced theirs was the one the future would be kept in.

Four years later, on a field above Quebec, the question of which language North America would keep its books in was settled by neither of them, and not by argument, and not by lexicography.

It took about fifteen minutes.
