For thirty-five parts, this series has kept a roster of names: a former FBI director, a state attorney general, a Federal Reserve governor, a retired Navy captain turned senator, a congresswoman whose home was searched twenty-two days before an election. Each target is a person.

This instalment adds something different. The new entry is a job description.

On Monday, November 9, 2026, the White House counsel, Will Scharf, signed a three-page letter to Representative Robert Garcia of California, the Democrat who will chair the House Oversight Committee when the new Congress is sworn in. As Charlie Savage, Jonathan Swan and Michael Gold reported for The New York Times on November 11, 2026, the letter warned that aggressive oversight of the administration “may cross the line into blatant criminality.” Punchbowl News reported the letter first, and the White House later shared it. Garcia’s office said on the morning of November 11 that it had not yet received it, which tells you who the letter was written for. It was not Robert Garcia.

What the letter says

Most of the three pages are a catalogue of Democratic post-election rhetoric, and Democrats supplied plenty of it. As the returns came in, Garcia told administration officials to “lawyer up,” a line Max Cohen and his colleagues at Punchbowl News carried in their November 4, 2026 preview of the new majority’s investigations. Others promised subpoenas “like candy” or “Mardi Gras beads.” One representative-elect said he looked forward to watching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth “squirm.” From that material Scharf concludes that Democrats have shown “a clear intent to transgress” the constitutional limits on congressional investigations, and that their inquiries will be used “to harass and aggrandize” rather than to serve any legitimate legislative end.

Up to that point, the letter is a legal brief with an attitude, and there is real doctrine underneath it. Congress may compel testimony and documents in service of a valid legislative purpose: deciding whether a new law is needed, funding a programme, weighing impeachment. It may not subpoena simply to expose or embarrass. The Supreme Court tightened those limits in Trump v. Mazars in 2020, when it sent the fight over Trump’s financial records back to the lower courts with a stricter test for subpoenas aimed at a president’s personal papers. Had Scharf stopped there, he would have written the standard opening move of every administration that has ever faced a hostile House.

He did not stop there. The paragraph that made the news warns that threats to executive officials meant to deter them from public service, or process meant “merely to abuse or instill fear,” may be criminal, and that issuing unlawful subpoenas intended to deprive citizens of their constitutional rights “may warrant further investigation.”

That passage cites no precedent. The Times noticed. So should everyone else.

The statute he didn’t name

The phrase about depriving citizens of their constitutional rights is the language of 18 U.S.C. § 242, which makes it a federal crime to deprive a person of constitutional rights while acting under colour of law. Its companion, § 241, covers conspiracies to do the same. These are Reconstruction-era statutes, revived in the civil rights era, with a specific clientele: police officers who beat suspects, prison guards who abuse inmates, sheriffs who once looked the other way while mobs did the work. The law exists because the state’s power to arrest, detain and use force can be turned on the people it is meant to protect.

A House subpoena arrests no one. It detains no one. A person who receives one and thinks it overreaches has a remedy, and it is the one Donald Trump used for years: refuse, go to court and argue. Trump’s lawyers fought the House subpoena for his financial records from 2019 until a settlement in 2022 narrowed what Congress ultimately received. Along the way, the litigation ran out the clock on disclosure before the 2020 election. That is how the system is designed to work.

Peter M. Shane, who teaches public law at New York University and co-wrote a casebook on the separation of powers, made the point plainly to the Times: an occasional subpoena may be quashed as outside a committee’s jurisdiction, but that is not a criminal offence.

The clause he didn’t mention

There is a larger problem, and Scharf, a 2011 graduate of Harvard Law School, did not address it at all.

Article I, Section 6 of the Constitution provides that for any speech or debate in either house, members shall not be questioned in any other place. The Speech or Debate Clause is older than the Constitution. It was borrowed from the English Bill of Rights of 1689, written after Stuart kings had made a habit of prosecuting members of Parliament who annoyed them. Its entire purpose is to stop an executive from using the criminal law against legislators for doing their jobs.

The Supreme Court has read it broadly. In Gravel v. United States in 1972, the Court extended its protection beyond floor speeches to the legislative acts of members and their aides. In Eastland v. United States Servicemen’s Fund in 1975, it took up the precise question Scharf’s letter raises: is issuing a subpoena a protected legislative act? The Court said yes. The power to investigate is part of the power to legislate, and a subpoena is how Congress investigates. A committee that issues one cannot be sued over it, let alone prosecuted.

So the one category of congressional conduct the letter singles out as potentially criminal is the one category the Supreme Court has explicitly placed beyond the executive’s reach. That is not a gap in Scharf’s argument. It is the whole argument, missing.

The crimes that actually exist

It is worth noticing where the criminal law does reach in the relationship between Congress and the people it subpoenas, because it runs in exactly the opposite direction.

Contempt of Congress is a federal crime under 2 U.S.C. § 192. It applies to witnesses who defy subpoenas, not to the lawmakers who issue them. Two of Trump’s own advisers have been convicted under it: Steve Bannon in 2022 and Peter Navarro in 2023, both for ignoring subpoenas from the House committee investigating January 6. Both served time.

If anyone in Washington has a documented record of criminal exposure arising from congressional subpoenas, it is the president’s circle, and that exposure came from refusing them. Scharf’s letter takes the only crime the law recognises in this relationship and turns it inside out, so that the people asking the questions become the suspects and the people declining to answer become the victims.

Garcia, for his part, has already said which tools he means to use. He told Max Cohen of Punchbowl News on November 3, 2026 that the majority can go to the courts on the House’s behalf, can seek contempt, civil as well as criminal, and can issue subpoenas. Those are the remedies the law provides when a witness refuses, and every one of them runs through a judge. The man being warned about criminality has described a plan that ends in a courtroom. The letter warning him describes one that ends in an indictment.

Run it through the test

In Part XXVI, “The One That Was Real,” this series set out a rough test for telling a genuine prosecution from a manufactured one. Real cases tend to predate the grievance, come from career prosecutors working across administrations, rest on the defendant’s own words, and survive contact with a judge. Manufactured cases postdate the grievance, depend on handpicked prosecutors or venues, and collapse when they meet a grand jury or a court.

Scharf’s threat is not a prosecution yet, but it can be graded on the same rubric, and it fails every line before anyone has been charged.

It postdates the grievance, and the sequence is tidy. On Saturday, November 7, 2026, President Trump announced on Truth Social that he was “ordering the Department of Justice to look into this criminal act,” the act being Democratic leaders’ talk of investigating his administration. On Sunday, November 8, Dana Bash read that order back to Attorney General Todd Blanche on CNN‘s State of the Union and asked what the crime was. Blanche said it depended on what was said, and complained that members of Congress had “done nothing but threaten everything that’s happening in this administration.” Asked whether lawmakers could be imprisoned, as Trump had suggested, he said it depended on the crime. Then he aimed a line at Garcia’s committee: “I would be very worried if I was a member on that committee.” On Monday, November 9, Scharf put it on White House letterhead. All three followed an election that handed Democrats both chambers. It comes from the president’s former defence lawyers: Scharf and Blanche both once represented Trump personally. It rests on nobody’s words but post-election bluster, none of which describes a crime. And it cannot survive contact with a court, because the controlling precedent is half a century old and squarely against it.

There is one more thing the test was built to notice. Manufactured cases borrow from one another. The theory in Scharf’s letter, that investigating Trump’s people can itself amount to depriving them of constitutional rights, is the theory behind the “grand conspiracy” inquiry in South Florida, which this series followed in Part XXIX and Part XXXI. Prosecutors there have spent much of the second term examining whether the FBI and intelligence officials who investigated Trump conspired to violate his rights. The inquiry has produced no indictment, and several of the loyalists who worked on it recently resigned. A theory that has failed against former officials with no special immunity is now being pointed at sitting legislators who hold the strongest immunity in the Constitution.

Who the letter is really for

If the threat cannot work in court, why write it?

Because a threat does not need to work in court to work. Consider what Representative Jim Himes of Connecticut, the incoming Democratic chairman of the House Intelligence Committee, told Dustin Volz and Julian E. Barnes of The New York Times the same week. Alongside closer scrutiny of the CIA’s covert actions, Himes wants to know why defence and intelligence contractors donated millions of dollars to what he called Trump’s “vanity projects” around Washington, including the White House ballroom and a planned 250-foot triumphal arch. He said the committee would want to know whether those companies were promised anything, or threatened, and that it could subpoena them and hold public hearings, which is rare for the intelligence panel. He will not be alone. Jared Huffman of California, the likely incoming chair of the Natural Resources Committee, has promised to look at the same “vanity projects,” the ballroom included.

They have already started. As Evan Halper reported for The New York Times on November 12, 2026, the top Democrats on the House Science and Natural Resources committees have warned more than 60 companies, individuals and agencies to preserve documents ahead of investigations next year. The recipients include USA Rare Earth, the Oklahoma mineral company in a government investment deal that raised money through Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's former firm, Cantor Fitzgerald; Lithium Americas, in which the administration has taken a 5 per cent stake; SoftBank, a $50 million donor to Trump's presidential library; large oil companies; and the Trust for the National Mall, which Democrats say the administration sought to use to conceal the identity of corporate donors to the ballroom. "There have been firings, coercing people to resign, impounding money and possible corruption," said Representative Zoe Lofgren of California, the likely incoming chair of the Science Committee. The White House reply had the same shape as Scharf's letter: "If Democrats choose to pursue sham investigations and radical left policies, they are choosing to work against the American people." Those 60-odd recipients are exactly the readers the letter was written for.

Those contractors live on federal money. Their lawyers will read Scharf’s letter, and what they will read is that the administration regards aggressive subpoenas as potentially criminal and is willing to say so on White House letterhead. No federal judge would accept that position. But a general counsel deciding how quickly to comply, how much to hand over and how hard to fight does not need a judge’s view. She needs to know which way the wind is blowing from the building that awards her company’s contracts.

That is the real audience. It is not Garcia. By November 11 the White House’s public line had hardened into a slogan, the administration “will not be extorted” by congressional investigations, as David A. Graham noted in his newsletter for The Atlantic that evening. Garcia’s answer was that Congress will not be deterred by “baseless threats from a weaponized DOJ or from the President’s lawyers.” Extortion is an odd word for a committee asking for documents it has a constitutional power to request. It is an even odder word from an administration whose own threat comes with a statute attached. Either way, the audience is every witness, contractor and career official who might be asked to cooperate. In TACO terms, this is the Threaten stage, and the threat is the product. It does not have to be carried out to raise the price of cooperating.

There may be one more reason for the timing. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, the likely incoming chair of the Judiciary Committee, told Cohen in an earlier interview for Punchbowl News, published September 28, 2026, that his committee will focus on what Democrats see as the weaponization of the Justice Department. In January, the subject this series has tracked from the outside for thirty-five parts acquires a committee with subpoena power. A warning that subpoenas may be criminal reads differently when the next subpoenas may be about the prosecutions themselves.

The obstacles that are real

None of this means the new majority will get what it asks for. The genuine hurdles are not in Scharf’s letter, and they are higher than his threat. Adam Liptak, the paper’s chief legal correspondent, set them out in his Docket newsletter for The New York Times on November 12, 2026.

The first is the 2024 immunity decision. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that “Congress cannot act on, and courts cannot examine, the president’s actions on subjects within his ‘conclusive and preclusive’ constitutional authority,” and listed among them issuing pardons, firing subordinates and deciding “which crimes to investigate and prosecute.” That last item sits squarely on the subject Raskin’s committee means to examine. An inquiry into whether this Justice Department has been weaponised will run straight into the argument that the president’s choices about whom to prosecute are beyond Congress’s reach.

The second is enforcement. The administration is unlikely to hand over documents or let officials testify, and the Supreme Court has never decided whether Congress can sue the executive branch at all. In 2020 the D.C. Circuit let the House Judiciary Committee sue to compel testimony from Don McGahn, Trump’s former White House counsel, and let the full House sue over border-wall spending. The Supreme Court later vacated the border-wall ruling as moot, at the request of the Biden administration, whose solicitor general, Elizabeth Prelogar, warned that letting the House sue the president “would inject the federal courts into fundamentally political disputes.” The question is still open, and one of the precedents most helpful to the House was wiped out at the request of a Democratic administration.

The third is privilege. In August the Office of Legal Counsel issued a memorandum arguing for a vast expansion of executive privilege, including over the president’s conversations with people outside government. It cited Mazars eight times. Payvand Ahdout, a University of Virginia law professor who studies these fights, told Liptak that courts “frequently engage in avoidance techniques” to keep from ordering another branch to act, though “the judiciary is not a monolith” and Congress may find allies in the lower courts.

Set beside those obstacles, Scharf’s letter looks less like legal strategy than theatre. The administration already has strong defences it can argue before a judge, built by a Supreme Court over six years. It reached anyway for the one weapon it cannot lawfully use, the threat of prosecution. That choice is the tell. A government confident of winning in court does not need to warn the other side’s chairmen that they might be criminals.

The precedent that wasn’t

Trump’s defenders will say this is a hostile House getting a taste of its own medicine, and that Democrats have openly promised a subpoena circus. They have a point about the rhetoric. “Lawyer up” is not the language of a legislator weighing whether a new statute is needed, and the administration’s lawyers will quote it back in every Mazars fight to come. But the same Punchbowl preview that carried Garcia’s line also listed what he plans to investigate: what he calls Trump family corruption, abuses at DHS and ICE, corporate corruption and the Epstein scandal. Each of those sits on ground where Congress writes laws and funds agencies, which is the test Mazars actually sets. In that same September interview, Garcia and Raskin both said they had no appetite for a third impeachment of Trump; Garcia said the priority was slowing the Trump agenda. Some Democratic subpoenas will still be overbroad, and some will be quashed. That is what courts are for.

But the comparison that matters runs the other way. When Republicans narrowly won the House in 2022, scores of them had campaigned on investigating Hunter Biden’s business dealings and Dr. Anthony Fauci’s handling of the pandemic, and at least one candidate vowed to prosecute Fauci. Once in office, they followed through on much of it. Biden’s lawyers fought subpoenas they considered improper. There is no public record of the Biden White House writing to a committee chairman to warn that his oversight might be criminal and that the Justice Department could look into it.

Nor did Trump’s own first administration go that far. After Democrats took the House in 2019, Trump vowed to fight “all” subpoenas, and his administration largely did, in court. The Times judged that the new threat goes even further than the stance he took in his first term. The escalation is the story.

The scorecard

Garcia joins the Democratic lawmakers already on this list. It is not his first brush with this Justice Department: he told Bash on the same programme that it once sent him a letter threatening to investigate remarks he had made on CNN. But he is the first to be added before he has done anything at all as chairman. He has been warned about subpoenas he has not issued, from a committee he does not yet chair, in a Congress that has not yet been sworn in.

That is new. Until now, the Enemies List has tracked what this administration does to people after they cross it. Scharf’s letter is an attempt to stop the crossing before it starts. As law, it will almost certainly fail. Whether it works as a warning depends on everyone who reads it except the man it was addressed to.
