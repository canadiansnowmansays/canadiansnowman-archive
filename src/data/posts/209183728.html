There has been some confusion on the yard this week, so the Office of the Playground Monitor would like to issue a clarifying bulletin on the rules of turn-taking, effective immediately and subject to revision by whichever child is currently winning.

On Tuesday, Iran fired missiles at American assets in Jordan. U.S. Central Command intercepted all five. By Wednesday, the President of the United States was telling reporters in the Oval Office that it was, in his words, “our turn” to hit back — that Washington would be “hitting them very hard,” because Tehran, in his estimation, already knew what was coming and had, in fact, asked that it not happen. He also mentioned he’d be “quite disappointed” in President Xi Jinping if China kept arming Iran, the way a teacher might tell one boy she is “disappointed” in the friend who keeps handing his combatant rocks.

This is, on its face, a foreign policy crisis involving live munitions, a contested strait, sanctioned shadow fleets, and the government of a nuclear-adjacent state. It is also, on the evidence of the president’s own language, recess. So let us treat it as recess, since that is the framework being offered to us, and consult the rulebook accordingly.

Rule One: Whoever hit second gets to hit next, and this is called justice. The essential logic of “our turn” is that violence, properly sequenced, becomes fair. Iran hit; therefore the United States hits; therefore the ledger is even, or would be, if either side kept a ledger that ever actually closed. In practice, turns do not alternate toward zero. They alternate toward more turns. This is not a flaw in the system. On the playground, as apparently in the Situation Room, it is the entire point of having a system: it lets you keep hitting while insisting you are simply following the rules.

Rule Two: If the other kid says “please don’t,” that is proof he’s scared, not proof he’d like you to stop. The president told reporters Iran was “asking us not to do it” — offered as evidence of Tehran’s fear, not considered, even briefly, as a request one might honor. On a real playground, a monitor hearing “please don’t hit him” would generally regard that as the opening of a conversation. In this rulebook, it is treated as a scouting report.

Rule Three: You may recruit other children into your dispute, and then be “disappointed” in them for participating. China, in this analogy, is the kid at the far end of the yard who sells firecrackers. The president says he’d be disappointed in Xi Jinping for supplying weapons to Tehran — a mild word for a serious accusation, and a strange one to choose for a supplier one has spent years happily trading tariffs and threats with. Disappointment is what you say about a friend who let you down. It is a curious register for a rival superpower allegedly arming your declared enemy.

Rule Four: Sanctions are the participation trophies of statecraft. This week’s sanctions targeted two Iranian firms accused of running what the Treasury Department described as an insurance racket in the Strait of Hormuz — companies allegedly forcing commercial vessels to buy coverage against risks that Iran itself created, then pocketing the proceeds for the regime. This is genuinely grim, extractive behavior, deserving of real scrutiny. It is also the kind of thing that gets a press release and a firm name-check rather than an actual policy shift, which is the diplomatic equivalent of writing a bully’s name on a clipboard while he continues taking lunch money in full view of the clipboard.

Rule Five: Whoever narrates the fight gets to decide who started it. By the time this cycle of strikes reaches the history books, “our turn” will likely have been smoothed into something more solemn — a “measured response,” a “necessary deterrent action.” The president’s actual phrasing, offered live and unscripted to reporters, is more honest than anything a communications shop will later draft to replace it. It reveals the operating theory in its rawest form: this is a fight being kept alive by scorekeeping, and scorekeeping requires an audience.

Rule Six: The size of your friend group is proof of righteousness, not proof of anything else. Iran’s strike on U.S. assets in Jordan marked, by most accounts, a departure from the recent pattern, in which Tehran had mostly been responding to American and Israeli strikes rather than initiating new ones. That departure is being treated, understandably, as an escalation worth answering. But answered how, and for how long, are questions that tend to get settled less by law than by headcount — how many allies stand behind each side when the bell (which, again, does not exist) fails to ring. On a real playground, the kid with the bigger group of friends usually wins the argument about who started it, regardless of who actually did.

Rule Seven: A drone over Egypt is somebody else’s recess, until suddenly it’s yours. The president mentioned, almost as an aside, being briefed on a drone attack on tankers off the Egyptian coast — a separate incident, a separate set of ships, a separate stretch of water, folded casually into the same Oval Office remarks as the missile interception and the sanctions and the disappointment aimed at Beijing. This is how regional conflicts actually metastasize: not through a single declared war, but through an accumulating list of “separately”s, each one small enough to mention in passing, until the yard itself has gotten too big for any one monitor to watch at once.

NOTES FROM THE PARENT-TEACHER CONFERENCE

It would be reassuring to report that some adult in this scenario is playing the traditional adult role — separating the children, confiscating the rocks, assigning detention until everyone calms down. No such adult appears in the transcript. What exists instead is a rotating cast of children, some larger than others, each insisting that the last blow landed justifies the next one, while a handful of nervous bystanders (shipping insurers, Gulf monarchies, oil markets) hover at the edge of the yard hoping nobody notices them flinching.

The closest thing to institutional oversight is the Treasury Department’s sanctions list, which functions, at best, as detention slips handed out after the fact to the kids who ran the black-market candy operation on the side rather than the ones actually swinging. It punishes the racketeering that grew up around the conflict without touching the conflict’s engine. This is not nothing. It is also not recess ending.

A CLOSING NOTE FROM THE MONITOR’S OFFICE

The trouble with recess, as any parent will tell you, is that it eventually has to end, usually because a bell rings or someone gets sent to the office. International conflict lacks both mechanisms. There is no bell. The office, in this case, is occupied by the boy who keeps insisting it’s his turn.

Until an adult intervenes — and it is not entirely clear which government, if any, currently qualifies as the adult in this yard — the rule holds: whoever hit last was owed a hit back, and whoever hits next will be owed one in return, and the strait stays contested, and the ships still pay their racket-insurance, and the only thing anyone seems fully confident about is whose turn it currently is.

A BRIEF WORD ON THE SCOREBOARD ITSELF

There is a temptation, watching any of this from Toronto, to feel a certain smugness about not being one of the children in the yard. Resist it. Canada does not run the playground, but it stands close enough to the fence to catch the occasional stray rock — oil markets, shipping insurance rates, alliance obligations that get invoked selectively depending on which turn is currently being taken. Neutral observers of a schoolyard fight are rarely as neutral, or as safe, as they’d like to believe. The strait stays contested whether or not you have a dog in the fight, and the sanctions list grows whether or not your flag is on any of the ships paying the racket-insurance this week.

The safest position in a yard with no bell and no monitor is not neutrality. It’s proximity to the exit. Whether any of the relevant parties — American, Iranian, or otherwise — are actually looking for one is, at this point, the only question that still matters.
