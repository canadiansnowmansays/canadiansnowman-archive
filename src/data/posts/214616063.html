by Stephen Gordon

This series was always going to have to face essay 1,666, because it is the year the most feared number in the Western world finally came due on the calendar. Every other number in this project acquired its meaning by accident — a battle happened to fall on it, a charter, a fleet. This one arrived pre-loaded. For sixteen centuries, Christendom had known exactly which year the Beast was booked for, because the Book of Revelation had published the figure — let him that hath understanding count the number of the beast: for it is the number of a man; and his number is six hundred threescore and six — and the arithmetic of dread required only patience. As the date approached, the pamphlets multiplied; preachers in England, a country that had just executed a king and restored his son, read the times with the confidence of men holding the answer key; and I note for the fullness of the record that the fever was not even confined to Christendom — 1666 was the very year the great messianic movement around Sabbatai Zevi crested and collapsed across the Jewish world, as if the entire hemisphere had circled the same square on the calendar. Humanity had made an appointment with a number. Humanity, whatever its other failings, keeps that kind of appointment.

Before we see what showed up, I owe you a footnote on the number itself, because it is the best joke in the essay and it was sixteen centuries old before anyone in 1666 got to hear it. The instruction in Revelation is not “fear the number.” It is count the number — it is the number of a man, says the text, practically winking. And the scholars’ longstanding solution is that 666 is gematria: letters-as-numerals, in this case almost certainly the name and title of the Emperor Nero rendered into Hebrew characters and summed. Which is to say: the most terrifying figure in scripture is, on the strongest reading, a political cipher — a first-century writer, under an empire it was fatal to criticize openly, encoding the tyrant’s name as an arithmetic problem so that the faithful could read what the censors could not. The Beast’s number is not a prophecy. It is an encryption. This series has filed fifteen hundred essays on numbers that conceal things — inflated crowds, rounded wars, deleted days — and here, at the bottom of the most dreaded number of all, we find the opposite: a number concealing a truth from power, samizdat in base ten, waiting sixteen hundred years for its readers to stop trembling and start counting, exactly as instructed. Nobody in 1666 did. Fear is the enemy of arithmetic; it always has been; that may be the most practical sentence this series has ever produced.

Now, what showed up. And I concede at once that the year made a magnificent case for the pamphleteers.

It arrived, first of all, on the heels of the plague. The Great Plague of London had burned through 1665 — the old enemy of essay 1,347, three centuries on, still working the same trade — and killed, by the reckonings of the time, on the order of a hundred thousand Londoners, something like a quarter of the city. The Bills of Mortality — London’s weekly published death counts, a genuinely heroic piece of civic bookkeeping — tracked it parish by parish, and readers of this series will know to add the standing caution: the Bills undercounted, as official tallies of catastrophe reliably do, the poor and the dissenting dead slipping through the columns. And then, in the small hours of September 2, 1666, in a bakery on Pudding Lane, the fire started.

It burned for four days, driven by an east wind through a medieval city of timber and pitch, and when it was done it had consumed the heart of London: some thirteen thousand two hundred houses by the standard count, eighty-seven parish churches, the Guildhall, the Royal Exchange, and old St Paul’s Cathedral itself, whose lead roof ran molten in the streets. A hundred thousand people were homeless. The medieval city, in its physical fact, was simply gone. If you were a pamphleteer who had spent the spring promising that the year of the Beast would bring judgment by fire, you did not have to strain the evidence. Plague, then fire, in consecutive years, on the appointed number. I have never encountered a superstition better served by its schedule.

And now the entry that earns this fire its place in the Numbers Game, because you know this series does not cover disasters for their smoke. The official death toll of the Great Fire of London — the verified, recorded count of the dead, in a conflagration that destroyed the homes of a hundred thousand people — is six. Some traditions say eight. Single digits, in any case, and I will let that sit on the page a moment, because nobody has ever believed it, and nobody should. The fire burned hot enough to melt church bells and run lead like water; it moved through crowded lanes faster than the old and the bedridden could; and a count of the dead that requires an identifiable body was never going to find what that furnace left. Modern historians suspect the true figure was far higher, hidden in the same column where official tallies always hide their overflow: the poor, the unregistered, the unfound. The most famous fire in history has a body count that is less a number than a confession — a statement of what the record was able to see, mistaken for a statement of what happened. This series has a name for that mistake by now. It is the oldest one in the files.

The fire also demanded a culprit, catastrophes being unwilling to file as accidents, and it got one: a French watchmaker named Robert Hubert confessed to starting it, was disbelieved by nearly everyone involved in trying him — the evidence suggested he had not even arrived in London until after the fire began, and the bakery family itself did not recognize him — and was hanged anyway, the era’s justice preferring a wrong entry to a blank one. And then the lie was set in stone, literally: the Monument to the fire, raised in the 1670s — a column 202 feet tall, standing 202 feet from the site of the bakery, grief denominated in feet, the memorial itself a measurement — later had an inscription added blaming the burning on the treachery and malice of the popish faction. The carved accusation stood for a century and a half, was chiselled out, restored, and finally deleted for good in 1830. Attend to the accounting: a false entry, posted in marble, corrected after 164 years. Stone, it turns out, is just a slower ledger. Essay 1,633 gave you the record for institutional lag at 359 years; the Monument comes in at a brisk century and a half, and I record with mild despair that in this series’ files, 164 years now qualifies as responsiveness.

So there is the Beast’s year as London lived it: plague, fire, a scapegoat, a lie in stone. If the essay ended here, the pamphleteers would win it. But fifty-odd miles north of the ashes, in a Lincolnshire farmhouse called Woolsthorpe, the actual apocalypse of 1666 was taking place, and it was silent, and it involved no smoke whatsoever.

Cambridge University had closed for the plague — the same closure that emptied the colleges through 1665 and 1666 — and among the students sent home was a farmer’s son, twenty-three years old, undistinguished in the records, named Isaac Newton. And in those roughly eighteen months of enforced quarantine, working alone at the family manor, he did the following, per his own later account, which the scholarship substantially confirms even while noting the old man burnished the timeline: he developed the method of fluxions, which the world now calls calculus; he bought a prism at a fair and unwove white light into its spectrum, discovering that colour is not a modification of light but a component of it; and he began the train of thought about falling bodies — the famous apple enters here, and I apply the standing tongs, the apple being a story Newton himself told in old age, and old men improve their orchards — that led him to compare the fall of an object at the Earth’s surface with the perpetual fall of the Moon in its orbit, and to find that a single law, weakening with the square of the distance, would account for both. The plague year. The annus mirabilis, the scholars named it: the year of miracles. Every element of modern physical science — the mathematics of change, the nature of light, the law that binds the apple to the Moon — traces to one young man in one farmhouse in the one year the entire hemisphere had reserved for the end of the world.

And here, at last, is the idea at the centre of this essay: the world did end in 1666. Just not the one that was watching for it. The world that ended was the uncalculable one — the world in which the heavens were a mystery, light was a mystery, motion was a mystery, and a dreaded number could sit on the calendar like a loaded weapon because no one could say what governed anything. That world, which had lasted since the beginning, had roughly twenty years left when the fire went out; essay 1,687 will read its obituary. The pamphleteers scanned the sky for the Beast and missed the actual transformation entirely, because it looked like a young man doing sums — and I have come to believe this is the general case. The apocalypses that arrive are rarely the ones with the publicity. The calendar’s dreaded number produced a fire that rebuilt a city in brick; the farmhouse’s quiet arithmetic produced the world you are reading this on. Fear counted down to 1666 and got smoke. Understanding counted through it and got everything else. The instruction in Revelation, it turns out, was right all along, and nobody took it: let him that hath understanding count.

Dior was offered an apple this morning in commemoration, and the experiment enriched the literature: dropped from waist height, the apple described Newton’s predicted path for approximately one third of the distance before being intercepted, husky reflexes operating well inside gravitational time. She then declined to eat it, apples being, in her taxonomy, a ball that lies about itself. I have entered the result in the notebook as follows: the law of universal gravitation holds in this household subject to a standing exception, and the exception has amber eyes, and Newton never tested against a competent defence.

The count moves on, as it does. The next landmark is essay 1,687, and it is the one the whole century has been building toward: the year the quarantine arithmetic, grown to full strength, was published — three books, in Latin, laying out the mathematical frame of the universe entire, the same law running the tides, the Moon, the comets, and the apple. The auditor of essay 1,609 counted four new moons. The book of 1687 audits everything else.

The Beast got a year, a fire, and a column 202 feet tall. The farm boy got the universe. Count for yourself which number came due.

See you at 1,687, when the books of heaven are opened, and balance.
