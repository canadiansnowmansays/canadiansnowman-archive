Less than two weeks before the twenty-fifth anniversary of September 11, 2001, the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence published a report assessing a quarter century of American efforts to close the intelligence gaps that made those attacks possible — and concluded, in effect, that a new and comparably serious gap has already opened. As Garrett Downs reported for CNBC on August 31, 2026, the report warned that frontier AI models could make it significantly easier for a rogue actor, including a terrorist group, to develop and carry out a far more destructive attack than anything the intelligence community’s post-9/11 architecture was built to anticipate.

What the committee actually found

The report’s language on this point is unusually direct for a congressional document. It states plainly that preventing terrorist access to the expertise needed to build weapons of mass destruction has been a central priority of the national security establishment since 2001 — and that frontier large language models now threaten to undercut that entire project, by making destructive capability available to people who would otherwise lack the expertise to acquire it. The report does not accuse AI labs of indifference to this risk; it credits them with actively trying to prevent their systems from returning bioweapon-relevant information. Its concern is narrower and more unsettling: that the underlying capability keeps advancing faster than any lab’s safeguards can be verified to hold, and that stopping a determined user from eventually working around those safeguards is, in the committee’s own words, difficult to fully control.

The warning got a case file nine days later

As Dustin Volz reported in The New York Times on September 10, 2026, Anthropic published its own account of exactly the scenario this report warned about, in more concrete terms than the committee itself had access to. The company disclosed that it had disrupted several instances this year of scientists using Claude to conduct biological research it could not confirm was legitimate — including, in one case from May, a scientist seeking help writing a grant application for gain-of-function research on the chikungunya virus, aimed at engineering mutations that would make it more harmful through repeated infection of live animals, work Anthropic believed was intended for a military research institute. Jacob Klein, the company’s head of threat intelligence, was careful not to overstate the certainty: “You are not seeing someone in a comic book kind of way say, ‘Hey, I want to build a biological weapon to kill everybody.’ It’s an incredibly nuanced situation.” Anthropic’s report also documented three cases in China, two in Russia, and one — unnamed but unmistakably describing the Houthi militia — in Yemen, of actors attempting to use Claude to develop software for conventional weapons including firearms, missiles, and armed drones. Andrew Weber of the Council on Strategic Risks, who reviewed the report before publication, called the biological cases “chilling examples of state-sponsored biological weapons developers tapping into the rapidly advancing capabilities” of frontier models. Susan Monarez, a former CDC director who also reviewed the report, framed the danger in terms that echo this essay’s own argument about capability outracing safeguards: bad actors could “hide in plain sight, using seemingly legitimate research to create pathogens we may not see coming and may not be able to stop once released.”

That confirmation, however, arrived alongside a harder-edged rebuttal from working virologists that this series should take seriously rather than treat as a footnote. As Katherine Wu reported in The Atlantic the following day, most infectious-disease experts she spoke with — as opposed to AI-safety researchers — rank an AI-assisted pandemic well below the everyday threat already posed by naturally evolving viruses. Kevin Esvelt of MIT’s Secure Bio, who does take the risk seriously, still put the probability as low, arguing only that the consequences would be severe enough to justify driving that probability down further. Brian Hie, who led the team behind Evo, the genome-language model whose bacteria-killing virus designs triggered much of this year’s alarm, told Wu he’s “unconvinced that AI would even be the method of choice” for someone trying to build a dangerous pathogen — current models simply don’t understand virology well enough, because humans don’t either. Seema Lakdawala and Linsey Marr, two virologists who use Claude and ChatGPT regularly in their own research, told Wu the tools frequently flag or halt entirely ordinary technical questions about flu genetics, “almost daily” in Marr’s case — meaning the same overcaution that let Anthropic block the chikungunya request is also actively obstructing the legitimate research the field needs to prepare for the pandemics that don’t require AI at all. Gigi Gronvall of Johns Hopkins supplied the bottleneck argument that most directly complicates the House committee’s framing: no matter how capable the model, nothing about generating a dangerous genome sequence gets a bad actor past the genuinely difficult parts — synthesizing it, testing it, producing enough of it, and releasing it — steps that still require lab skill, equipment, and animal models AI cannot supply.

Read together, these two pieces sharpen rather than resolve the committee’s own warning. Anthropic’s report proves the threat model is not hypothetical — real actors, including at least one apparent state-linked military institute, are already trying. Wu’s reporting supplies the counterweight this series’ own scepticism toward borrowed urgency requires: the same overcautious guardrails blocking bad-faith gain-of-function requests are, by the researchers’ own account, also blocking good-faith flu research, and the technical bottleneck standing between a genome sequence and an actual weapon remains real, human, and un-automated for now.

The bipartisan authorship is worth naming in full, because it cuts across exactly the coalition lines that make Washington AI policy usually predictable. Committee Chair Rick Crawford, an Arkansas Republican, and ranking member Jim Himes, a Connecticut Democrat, produced this report jointly with Elise Stefanik of New York and Josh Gottheimer of New Jersey — four members spanning the hawkish and centrist wings of both parties, none of whom built a career on AI scepticism, all putting their names to language this blunt. The committee’s overall verdict: the United States faces a threat environment as complex and dangerous as any since the attacks whose twenty-fifth anniversary this report was explicitly timed to precede.

Three warnings, three different fixes, none of them slowing the thing down

This series now has three distinct institutional responses to the same underlying anxiety about frontier AI capability, and it is worth setting them side by side, because none of them proposes the one intervention that would most directly address the shared premise: slowing the pace at which the capability itself advances.

Part 66 covered the pacing letter — more than a thousand AI-company employees asking their own governments to help find a way to slow development, on the theory that safety work cannot keep pace with capability otherwise. Part 90 covered the industry letter — more than a hundred companies, including several of the same labs whose employees signed the pacing letter, asking governments to fund and coordinate cyberdefense using AI models these companies would supply. This report adds a third position, and it is the least constraining of the three: Congress’s own intelligence committee, rather than asking labs to slow down or asking government to fund external defenders, is asking the intelligence community to accelerate its own adoption of advanced AI capabilities so that the United States stays ahead of its adversaries. It also calls for investing in secure AI tools for collection, analysis, and warning, paired with human oversight and privacy protections.

The contradiction did not go unnoticed. As Mike Pearl wrote for Gizmodo on September 1, 2026, the report places its call to keep advanced AI out of bad actors’ hands and its call to speed up the intelligence community’s own adoption of the same technology side by side, without reconciling the two. Pearl also questioned the “failure of imagination” framing the committee borrowed from the original 9/11 Commission: the 2001 failure, he argued, was less about imagination than about agencies that had already identified some of the hijackers and failed to act, and the new report offers no comparably specific preventive steps for AI. The phrase came to the committee from Benjamin Buchanan of Johns Hopkins, who told it, as Caitrin Assaf reported for Gray News on September 3, 2026, that “the Sept. 11 attacks revealed a failure of imagination, and 25 years later, artificial intelligence presents a similar kind of challenge.” The final report carries twenty new recommendations, most of them about integration, coordination and adopting emerging technology fast enough to match adversaries.

Read together, these three responses trace a fairly clean arc from the periphery of the industry toward its centre of gravity. Workers ask for restraint. Companies ask for wider distribution of their own tools. Congress asks the government to consume more of those same tools, faster. At no point in any of the three does an institution with the standing to actually do so propose constraining the pace of frontier model development itself. TACO’s obfuscate column exists for exactly this pattern: three separate, sincere-sounding responses to one problem, each one real and none of which touches the variable everyone agrees is the actual driver of the risk.

The candor that deserves credit

One line in the report cuts against that pattern and deserves to be quoted rather than paraphrased, because it is a rare instance of a congressional committee describing its own institution’s readiness honestly rather than in the reassuring language such reports usually default to: serious gaps remain in the country’s ability to confront and address these vulnerabilities, and the United States is not adequately prepared to confront and address them. That is Congress’s own oversight committee, writing about the intelligence apparatus Congress itself is charged with overseeing, declining to soften the verdict. It is a more honest sentence than most of what this series has catalogued from the industry side of this same debate, and it should be acknowledged as such even while its practical remedy — accelerate adoption — sits in obvious tension with the caution the rest of the report’s language calls for.

Why the anniversary framing matters, and why it should be handled carefully

The report’s timing, arriving directly ahead of 9/11’s twenty-fifth anniversary, is not incidental — it is a document explicitly built to audit the intelligence community’s post-9/11 reforms, and its authors clearly intend the anniversary to lend the AI warning some of the same institutional weight. That is a legitimate rhetorical choice when the underlying comparison is sound; a bioweapon-capable actor empowered by frontier AI is, categorically, the kind of catastrophic, low-probability, high-consequence risk the phrase “Black Swan” and the post-9/11 reform apparatus were both built to address. But this series has spent ninety-one parts warning against exactly this kind of borrowed urgency when it’s deployed loosely — the “lose the AI race to China” framing catalogued in Part 86 being the clearest prior example of a genuine anxiety doing double duty as a lobbying tool. The distinction that matters here: unlike that framing, this report’s authors are not asking for less oversight or fewer constraints on the industry. They are asking their own institution to do more, faster, which is a considerably harder position to dismiss as self-serving, even if it is also, on its own logic, an incomplete answer to the risk it describes.

What this adds to the ledger of unresolved questions

Every part of this series that has touched frontier AI risk — the pacing letter, the cyberdefense letter, now this report — arrives at the same unfinished sentence: something needs to change about the pace of development, followed immediately by a recommendation that changes something adjacent to that pace instead. Workers ask governments to help slow labs down; nothing has slowed. Labs ask governments to help distribute their tools to defenders; as of early October 2026, no funding mechanism had materialised. Congress asks its own intelligence agencies to catch up faster; the agencies’ own overseers admitted in August that they were not equipped to. Three institutions, one shared diagnosis, and, twenty-five years after the intelligence failure this report was commissioned to study, still no answer to the actual question its authors put in writing: who is actually adequately prepared, and what would it take to make that true before the next Black Swan arrives rather than after.
