I. A Greeting Presidents Don’t Usually Give

Presidents typically meet foreign heads of state at the White House. On Wednesday evening, 23 September 2026, Donald Trump instead drove out to Joint Base Andrews and stood on the tarmac for forty-five minutes waiting for an Air China jet to land. When it did, at 5:39 p.m., Xi Jinping and his wife, Peng Liyuan, descended to a red carpet lined with U.S. service members, a military flyover, and the national anthem, at the end of which Trump gave Xi a pat on the shoulder. It was, by the standards of five essays’ worth of choreography this series has already catalogued, still an escalation — the honour guard rehearsals Part II tracked, the ballroom Part II found unfinished, all of it building to a greeting American presidents reserve for the rarest occasions.

Zolan Kanno-Youngs and colleagues, live-blogging the arrival for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, captured the ceremony in real time: the red carpet unrolled specifically for the occasion, two children standing by with flowers, Trump and Melania departing afterward in “The Beast” while Trump boarded Marine One for the White House. Thursday brings a second welcome ceremony at the White House itself, a full day of talks, a state dinner, and a tour of the National Archives — the full two-day treatment this series predicted back in Part II, now actually happening.

II. The Number Nobody Guessed

Part VI closed on an open question: would the trade truce extend for the six months Washington had proposed, or the longer window Beijing wanted? Neither. Scott Bessent, talking to Fox News from the tarmac within minutes of Xi’s arrival, announced the two countries had agreed to extend the truce to 10 January 2027 — just under two months past its original 30 November expiration, shorter than either side’s publicly reported opening position. “This will give us more time to see what we can do on the economic front,” Bessent said. Whatever else Thursday’s talks produce, the truce itself has become exactly what Part VI’s “shin-kicking” framing predicted it would: not a settlement, a placeholder renewed just long enough to avoid a crisis before the next placeholder is needed.

III. Who Wasn’t Filming

The arrival ceremony should have been the kind of moment television exists for. Instead, no major network camera captured it. Since Monday, the five networks that operate the White House press pool — ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox News, and NBC — have declined to film Trump’s public events in solidarity with CNN, MS NOW, and Politico, all barred from White House grounds over coverage Trump has called unfair. A federal judge, Timothy J. Kelly, heard arguments on the ban’s legality that same afternoon and declined to rule from the bench, so camera crews that had set up at Andrews on the chance of a favourable ruling packed up and left before Xi’s plane touched down. The Associated Press captured footage and made it available to subscribers but declined to stream it on its own platforms, saying it stood “with our colleagues at other news organizations who have had their access restricted.” What filled the gap were One America News, the Right Side Broadcasting Network, and LindellTV — a landmark moment in the relationship between the world’s two largest economies, and the cameras that would normally have broadcast it to the country sat dark.

IV. The Split-Screen

Erica L. Green, reporting for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, documented just how far Trump has gone to avoid confrontation with Xi even as the list of grievances against Beijing has grown genuinely long this year: the “sinister election meddling” accusation aired in a prime-time address, Chinese satellite imagery reportedly supplied to Iran’s military, Beijing’s continued oil purchases from Tehran in defiance of sanctions. None of it has changed Trump’s posture. Senator Roger Wicker, the Republican chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, used a Tuesday floor speech to say he would have advised against inviting a “brutal, unelected and oppressive dictator who seeks to dominate his neighbors and whose massive military arsenal is aimed directly at the United States of America” — a description of Xi from a member of Trump’s own party, days before the state dinner. Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s response leaned entirely on realism rather than rebuttal: “The idea that we would not interact with them at the highest levels is irresponsible. It’s outrageous. We have to.”

Julian Gewirtz, a Columbia scholar and former Biden-era China official, gave Green the line this essay has been circling since Part IV: “Trump is displaying an extraordinary degree of deference to Xi. He is lavishing honors on China’s leader while downplaying threats to American interests. Beijing could scarcely have asked for a more accommodating host.” His sharper question — “What is Trump getting for America in return for all this ceremony?” — remains unanswered by anything Wednesday actually produced. R. Nicholas Burns, the top U.S. diplomat in Beijing under Biden, named the rule this violates plainly: “treat your allies better than your adversaries,” and warned that being “unremittingly uncritical of the Chinese” sends America’s actual allies a message about where Trump’s priorities sit. Trump’s own quotes, gathered by Green across several recent appearances, read like a man actively working to defuse his own grievances before he’s even sat down with Xi: asked about Chinese satellite support for Iran’s military, he offered “they basically do what we do”; asked whether China spies on the U.S., “I say you’re right, and we spy on them too”; describing the Iran war generally, “small potatoes.”

V. The Lame Duck in the Room

Jonathan Czin, the Brookings researcher and former CIA China analyst this series first quoted in Part V and Part VI, gave Green a sharper, more dated version of the asymmetric-clocks argument those essays already built. Xi is “going to be keenly aware of the fact that just weeks from now, Trump is in all likelihood going to be at best a lame-duck president, and at worst, he’s going to be politically maimed at home and have to deal with divided government.” Xi, per Czin, isn’t negotiating for this week’s headlines. He’s managing “how am I going to keep this gentlemen’s agreement going with the United States” — buying time, already planning for whoever comes after Trump. It’s the same patience this series has documented since Part V, now stated with an actual expiry date attached to Trump’s own leverage.

VI. The Pattern This Fits

Ruth Ben-Ghiat, the NYU historian of authoritarianism, placed Wednesday’s warmth inside a pattern rather than treating it as a one-off: Trump’s affinity for Putin, his summer praise for Kim Jong-un’s leadership (delivered while cancelling long-running military exercises with South Korea, a treaty ally), and now Xi. She pointed to the specific contradiction Green’s reporting surfaced days earlier — Trump’s declaration that “communism is the greatest threat to our country in its history” sitting alongside his fawning over the leader of one of the world’s most powerful communist states — and to an exchange from the week before, when a reporter noted China is among the most repressive countries in the world for journalists and Trump answered, “I don’t know about that. I think we’re repressive also,” the same day he formalized the press ban this essay’s own Section III just documented. “This meeting of head of states has importance for the optics it generates,” Ben-Ghiat told Green. “Two powerful strongmen, meeting as equals, at least in Trump’s mind — and for the potential for deals conducted in a nontransparent fashion.”

VII. The Public Doesn’t Seem to Mind

While Trump’s own posture toward China has softened, American public opinion has moved in the same direction alongside him — or perhaps ahead of him. Anton Troianovski, reporting for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, rounded up polling that shows a genuine shift: Pew found 54 percent of Americans now hold a “cold” view of China, down from 68 percent in 2021. The Chicago Council on Global Affairs found the share of Americans favouring “friendly cooperation and engagement with China” rose from 40 percent in 2024 to 57 percent this July — 68 percent of Democrats, but also 43 percent of Republicans. Gallup’s favourability number, 34 percent, is more than double its 2023 low. None of the polling identifies a single cause; researchers point to fading Covid memories, growing awareness of Chinese tech, and — pointedly — disaffection with Trump’s own trade wars. Laura Silver of Pew connected the generational divide directly: younger Americans “have known China as a superpower” rather than the developing country of Tiananmen-era memory. Nick Zeller of the Carter Center offered the sharpest read of what’s actually driving the numbers: “You see fears of what’s happening to America be refracted through assessments of China. This positive assessment toward China has more to do with American political tumult domestically than it does China itself.” Bruce Stokes of the German Marshall Fund put a number on how much room that shift has opened for Trump’s own accommodation: a year or two ago, “politicians could propose almost any kind of tough policy against China and the public would be behind it.” That license has visibly weakened.

VIII. What Comes After Washington

This summit was never going to be the last one. Edward Wong and Michael Crowley, reporting for The New York Times on 23 September 2026, confirmed Trump has invited Vladimir Putin to December’s G20 summit in Miami — Putin’s first in-person G20 appearance since 2019 — and that Xi is expected to attend the same gathering. Separately, Rubio confirmed Trump will travel to Shenzhen in mid-November for the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, where he’ll meet Xi again, their third face-to-face meeting inside a single year. Whatever gets settled or left unsettled at this week’s summit has an expiration date built in twice over: the truce itself runs out 10 January, and the next occasion for renegotiating it arrives even sooner, in Shenzhen, before the year is out.

IX. The Scorecard, One More Time

None of Wednesday’s developments resolve the TACO scorecard this series has tracked since Part I. If anything, they confirm its most recent entry: Bessent’s own “shin-kicking under the table” metaphor from Part VI describes Wednesday’s choreography precisely — warmth on camera, genuine grievances (Iran, satellite imagery, election-meddling allegations, the muted arms sale this series has tracked since Part IV) parked off to the side, unmentioned by either leader in public. The truce extension is Accommodate, dressed as diplomacy. The unanswered grievances are Obfuscate, exactly as this series predicted back when Taiwan was the only subject getting that treatment. What’s new this week is the audience: an American public considerably less inclined than it was two years ago to punish a president for extending China that grace.

Thursday’s actual talks — the Trump-Xi meeting itself, whatever gets said about Taiwan, whatever the AI dialogue produces beyond Wednesday’s notification-system framing — are still to come. This entry covers arrival day only.
