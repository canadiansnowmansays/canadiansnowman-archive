I. The Promise in the 2004 Letter

As Kate Conger reported for The New York Times, when Google went public in 2004, Larry Page told shareholders the company believed “a well-functioning society should have abundant, free and unbiased access to high-quality information.” For twenty years, Google kept something like that promise by acting as a gateway — a list of links pointing outward, to millions of sites it didn’t own and didn’t need to. That was the entire business model: send people away, and they’ll come back next time they need to leave again, and Google would answer people’s queries with hyperlinks rather than conclusions.

II. The Gate That Stopped Opening

AI Mode inverts the model. Since last year, Google has revamped its search around it, replacing lists of hyperlinks with conversational responses written by Gemini, and this year changed its search box for the first time in twenty-five years to let people add photos and videos to queries and assign AI “agents” to run searches on their behalf. Instead of links, Gemini writes the answer directly in the results, and increasingly, that answer is where the visit ends.

Users are now writing queries three times longer than the keyword-heavy questions traditional search trained them to type — a figure Google itself disclosed at its developer conference in May, framing longer queries as evidence people are engaging more deeply with the product rather than evidence they’ve stopped needing anywhere else to go. Three separate studies tallied by the Times found people spending one to nine additional minutes inside AI Mode compared with traditional search. One October study from Growth Memo, a newsletter focused on search and marketing, found that in roughly three-quarters of AI Mode sessions, users never left the interface for an outside website at all.

Cloudflare’s own data shows the consequence at the scale of the entire web: more than half of all traffic is now non-human, and human traffic to finance, publishing, and retail sites fell nearly 40 percent between June 2025 and April 2026 — a statistic that would have sounded like paranoia five years ago and now reads as measurement. Cloudflare’s own framing is blunt: the web’s fundamental economic model has changed, and tools like AI Mode could put the open web in peril if left unchecked. Where someone might once have searched a soccer player’s World Cup performance and clicked through to a sports site or a social-media thread for commentary, a bot now crawls the web for that information and returns the statistics directly, and the user never leaves Google at all — the click that used to fund the sports site’s ad revenue simply doesn’t happen anymore, replaced by a summary assembled from that same site’s own reporting.

III. The Company Disputing Its Own Numbers

Google’s response has been to dispute the framing rather than the underlying shift. In a blog post last August, Liz Reid, a Google vice president of search, wrote that the company’s ethos hadn’t changed and that it was sending out a “relatively stable” number of clicks, numbering in the billions; she called reports of a search-traffic decline “inaccurate” and reliant on “flawed methodologies,” adding, “We care passionately — perhaps more than any other company — about the health of the web ecosystem.” A company spokesman later reiterated to the Times that Google’s AI search features “send billions of clicks to the web every week,” and separately argued that the Growth Memo study finding 75 percent of sessions never left AI Mode was itself flawed, relying on a small sample and tasks that didn’t reflect normal user behavior.

Both of those things can be true at once — billions of clicks a week, and a much larger proportional decline in the traffic that used to flow to any single publisher — which is exactly what a still-large absolute number sitting next to a shrinking share looks like from the outside. Nilay Patel, editor in chief of The Verge, has warned publishers for years about what he calls “Google Zero,” the moment traffic from Google plummets to nothing. “For publishers, Google Zero is already here,” he told the Times. “It wasn’t until AI Mode arrived that people could see that the slow creep was about to hit a tipping point.”

IV. The Publishers Paying for It

The clearest casualty in Conger’s reporting is Vox Media. Jim Bankoff, the company’s chief executive, told the Times that the “decimation of search traffic” directly influenced his decision to explore other ways of reaching readers, including building out a podcast network, and ultimately to sell about half the company to James Murdoch. In June, The Verge and several other Vox Media sites were sold to Penske Media. Patel’s own summary of the contradiction he’s watched play out for years: “Google has often said it was ‘sending out more traffic and the web is bigger than ever.’ And then right next to that is a bunch of publishers whose businesses are getting destroyed.”

Since publishing Reid’s blog post, Google has added still more AI to search, while also introducing features meant to counter exactly this complaint — website previews inside some AI Mode responses, a “preferred sources” tool letting users specify which outlets they want cited more often, and a new profile system to help publishers and creators promote their own work inside search. Whether those additions meaningfully redirect traffic back to the sites AI Mode is displacing, or simply soften the optics of the displacement, is precisely the question Google’s own dueling statistics leave unresolved.

V. Wikipedia, Paying to Be Read

The clearest evidence that this isn’t merely a publisher complaint sits with an institution that has no ad-supported business model to protect: Wikipedia. The Wikimedia Foundation reported an 8 percent drop in human visitors over the past year, even as bot traffic scraping the site for AI training rose. “My work is to ensure that people don’t forget about us because Google’s user interface changed,” Selena Deckelmann, Wikimedia’s chief product and technology officer, told the Times. Wikipedia has started promoting its own app and social channels specifically to route around Google’s interface, and now charges Google and other AI firms for training access to the same content Google’s answers are quietly built on. That is the shape of the gap in miniature: a nonprofit encyclopedia paying licensing fees to the company whose product is displacing the readers who used to fund it by clicking an ad on the way through.

Not everyone reads the shift as existential. Tori Noble, a staff attorney at the Electronic Frontier Foundation, told the Times that whether Google answers with links or an AI summary, “I think Google is still facilitating access to the open internet. It’s not a closed universe,” she added, drawing a distinction with genuinely walled platforms like Facebook that require a login to enter at all. That’s a fair distinction on its own terms — AI Mode isn’t Facebook, and nobody needs a login to leave it. It’s also a lower bar than the one Larry Page’s 2004 letter set for the company, and a lower bar than the one Google itself still claims, in Reid’s language, to be clearing. Noble’s comfort with the current arrangement and Bankoff’s experience of watching half his company get sold off are both accurate descriptions of the same product; the difference is simply whether you’re the platform doing the answering or the publisher whose article the answer was quietly built from.

VI. The One Regulator That Moved

Only one government body has actually forced a structural change rather than accepting Google’s account of its own traffic: Britain’s Competition and Markets Authority, which last month mandated that Google change its AI search tools in the United Kingdom to address publishers’ concerns. The regulator ordered Google to include clear links attributing content in chatbot responses, aimed at helping send traffic back to websites, and to let website operators opt out of having their content appear in AI summaries at all — with the company then applying the changes across search globally rather than confining them to the UK. The order didn’t ask Google to stop summarizing web content in AI Mode, or to send a fixed share of traffic back to publishers — it asked only for attribution and an opt-out, the floor of what accountability could look like rather than anything resembling a ceiling. That the floor still had to be legislated, in a market Google doesn’t operate in as dominantly as it does in the U.S., is itself instructive.

That a single national regulator produced more concrete accountability here than anything in the American system says something about where this series has consistently found the Gap sitting — not in the absence of possible remedies, but in the absence of anyone empowered and willing to require them before the damage compounds.

VII. TACO, Applied to a Vanishing Click

Run this through the TACO framework this series has used since Part I. Threaten doesn’t fit; nothing coercive is happening, just an interface quietly absorbing what used to be a redirect. Accommodate is Google’s preview links, “preferred sources” settings, and creator-promotion tools — real features, layered onto a design that still defaults to keeping the user inside Google’s own answer, and announced in the same window as Reid’s dispute over the underlying numbers. Capitulate, a redesign that treats sending people elsewhere as the product’s actual purpose again rather than an option to configure, exists only where the UK forced it. Obfuscate is the dispute over methodology itself — Google contesting the 75-percent figure and the “flawed” sample size behind it, rather than the underlying direction every other data source, including Cloudflare’s and its own click totals, points toward.

VIII. The Gap, at the Scale of the Entire Web

This series has spent sixty-seven parts describing the Gap as the space between what a company builds and what anyone outside the company can compel it to disclose or repair. This is the same extraction running at the scale of the web itself — not researchers, but attention, clicks, and the ad revenue that used to fund the sites Google once sent people to. Tim Berners-Lee, who invented the web in 1989 envisioning a tool for global collaboration without borders, wrote in a 2024 essay that tech companies had already spent years building “walled gardens” focused on maximizing profit over public utility, and that “the rapid advancement of A.I. has exacerbated these concerns.”

What this month’s reporting shows is a company that spent twenty years profiting from the web’s openness now building, click by click, the largest walled garden the open web has ever had — and calling it, with a straight face, an improvement in how people find information. Much of Berners-Lee’s original vision has already eroded gradually, chipped away over years by platforms like Apple and Meta building tech ecosystems where users could get nearly everything they needed under one roof, never needing to leave. What AI Mode adds isn’t a new kind of walled garden so much as the first one built by the company that spent two decades positioning itself as the walled garden’s opposite — the neutral doorway everyone else had to build their own walls to compete with.
