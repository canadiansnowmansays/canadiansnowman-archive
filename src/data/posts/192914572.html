“If it takes 100 blows of the axe to cut down a tree, you don’t say the first 99 were useless.” — An Iranian friend, WhatsApp, 2022

There is a question that gets asked about Iran in polite company — in think tanks, in diaspora living rooms, on X threads between exiles who haven’t agreed on anything since 1979 — and it goes like this: What do Iranians actually want?

The question used to have a reasonably clear answer. Now it has a fault line.

Understanding how we got from the answer to the fault line requires going back further than the bombs. It requires going back to the minders. And then, uncomfortably, it requires going forward — past the minders, past the massacres, past the bombs — to a murder in Canada, and to a movement that claims to speak for Iranian democracy while behaving like the thing it says it wants to replace.

I. The Women Who Stood Between

Laura Secor is an American journalist who visited Iran five times between 2004 and 2012, each visit mediated by a government-assigned handler whose job was simultaneously to protect her, surveil her, and prevent her from seeing anything worth seeing. Her account of those years — published in The Atlantic as “Someday in Tehran” — is one of the most honest pieces of long-form journalism about Iran’s democratic tragedy that I have read. I commend it without reservation.

What Secor observed across five visits was not simply repression. It was something more architecturally interesting: a system that conscripted ordinary people into its own enforcement, that gave the indifferent a professional stake in the regime’s survival, that converted private ambivalence into public complicity. Her first minder, Pardis, was not a true believer. She was an orphan, unlucky in love, risking her job if her foreign charge slipped away. She had no ideology. She had consequences. The Islamic Republic did not need true believers to function. It needed people who couldn’t afford not to comply.

This is how authoritarian systems persist long past the point where their founding ideas have lost all moral credibility. Not through universal conviction. Through the distribution of small stakes.

II. The Infrastructure of Hope

What had drawn Secor to Iran in the first place was something genuinely remarkable in the context of the Muslim world: a democratic reform movement built by former revolutionaries.

The reformists of the 1990s were not liberals who had never been near power. They were insiders — hostage-takers, religious philosophers, founders of the security forces — who had fallen from favour and spent a decade remaking themselves as advocates of incremental democratic change. They produced a theoretical literature. They mobilized young people. They won elections. Under President Mohammad Khatami (1997–2005), semi-independent newspapers appeared. NGOs formed. Student activists organized. The democratic infrastructure was real, if fragile: it existed in the form of trained people, civic expectations, and a political network connecting society to the state.

Khamenei spent Khatami’s entire presidency trying to kill it. His apparatus tortured journalists until they confessed to invented conspiracies on national television. It shot a political theorist on the steps of Tehran’s city hall. It eventually succeeded in closing virtually every reformist publication and excluding reformist candidates from most public offices.

The last great test of whether any of this infrastructure could withstand the pressure was 2009.

III. The Green Movement and What It Proved

The Green Movement was not a Twitter revolution. It was a movement with history, experienced leaders, articulated ideas, a pragmatic strategy, and a networked constituency. When millions poured into the streets after what was almost certainly a stolen election, they did not demand the end of the Islamic Republic. They followed the cautious, legalistic reformist playbook. They held placards that read WHERE IS MY VOTE? They were, in their disciplined restraint, making the most sophisticated argument available to them: adhere to your own rules.

The Islamic Republic’s response was categorical. Bullets. Tear gas. Batons. Torture. Show trials. A shipping container in the desert where lesser-known activists were remanded, raped, and killed. By 2010, even publishing a photograph of former President Khatami was forbidden. The movement’s leaders — Mousavi and Karroubi — were placed under house arrest that would last fifteen years.

What the Green Movement proved, definitively, was that the Islamic Republic would not negotiate. The reformist strategy — pressure from below, negotiation at the top — had one fatal assumption: that at the top, someone was listening. Khamenei never was.

After 2009, the democratic infrastructure was, as Secor puts it, “mostly torched.” What remained was the yearning and the anger, unmoored from any organizing framework capable of channelling them toward a realistic outcome.

IV. The Hundred Blows

What followed was a cycle that became almost unbearable to watch from the outside. Protests erupted in 2017, in 2018, in 2019. Lower-class Iranians in provincial cities chanted “Death to Khamenei” with a directness that the reformist era would never have permitted. The world held its breath. The Islamic Republic killed. The world exhaled.

In 2022, the Women, Life, Freedom uprising changed the symbolic register entirely. The hijab — simultaneously a tool and a symbol of state suffocation — became the site of civil disobedience without precedent in the Republic’s history. Women removed it publicly, en masse, as an act of self-reclamation. The regime killed approximately 500 people and imprisoned perhaps 20,000. It also, for the first time, began losing the enforcement fight: women began appearing uncovered in public with relative impunity, and the morality police largely retreated from the most aggressive street-level enforcement.

A small thing. But new. A crack in the ironwood.

Then, in January 2026, the Islamic Republic answered with something qualitatively different: a massacre of world-historic proportions. At least 6,000 dead, by conservative estimates. Possibly more than 30,000. Something broke inside of nearly every Iranian who had been watching, or ignited: a fireball of rage and trauma.

One hundred blows of the axe. Some of them had landed.

V. The Fault Line

And then the bombs came. American and Israeli bombs, falling on Iranian soil, killing the man in the bunker.

And here is where the democratic dream fractures into something that cannot be simply described as hope deferred.

Secor identifies the fault line with precision. On one side: those who still hold sacred the revolution’s broadest animating impulses — the rejection of monarchy, the rejection of American dominion, the assertion of Iranian sovereignty over Iranian resources and Iranian political fate. These were not Khamenei’s values, as his record of corruption and kleptocracy proved abundantly. But they were, in 1979, the values of a broad coalition that included liberals and leftists alongside the Islamists who ultimately consumed all the others.

On the other side: those who have concluded that not only the Islamic Republic, but the revolution itself, was a wrong turn — and who have therefore embraced both the son of the deposed Shah as a future leader, and American military force as the means of installing him.

These two positions are not merely different. They are, to each camp, anathema.

One of Secor’s old contacts — an exiled activist — told her privately that she had reached the point of fantasizing about returning as an armed resistance fighter: the nonviolent path had simply reached a dead end. Another contact sent a different message: if the Iranians celebrating the violent destruction of their country come to power by means of it, can they really be said to be pro-democratic? How will they treat their opponents?

It is the second question that I cannot get out of my head. And it is the second question that now has an answer — or at least a data point — from an unexpected source.

Canada.

VI. The Prince and the Rottweilers

Illustration by Mark Harris. Sources: Getty; Voice of America; James Willoughby / Getty.

Robert F. Worth, writing in The Atlantic this week in a piece titled “The Iranian Royalists’ Thuggish Edge,” has produced the most thorough English-language examination I have seen of what the Pahlavi movement has actually become — as opposed to what it says it is.

The portrait is not flattering.

Reza Pahlavi, the sixty-five-year-old son of Iran’s last Shah, has been the most visible figure in the Iranian opposition for decades, largely by default. He says the right things about democracy. His personal brand was built on nonviolent resistance and the teachings of Gene Sharp. His motto for years was “Today, only unity” — a deliberate signal that he wanted a cohesive front against the regime, not a coronation.

But about a decade ago, according to Worth’s reporting, Pahlavi brought on two young deputies — Saeed Ghasseminejad and Amir Etemadi — who remodelled the operation. Ghasseminejad had spent eight years at the Foundation for the Defense of Democracies, a right-wing Washington think tank with deep ties to Netanyahu. The new advisers had a message for their boss: the old hatred of the Pahlavi dynasty had faded inside Iran, replaced by a manufactured nostalgia for the pre-revolutionary era, fed by satellite television’s gauzy images of parties and beaches with no mention of SAVAK, the Shah’s secret police. A new revolutionary mood was rising. The prince could ride it.

The price of admission, it turned out, was the elimination of dissent within the opposition itself. Worth quotes a former associate: these advisers believe that “crushing the opposition is as important as fighting the regime. They really believe Pahlavi can’t be effective unless he’s the only voice.”

An older associate of the prince described the two advisers to Worth as “rottweilers” — obsequious to Pahlavi, hostile and rude to everyone else. Those who fell out with them found themselves targeted by coordinated online harassment campaigns, called lackeys of the regime, told to shut up. When one critic’s elderly father died in Iran in 2024, members of the prince’s online ecosystem posted insults about the dead man.

The economic adviser, Ghasseminejad, has repeatedly posted about his desire to see regime officials hanged after their overthrow. The prince’s movement, which says it wants democracy, adopted an enforced loyalty test that would be familiar to anyone who has watched MAGA operate in America: you are either with us, or you are with the enemy. One of Pahlavi’s own advisers posted it explicitly: “You are either with Prince Reza Pahlavi or with the Islamic Republic.”

TACO, my analytical friends. Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The Pahlavi operation runs the first three in sequence and deploys the fourth when anyone notices.

VII. The Murder in Canada

In early February, while the world was watching the Persian Gulf, an outspoken Iranian exile named Masood Masjoody disappeared in Canada.

Masjoody was a mathematician and an activist based in Canada who had started as a Pahlavi admirer before becoming one of the movement’s most forceful critics. He had named two Pahlavi supporters — Mehdi Ahmadzadeh Razavi and Arezou Soltani — claiming they were plotting to silence him. When his body was found on March 6, the Canadian police charged Razavi and Soltani with first-degree murder. Court documents suggest the pair consulted a naturopath in Vancouver in an effort to obtain a deadly substance.

Days after Masjoody was last seen alive, ten other well-known diaspora figures were tagged in an anonymous message on X: “Soon you’ll have to find the corpses of many.”

Worth is careful to note that there is no evidence linking Pahlavi or his advisers to the murder. I will be equally careful. But the killing happened inside a political ecosystem that Pahlavi’s operation built, or at minimum tolerated and fed — an ecosystem of enforced loyalty, online harassment campaigns, and the systematic dehumanization of critics. When you spend years telling your supporters that anyone who questions the prince is a traitor working for the regime, you are building the architecture within which someone, eventually, decides that traitors deserve what traitors get.

I am watching this from Toronto. The man was murdered in Canada. That is not an abstraction. That is my country.

VIII. The Astroturf Crown

There is a further dimension that Worth documents, and it is one that should give pause to anyone who points to Pahlavi’s popularity inside Iran as evidence of an organic democratic mandate.

In April 2023, Pahlavi visited Israel, where he was received by Netanyahu and the intelligence minister as though he were already a head of state. He promised that a post-Islamic Republic Iran would instantly recognize Israel. The visit, as one former associate told Worth, “broke a taboo” and propelled Pahlavi to a different stage.

What followed was documented by a social media analyst named Geoff Golberg for the National Iranian American Council: a massive coordinated influence campaign involving nearly five thousand inauthentic accounts, producing hundreds of millions of posts praising Pahlavi and attacking organizations that favoured American diplomacy with the Islamic Republic. Golberg found connections between some of these accounts and official Israeli government accounts. Haaretz subsequently reported that a private entity receiving Israeli government support had been established specifically to promote Pahlavi, recruiting native Farsi speakers.

Meanwhile, Iran International — a satellite television network set up under Saudi auspices in 2017, which has reportedly lost hundreds of millions of dollars while keeping its ownership and finances secret — has heavily promoted him.

None of this means Pahlavi’s support is entirely manufactured. A 2024 survey by a respected Iranian polling organization found that 31 percent of respondents supported Pahlavi — more than three times the level of any other figure. Khamenei, at the time, polled at 9 percent. Narges Mohammadi, the imprisoned Nobel laureate, rated 5 percent. The support is real enough. But it exists inside an information environment that has been systematically shaped by state actors — Israeli, Saudi, and possibly others — whose interests in Iran are not organized around the question of Iranian democracy.

Pahlavi’s own response to the war’s outcome has been revealing. Worth reports that supporters on his nonprofit board called for Tehran to be bombed with five thousand targets daily, described regime officials as “animals,” and expressed fury at the peace talks. One prominent supporter proposed that the U.S. military secure a landing zone inside an Iranian city so the prince could be physically delivered to his kingdom. Pahlavi himself has been touring Europe accusing governments that didn’t participate in the war of appeasement.

This is not the language of Gene Sharp. This is the language of a movement that has abandoned nonviolence in everything but its press releases.

IX. The Second Question

Can people who came to power through violent external intervention really be said to be pro-democratic? How will they treat their opponents?

The question that I could not get out of my head now has, if not an answer, a body of evidence.

They will treat their opponents the way the Pahlavi movement has treated its opponents for the past decade: with loyalty tests, coordinated harassment, the language of treason, and — in at least one case — something worse. They will fundraise among wealthy Iranian Americans, some of whom, as Worth reports, ask the obvious question: “Why replace one dictator with another?” They will conduct themselves like a monarchy while calling themselves democrats. They will TACO their way through every press conference.

History is not encouraging here. The United States helped overthrow Iran’s last democratic government, in 1953, and installed the Shah whose kleptocracy and SAVAK secret police made the 1979 revolution not only possible but, to many Iranians, necessary. The lesson drawn by that generation — that American intervention had stolen Iranian sovereignty once before — is not an irrational lesson. It is the correct lesson from a historical event that actually happened.

This does not mean that the Islamic Republic was better than the Shah. It was not. It does not mean that Khamenei deserved to die peacefully in his bed. He did not. It does not mean that the bombs accomplished nothing for the Iranian people. They may have accomplished a great deal.

It means that the question is not rhetorical. It is constitutional. It is the question that determines whether what emerges from the rubble is a new Iran or a new version of an old arrangement: outside powers choosing Iran’s government, Iranians paying the price.

X. What the Twenty Years Proved

Here is what the twenty years between Khatami and the bombs proved:

The Iranian people do not lack the desire for democracy. They demonstrated it in 2005, in 2009, in 2017, in 2019, in 2022. They demonstrated it with newspapers and NGOs and silent crowds holding placards and women removing their headscarves in front of morality police. They demonstrated it with a philosophical and theoretical literature produced by former revolutionaries who were honest enough to reckon with what they had built and brave enough to say it was wrong.

What they lacked was an adversary willing to be moved by any of it.

The Islamic Republic was not, as Secor observed, a simple dictatorship dependent on one man’s personality. It was an institutional architecture — civilian and military — that perpetuated itself across personnel changes, that ran networks of violence through virtually every power centre, that retained ideological loyalty in enough of the security apparatus to kill, reliably, whenever killing was required.

That architecture is now, at minimum, severely damaged. Possibly broken. The man at the top of it is dead. His son — reportedly injured, reportedly hiding, reportedly greeted by students hanging toy rats from university trees — does not appear to be a figure capable of commanding the loyalty that institutional survival requires.

What replaces it is the question. And the answer will be determined not primarily by what Iranians want — though what Iranians want matters enormously — but by who controls the security forces, who controls the oil, who controls the foreign relationships, and what Washington and Tel Aviv decide they can live with.

That is not democracy. That is geopolitics wearing democracy’s clothes.

And the man the geopolitics has chosen — the man with the rottweiler advisers, the Israeli social media operation, the Saudi-funded satellite network, the supporters who call for carpet-bombing Tehran and fantasize about a military-escorted coronation — is not the man the democratic movement built. He is the man who arrived after the democratic movement was destroyed, wearing its borrowed language, backed by its executioners’ rivals.

XI. Someday in Tehran

Secor ends her essay with a refusal to foreclose. She cannot imagine a way the war ends in Iranian liberation. She cannot imagine the Islamic Republic deciding to yield. But she will not say these things definitively — not when every phone call ends with a promise: that we’ll continue the conversation, someday, in Tehran.

I find that ending both honest and courageous. It refuses the false comfort of either pessimism or optimism. It insists on the human scale of a political tragedy that is easy to discuss in the abstract and devastating to live in the particular.

What I take from twenty years of watching Iran — from the outside, from Toronto, from a position of safety that I am not inclined to dress up as insight — is this:

The democratic aspiration is real. It is not a Western projection. It was built by Iranians, theorized by Iranians, tested by Iranians at enormous personal cost, and it has survived every attempt to kill it. The women who took off their headscarves in 2022 were not doing so because Washington told them to. The students who hung toy rats from university trees were not following a foreign script.

But the man who claims to carry that aspiration forward — the prince in exile, the heir to the Lion and Sun — has built a movement that answers the question Secor’s friend asked. Can people who came to power through violent external intervention, who demand absolute loyalty, who deploy MAGA tactics against their own diaspora, who celebrate bombing campaigns while living comfortably in the Washington suburbs — can they really be said to be pro-democratic?

The evidence says no.

The conversation continues. Someday, perhaps, in Tehran.

XII. The Numbers

Day 72 of the U.S.-Israeli military campaign against Iran. The Strait of Hormuz remains blocked. Brent crude closed at approximately $101/barrel. A cease-fire was declared on April 7; Pahlavi’s supporters are lobbying for its reversal.

Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana remains the only Republican senator to have voted against the Authorization for Use of Military Force.

Narges Mohammadi remains imprisoned in Evin Prison, Tehran. Nobel Peace Prize laureate, 2023. She polled at 5 percent in a 2024 survey of Iranian political preferences. In a just world, that number would be higher. In a just world, she would not be in prison. In a just world, polling would not be the only language left.

The dead in January are estimated at between 6,000 and 36,000, depending on the source. Pahlavi has recently begun saying 50,000.

The conversation continues. The dead do not.
