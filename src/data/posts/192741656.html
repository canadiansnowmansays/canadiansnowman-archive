Berliners celebrate atop the Berlin Wall on the night of November 9, 1989. The wall that had divided the city — and symbolized the division of Europe — for twenty-eight years fell not through military force but through the accumulated weight of a system that had lost the consent of its subjects.

On November 9, 1989, the Berlin Wall fell.

It did not fall because the West won a military engagement. It did not fall because of a diplomatic breakthrough or an economic agreement. It fell because East German citizens — hundreds of thousands of them, then millions — simply stopped being afraid of their government, walked to the checkpoints, and demanded to cross. The guards, receiving no orders, stood aside. The crowd pushed through. The wall that had divided Europe for twenty-eight years, that had been the most visible symbol of the Cold War’s division of the world into two armed camps, ceased to exist not with a bang but with a cheer.

The American century had produced this moment. The patient containment strategy that George Kennan had designed in 1946, the alliance architecture that NATO embodied, the economic competition that the market economies had won so decisively — all of it had contributed to the implosion of the Soviet empire. The policy of containment, pursued through seven presidential administrations over forty years, had worked. The Cold War was over. America had won.

What came next was the most consequential decade in the history of American power — and, in retrospect, the decade in which the seeds of the current catastrophe were most thoroughly planted.

The Unipolar Moment

Charles Krauthammer, writing in Foreign Affairs in 1990, coined the phrase that would define the decade: the “unipolar moment.” For the first time since the Roman Empire, he argued, a single power dominated the international system so completely that no credible rival existed. The Soviet Union was dissolving. China was still decades away from great-power status. Europe was integrating but not militarizing. Japan’s economy was impressive but its strategic ambitions were minimal. The United States stood alone at the apex of the international system, its military unchallenged, its economy dominant, its values triumphant.

Krauthammer’s analysis was accurate. What he got wrong — what almost everyone in Washington got wrong in the 1990s — was what America should do with this unprecedented position.

The temptations were obvious and, in retrospect, nearly irresistible. If you are the world’s only superpower, the constraints that previously limited your freedom of action no longer apply. The Soviet nuclear deterrent had forced American strategic caution for forty years — every action had to be calculated against the risk of escalation to superpower confrontation. Now that constraint was gone. America could act where it wished, when it wished, with no peer competitor to say otherwise.

Francis Fukuyama in the early 1990s.

Francis Fukuyama’s “The End of History,” published in 1992, provided the intellectual framework for this mood. History — understood as the competition between ideological systems — was over. Liberal democracy had won. The remaining task was simply the extension of liberal democratic governance to the parts of the world that had not yet achieved it. The long struggle was finished; what remained was administration.

It was, as historical predictions go, spectacularly wrong. History had not ended. It had merely paused to reload.

But in the 1990s, the mood of American triumphalism was genuinely intoxicating. The budget surpluses of the Clinton years, the technology boom, the stock market’s apparently inexorable rise, NATO’s expansion eastward, the successful interventions in Bosnia and Kosovo — all of it fed a growing sense that American power was not merely dominant but benign, not merely strong but righteous, not merely effective but infallible.

This sense of infallibility was the unipolar moment’s most dangerous product. Power without constraint breeds overconfidence. Overconfidence breeds strategic errors of a kind that constrained power rarely makes. And strategic errors made from a position of overwhelming dominance tend to be larger, more expensive, and more consequential than strategic errors made from a position of competitive equality — because the dominant power can sustain failures that would destroy a weaker actor, and therefore receives no corrective feedback until the failures have accumulated to catastrophic scale.

What the 1990s Got Right

It would be unfair, and historically inaccurate, to characterize the unipolar decade as pure hubris. There were genuine achievements.

The reunification of Germany — managed through extraordinary diplomatic skill by George H.W. Bush and his Secretary of State James Baker — was accomplished without triggering the security panic in Moscow that many had feared. The Soviet Union’s dissolution was negotiated rather than violent. Fifteen new independent states emerged from the wreckage of the Soviet empire without the kind of catastrophic conflict that the dissolution of previous empires had produced.

NATO’s expansion — bringing Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic into the alliance in 1999, and eventually extending to the Baltic states and beyond — gave the newly democratic countries of Central and Eastern Europe the security guarantees they needed to consolidate their democratic transitions. This was genuinely good for the people of those countries, and it was accomplished through voluntary accession rather than coercion.

A photograph of the ceremony admitting Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic to NATO on March 12, 1999 — the first post-Cold War expansion.

The Gulf War of 1991 was, as Part III noted, a model application of the Powell Doctrine — a genuine multilateral coalition, UN authorization, clear and limited objectives, and a clean exit when those objectives were achieved. Bush’s decision not to march to Baghdad — widely criticized at the time as a failure of nerve — was, in retrospect, a display of exactly the strategic discipline that the post-war order required. The objectives were limited because the authorization was limited. The coalition held because the objectives were proportionate to what the coalition could support.

And the interventions in Bosnia and Kosovo — however late and however imperfect — demonstrated that the United States and its NATO allies were capable of using military force to stop genocide and ethnic cleansing, even when no direct American interest was at stake. The humanitarian intervention doctrine that emerged from these experiences — the “responsibility to protect” — was a genuine extension of the post-war order’s logic: the idea that state sovereignty was not absolute, that governments had obligations to their citizens, and that the international community had the right to intervene when those obligations were catastrophically violated.

These were real achievements. They should not be erased from the ledger.

What the 1990s Got Wrong

But the unipolar decade also planted the seeds of the current catastrophe in ways that became visible only in retrospect.

The first error was the failure to build a post-Cold War security architecture that included Russia.

George Kennan — the architect of containment, perhaps the most clear-eyed strategic thinker American diplomacy produced in the twentieth century — warned in 1997 that NATO expansion was a historic mistake. Writing in the New York Times, he argued that expanding NATO eastward would inflame Russian nationalism, revive Cold War habits of mind, and make it harder to build the stable European security architecture that the end of the Cold War had made possible. “I think it is the beginning of a new cold war,” he wrote. “I think the Russians will gradually react quite adversely and it will affect their policies.”

He was right. It took twenty-five years for his prediction to be fully vindicated — longer than he expected — but Vladimir Putin’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine was, in significant part, the product of the security grievances that NATO expansion had generated over three decades.

This is not to say that NATO expansion was wrong in its humanitarian and democratic dimensions — the countries that joined did so voluntarily, and their security was genuinely improved by membership. It is to say that the expansion was managed without adequate attention to Russian security perceptions, and that the failure to build a European security architecture that addressed those perceptions was one of the great strategic oversights of the unipolar decade.

The second error was the failure to manage China’s rise with sufficient strategic foresight.

Chinese trade officials at the ceremony marking China’s admission to the World Trade Organization, December 2001. China’s WTO membership was sold as a mechanism for moderating Chinese political development .

China’s admission to the World Trade Organization in 2001 was sold to the American public as a mechanism for moderating Chinese political development — the theory that economic integration would produce political liberalization. It did not. What it produced instead was the fastest industrial development in human history, transforming China from a middle-income developing country into the world’s largest manufacturer and second-largest economy in roughly twenty years.

The United States benefited from this transformation in the short term — cheap Chinese goods held down American inflation, and Chinese purchases of US Treasury bonds helped finance American deficits. But the medium-term consequences — the hollowing out of American manufacturing, the transfer of supply chain dominance to China, the creation of the economic base from which Chinese military power would eventually be funded — were foreseeable, and were foreseen, and were ignored because the short-term benefits were too politically convenient to resist.

The third error, and the most consequential, was the intellectual corruption that triumphalism produced in American strategic thinking.

In the 1990s, a school of thought emerged in Washington that would eventually call itself neoconservatism — the belief that American power was not merely dominant but uniquely capable of remaking the world in the American image, that the export of democracy by force was both possible and desirable, that the constraints of multilateral institutions were obstacles to American effectiveness rather than sources of American legitimacy.

The neoconservatives drew their intellectual genealogy from a critique of Cold War containment — the argument that Kennan’s patient, defensive strategy had accepted too much Soviet expansion, and that a more assertive American policy could have rolled back communism rather than merely containing it. In the 1990s, emboldened by the Soviet collapse and the Gulf War’s success, they argued that the unipolar moment was the opportunity to complete what containment had begun — to transform the world through the active promotion of American values, backed by American military power.

They were appointed to positions of authority in the administration of George W. Bush.

September 11 and the Unipolar Moment’s End

At 8:46 on the morning of September 11, 2001, American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower of the World Trade Center in New York City. Seventeen minutes later, United Airlines Flight 175 struck the South Tower. At 9:37, American Airlines Flight 77 struck the Pentagon. At 10:03, United Airlines Flight 93 — whose passengers had learned of the other attacks by cell phone and attempted to retake the aircraft — crashed into a field near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. The intended target was almost certainly the United States Capitol.

2,977 people died. It was the deadliest foreign attack on American soil in history.

The attacks demonstrated something that the unipolar decade had obscured: that overwhelming military dominance does not protect against all threats. Nineteen men with box cutters and a willingness to die had killed nearly three thousand people and inflicted economic damage that would eventually total trillions of dollars. The architecture of American power — aircraft carriers, nuclear weapons, the most sophisticated military force in history — had been irrelevant.

It was the unipolar moment’s verdict on itself. The decade of American dominance had not produced a safer world. It had produced a world in which the grievances accumulated by American policy — in the Middle East, in the treatment of Muslim populations, in the support for authoritarian regimes in service of the petrodollar system — had incubated a movement willing to attack the American homeland directly.

The specific grievances that motivated the 9/11 attackers were complex and specific. But the structural conditions that made their attack possible — the radicalization of a generation of young Muslim men by the combination of American military presence in Saudi Arabia, American support for corrupt and repressive Arab governments, and the perception of American indifference to Palestinian suffering — were the product of choices made in the unipolar decade and before it.

The order’s founding premise — that American power required broad legitimacy to be sustainable, and that legitimacy required the benefits of American power to be broadly shared — had been tested by the unipolar decade and found inconvenient. The bill arrived on September 11, 2001. Keenleyside had been dead for nine years by then, but the institutions he helped build were still standing — just barely, and now under the most severe pressure they had yet faced.

What came next — the response to the 9/11 attacks, and the decisions made in its shadow — would prove to be the most consequential strategic failure in American history since Vietnam.

George Kennan, the architect of the Cold War containment strategy, photographed in his later years. Kennan warned in 1997 that NATO expansion would prove a historic mistake. He had also warned, throughout the 1990s, against the triumphalist reading of the Soviet collapse — arguing that the West had not so much won the Cold War as survived it, and that survival was no license for arrogance. He lived to be 101, dying in 2005, watching as his predictions were validated one by one. Hugh Keenleyside, a generation older and dead thirteen years by then, had shared Kennan’s core intuition: that the greatest danger to American leadership was not external adversaries but internal overconfidence — the belief that dominant power needed no constraint, no legitimacy, no consent from those on whom it was exercised. Both men were products of the same mid-century tradition of institutional realism. Neither was listened to at the moment that mattered most.
