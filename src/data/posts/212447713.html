For six months the Strait of Hormuz has been the whole argument. Iran closed it, the United States bombed to reopen it, and every negotiation since has been an attempt to settle who controls a twenty-one-mile channel.

Meanwhile, quietly and at enormous expense, the region built its way around the problem. Saudi Arabia pumped its crude to the Red Sea and loaded it at Yanbu. Iraq pushed more oil through the pipeline to Turkey and trucked the rest out through Syria. The Emirates shipped from Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman. Japan and South Korea began negotiating tank farms to hold Gulf crude thousands of miles from the fighting. Billions of dollars, spent on the assumption that the way to survive this war was to route around it.

On Saturday, Iran’s new security chief announced that he intends to close the detour.

I. Not a Single Drop

As Erika Solomon reported for the New York Times on August 23, Mohsen Rezaei gave his first extensive interview since taking over the Supreme National Security Council two weeks ago, and used it to threaten Iran’s neighbours.

Any country that becomes a partner in creating economic restrictions against Iran, he said, will be regarded by Tehran as an enemy. Iran would seek to prevent even a single drop of oil from leaving the region. It would try negotiation first with any state perceived to be joining the American pressure campaign — and failing that, would move to block oil not merely through the strait but from the Persian Gulf altogether.

Then the sentence that matters most. Countries cannot join America in imposing an economic blockade while Iran continues to allow oil to leave the Gulf. Iran is not currently interfering with the alternative routes, he said. But it will target those routes as well.

Read that against the map. The alternative routes are the East-West pipeline to Yanbu, the Iraqi line into Turkey, the tankers loading at Fujairah, the trucks crossing into Syria. They are the infrastructure the Gulf states built precisely so that Iran’s control of one waterway would stop mattering. An analyst quoted in this series a fortnight ago observed that the pipelines are sitting ducks. Iran’s new security chief has now said he intends to shoot them.

He also, at one point, asked aloud whether Iran should pursue building a nuclear bomb — a question Tehran has spent decades insisting it had already answered in the negative, now reopened by the man who runs its national security council.

Mohammad Ghaderi, a Tehran-based foreign affairs analyst, describes the new leadership as changing the deterrence equation from purely defensive measures to an offensive approach across all spheres, the economy included, in order to impose greater costs on Washington and its allies.

There is a corrective worth holding onto. Esfandyar Batmanghelidj of the Bourse & Bazaar Foundation points out that Iran’s isolation has made it more dependent on neighbours like the Emirates and Iraq than they are on Iran, and that Rezaei’s threat is meant to suggest their fates are bound together. Which is what a government says when the dependency runs the wrong way.

II. Two Iranians, One Weekend

On the same weekend that Rezaei promised to stop every drop of oil leaving the Gulf, President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran was engaged in a full-scale economic, military and security war, that his government was trying to counter the pressure, and that they had stood firm and were trying to be servants of the people in the face of an unequal war.

Pezeshkian continues to argue for a return to negotiations, on the reasoning that Iran should deal while its hand is strong. Rezaei, in the same news cycle, urged Iranians to launch an economic jihad — to reduce their reliance on subsidies and imports and build self-sufficiency through local production. Every home, he said, can become a launcher in the economic war. Every neighbourhood can become a missile base.

Anyone in Washington hoping to negotiate an end to this war should study those two statements carefully, because they are the answer to the question of whom one would negotiate with. The president wants a deal. The security council wants a siege. And the security council is the body that was reorganised this month with hardliners installed at every level, while the president is the man Tehran’s hardliners have spent the summer attacking for pursuing diplomacy at all.

III. What Economic Jihad Costs

Rezaei is asking for austerity from a population that has already run out of room.

Mahdi Ghodsi, an economist at the Vienna Institute for International Economic Studies, puts Iranian inflation at ninety percent — a level, he says, with no precedent in the country’s modern history. The rial has lost a further forty percent since the pre-war collapse that produced the nationwide protests security forces put down by killing thousands. War damage and the American blockade together have cut Iran’s energy production by fifteen to thirty percent. Food prices are soaring, unemployment is climbing, and the government is struggling to keep petrol subsidised while being fully aware of what happens when it stops.

Iran’s central bank governor, Abdolnaser Hemmati, said last week that oil exports have fallen close to zero.

That last figure sits awkwardly beside evidence in this series only days old. Brett Erickson of Obsidian Risk Advisors — quoted by Steven Erlanger for the Times on August 19 — said a week ago that Iran was running ahead of its budgeted oil revenue for the year, because the war has driven prices so high that reduced volumes still clear the target. Both statements can be true at different moments; a central bank governor may also be making a political point about sanctions rather than an accounting one. But readers of this diary should know that Tehran's own officials are describing the economy in terms its own numbers do not entirely support, and that the same has been true of Washington's.

Ghodsi’s warning is the one to watch: the Emirates has already halted trade, and if Turkey follows, the consequences would be dire. His summary is that this is an extension and expansion of the war until Iran reaches an agreement, and that it will be worse for Iran than for the regional or global economy.

Which is probably right, and is not the same as saying it will work.

IV. The President’s Own Precedents

The American side of this has now been stated with unusual clarity. In a Times video report from Tehran on the toll the sanctions are already taking, the president is heard laying out the case: the blockade is in place, the toughest sanctions in history are coming, and this will work. It worked in Venezuela once the blockade went up. It is working in Cuba right now. It is going to work in Iran. And, in his words, we are going to collapse this regime.

Three things are worth pulling out of that.

The first is that regime collapse is back. For most of the summer the war’s stated purpose narrowed steadily — from overthrowing the government and ending the nuclear programme in February, to reopening a waterway by August, with the nuclear question demoted to third position in a negotiating queue. The president has now restored the original objective and assigned it to a sanctions campaign. This is the same government that signed a document in June pledging not to interfere in Iran’s internal affairs.

The second is Venezuela. The president cites it as the case where the blockade worked. What actually concluded the Venezuelan business, as this series recorded in January, was Delta Force removing Nicolás Maduro from his bed. If Venezuela is the model, the model requires commandos — and his own Treasury Secretary said on the record last week that going all in on economic warfare likely means there will not be a large-scale military restart.

The third is Cuba, and here I have to declare an interest, having spent a good part of the past year writing about it. The president says the embargo is working in Cuba right now. It has been in place since 1962. Sixty-four years, eleven American presidents, and a government in Havana that has changed hands twice, both times within the same family and neither time because of Washington. If Cuba is the evidence that economic pressure collapses governments, then the timeline being proposed for Iran is measured in generations, and the Iranian who will see the result has not been born.

There is a reason the comparison came so readily to him. Sanctions regimes are the only foreign policy instrument that never has to admit failure. A war ends and can be judged. An embargo simply continues, and every year it fails to work is reframed as a year in which pressure was building.

V. The Ship That Went Home

One American ending did arrive this week, and it deserves recording.

As Tracy Mumford reported on the Times podcast The Headlines on August 21, the aircraft carrier Abraham Lincoln has left the Middle East, bound for San Diego — a journey of thirteen thousand miles that may take a month or more. It had been at sea since November, months longer than anyone planned, without the port calls that let a crew rest. Reports of deteriorating conditions aboard had been accumulating for weeks. Earlier this month, hundreds of family members flooded a town hall with Navy officials, demanding to know what was happening to their sailors.

The families got their answer before Congress did.

Set that beside the other American military story of the month — Major Jason Watson, an airman arrested after calling for the president’s removal over this war, who has said he is prepared to accept the punishment for his dissent. Two forms of refusal, one from a room full of relatives and one from a single officer, in a war that has produced almost no public dissent from the institution fighting it.

VI. By the Numbers

  • 90% Iranian inflation, per the Vienna Institute — no precedent in the country’s modern history.

  • 40% further decline in the rial since the pre-war collapse that triggered the protests.

  • 15–30% reduction in Iranian energy production from war damage and blockade combined.

  • “Close to zero” — Iran’s central bank governor on the country’s oil exports.

  • 64 years of the Cuban embargo, cited by the president as evidence that embargoes work.

  • 13,000 miles from the Gulf of Oman to San Diego.

  • $4.10 a gallon for petrol in the United States, up 38 percent; diesel $5.55, up 48 percent.

  • 18 American service members killed; 653 wounded, still split across two Pentagon webpages.

  • Narges Mohammadi remains in Evin Prison, in a country where every neighbourhood is now to be understood as a missile base.

VII.

Scott Bessent is expected to describe the economic D-Day today. Whatever he announces, two things about it are already known.

The first is that Iran has pre-announced its response, and the response is not capitulation but the targeting of everything the region built to survive the last six months. The Gulf states spent billions constructing an exit from this war. Tehran has now told them the exit is a target.

The second is that the president has named his own precedents, and both of them argue against him. One required soldiers he has ruled out sending. The other has been running since Kennedy was alive.

Rezaei says every home can become a launcher and every neighbourhood a missile base. He is speaking to a population facing ninety percent inflation, and what he is really telling them is that the state has no further resources to offer and would like their kitchens instead. That is not a strategy of strength. It is what a government says when it has decided its people can absorb more than its enemies can, and intends to find out.

Washington has decided the same thing about Iranians. On that one point, and perhaps only that one, the two governments are in complete agreement.
