This series has argued that India holds together by containing its contradictions rather than resolving them. The civilization is the oldest continuous experiment in managed diversity on earth. The democracy is the largest and most improbable. The constitution is the most radical. And the contradictions — between Hindu and Muslim, between upper caste and Dalit, between north and south, between the billionaire and the subsistence farmer — have been held in suspension for seventy-nine years by a combination of institutional design, political pragmatism, and the sheer stubborn refusal of 1.4 billion people to be anything other than Indian.

But contradictions, contained long enough, can become fractures. And fractures, if they widen, can break things.

This essay examines what could break India — not because I believe it will break, but because the forces that threaten it are real, they are accelerating, and the mechanisms that have historically contained them are under greater strain than at any point since Partition.

I. The Hindu-Muslim Fracture

The most dangerous fracture in Indian life is the one the Babri Masjid demolition opened and the BJP’s governing project has widened: the relationship between India’s Hindu majority and its 200 million Muslim citizens.

The numbers alone should compel attention. India’s Muslim population is larger than the entire population of every Muslim-majority country on earth except Indonesia and Pakistan. Two hundred million people is not a minority in any meaningful sense of the word — it is a nation within a nation, and its security, dignity, and political representation are a test of the constitutional order’s credibility.

Under the BJP, that test is failing. The Citizenship Amendment Act of 2019 explicitly excluded Muslims from a fast-tracked citizenship pathway offered to refugees from India’s neighbours. The proposed National Register of Citizens — which would require every Indian to prove their citizenship through documentary evidence — threatens to render millions of Muslims (and poor people of all faiths who lack documentation) effectively stateless. Mob lynchings of Muslims accused of cattle slaughter continue. Bulldozer justice — the extrajudicial demolition of Muslim homes and businesses as collective punishment — has become a governing tactic in BJP-ruled states. The mainstream media environment, dominated by pro-government channels, routinely demonizes Muslims in language that would be recognizable to anyone who has studied the propaganda dynamics of ethnic conflict elsewhere.

I write about this carefully because I am an outsider. I do not experience anti-Muslim discrimination in India. I do not live with its consequences. But I have written extensively in other series about how democracies degrade — about the process by which a majority convinces itself that its dominance is natural, that the minority’s complaints are exaggerated, and that the erosion of the minority’s rights is not discrimination but the restoration of an authentic national identity. The pattern is consistent across continents and centuries. What is happening to Muslims in India rhymes with what has happened to minorities in other democracies before the rhyme turned to catastrophe.

Whether India will follow that pattern to its conclusion — whether the Hindu-Muslim fracture will widen into something irreparable — is a question no one can answer with certainty. What can be said with certainty is that the constitutional mechanisms designed to prevent it — judicial independence, press freedom, federalism, the secular framework itself — are weaker today than at any point since the Emergency.

II. Kashmir

Kashmir is not a fracture. It is a wound that has been open since 1947 and that no Indian government has been willing to heal because healing it would require concessions that no Indian government believes it can survive.

The revocation of Article 370 in August 2019 — which stripped Jammu and Kashmir of its special constitutional status, dissolved the state into two union territories under direct central rule, and was accompanied by a communications blackout, a security lockdown, and the detention of the entire Kashmiri political leadership — was presented by the BJP as the completion of India’s integration. To many Kashmiris, it was an annexation.

The numbers tell one story: insurgent violence in Kashmir has declined since 2019. The human rights picture tells another: arbitrary detentions, the use of the Public Safety Act to hold Kashmiris without charge, the suppression of press freedom, and the demographic changes that critics argue are designed to alter Kashmir’s Muslim-majority character. The situation is stable in the way that military occupation is always stable — it suppresses the symptoms without treating the disease.

Kashmir matters beyond its own borders because it is the issue that connects India’s domestic fractures to its international vulnerabilities. It is the reason India and Pakistan remain locked in nuclear confrontation. It is the issue China exploits to pressure India. It is the issue the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation raises at every session. And it is the issue that most directly contradicts India’s claim to be a democracy — because a democracy that governs eight million people under effective military rule, without meaningful political representation, and with systematically restricted civil liberties, is a democracy with an asterisk.

III. Caste Persistence

Seventy-six years after Ambedkar’s constitution abolished untouchability, caste remains the most durable social structure in Indian life.

The reservation system has created a significant Dalit and OBC middle class. Dalit political parties — particularly the Bahujan Samaj Party in Uttar Pradesh — have won elections and governed states. Individual Dalits have risen to the presidency, to corporate leadership, to academic distinction. The formal legal architecture of caste discrimination has been dismantled.

The informal architecture has not. Honour killings of inter-caste couples — particularly when a Dalit man marries an upper-caste woman — continue. Manual scavenging — the cleaning of dry latrines and sewers by hand, performed almost exclusively by Dalits — persists despite being illegal. Caste-based violence in rural areas remains common and commonly unpunished. The digital economy has not dissolved caste — it has, in some cases, reproduced it, with caste networks operating on matrimonial websites, professional networks, and social media platforms.

The BJP’s relationship with caste is complex. The party has successfully expanded its base among OBC and even some Dalit communities by combining Hindu identity politics with development promises. Modi himself is from an OBC caste — a fact his political apparatus deploys strategically. But the RSS’s ideological framework is Brahminical in origin, and the Hindutva project’s emphasis on Hindu unity tends to suppress caste-based political mobilization, which the party sees as a threat to its consolidation of a unified Hindu vote bank.

Ambedkar’s warning resonates across the decades: political democracy, he argued, without social democracy — without equality of condition, not merely equality of legal status — would eventually collapse. India has built the political democracy. The social democracy remains unfinished.

IV. Water

The fracture that receives the least political attention and may ultimately prove the most consequential is water.

India is facing a water crisis of existential proportions. Groundwater — which supplies roughly 40 percent of India’s water needs — is being extracted at unsustainable rates. The Central Ground Water Board has classified nearly 17 percent of India’s groundwater assessment units as “over-exploited.” The Himalayan glaciers that feed the Ganges, the Indus, and the Brahmaputra — rivers on which hundreds of millions of people depend — are retreating. The monsoon, which still determines the fate of Indian agriculture, is becoming more erratic under climate change — more intense in shorter bursts, with longer dry periods between.

Chennai, India’s sixth-largest city, effectively ran out of water in 2019. Bangalore faces chronic water shortages. The Niti Aayog — the Indian government’s policy think tank — warned in 2018 that 21 Indian cities would run out of groundwater by 2020, a prediction that was exaggerated in its timeline but not in its direction.

Water scarcity intersects with every other fracture. It exacerbates rural poverty. It drives migration to already-stressed cities. It sharpens interstate competition — disputes over river water between Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, between Punjab and Haryana, between upstream and downstream states — that the federal system struggles to mediate. And it sits beneath the agricultural crisis that drives farmer suicides, farmer protests, and the rural distress that is the chronic illness of Indian economic life.

Climate change will make all of this worse. India is among the countries most vulnerable to rising temperatures, sea-level rise, and extreme weather events. The economic costs — in lost agricultural productivity, in infrastructure damage, in public health impacts — will fall disproportionately on the poor, the rural, and the already marginalized. India’s climate policy — ambitious in rhetoric, constrained by the reality that 800 million people still depend on subsidized food — is caught between development imperatives and environmental necessity.

V. Inequality

India has billionaires and it has 800 million people on food subsidies. Both facts are true simultaneously, and together they constitute the most explosive fracture in Indian economic life.

The Oxfam India Inequality Report has documented, year after year, that the richest 1 percent of Indians own approximately 40 percent of the country’s wealth, while the bottom 50 percent own less than 3 percent. The ratio has worsened since liberalization. The growth that India has celebrated — 6 to 7 percent GDP growth, the march toward a $5 trillion economy — has been unevenly distributed in ways that are not merely unjust but structurally destabilizing.

Youth unemployment is the most politically dangerous expression of this inequality. India adds roughly 12 million people to its working-age population every year. The economy does not generate 12 million jobs. The result is a generation of educated, aspirational, digitally connected young people who cannot find employment commensurate with their expectations. This demographic — young, frustrated, available — is historically the most volatile constituency in any political system. It can be mobilized by hope or by anger. Under Modi, it has been mobilized by both — hope for development and anger at a nebulously defined “other” (Muslims, Pakistanis, secularists, the “Khan Market gang”) that the BJP’s communication machine presents as obstacles to India’s rightful destiny.

The combination of extreme wealth inequality and mass youth unemployment, in a country with nuclear weapons and 200 million Muslims, is not an academic concern. It is a threat matrix.

VI. The Northeast

India’s northeastern states — Assam, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram, Meghalaya, Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh, and Sikkim — are connected to the Indian mainland by a narrow corridor called the Siliguri Corridor, sometimes known as the “chicken’s neck,” only 22 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. The region is home to hundreds of ethnic groups, dozens of insurgencies, and a relationship with the Indian state that is defined by distance, neglect, and periodic violence.

The people of the Northeast are ethnically, linguistically, and culturally distinct from the rest of India. Many communities are Tibeto-Burman or Mon-Khmer in origin, physically indistinguishable from Southeast Asians, and culturally closer to Myanmar than to the Hindi heartland. Northeastern Indians face routine discrimination in mainland India — they are called racial slurs, treated as foreigners in their own country, and subjected to a level of casual racism that most Indians do not acknowledge.

Manipur erupted in ethnic violence in 2023 between the Meitei majority and the Kuki-Zo tribal minority. The violence — which included sexual assaults, arson, and displacement of thousands — continued for months while the central government’s response was slow and widely criticized. Modi did not visit Manipur or speak publicly about the violence for over two months.

The Northeast is India’s forgotten fracture. It does not feature in Bollywood films. It does not produce prime ministers. Its people are not represented in the national imagination. But it contains live insurgencies, active ethnic conflicts, unresolved territorial disputes, and a population that has never been fully convinced that Indian democracy includes them.

VII. The Stress Test

Every fracture examined in this essay — Hindu-Muslim, Kashmir, caste, water, inequality, the Northeast — is individually manageable. Indian democracy has managed each of them, with varying degrees of success, for decades. The danger is not any single fracture. The danger is convergence — the possibility that multiple fractures widen simultaneously, that the mechanisms of containment fail at the same moment, and that the shock exceeds the system’s capacity to absorb.

The Emergency was a single fracture — a political crisis created by a single leader. India repaired it in twenty-one months. Partition was a catastrophic fracture that killed millions but was ultimately contained within a constitutional framework. What has not been tested is whether Indian democracy can survive the simultaneous intensification of communal polarization, economic inequality, environmental crisis, and institutional degradation — all presided over by a government whose ideology is, at its core, a rejection of the pluralist framework that has held the contradictions together.

This is not a prediction. It is a warning. And warnings, as history demonstrates, are only useful if someone listens.
