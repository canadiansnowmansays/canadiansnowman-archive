I. The Helicopter

On Monday evening, a U.S. Army Apache helicopter gunship went down near the Strait of Hormuz. The two crew members — a pilot and a gunner — were rescued within two hours by a Navy Corsair sea drone, in what the military described as the first rescue of its kind by an autonomous surface vessel. Both were in stable condition. The Trump administration did not publicly acknowledge the incident until the New York Times reported it Monday night.

On Tuesday morning, Trump declared on Truth Social that Iran had shot down the Apache and that the United States “must, of necessity, respond to this attack.”

He did not say how Iran had brought it down. A U.S. official later said a one-way Iranian Shahed drone had hit the aircraft. Iran denied conducting any aerial military operations near the strait in the relevant period. U.S. military investigators were, at the time of Trump’s announcement, still trying to determine whether the Iranian drone hit the Apache on purpose or in what one official described as “a reckless accident” in congested airspace off the Omani coast. Military specialists publicly debated whether a Shahed drone was even capable of hitting a moving target.

The attribution was unresolved. The retaliation was not.

At 5 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday, U.S. Central Command launched strikes against Iranian military targets in southern Iran — “a proportional response to unjustified Iranian aggression.” Iran responded with drones and missiles at U.S. bases in Bahrain and Jordan. All intercepted. No American casualties.

On Wednesday morning, Trump posted on social media: “They’ve taken too long to negotiate a deal that would have been great for them, now they will have to pay the price!!!” Then he told reporters in the Oval Office: “We’re going to hit them hard again today.” At 5:15 p.m. Eastern, Central Command launched a second wave of strikes — heavier, hitting a wider array of targets across southern Iran, 49 Tomahawk missiles plus fighter jets. Trump told Fox News the bombing would pause but resume Thursday night if Iran did not “capitulate in negotiations.”

Capitulate. That is the stated condition.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, speaking to reporters at MacDill Air Force Base in Tampa on Wednesday: “If we need to negotiate with bombs, we’ll negotiate with bombs. And we’re very good at it. Nobody better in the world.”

When asked whether the strikes could target civilian infrastructure — bridges, electrical grid — Hegseth did not answer. He said the question was “impugning the motives” of the U.S. military. “We will hit them hard, on our terms.”

A third wave of strikes concluded at 4:30 a.m. Thursday local time.

Iran’s foreign ministry issued a statement Thursday morning: the latest round of U.S. strikes had “effectively rendered the cease-fire of April 8, 2026, meaningless.”

Then Thursday morning Trump posted that the United States would be hitting Iran “VERY HARD TONIGHT,” and mused on Fox & Friends that he might take Kharg Island — the heart of Iran’s oil economy — and assume “total control of their Oil and Gas Markets, much like we have with Venezuela, which is working out brilliantly.” Then, in the same phone call: “My preference has always been to take Kharg Island. I don’t know that America has the stomach for that. I think they’d like to see us come home.”

At 1:36 p.m., he cancelled the strikes. The Pakistanis had told him “we have a deal.” He posted that discussions had “been brought to the highest level of Iranian leadership” and approved. That afternoon in the Oval Office: the deal was “in pretty final shape.” “A very strong memorandum of understanding.” “That’s a little conceptual.” Nuclear issues still being worked out “conceptually.” Signing could happen “maybe over the weekend, in Europe.” He would not attend — he had a UFC fight on the White House lawn for his 80th birthday on Sunday. Vance, Witkoff, and Kushner would go.

Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman, within the hour: “Nothing has been finalized.” Iran had not reached “a final conclusion about the agreement.” Iran would not compromise on its red lines.

That evening, at a telerally for the Georgia governor’s race: “We ended the war with Iran today.”

Friday morning, Iranian state media published the outline of a fourteen-point draft memorandum of understanding. Bloomberg reported a signing in Switzerland as soon as Sunday, ahead of the G7 summit in Evian. Global stocks surged. Oil fell. The market priced it as real.

Day 103. The war that was “not a big thing” may end, possibly, over the weekend, possibly in Switzerland.

II. What the Title Means

Mr. President, it is okay not to retaliate.

This is not a pacifist argument. It is not a legal argument, though the legal questions around the Apache attribution, the water facilities, and the tanker strikes are serious and unresolved. It is a strategic argument, stated as plainly as possible by someone watching from outside the blast radius.

The Apache attribution was uncertain. The military said so. Iran denied it. Specialists doubted the weapon’s capability. The two crew members were safe. A proportional, measured non-response — a statement of concern, a demand for explanation, a warning — was available. It was not chosen.

The choice made instead: three waves of strikes in seventy-two hours, a declared condition of Iranian capitulation, a threat to seize Kharg Island, and a cease-fire that one party has now declared meaningless.

The TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — has run throughout this war on diplomatic positions. This week it ran on the war itself. Trump threatened on Tuesday. Iran signalled de-escalation on Monday — then was struck anyway. The obfuscation is the “self-defense” framing Central Command applies to strikes that Hegseth explicitly said were not in response to any particular military action but were meant to pressure Iran to accept Trump’s terms. The capitulation demand is stated openly. The word is Trump’s own.

Pete Hegseth said “negotiate with bombs.” Aaron David Miller of the Carnegie Endowment said on Monday, as the fifteen-hour war ended: “Trump launched a war of choice overestimating America’s military capacity and underestimating Iran’s. That is a box that Trump cannot get out of right now.” The box has not changed. The bombs have multiplied.

Iran’s lead negotiator Ghalibaf, Thursday: “Wrong strategies and impulsive decisions will reset the entire board for the worse, explode energy infrastructure and markets, and create an endless quagmire that you will be stuck in for years.”

By Thursday afternoon, Trump had not retaliated. The strikes were cancelled. A deal was, possibly, close. The title was always the argument. Thursday afternoon was the first evidence it was also advice he was capable of taking.

III. The Water

The New York Times Visual Investigations team published an analysis Thursday based on satellite imagery and verified photographs. A precision U.S. strike early Wednesday destroyed drinking water facilities in Bemani, Sirik County, Hormozgan Province — a village on Iran’s southern coast near the Strait of Hormuz.

Satellite imagery from June 9 shows two small water structures on a hill outside the village — light blue pipes, hilltop location, consistent with water distribution infrastructure. After the strikes: the smaller building’s roof collapsed. The larger building has a small hole punched through the centre of its roof. Weapon fragments photographed at the site were identified as remnants of a GBU-39 precision-guided glide bomb by researchers with the Open Source Munitions Portal. The GBU-39 is a 250-pound class precision weapon. The clean hole and limited blast damage are consistent with a deliberate precision strike on the building’s centre. Both structures stood outside the village with no other infrastructure in the immediate vicinity.

The Times noted: “It is unclear if the U.S. intentionally struck the water facilities, or knew what was in the buildings. Deliberately targeting civilian infrastructure could constitute a war crime under international law.” A Central Command spokesman said the military was aware of the reports and was investigating.

The two tanks had a combined capacity of 2.5 million litres. Their destruction cut water to 20,000 people in Kuhestak and ten surrounding villages for at least twelve hours. Mobile tankers were dispatched. A bypass line was built.

The temperature in Kuhestak on Wednesday: 96 degrees Fahrenheit. Hormozgan Province ranks among the hottest places on earth in summer, occasionally reaching 122 degrees.

A damaged building in Bemani, southern Iran, seen on a photo published by Iranian media, where a surviving wall still urges residents not to waste water: “Water is the pulse of life.” A Times analysis points to U.S. precision strikes that destroyed this structure and a second nearby.Credit...Rokna

Iran’s President Pezeshkian: “Critical infrastructure is the lifeline of people’s lives. Threats to transportation networks, power systems and water facilities are not a show of strength, but a sign of desperation.”

Manoochehr Shirzaei, an Iranian environmental expert and geophysicist at Virginia Tech: “In a region already facing extreme heat, chronic water scarcity, and a rapidly warming climate, the loss of drinking-water infrastructure is more than physical damage. It threatens the health, resilience, and daily survival of entire communities.”

The United States struck a water tank in 96-degree heat and did not comment on it. That sentence is the week in a frame.

IV. The Sailors

The Marivex was a Palau-flagged oil tanker. On Monday, a U.S. fighter jet from the USS Abraham Lincoln fired into its engine room after the crew failed to comply with directions to turn back from an Iranian port. One lifeboat was destroyed by the strike. The second was inaccessible. Twenty-four Indian crew members sent frantic SOS messages for more than two hours.

“We have fire onboard, we have fire onboard. And vessel is sinking. Please help, please help… We are all crew Indian. 24 crew.”

All were eventually rescued by Omani military helicopter. Three crew members of a second tanker, the Settebello — also carrying Indian sailors, struck Wednesday — were confirmed dead Thursday morning by India’s shipping minister. The Indian government summoned a top U.S. diplomat in New Delhi to protest. A third commercial vessel with Indian crew — the Jalveer — was struck Thursday. Twenty seafarers rescued.

Three tankers. Three vessels with Indian crews. Three dead. Four days.

India’s foreign ministry: “The continuing incidents of attacks on shipping in the region are deeply worrisome and a direct result of the ongoing conflict in the region.”

Central Command has disabled eight non-compliant vessels since April 13, turned away 134 ships, and allowed 42 humanitarian vessels to pass. The blockade is the mechanism. The Indian sailors are the cost. The State Department said it was “in direct contact with the government of India regarding this matter.” The U.S. ambassador to India was out of the country.

V. The Fifteen-Hour War and What It Settled

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu of Israel in Jerusalem in March.Credit...Pool photo by Ronen Zvulun

The week began with a brief convulsion that the New York Times‘s David Halbfinger diagnosed with unusual precision. Israel struck Beirut’s southern suburbs Sunday. Iran retaliated with missiles on northern Israel — the first direct Iranian strike on Israel since the April cease-fire. Israel hit Iran’s largest petrochemical complex at Mahshahr. Iran hit back. Trump called Netanyahu Monday morning, told him a breakthrough in nuclear talks was days away, and persuaded him to stand down. Netanyahu, silent for twenty hours, emerged to say “fire is on hold.” Iran’s Revolutionary Guards signalled cessation. Schools reopened Tuesday.

Fifteen hours of direct Iran-Israel exchanges, then a phone call, then silence.

Halbfinger’s verdict: Israel now understands that Hezbollah can attack northern Israeli communities, Israeli counter-strikes on Hezbollah may prompt Iranian missiles on Israel, and Trump may prevent Israel from hitting back too hard or too long. Iran has established the equation it wanted. “This was a major change,” said Raz Zimmt of the Institute for National Security Studies in Tel Aviv. Iran’s hard-line government has concluded that “President Trump doesn’t want to go back to war, so they can take some risks.”

Netanyahu’s domestic bind opened immediately. Gadi Eisenkot — former IDF chief of staff, currently leading Netanyahu in polls as Israelis’ preferred prime minister — released a campaign ad using Trump’s voice: “He’ll do whatever I want him to do.” Tagline: “The U.S. has a leader. Israel has no leader. That’s all the difference.” Naftali Bennett: “the past 24 hours will be remembered as the beginning of normalizing a reality of drip-by-drip attacks from Iran.”

The fifteen-hour war settled nothing strategically. It clarified the bind Israel is in, produced the most useful campaign material Netanyahu’s opponents have had in months, and set the stage for the Apache retaliation sequence that followed three days later.

VI. The Deal That Isn’t — And Why It Might Be

A rally in Tehran on Monday after Iran and Israel exchanged fire.Credit...Arash Khamooshi/Polaris for The New York Times

The New York Times published a reconstruction this week — by Erika Solomon, Farnaz Fassihi, and Tyler Pager — that explains, more clearly than anything previously reported, why there has been no deal.

In the very first round of talks after the April cease-fire — in Pakistan, in April — Trump’s envoys, Steve Witkoff and JD Vance, told the Iranians that Trump would accept a deal in which Iran suspended nuclear enrichment for ten years. Iranian officials agreed to that proposal. Trump then turned around and insisted on twenty years. That reversal was, per two mediators and an official with direct knowledge, a major reason the Pakistan talks collapsed.

Iran’s supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, is in hiding — communicating via courier system, taking days to respond to tweaks in the framework. On the other side: a president so unpredictable that his own envoys cannot negotiate reliably on his behalf. In the latest round, as both sides waited days for Khamenei’s response, Trump added new conditions on Iran’s nuclear program and frozen assets. Iranian officials described it as validating Tehran’s mistrust entirely.

The frozen assets problem: previous drafts allowed Iran access to up to $24 billion frozen in regional banks. The fourteen-point document published by Iranian state media Friday goes further — it reportedly requires the release of half of Iran’s frozen funds before final negotiations even begin, the suspension of oil sanctions, and the lifting of the naval blockade as preconditions. U.S. reconstruction commitments of at least $300 billion. All American forces to withdraw from Iran.

These are Iranian terms, published by Iranian state media, unconfirmed by Washington. They may be an opening position dressed as a closing one. They may be the actual document. The gap between the two possibilities is the gap this war has occupied for two months.

The nuclear timeline: U.S. officials say the current negotiating framework would grind Iran’s nuclear program to a halt “for about 15 years.” Not twenty, as Trump has publicly demanded. Fifteen — the same sunset clause in the JCPOA that Trump spent years denouncing and tore up in his first term. The deal he destroyed to get a better deal may produce an identical deal, at the cost of thirteen American lives, seventeen hundred Iranian civilian deaths, and a global energy crisis.

On Thursday, Trump told the Oval Office: “It’s a very strong memorandum of understanding. That’s a little conceptual.” By Friday morning, it had fourteen points and a venue — Switzerland, possibly Sunday, ahead of the G7.

Sanam Vakil of Chatham House: a preliminary deal will emerge “not because anyone wants one, but because both sides need one.” That moment may have arrived.

VII. The Iranians

Morteza Nikoubazl / NurPhoto / Getty

The Atlantic published an economic portrait of Iran this week by Arash Azizi alongside Farnaz Fassihi’s human voices dispatches. Together they are the war’s civilian record — the people for whom this conflict is neither a negotiation nor a cease-fire but the texture of daily life.

At least one million Iranian jobs lost since February — possibly two million. Iran’s official inflation rate: 85 percent. Food inflation: 130 percent. Edible oils: 266 percent. Meat and chicken: 169 percent. The rial: one U.S. dollar now buys 1.75 million Iranian rials. Monthly minimum wage after a recent 60 percent increase: approximately $100. Iran may experience double-digit economic contraction this year — “a calamity with little precedent in its modern history.”

Sara, 33, graphic designer, Tehran: “Let me be honest with you. I have no idea how I’ll survive.” Majid, 27, computer engineer, laid off: “No jobs and massively increased prices. It basically means we are fucked.” Alireza, 33, dairy factory worker: let go last month when his factory closed. Billboards across Tehran now advertise installment plans to pay for basic goods.

And then, this week, the bombs returned.

Vahid, 37, Tehran, after Thursday’s whipsaw from “VERY HARD TONIGHT” to strikes cancelled to “we ended the war with Iran today”: “They go to war at night, they stop the war by morning, it’s all ridiculous. Either fight or don’t fight. We are fed up.”

Reza, 48, company manager, who took his wife north to the Caspian to wait out the tensions: “Work is very slow, businesses are paralyzed because of fluctuating prices, it feels like our life is on hold right now.”

Mahasti, 65, health sector worker: the blockade continuing even without bombs is “as bad as bombs being dropped.” “Our lives are just getting more and more difficult by the day. If it’s not war, it’s sanctions or blockade, always something.”

Streams of Tehran residents fled north Thursday on all three Caspian roads, creating traffic jams, after Trump’s morning threats. Then Trump cancelled the strikes. The traffic jams continued anyway. Nobody trusted the cancellation.

Iranian President Pezeshkian, at a ceremony commemorating the late Ayatollah Ali Khamenei: “We must get out of this no war, no peace situation. War is definitely not to the benefit of the country.” But military aggression, he added, would not make Iran surrender.

Azizi’s closing line from The Atlantic: “Iran has withstood immense military pressure for months and claims triumph on the battleground. But its tattered economy remains its Achilles’ heel.”

The population that was supposed to rise up and overthrow the government has instead been flattened by economic catastrophe and is asking, exhaustedly, for the war to end. Ellie Geranmayeh of the European Council on Foreign Relations: the war “has arguably left the regime more powerful than it was.”

VIII. The Economics

The war reached into European monetary policy this week. The European Central Bank raised interest rates Thursday in response to war-driven inflation. U.S. consumer prices rose 4.2 percent in May year-on-year — the highest since April 2023 — driven almost entirely by energy, which as a category rose 23.5 percent. The World Bank said Thursday that the war is slowing global economic growth to its weakest pace since the Covid-19 pandemic.

The U.S. Energy Information Agency: global oil consumption in 2026 is expected to fall by more than one million barrels per day from last year. Before the war, EIA had forecast consumption would increase by 1.2 million barrels. The Strait of Hormuz will not return to normal traffic until early 2027. Eight vessels transited over one recent weekend. Normal traffic: 3,000 ships per month.

Oil moved with the precision of a seismograph tracking Trump’s Truth Social posts. Brent Crude rose when he threatened Kharg. Fell more than 3 percent below $90 a barrel the moment he cancelled the strikes at 1:36 p.m. Thursday. Rose again when he threatened more strikes. On Friday morning, when Iranian state media published the fourteen-point draft and Bloomberg reported a Swiss signing, oil fell again — U.S. crude futures down 1.61%, Brent down 1.75%. European stocks surged nearly 2 percent. The market cannot evaluate the credibility of the claim. It reacts to the signal.

Trump said in the Oval Office Thursday that “the stock market likes the deal.” He was right. It also liked the previous thirty-eight announcements of imminent peace. The thirty-ninth arrived with fourteen points and a venue. The market is treating that as meaningful. Whether it is remains, as of this writing, a little conceptual.

Gas: $4.15 national average, up 39 percent since February 28. Diesel: $5.30, up 41 percent. The Atlantic examined the Iranian economic wreckage this week alongside the military record — one million jobs lost, double-digit contraction possible, 85 percent official inflation. The two economies most damaged by this war are the Iranian civilian economy and the American working-class household. Both are waiting for the same thing.

IX. Kharg

Kharg Island 2026 Planet Labs PBC, via Reuters

Kharg Island sits in the Persian Gulf approximately 25 kilometres off Iran’s southwest coast. Approximately 90 percent of Iran’s oil exports flow through it. Taking it would be the most significant act of military aggression against Iran since the war began. It would almost certainly trigger a comprehensive Iranian response across the Gulf. Trump’s own advisers oppose it. The U.S. is dangerously low on long-range weapons stocks. The depletion of munitions has already harmed America’s military posture in Europe and Asia.

Trump has threatened Kharg repeatedly. Iran has consistently called his bluff. On Thursday morning he threatened it again — then, on Fox & Friends, said he didn’t know if America had the stomach for it.

The threat and the retraction in the same breath. That is where this war lives — between the thing being threatened and the thing being admitted, in the gap between “VERY HARD TONIGHT” and “I think they’d like to see us come home.”

IRGC General Majid Mousavi: “You are making the holy Strait of Hormuz unsafe? We will turn the entire region into hell from across Iran.”

Iran’s commander of military headquarters, Thursday: “Either everyone will be able to export oil and gas, or no one will.”

UN Secretary General António Guterres, Wednesday: “The cease-fire is more like a lesser-fire.”

Araghchi, Iran’s foreign minister: “Leave our region if you want to be safe.”

By Friday morning, the deal that would make Kharg unnecessary — reopen the strait, lift the blockade, begin the reconstruction — had fourteen points and was being discussed for Sunday in Switzerland. The island is still there. The threat is, for now, in abeyance.

X. The Pattern

This diary has tracked the military and diplomatic record of this war from its first day. A parallel series on this Substack — The Baseline Problem — has been tracking something harder to document: whether the man making these decisions is capable of holding a position across twenty-four hours.

Part XVII of that series, published Thursday, introduced a sixth category of observable behaviour: consequence blindness. The apparent inability to perceive the downstream effects of one’s own decisions as relevant to the decisions that follow. Consider the evidence of a single week: at least thirty-eight peace announcements, each followed by escalation or silence. A covert naval operation announced from the Oval Office because, in the president’s own words, “I wanted to say it so badly, I didn’t want to ruin it.” A cease-fire declared meaningless by one of its own signatories. Twenty thousand people without water in 96-degree heat. Three Indian sailors dead. An eleven-year-old girl in Bahrain injured by intercepted drone debris.

The Baseline Problem’s diagnosis: the loop between action and consequence — the basic cognitive architecture that connects “this information is classified for a reason” to “therefore I will not announce it to reporters” — did not close.

The Iran war has removed the academic quality from that question. The water tanks in Sirik are the proof. The thirty-eight announcements are the proof. The Thursday morning Kharg Island threat — retracted in the same phone call — is the proof. And then, at 1:36 p.m., the Pakistanis told him there was a deal, and the strikes were cancelled, and by evening he was telling a Georgia telerally that the war was over. By Friday morning, Iranian state media had published fourteen points and Bloomberg had a venue.

The last voice in the room was dovish. The loop closed — for one afternoon, and then perhaps for a weekend, and then perhaps for longer.

Riccardo Alcaro of the Institute of International Affairs in Rome: “If the Iranians perceive this military power is to force them to accept concessions they are unwilling to accept, they are much more likely to respond by escalating than by giving in.”

Sascha Bruchmann of the International Institute for Strategic Studies, asked what to call the current state of the conflict: “What do you even call this?”

Vahid, 37, Tehran: “Either fight or don’t fight. We are fed up.”

So, for the record, is the rest of the world.

XI. It’s OK

Mr. President, it is okay not to retaliate.

It was okay on Tuesday when the Apache went down and the attribution was uncertain and the two pilots were safe. It was okay on Wednesday when a second wave of strikes destroyed a water tank in 96-degree heat. It was okay on Thursday morning when the third wave concluded and the cease-fire had been declared meaningless and the Qatari mediators had already left Tehran empty-handed.

And then, at 1:36 p.m. on Thursday, you didn’t. The strikes were cancelled. Brent Crude fell below $90. Tehran residents who had fled north on jammed highways checked their phones and wondered if it was safe to go home. They didn’t fully trust it. They had been here before. So had the rest of us.

The deal Trump described Thursday afternoon — and that Iranian state media outlined in fourteen points on Friday morning — is, in its essential architecture, the deal that existed before this war began. The JCPOA his administration tore up had a fifteen-year sunset clause. Iran reaffirmed in that agreement’s first paragraph that it would never seek, develop, or acquire nuclear weapons. Trump called it weak. He withdrew. Tehran accelerated its program. A war began. Thirteen Americans died. Seventeen hundred Iranian civilians died. Three Indian sailors died. An eleven-year-old girl in Bahrain was injured by shrapnel. A water tank in the desert had “Water is the pulse of life” painted on its wall and a GBU-39 through its roof.

And now, perhaps, a memorandum of understanding. Fourteen points. To be signed in Switzerland, possibly Sunday, by Vance and Witkoff and Kushner while the president hosts a UFC fight for his 80th birthday. Iran’s IRNA called the timing “media speculations.” Israel has not commented. The deal is, as of this writing, still — in the president’s own word — conceptual.

But for the first time in one hundred and three days, the concept has fourteen points, a venue, and a date.

Whether it holds is the question this diary cannot answer. What it can say is this: the cease-fire of April 8, 2026 was declared meaningless on Day 103 by one of its own signatories. The war that was “not a big thing” produced, in one week, three waves of U.S. strikes on Iran, two rounds of Iranian retaliation across the Gulf, a satellite-confirmed precision strike on a drinking water facility, three dead Indian sailors, a formal closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and a president who threatened Kharg Island and a UFC birthday party in the same breath.

It is okay not to retaliate, Mr. President. It was always okay. The question — the one this diary and The Baseline Problem have been asking since February 28 — is whether that is a choice that can be made and held, or whether the next voice in the room will change everything by morning.

On Thursday afternoon, it held. On Friday morning, it was holding still.
