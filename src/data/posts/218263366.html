The Donation That Didn’t Arrive

Greg Brockman, OpenAI’s president, and his wife, Anna, pledged $50 million to Leading the Future, the industry super PAC, in two instalments of $25 million. The second never came. Theodore Schleifer and Mike Isaac reported for The New York Times on 30 September that Brockman told staff in a June Slack message that he felt “terrible” that the PAC “keeps reflecting on the company”, and that he had “no plans to donate more at this time”. OpenAI had already published a blog post saying, in bold, that “no outside political group speaks for OpenAI”. The PAC is not short of money: Andreessen Horowitz has given it $50 million, and it had about $43 million on hand at the end of June. But when the company’s own president decides his political giving has become bad for business, the industry’s reputation problem has reached the people writing the cheques.

The Country With No Guardrails

While other governments look for brakes, Argentina is advertising the absence of them. Emma Bubola and Daniel Politi reported for The New York Times on 29 September that President Javier Milei told the UN General Assembly of his country’s “commitment to not preemptively regulate A.I.” and is pitching Patagonia, cold, empty and rich in shale gas, as a home for data centres. His government is also drafting a law for “nonhuman corporations” run by AI agents. After criticism, the bill now requires one human to bear legal liability, and its main purpose is tax incentives. Yuval Noah Harari warned that such firms would sit beyond the reach of criminal law, because jail, the sanction that deters human executives, “is irrelevant to A.I.s”. Milei’s media strategist, meanwhile, once tried to promote a pro-government influencer who turned out to be AI-generated. He called the discovery “totally inoffensive”.
