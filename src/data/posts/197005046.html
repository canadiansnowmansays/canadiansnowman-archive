By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Rep. James Clyburn, whose South Carolina district faces elimination after three decades. First elected in 1992 under the maps shaped by VRA redistricting.

James Clyburn was first elected to Congress in 1992. He is now one of the longest-serving and most influential House Democrats in American history — a former majority whip, the man who arguably saved Joe Biden’s 2020 presidential campaign with a single endorsement before the South Carolina primary, a figure whose biography spans from the civil rights movement to the present moment in ways that make him a walking encapsulation of what the Voting Rights Act made possible.

He would not be there without the 1992 redistricting.

He knows this. He said so directly this week, in an interview with the New York Times’s Carl Hulse: “And about half of the members of the Congressional Black Caucus wouldn’t be there.”

The South Carolina legislature is now taking steps to eliminate his district.

This is the full arc of the 1992 bargain — what it produced, what it cost, and what is being done to it now. Understanding it is essential to understanding why what is happening in Tennessee, Louisiana, Alabama, and South Carolina is not an aberration from American political history. It is, as Alabama advocate Anneshia Hardy told the Atlantic this week, “a textbook example of how power operates in this country.”

What Happened in 1992

The story begins not with Democrats but with Republicans.

In the late 1980s, the Republican Party had been in the House minority for nearly 40 years. White Southern conservatives were migrating toward the GOP, but the Democratic Party’s hold on Southern congressional seats remained powerful — built on decades of incumbent entrenchment and on coalitions of white and Black voters that had elected white Democrats across the region for generations.

Then came a series of court decisions interpreting the Voting Rights Act’s 1982 amendments to require that states draw districts giving minority voters a genuine opportunity to elect representatives of their choice. The practical question became: how do you draw those districts?

Republicans had an answer. And they were extraordinarily helpful in providing it.

Conservative groups bankrolled by wealthy Republicans joined with liberal civil rights organizations to educate minority advocates about how to shape new districts. Republican operatives provided free access to expensive computer software that could model district configurations. The logic was not complicated, even if its candour was unusual: if you pack Black and Hispanic voters into new majority-minority districts, you simultaneously dilute Democratic strength in the surrounding areas — areas where coalitions of white and Black voters had been electing white Democrats for decades. Removing those minority voters from the coalition destroys the coalition. The surrounding districts flip Republican.

It worked beyond what anyone predicted.

The 1992 election produced a historic class of Black and Hispanic lawmakers — including Clyburn, the first African American to represent South Carolina in Congress since Reconstruction; the first Black members from North Carolina, Virginia, Alabama, and Florida since the nineteenth century; and three new Black representatives from Florida alone. The Congressional Black Caucus surged. Minority representation in Congress increased dramatically.

Two years later, Republicans took the House for the first time in 40 years.

The surrounding districts, stripped of their minority Democratic base, had gone Republican. The Southern strategy had found its cartographic completion. Benjamin Ginsberg, then the general counsel of the Republican National Committee, described the redistricting as ending “the Democrats’ shameful practice of slicing, dicing and fracturing racial minority communities in order to prop up white incumbents of the Democratic Party.”

It was a statement of breathtaking audacity. The party that had spent decades exploiting racial resentment to peel white Southerners away from the Democratic coalition was now positioning itself as the champion of minority representation — because championing minority representation in the right places destroyed Democratic power everywhere else.

Professor Allison Mitchell of Notre Dame, writing in Time magazine, called the 1992 coalition between Black Democrats and conservative white Republicans “incredibly successful.” “The 1992 election proved that the coalition between Black Democrats and conservative white Republicans delivered for both sides,” she wrote.

John Lewis in the 1970s. He warned in 1992 that creating majority-minority districts at the expense of broader coalitions would empower Republicans long-term.

John Lewis warned at the time that it wouldn’t last. He said the new maps would empower Republicans by weakening the progressive coalition of white and Black voters in the South. He was right. He was overruled by those who argued that guaranteed minority representation was worth the cost — that the alternative, in which Black voters remained a junior partner in white-dominated Democratic coalitions, was itself a form of disenfranchisement.

“Gerrymandering was done to keep Black folks out,” Clyburn said this week. “If you gerrymander to keep them out, you’ve got to gerrymander to bring them in.”

He is right. He is also watching the maps being redrawn to keep them out again.

The Consolidation

For thirty years after 1992, Republicans consolidated their power across the South. They claimed virtually every statewide elected office. They built supermajorities in multiple state legislatures. They made the South, which had been the most reliably Democratic region in America for a century after the Civil War, into the most reliably Republican region in the country.

During this consolidation, the majority-minority districts served a specific function. They were where Black representation lived — protected enclaves, legally required and judicially enforced, where the VRA’s guarantee of minority voting power produced Black members of Congress even as everything around them turned red.

South Carolina’s 6th Congressional District, long represented by James Clyburn

Clyburn’s district in South Carolina. Bennie Thompson’s district in Mississippi. Steve Cohen’s district in Memphis. The districts in Louisiana that produced Cleo Fields and Troy Carter. The district in Alabama that, after years of litigation, produced Shomari Figures in 2024.

These were not just seats. They were, as Carl Hulse notes, “rare and coveted platforms, lifting Black leaders to prominence in the Democratic Party and the broader political arena.” They were where careers were built. Where Evan Turnage, 34, went home to Mississippi to run and lost narrowly and planned to come back. Where the pipeline ran.

Republicans tolerated these districts for thirty years because they needed to — the VRA required them. And perhaps also because, having achieved their strategic goal in 1994, the majority-minority districts were no longer a threat. They were contained. One safe Democratic seat in a state where Republicans hold everything else costs the Republican Party nothing.

The Elimination

Last week’s Supreme Court ruling in Louisiana v. Callais removed the legal requirement to tolerate them.

Partisan control of redistricting before the 1992 elections. Republicans strategically supported majority-minority districts to flip surrounding seats

The Court held that majority-minority districts are now permissible only as remedies for proven intentional racial discrimination — a standard so high, in a political environment where everyone documents their motives in partisan rather than racial terms, as to be effectively unreachable. Republicans no longer need to maintain districts they built to serve their own strategic interests. They can eliminate them. And having consolidated enough power to win Southern districts without the minority voter dilution strategy that created those districts, they no longer need the strategy either.

The elimination is now underway.

Tennessee carved Memphis into three pieces in seven days. Louisiana suspended its primaries to redraw its maps. Alabama held legislative votes during a tornado watch, flooding its own state capitol, to preserve its ability to redistrict. South Carolina’s lower chamber voted this week to consider eliminating Clyburn’s district — the seat he has held for thirty-two years, the seat whose existence the Republican Party helped engineer in 1992.

Anneshia Hardy, executive director of Alabama Values, was at an event at the Equal Justice Initiative in Montgomery when the Callais ruling came down. She got back to her car and wept. “This feels like the echoes of the ‘southern strategy’ of the ‘60s,” she told the Atlantic. “This is diluting Black political power.”

She is describing the completion of a circle. The Southern strategy of the 1960s — exploiting racial resentment to move white Southern conservatives into the Republican Party — created the political environment in which Black voters needed protected districts to have representation at all. The 1992 redistricting created those districts, at the cost of Democratic power in the surrounding areas. The 2026 Callais ruling eliminates the protection, in a political environment where Republican power in the South is now so consolidated that dispersing Black Democratic voters into surrounding districts simply adds more Republican votes to already-safe Republican seats.

The 1992 bargain has been fully collected.

What Lewis Saw

John Lewis’s warning in 1992 was about the fragility of the arrangement. Guaranteed representation in contained districts was not the same as political power. It was representation without coalition — the ability to send Black members to Congress from safe seats, without the broader political influence that comes from being a decisive voting bloc in competitive districts.

President Lyndon B. Johnson signs the Voting Rights Act of 1965, surrounded by civil rights

For thirty years, that warning looked excessive. The majority-minority districts produced the Congressional Black Caucus. They produced Clyburn and Thompson and Cleo Fields and eventually Barack Obama, whose coalition was built partly on the mobilisation the CBC’s presence made possible. The districts worked.

What Lewis saw — what he could not convince enough people to see in 1992 — was that the arrangement depended entirely on the legal enforcement mechanism that made Republicans maintain the districts against their strategic interest. Remove the enforcement, and the incentive structure collapses. Republicans built the cage because the court made them. The court has now unlocked the door.

The CBC, which did not exist until 1971 and grew to more than sixty members under the VRA’s protection, will shrink. Clyburn said this week that a massive turnout in November to change Congress is the only path to restoring the law’s intent — to pass the John Robert Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act and rebuild what the Supreme Court has demolished.

He is right about the remedy. The political arithmetic of whether that Congress can be assembled, in an environment where the redistricting war has given Republicans a structural advantage that allows them to lose the national popular vote by 2.5 points and still hold the House, is the question the rest of this series will track.

The Speed as Signal

The Atlantic’s Russell Berman and Yvonne Wingett Sanchez noted this week that the speed and blunt force of the Republican response to Callais “has been astonishing.” Louisiana invoking emergency powers meant for natural disasters to suspend an election already underway. Alabama holding votes during a tornado watch while floodwaters entered the state capitol. Tennessee moving from ruling to enacted map in seven days.

This speed is not chaos. It is preparation. Florida drew its map in anticipation of the ruling before it was handed down. Tennessee had its map ready. The Republican state legislative apparatus had been planning for a favourable Supreme Court ruling for years — building the legal arguments in the Callais litigation, maintaining the political pressure on Republican governors and legislatures, watching the Court’s conservative majority signal its direction through a decade of narrowing decisions.

What looks like a sudden rush is the execution of a prepared plan. The 1992 bargain took thirty years to fully collect. The collection itself took one week.

Omar Noureldin, who now leads litigation at Common Cause, put it plainly to the Atlantic: “These other states are using the Callais decision as pretext to do what they wanted to do anyway.”

They wanted to do it anyway. The ruling gave them the legal cover. The speed is the measure of how long they had been waiting.

The View From Here

I have spent twenty-six parts of this series asking whether American democracy has the institutional resilience to fight back against a coordinated assault on the structures of electoral fairness.

The 1992 history that Carl Hulse has reconstructed this week adds a dimension to that question I had not fully incorporated. The assault is not entirely external to the democratic system. It is, in significant measure, the democratic system operating as designed — parties maximising their advantage within the rules, until the rules change, and then maximising their advantage under the new rules.

Republicans used the courts to require majority-minority districts in 1992 because it served their interests. They used the courts to eliminate the VRA’s enforcement mechanism in 2013, 2021, and 2026 because that served their interests. The interests changed. The strategy adapted. The institution — the Court, the legislature, the redistricting process itself — was the tool in both cases.

From outside, this looks like the perversion of democratic institutions. From inside, it looks like politics. The difference between those two descriptions is where the deepest argument about American democracy lives.

James Clyburn was brought to Congress by a 1992 bargain between Black Democrats who wanted representation and Republicans who wanted power. The bargain delivered both. Thirty-two years later, the Republican half of the bargain is being dissolved — because the power is now sufficient to sustain itself without the representation it once required.

His district may survive the legal challenges. The cases are in court. Nothing is final.

But the bargain is over. What comes next depends on whether the party that once needed Black voters to dilute has misjudged, as John Lewis might have predicted, the long-term cost of no longer needing them at all.
