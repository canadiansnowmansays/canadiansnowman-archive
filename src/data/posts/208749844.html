Venice should not have existed. It occupies a lagoon of mudflats and marsh islands at the head of the Adriatic, has no arable land to speak of, no timber, no metal, no stone, and no fresh water beyond what it collects from rainfall in cisterns. Its original population were refugees from the mainland fleeing successive waves of invasion in late antiquity, who settled on the islands for the straightforward reason that nobody with an army wanted them. Every material thing the city required — food, building stone, wood for ships and pilings, iron — had to be brought in from somewhere else. By the standards this series has been applying to Samarkand, Kashgar, and Dunhuang, Venice had no natural advantages whatsoever.

What it had was position and the absence of alternatives. The lagoon sat at the northern end of the Adriatic, which is to say at the point where Mediterranean shipping came closest to the passes over the Alps and the markets of central Europe beyond them. A city with no land had to live by water, and a city living by water at that particular spot was positioned to move eastern goods into Europe and European goods east. Venice’s first exports were fish and salt, which the lagoon did produce. Everything after that was other people’s cargo.

The city’s political relationship with Byzantium shaped its early commercial position decisively. Venice began as a nominally Byzantine possession and spent centuries in an ambiguous relationship with Constantinople — formally subordinate, practically independent, and commercially indispensable to a Byzantine state that needed naval support against Norman and later Turkish pressure. The Golden Bull of 1082, issued by Alexios I in exchange for Venetian naval assistance, granted Venetian merchants exemption from customs duties throughout the empire, which was an extraordinary concession and gave Venice a decisive cost advantage over every rival in the eastern Mediterranean. The arrangement was resented in Constantinople almost immediately, and Byzantine attempts to walk it back, including a mass arrest of Venetian residents in 1171, poisoned the relationship for generations.

Which brings the Fourth Crusade into a series about trade routes, where it belongs more than it belongs in most accounts of the Crusades. The expedition of 1202 to 1204 was chartered to attack Egypt. It was financed by Venice, which built the fleet on the strength of a contract for a crusader army that failed to materialize in the promised numbers and consequently could not pay. Doge Enrico Dandolo — reportedly in his nineties and blind, which has not stopped him becoming one of the more vivid figures in medieval history — proposed that the crusaders work off the debt by helping Venice reduce the rebellious Adriatic city of Zara, a Christian town, which they did, drawing excommunication. From there the expedition was diverted again, by a combination of Byzantine dynastic politics and crusader debt, to Constantinople, which it besieged and, in April 1204, sacked with a thoroughness that Byzantine writers never forgave and that arguably crippled the empire permanently.

Venice’s share of the settlement was carefully chosen and reveals exactly what the Republic was: it took no interest in inland territory and claimed instead a string of ports, islands, and harbours — Crete above all, along with holdings in the Aegean and on the Greek coast — that together constituted a maritime network linking Venice to the eastern Mediterranean. The Republic acquired, in one transaction, the infrastructure of a trading empire and almost none of the burdens of a territorial one. It also carried off, among a great deal else, the four bronze horses that stood on the facade of San Marco for the next eight centuries.

The commercial machinery Venice built on this foundation was sophisticated and deserves description, because the institutions matter more to this series than the plunder does. The colleganza, the standard Venetian partnership contract, allowed a sedentary investor to provide capital and a travelling merchant to provide labour and risk, splitting profits on agreed terms — a structure functionally near-identical to the Islamic qirad that Part X of this series described, and whose relationship to it remains a live scholarly argument. Venice developed marine insurance, double-entry bookkeeping practices that spread through Italian commercial culture, and a bond market in state debt that let the Republic finance wars against future tax revenue. The state itself ran the mude, convoys of state-owned galleys auctioned annually to merchant operators, sailing on fixed schedules to Alexandria, Beirut, Constantinople, the Black Sea, and eventually Flanders and England. This was a public utility for commerce: predictable, protected, and available to any Venetian citizen who could afford a share, which distributed the trade’s profits considerably more broadly through the citizen body than a private monopoly would have.

The Arsenal was the physical expression of the same instinct. A state-owned shipyard employing thousands at its height, organized on a sequential production system that visitors described with astonishment, it could assemble a galley from prefabricated components in a matter of hours during emergencies. Dante put it in the Inferno, using the boiling pitch of the Arsenal as an image of hell, which is either a compliment or its opposite depending on how one reads it.

Venice was not without competitors, and the rivalry with Genoa deserves more than a footnote because the two republics fought four full-scale wars across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries over precisely the question of who would control access to eastern goods. Genoa had its own network — the Black Sea colonies at Caffa and Pera that Part VII of this series encountered as the entry point for the Black Death, and a commercial reach that ran to the Atlantic and eventually supplied the financial expertise behind Iberian expansion. The decisive engagement came at the War of Chioggia between 1378 and 1381, fought largely within the Venetian lagoon itself, which Venice won at enormous cost and which left Genoa permanently reduced as a Mediterranean naval power. Genoa’s subsequent redirection toward banking and toward financing Spanish and Portuguese ventures is one of the quieter hinges in European history: the capital and the expertise that funded Atlantic expansion came substantially from the losing side of an argument about who would control the Mediterranean end of the Silk Road.

The physical arrangement of Venetian commerce is worth picturing, because it was unusually deliberate. The Rialto was the financial centre — banks, insurance, the exchange, and the wholesale markets — and the Republic legislated its function carefully. Foreign merchants were required to lodge and trade in designated buildings: the Fondaco dei Tedeschi housed German traders, the Fondaco dei Turchi the Ottomans, with warehouse space below and living quarters above, and Venetian officials supervising transactions. This was partly surveillance and partly taxation, and it also functioned as a service the merchants valued — secure storage, guaranteed weights, an enforceable legal framework, and brokers who spoke the languages. The word ghetto itself is Venetian, from the foundry district where the Republic confined its Jewish population from 1516, an arrangement that combined the same instincts toward control and toward commercial utility in a form nobody should be comfortable admiring. Venice’s tolerance, like the Tang’s and the Mongols’, was a policy rather than a principle, extended where it paid and constrained wherever the Republic judged constraint useful.

What all of this was for was the goods arriving from the east, and here Venice’s position in this series becomes precise. Venice was not on the Silk Road in any meaningful sense; almost no Venetian ever travelled its length, and the Polos are famous partly because they were exceptional. Venice sat at the terminus, buying eastern goods from Muslim intermediaries at Alexandria, Damascus, and the Black Sea ports, and reselling them into Europe at a markup that funded the entire civilization. Pepper and spices dominated by value. Silk, cotton, sugar, dyestuffs, and Chinese ceramics moved alongside them. Going the other way went European woollens, metals, timber, and silver — the same imbalance Rome had complained about, resolved the same way, with bullion flowing east because Europe still had little that Asia wanted.

The Republic’s relationship with its Muslim trading partners was, accordingly, pragmatic to the point of scandalizing the rest of Christian Europe. Venice traded with the Mamluks throughout periods when the papacy was prohibiting commerce with Muslim powers, negotiated commercial treaties with the Ottomans within years of fighting wars against them, and maintained a permanent diplomatic and commercial presence in Constantinople after 1453. A Venetian ambassador’s reports — the relazioni that returning envoys were required to deliver to the Senate, and which survive in quantity — read as some of the most clear-eyed political intelligence produced anywhere in early modern Europe, precisely because the Republic needed accurate information rather than flattering information.

The Republic’s constitution deserves a paragraph, since it was the mechanism that kept a fabulously wealthy merchant oligarchy from tearing itself apart in the manner of nearly every other Italian city-state. Venice was governed by an elaborate apparatus of interlocking councils, with the Doge elected for life through a selection procedure of almost comic complexity — alternating rounds of lottery and ballot, running to some ten stages, designed specifically to make the outcome impossible to fix. The Doge’s actual powers were tightly constrained, his family barred from various offices, his estate audited after death. The Serrata of 1297 closed the Great Council to families not already enrolled, converting a merchant republic into a hereditary aristocracy of some hundreds of families — a narrowing that would ordinarily invite revolt, and largely did not, because the mude system and the colleganza gave non-noble Venetians real access to the profits of trade even while excluding them from office. The city experienced almost no successful internal coups across a millennium, which in the context of medieval Italy is the more remarkable achievement than the wealth.

The eastern trade also left Venice looking unlike anywhere else in Europe, in ways still visible. San Marco is a Byzantine building in its plan and its mosaics, its treasury filled with loot from 1204 and with objects acquired by ordinary purchase over centuries. The city’s palazzi carry pointed arches and tracery that owe as much to Cairo and Damascus as to Gothic France. Venetian glassmakers on Murano worked techniques with Islamic and Byzantine antecedents, and produced enamelled and gilded wares specifically for export to Mamluk and Ottoman buyers. Venetian painters used pigments — ultramarine ground from Afghan lapis lazuli above all — that arrived through the same channels as the pepper, which means the blues of Bellini and Titian are a direct material consequence of a trade route running back to a mine in Badakhshan that had been supplying the world since the Bronze Age. A city that produced nothing had absorbed everything.

The maritime empire Venice assembled after 1204 — the Stato da Mar — is worth a moment because holding it turned out to be considerably harder than acquiring it. Crete, the largest and most valuable possession, was governed as a settler colony with Venetian nobles granted estates, and it revolted against Venetian rule repeatedly across four centuries, including a major rising in the 1360s in which some of the Venetian colonists themselves joined the Greek population against the metropole. The Republic held it anyway until the Ottomans took it after a siege of Candia that ran from 1648 to 1669 — twenty-one years, one of the longest sieges in recorded history, and a conflict that consumed Venetian resources on a scale the Republic could no longer afford. Cyprus had gone in 1571, the year of Lepanto, in a campaign whose atrocities at Famagusta were still being invoked in Venetian propaganda a century later. The pattern is consistent and instructive: the trading network that made Venice rich required naval bases, the naval bases required defending, and defending them against a land empire with vastly greater manpower eventually consumed more than the trade produced. Middleman positions are cheap to hold only while nobody large is interested in taking them.

The decline arrived from the direction the previous instalment described. Portuguese pepper reaching Lisbon by sea from 1501 onward broke the assumption that eastern goods had to pass through the eastern Mediterranean, and although the Venetian and Egyptian route remained competitive for longer than the standard account allows — Red Sea traffic recovered substantially in the mid-sixteenth century — the long-term direction was fixed. Atlantic powers with oceanic access and, eventually, colonial production of the goods in question did not need a Mediterranean intermediary. Venice’s response was to shift toward manufacturing, which worked for a time: Venetian glass, printing, textiles, and shipbuilding sustained the city through the sixteenth century. But the structural position was gone. The Republic survived, increasingly ornamental, until Napoleon extinguished it in 1797 without a fight.

There is a version of Venice’s story that treats it as a tale of decadence and decline, and the city’s later reputation as a carnival town for foreign visitors has encouraged it. A better reading, for the purposes of this series, is that Venice was the last and most successful of a very long line of middleman powers, and that it met the same end as all the others. Parthia, Sassanid Persia, the Sogdians, Palmyra, the Kara-Khanids, the Mamluks — every one of them built wealth on the same proposition, that whoever stands between the producer and the consumer can charge for the privilege, and every one of them eventually discovered that the proposition holds only until someone works out a way around. Venice’s run was longer and its institutions more durable than most. The proposition was the same, and so was the ending.
