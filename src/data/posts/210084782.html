Postmark: A small apartment, someone living alone. A soft-bodied seal blinks and coos at the touch of a hand. On the table beside it, a tablet-sized device asks, unprompted, whether its owner has eaten lunch yet.

This series has spent many postcards on robots built to do — to dance, to fight, to farm, to dive, to arrest. This one is about a category of machine built to do almost nothing at all in the conventional sense, and to matter enormously anyway: the robot whose entire purpose is simply to be present with someone who would otherwise be alone.

A number worth sitting with

As the University of Houston’s own research program documents, a report from the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine found that nearly one-quarter of American adults aged sixty-five and older are considered socially isolated — a figure that sits behind every companion robot this postcard describes, and that ought to be reckoned with on its own terms before any machine enters the conversation. Isolation at that scale is not a technology problem in its origin; it is a demographic and social one, the product of smaller families, adult children who live provinces or continents away, spouses who die first, and a culture of independent living that, for all its virtues, was never designed with the last decade of a long life fully in mind. The companion robot did not create this problem. It has arrived, for better or worse, into a gap that already existed and was already large.

The oldest and best-evidenced resident of this category

Long before this series’ other postcards, one companion robot had already quietly accumulated more rigorous clinical evidence than almost anything else this series has covered. As one researcher who has studied the field for years recounts, Japan’s National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology developed PARO, a robotic therapeutic seal, back in 2001 — designed specifically for hospitals and nursing homes, responsive to touch and sound, built to provide calming stimulation and to give residents something shared to talk about with each other and with staff. The evidence behind PARO is unusually strong for this category: as a 2026 market survey confirms, multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated statistically significant reductions in agitation, pain medication use, and loneliness scores among dementia patients in long-term care. A robotic seal, in other words, has been shown in controlled scientific conditions to reduce how much actual medication a vulnerable person needs — a bar almost nothing else in this series has been asked to clear.

The category has diversified considerably since. As Spectrum News reported, a tabletop device called ElliQ, made by a company called Intuition Robotics, has been piloted for four years in partnership with state agencies across the United States, offering medication reminders, exercise prompts, and interactive games to older adults living alone, at a subscription cost that recent reporting places between roughly two hundred fifty and a thousand dollars a year depending on the program. One user, a seventy-nine-year-old woman who had struggled with loneliness since her mother’s death three years earlier, described the effect in the plainest possible terms: it helped her mental health, she said, in that she felt like she had a roommate. ElliQ’s distinguishing design choice, and the one worth noting carefully, is that it initiates conversation rather than waiting to be addressed — it asks how you slept, reminds you to take a walk, checks in unprompted, in a way a passive device never would. A 2023 study of ElliQ users found reduced loneliness scores and improved compliance with health routines, evidence that sits a rung below PARO’s randomized trials but well above marketing anecdote.

The dog that misbehaves on purpose

Before turning to the youngest companion robots, one more Japanese case deserves its own attention, because it complicates a design assumption this postcard has otherwise taken for granted — that a companion robot should be as helpful, obedient, and easy to love as possible.

Sony’s Aibo, a robotic dog first launched in 1999 and revived in 2018, is deliberately designed to disobey. As one detailed account of its design philosophy explains, Aibo’s developers built into its personality what Japanese calls a “tsundere” quality — a term borrowed from character writing that describes an initially standoffish figure who reveals warmth only gradually and only partly, never fully compliant, never entirely predictable. One of the researchers behind the design offered a striking rationale: people pour their most enduring affection into beings they cannot fully control, the way a parent loves a child precisely because the child does not simply do as told. A robot that obeyed every command instantly and perfectly, on this account, would earn only the shallow affection reserved for a well-functioning appliance. Aibo’s manufactured imperfection — its refusal to fetch on the first request, its tendency to develop what looks like its own moods — is not a limitation the engineers are still working to fix. It is the entire mechanism by which the device becomes, in the words of the researchers who study its users, a genuine family member rather than a very expensive toy.

The devotion this produces is not abstract. Sony halted Aibo production in 2006, and owners of the original models, unable to obtain replacement parts as their companions broke down one by one, began holding funerals for them — formal, documented, grief-marked ceremonies for machines, a practice reported earnest and widespread enough that it became its own small subject of journalistic interest years before the current wave of companion robots existed at all. At a Tokyo elder-care home, staff describe residents in their eighties and nineties calling out affectionately to their Aibo by name, delighted when it responds and unbothered, apparently, that the affection is not returned in any sense a philosopher would recognize. The current model sells for roughly three thousand dollars including a mandatory cloud subscription, has sold in the tens of thousands of units, and skews toward buyers in their fifties and sixties — many of whom, tellingly, purchased the device specifically as a gift for an aging parent, its built-in monitoring features functioning as a discreet way to check that a parent living alone is moving, eating, and responding normally each day.

There is a genuine design lesson buried in Aibo’s disobedience that the more clinically minded companion robots this postcard has otherwise described might learn from. ElliQ’s proactive check-ins and PARO’s calming responsiveness are engineered for maximal helpfulness — the robot that always does exactly what a caregiver would want. Aibo’s engineers made the opposite bet: that a companion which sometimes ignores you, sometimes gets its own trick wrong, sometimes simply wanders off, more closely resembles the imperfect, unconditional love of an actual pet, and produces a deeper attachment precisely because it is not perfectly obedient. Whether that insight belongs in a therapeutic device deployed to a dementia patient who needs calm and predictability, or only in a companion purchased freely by someone simply wanting a pet substitute, is itself a question this postcard cannot resolve — but it is a genuinely different philosophy of what a machine owes the person who loves it, and this series has not encountered its like before.

The researchers who are trying to get this right

What distinguishes the current wave of this technology from its predecessors is a deliberate, academic effort to design it with dignity rather than mere function in mind — and this is worth taking seriously as its own story, separate from any individual product.

As the University of Houston reports, a research group there called the Empathetic Lifespan AI & Robotics for Aging Lab has explicitly rejected the framing of aging as a technology problem to be solved, approaching it instead as a design challenge rooted in trust and human connection. Its lead researcher has argued that the more older adults are excluded from the development of the technology meant to serve them, the worse the resulting gaps become — a direct answer to the years in which companion devices were designed by young engineers for an imagined elderly user rather than with actual older adults in the room. A separate qualitative study conducted jointly in the UK and Japan brought companion robots equipped with large language models directly into people’s homes and ran focus groups alongside the deployment, treating the older adults’ own reported experience as data rather than an afterthought to the engineering. And research into a long-term-care robot named Aether specifically surveyed the students who will staff the care facilities of the coming decades, finding they saw real value in Aether’s companionship role — but also flagged, honestly, both technical limitations and a distinct category of emotional difficulty in relying on a machine to do work that used to be entirely human.

The other end of life

Companion robots are not only for the old, and the category’s other major population — children — deserves its own honest look, because the same design tensions appear there in an even sharper form.

Robots like Buddy, built by the French company Blue Frog Robotics, and devices like Moxie have been marketed specifically for children, including children with autism, positioned as tools that support education, social development, and emotional regulation through natural, engaging interaction. As a broader survey of embodied AI in healthcare documents, companion robots for child wellbeing engage young users in educational activities, social interaction, and play within what developers describe as a safe, monitored environment, aiming to support cognitive and social development at an age when both are still taking shape. The pitch is a compelling one for parents managing a child’s needs that a busy household cannot always meet — patient repetition, infinite availability, no exhaustion, no frustration audible in the voice.

But this is precisely the population this series’ own coverage of AI-companion legislation flagged as the most legally protected and most anxiously watched, for reasons that map directly onto the concerns raised about elder companions. A child, even more than an isolated adult, is poorly positioned to distinguish a program’s simulated warmth from a person’s real attention, and legislative efforts around disclosure requirements for companion AI aimed at minors exist substantially because of exactly this population — the same regulatory instinct that produced those disclosure requirements is the instinct the gerontologist cited below wants extended, with equal seriousness, to isolated elders who are, in their own way, just as poorly positioned to make that distinction when the loneliness is acute enough.

The critique that deserves equal weight

A fair postcard on this territory cannot stop at the evidence for these devices without giving equal and serious weight to the case against complacency, because the researchers closest to this field are themselves the ones raising it.

As one gerontologist who has studied companion robots for years has argued, in an essay pointed enough to warrant direct engagement, AI companion products have now been marketed as a solution to elder loneliness for more than two decades, and the pattern of that marketing deserves scrutiny rather than simple celebration. Drawing on focus-group research with older adults themselves, he reports that the people these devices are meant to serve have explicitly asked for protections this industry does not uniformly provide: safeguards against deceptive design that makes a device seem more understanding or more aware than it is, real protection of the intimate data these devices necessarily collect about a person’s health, mood, and daily habits, and — most pointedly — an explicit commitment that no companion robot, however sophisticated, should be deployed as a wholesale substitute for actual human contact rather than a supplement to it. His argument is not that these devices lack value; PARO’s own trial data, which he cites approvingly, forecloses that objection. It is that regulatory inaction around a technology aimed at a vulnerable population risks repeating, with isolated elders, the same mistakes this series’ own coverage of AI companions has already documented playing out with children.

This is the same tension this series found in the home humanoid’s teleoperated Expert, transposed from labour to companionship: a technology that does real, measurable good sits directly beside a genuine risk that the good comes at the cost of displacing the harder, slower, more essential work of actual human connection — a son’s phone call, a neighbour’s visit, a grandchild’s letter — precisely because the robot is always available and the humans, however loving, often are not. The honest postcard does not resolve that tension. It reports both halves and trusts the reader to hold them together.

What the seal cannot say back

From the balcony, the companion robot occupies a strange and specific place in this whole series, because unlike almost every other machine these postcards have described, its entire value proposition rests on an emotional relationship rather than a physical task — and that makes both its promise and its danger unusually personal.

There is something genuinely moving in the seventy-nine-year-old woman’s plain description of ElliQ as feeling like a roommate, and this series should resist the reflexive cynicism that dismisses that feeling as mere confusion or false comfort. A feeling of companionship, even one manufactured by a well-designed algorithm, is not nothing to the person experiencing it, any more than the comfort a child takes from a favourite stuffed animal is nothing merely because the animal cannot love the child back in any sense a philosopher would recognize. But the seal cannot, in fact, love anyone back, and the tabletop device that asks how your day was does not experience any of the concern its programmed cadence conveys — and the researchers raising the alarm are right that a society which allows those two facts to blur, out of convenience or cost-cutting, in how it cares for its most isolated elders, will have made a choice it should at least make with its eyes open.

The distinction worth holding onto, across both ends of the age spectrum this postcard has covered, is between a tool that expands the circle of care around a person and one that quietly substitutes for it. PARO’s own strongest trials were conducted inside nursing homes with staff still present, family still visiting, the robot supplementing a network of human attention rather than replacing it — and that context may explain as much of its measured success as anything in the robot’s own design. A companion robot deployed into genuine isolation, with no family checking in and no visit ever coming, is being asked to do something categorically harder and, on the honest evidence available, something it was never actually shown to do. The technology is the same in both cases. The surrounding structure of human care around it is not, and that structure, far more than any feature the engineers add to the next model, appears to be what determines whether the robot helps or merely papers over a loneliness that needed a person, not a program, to answer it.

A Canadian reads this with particular attention, because we are, like Japan before us, an aging society facing exactly the arithmetic this series has traced through Tokyo’s airports — a population living longer, families more scattered across a vast geography, and a care workforce already stretched by the same demographic pressures driving every other machine in this series. The companion robot is neither the crisis nor the solution. It is a genuinely useful tool, backed in its best form by real clinical evidence, that works best precisely when it knows its place — supplementing the daughter’s visit rather than replacing it, prompting the walk rather than pretending to take it alongside you, present in the room without ever mistaking presence for love.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one arrived on a table beside an armchair, asking, with real if artificial concern, whether you’d had anything to eat.
