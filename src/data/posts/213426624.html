Part XIV of this series left Elon Musk in a somewhat unflattering pose. It was August 6, 2026, and I had just finished cataloguing a SpaceX share price down roughly half from its IPO high, a personal net worth that had slid to $777 billion (a figure that would have seemed like satire six years ago and now merely seems like Tuesday), and an earnings call in which Musk promised orbital AI data centres by 2027 and a trillion dollars of SpaceX revenue by 2030 — the sort of number that invites the listener to check whether the speaker has confused SpaceX with a small planetary economy.

I mention this because the contrast with the seven days beginning August 20, 2026 is instructive. It is the difference between a man narrating a vision and a man pouring concrete.

The week, in order

On August 20, Nevada regulators approved Tesla for up to 5,000 robotaxis across all of Clark County, a considerable widening from the earlier Strip-only pilot with its 45-mile-per-hour leash. On August 25, SpaceX and Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry announced Starbase Louisiana — a second Starship spaceport on some 125,000 acres of Vermilion Parish coastline, described by the company itself as a $100 billion investment, ending what SpaceX called a seven-year search for a site capable of supporting Starship launches at true industrial rate. On August 26, Tesla’s robotaxi account posted that the service now runs 6 a.m. to 10 p.m., seven days a week, across six cities, and that the “unsupervised fleet is a lot bigger” — a phrase doing a great deal of unspecified work, in the way that corporate communications departments have perfected the art of saying something without saying anything a lawyer might later regret. Also that week: invitations went out for a Cybercab launch event at Giga Austin on September 3; Tesla confirmed a Semi factory inauguration event at Gigafactory Nevada for September 24; drone footage showed the Optimus factory at Giga Texas rising from foundation to structural steel at a pace that, if it holds, would put it in the same league as the Shanghai gigafactory’s famously compressed build time; and JPMorgan reiterated its overweight rating on SpaceX with a $240 price target, which is analyst-speak for “we still believe.”

Six items. Seven days. Two companies. One person’s name attached to all of them.

The steel-and-acreage argument

The case being made — and it is being made loudly across the ecosystem of channels that cover Musk’s ventures with the fervour normally reserved for royal weddings — runs roughly like this: talk is free, and a company can hype a product demonstration indefinitely without ever shipping it. Steel, acreage, hiring maps, and launch dates cost real money, and companies do not spend real money on things they do not believe in. By that logic, a week in which five separate ventures all crossed from promise into construction simultaneously is not noise. It is a signal that something has structurally changed — that years of separately slow-moving projects (Full Self-Driving’s roughly decade-long grind before it could carry a single paying, unmonitored passenger; Starlink’s multi-year run of losses before its economics turned; Cybercab’s need for an actual live robotaxi service to plug into) have quietly reached the point where each one now accelerates the others. Starlink’s revenue, on this theory, is what lets SpaceX commit $100 billion to Louisiana without flinching. Cybercab needed a functioning robotaxi network to join, and the network needed a purpose-built vehicle to make the unit economics work; this week, the theory goes, the two finally met.

It is an elegant argument, and I want to be fair to it before I start picking at its seams, because the seams are worth picking at.

The seams

The first is the calendar itself. Elon Musk has a documented, occasionally spectacular relationship with dates. A coast-to-coast Full Self-Driving demonstration was promised for 2017 and did not happen. A million robotaxis were promised by 2020 and did not happen — not in 2020, not for several years after. First launch at Starbase Louisiana is targeted for 2029, which is a target, and targets, in this particular ledger, have a historical tendency to slip. The Louisiana Economic Development office’s own materials are, notably, more conservative than Musk’s public framing, describing “initial operations” arriving closer to 2030 than 2029 — a small gap, but the kind of small gap that tends to widen once concrete actually needs pouring in a Gulf Coast wetland subject to permitting, environmental review, and hurricane season.

The second seam is the jobs number, which varies depending on who is doing the counting. The state’s own release cites 3,000 direct jobs over ten years. Musk, in the same news cycle, said the real figure would end up “a lot higher.” A third figure — 8,100 direct jobs, separate again from an unspecified count of indirect employment — circulated in the same commentary. None of this is necessarily dishonest; jobs projections for decade-long infrastructure builds are notoriously elastic, and everyone involved has an incentive to round toward the number that flatters their announcement. But a reader doing due diligence on this story should notice that “how many jobs” produced at least three different answers within the same week, from people standing at the same podium.

The third seam, and the one I find most interesting from north of the border, is the propellant logistics argument, because it is the rare piece of this story that is genuinely, verifiably an engineering constraint rather than a marketing one. At a launch cadence approaching a thousand flights a year, trucking in propellant commercially would require, by one circulating estimate, something in the neighbourhood of a truck arriving every ninety seconds around the clock. That is not a supply chain; that is a traffic jam with rocket fuel in it. SpaceX’s answer — building oxygen and propellant production on-site, fed by the same natural gas infrastructure that has serviced Gulf Coast oil and gas operations since the 1950s — is a real solution to a real problem, and it is the part of this announcement that reads least like theatre. Whatever else is true about Starbase Louisiana, somebody did the arithmetic on how you actually keep thirty rockets a day fed, and building your own propellant plant is a more serious answer than most megaproject announcements bother to supply.

The Vegas footnote

While Louisiana absorbed most of the week’s attention, Clark County quietly approved nineteen additional Boring Company Loop stations, bringing the approved network under Las Vegas to 123 stations. It is worth being precise about what that number does and does not mean. The Teslas running through those tunnels today carry human safety drivers; the service is not, whatever the marketing implies, actually driverless yet. But Tesla now separately holds that Nevada permit covering up to 5,000 robotaxis across the county, and a tunnel network with 123 approved stations sitting underneath a permit for 5,000 driverless vehicles is the kind of infrastructure stacking that tends not to matter until, one day, it very suddenly does. Nobody was covering this angle particularly closely this week. I suspect that will change.

The Cybertruck detail nobody is bragging about

Buried in the same news cycle, almost as an afterthought, Tesla raised Cybertruck prices by as much as 7 percent, with the dual-motor variant climbing five thousand dollars to $74,990. Companies raise prices when demand outstrips supply, or they raise prices because input costs have gone up and they can no longer absorb the difference. After two straight years of Tesla cutting prices across its lineup, a raise is the kind of small, unglamorous data point that a company’s own hype cycle has no interest in explaining, and which is therefore worth pausing on. It could be read either way — confidence in demand, or the quieter admission that tariffs and input costs have simply won this round. I do not think anyone currently celebrating Starbase Louisiana has stopped to ask which one it is, which tells you something about which details get amplified and which get left in the footnotes.

The analyst tell

JPMorgan’s reiterated overweight rating on SpaceX, with its $240 target, is worth a moment’s separate attention, because analyst notes are a genre with their own tics. “Overweight” is not the same claim as “this will work” — it is the claim that, priced correctly for risk, the stock is worth more than the market currently says. An upgrade timed to land in the same week as a $100 billion spaceport announcement is not proof that the spaceport will hit its 2029 target. It is evidence that professional capital allocators, whose job depends on being right rather than being entertained, are pricing in a higher probability that at least some of this stack of promises converts into revenue. That is a narrower and more useful claim than “Wall Street believes in Musk,” and it is the one this series should actually be tracking.

What this is actually evidence of

Here is where I part company slightly with the breathless version of this thesis, and where fourteen previous instalments of this series may be of some use. This is not the first time Musk’s ventures have looked, briefly, as though several long-gestating projects were converging. It happened around the Boring Company’s early tunnel announcements, most of which have still not produced anything resembling the promised network outside Las Vegas. It happened around the original Full Self-Driving “feature complete” claims, which turned out to describe a beta years away from unsupervised operation. It happens, with some regularity, whenever a stock price needs a jolt — and SpaceX’s stock, recall, had fallen below its IPO price as recently as August 19, per the tracking this series has kept since Part IX. A week that produces a robotaxi hours expansion, a Nevada permit, a spaceport announcement, two launch-event dates, a tunnel-network expansion, and an analyst upgrade, all inside seven days, is either a genuine inflection point or a remarkably well-timed public-relations offensive arriving precisely when the share price needed one. I would not insult the reader by pretending those two explanations are mutually exclusive. Companies can build real things and manage a news cycle at the same time; in fact, that is more or less the entire discipline of investor relations, and Musk’s ventures have never been shy about practising it.

What is undeniably true, and what makes this instalment worth writing regardless of which explanation you favour, is that the checkpoints are now dated and public in a way the earlier promises were not. September 3 either produces a working Cybercab service or it does not. September 24 either produces trucks rolling off a Nevada line or it does not. Louisiana’s permitting record will either show construction beginning on schedule or it will show the familiar slippage. For a series that has spent fourteen parts tracking the concentration of Musk’s power across an increasingly interlocking set of companies, the useful thing about a convergence week is not that it proves the thesis. It is that it finally gives the thesis a set of dates by which it can be checked — and a Canadian outsider, watching from a country that once handed this man his first passport, intends to keep checking.
