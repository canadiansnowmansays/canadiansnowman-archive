By Stephen Gordon for CanadianSnowmanSays May, 2026

In Part 14, I documented the moment confabulation went public — April 29, when the President of the United States merged two active wars on camera, reciting Iran’s military destruction as though it described Ukraine, and stating that Ukraine’s currency “has no name.”

Confabulation, I wrote, is not lying. It is the brain generating plausible-seeming content to cover what it can no longer reliably retrieve. The person experiencing it believes they are remembering.

Forty-eight hours later, the president stood before an audience at The Villages — the world’s largest retirement community — and bragged that he could identify a bear.

He has been bragging about it ever since.

The Fifth Category

This series has tracked four categories of observable behaviour:

Disinhibition — the progressive removal of the internal governor that separates impulse from action. Compulsion — unable to stop, posting until 4 AM. Grandiosity — claiming to have motivated the Holy Spirit, posting AI images of himself as Jesus Christ. Confabulation — the confident, unconscious substitution of one reality for another.

Part 15 introduces a fifth: anosognosia.

anosognosia (n.) /əˌnɒsɒɡˈnəʊziə/ A neurological condition in which a person is unaware of their own impairment. Not denial — which implies knowledge of what is being denied — but a genuine inability to perceive one’s own cognitive dysfunction. Most commonly associated with damage to the frontal lobes, stroke, and neurodegenerative conditions including frontotemporal dementia.

A person in denial knows something is wrong and refuses to acknowledge it. A person with anosognosia does not know something is wrong. The distinction matters because denial can be overcome with evidence. Anosognosia cannot.

What happened between April 29 and May 5 is the most complete public demonstration of anosognosia this series has documented.

May 1: The Villages

Two days after confabulating two wars on live television, President Trump appeared at a rally at The Villages, Florida. (The Daily Beast: Donald Trump, 79, Flexes Cognitive Skills With Bonkers Bear Brag)

He did not address the confabulation. No correction was issued. No clarification was offered. Instead, the President of the United States told a crowd of retirees that he had taken three cognitive tests and “aced all of them.”

“I’m the only president to take a cognitive test,” he said. “I don’t think Obama could pass it.”

He then walked the audience through the test:

“You know, the first question is very easy. It’s a lion, a giraffe, a bear, and a shark. They say, ‘Which one is the bear?’”

He paused. The crowd cheered.

“Very standard test, but it gets really tough in the last ten questions.”

He then insisted that all presidents and vice presidents should be required to take cognitive exams before running for office — adding that if this requirement had been in place, “we wouldn’t be surprised at people like Barack ‘Hussein’ Obama, or Sleepy Joe Biden, getting ‘ELECTED.’”

Let the sequence of events sit for a moment.

April 29: cannot distinguish between two active wars he is simultaneously commanding. May 1: brags about identifying a bear on a test.

May 5: The Squirrel

Four days later, at the White House Small Business Summit — an event convened to discuss economic policy — the president returned to the subject.

“The first question is very easy,” he told a room of small business owners. “You have a lion, a bear, an alligator, and a, what’s another good..? A squirrel. They say, ‘Name the animals.’ And everybody says ‘Oooooh.’”

He claimed a doctor had told him it was the first time they had seen a perfect score.

He has now described this test at least three times in public over two weeks, each time as evidence of his fitness to serve as president. Each time, his description of the test — which one is the bear, name the animals — is presented as though it demonstrates remarkable cognitive achievement.

It does not.

What the Test Actually Is

Sample from the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) naming section. Patients are asked to identify three simple drawings (lion, rhinoceros, camel). This easiest portion of the 30-point dementia screening tool is what President Trump has repeatedly bragged about ‘acing’

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment — the MoCA — is a 30-point screening tool designed to detect early signs of Alzheimer’s disease and dementia. It is not an intelligence test. It is not an aptitude exam. It does not measure fitness for office. It measures whether there are signs that something is going wrong.

The animal identification portion asks patients to name three drawings. It is worth three of thirty points. It is the easiest section of the test — the warm-up, the portion designed to establish whether the patient can engage with the assessment at all.

The harder sections — the ones Trump refers to as “really tough in the last ten” — test delayed recall, serial subtraction, verbal fluency, abstraction, and orientation to time and place. These are the sections designed to detect the kind of impairment that would cause a person to, say, confuse two active wars or state that a country’s currency “has no name.”

Dr. Henry David Abraham, professor of psychiatry emeritus at Tufts University School of Medicine — one of the 36 physicians who, on April 30, signed the statement entered into the Congressional Record — offered a clinical analogy that deserves to be quoted in full: (The Daily Beast: Expert Warns Trump, 79, Over Brag He Aced Three Cognitive Tests):

“If you have one MoCA, that’s like taking your temperature. If a temperature is okay, you don’t come back every 10 minutes and take another temperature.”

The president has taken three MoCAs.

In clinical practice, repeated cognitive screening at short intervals is itself a diagnostic indicator. It suggests either that clinicians have ongoing concerns warranting continued monitoring, or that the patient is exhibiting symptoms that make reassessment necessary. Healthy patients do not take the same dementia screening three times. It is not a test you retake to prove you are smart. It is a test you retake when someone is worried.

Trump’s repeated boasting about the MoCA — “aced all three” — is not evidence of fitness. It is evidence that his physicians have screened him for dementia at least three times, and that he does not understand what that means.

That is anosognosia. Not denial. Not spin. A genuine inability to perceive that the test he is celebrating is the test designed to detect the condition he is exhibiting.

The Record Entered on April 30

Between the confabulation on April 29 and the bear brag on May 1, something happened that the President appears not to have registered.

On April 30, 2026, thirty-six physicians — neurologists, forensic psychiatrists, general psychiatrists, and other medical professionals with expertise in diagnosing cognitive disorders and evaluating dangerousness — issued a formal statement calling for the President’s immediate, lawful removal from office for medical reasons.

The statement was entered into the Congressional Record, Volume 172, Number 76, by Senators Sheldon Whitehouse and Jack Reed of Rhode Island. (U.S. Congress: Medical Concerns About President Donald J. Trump and His Fitness for Office)

This was not a press conference. It was not an op-ed. It was a formal medical assessment by credentialed professionals, submitted to the permanent legislative record of the United States Congress.

Their language was unequivocal:

“It is our professional opinion that the behaviors of Donald Trump, tragically, are neither momentary lapses nor political theater. It is our professional opinion that they reflect a rapidly worsening, reality-untethered, increasingly dangerous decline.”

They continued:

“If we were called upon under the 25th Amendment to judge the President’s present ability to discharge the duties of his office, we would have to conclude that he lacks the capacity to do so.”

They specifically raised the question of nuclear authority — whether Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Secretary of State Marco Rubio would be willing to remove the nuclear codes from the president’s control.

Thirty-six doctors. The Congressional Record. A call for removal. A direct question about who controls the nuclear arsenal.

And the next day, the president went to Florida’s largest retirement community and bragged about identifying a bear.

The Polling Has Crossed

This series began in June 2025, when the question of Trump’s cognitive fitness was confined to cable news segments and Twitter threads. Lawrence O’Donnell could ask “If Donald Trump gets dementia, how will we know?” and the question itself was considered provocative.

It is no longer provocative. It is now a majority position.

By February 2026, a Reuters-Ipsos poll found that 61 percent of Americans agreed Trump had “become erratic with age” — including 30 percent of Republicans. A Washington Post-ABC News-Ipsos survey found 56 percent doubted his mental sharpness and 51 percent questioned his physical health. (CNN: Polls Show Concerns About Trump's Mental Acuity Approaching Same Levels as Biden)

By April, YouGov/Economist found 48 percent of Americans saying Trump is suffering cognitive decline — 35 percent significant, 13 percent modest.

By May, a Washington Post-ABC News-Ipsos poll found 55 percent of Americans do not believe Trump is in good enough physical health to serve effectively. Multiple polls now show a majority doubting his mental fitness.

The trajectory is approaching the numbers that ended Joe Biden’s candidacy. Biden faced roughly 54 percent doubting his mental sharpness in February 2022. Trump is now in that range — with the critical difference that Biden was a candidate who could be replaced. Trump is a sitting president who can only be removed by constitutional mechanism.

The Congress That Stopped Asking

And then there is what happened on Capitol Hill this week — the development that prompted this essay.

Members of the House of Representatives were asked by reporters whether the president’s cognitive decline would affect his trip to China. Note the framing: not whether there is decline. Whether it would affect the trip.

Congressman Pat Ryan:

“It’s a huge factor. I mean, we’re in the midst of a war. He’s negotiating with China right now. And we can’t be sure if he’s not falling asleep in the middle of these meetings. We should all be very concerned.”

An unnamed congresswoman:

“I don’t trust that man to be able to cognitively make a complete sentence. Let alone negotiate with China, who’s so clearly three steps ahead of us right now.”

Representative McBride, asked about Trump’s cognitive decline and the China trip:

“Well, he wasn’t starting from a high bar.”

Read those responses carefully. These are not diagnostic opinions. These are not medical assessments. These are elected members of the United States Congress, on the record, planning around the assumption that the president cannot stay conscious during international negotiations.

They are not debating the baseline problem. They are operating within it.

The shift from “is there cognitive decline?” to “how does the cognitive decline affect our China strategy?” is the shift from diagnosis to prognosis. The debate is over. The question is now: what happens next?

The Deepfake Problem

One additional development warrants documentation because it represents a new complication for accountability.

On May 11, a video of Trump appearing to fall asleep during a White House maternal health event went viral within hours. This was not the first such incident — since taking office, Trump has been documented falling asleep roughly a dozen times in public, including at Cabinet meetings, at the U.S. Open, during his own military parade, while meeting foreign leaders, and during the Pope’s funeral. In December 2025 alone, he dozed off at least four times. (The New Republic: Cognitive Decline? These Were Trump's 11 Most Senile Moments This Year)

But the May 11 video immediately generated a familiar cycle: is it real, or is it AI-generated?

Previous “Trump falling asleep” videos have been confirmed as deepfakes — AI-edited footage designed to exaggerate or fabricate incidents. The existence of those fakes now provides cover for every real incident. (Snopes: Did Trump Fall Asleep During Meeting? Separating Satire from Real Footage) Each authentic video of the president losing consciousness in public is immediately contestable: “It’s AI.” “It’s manipulated.” “You can’t trust video anymore.”

This is a new dimension of the baseline problem that did not exist when this series began. The very technology that Trump himself uses — the AI-generated image of himself as Jesus Christ, the AI mockups of himself on the $100 bill — now functions as a shield against documentation of his decline. Real footage becomes deniable because fake footage exists.

The deepfake problem does not change the underlying clinical reality. It changes the evidentiary landscape. And in a political environment where the 25th Amendment requires a “clear and unmistakable triggering event,” the ability to contest every piece of visual evidence may be the single greatest barrier to constitutional action.

May 26

The White House has confirmed that the president will undergo his annual physical at Walter Reed National Military Medical Center on May 26 — nine days from now.

His last full physical was April 2025, when White House physician Captain Sean Barbabella described him as being in “excellent health” and “fully fit” to serve. A follow-up visit in October 2025 — initially called “routine” before being re-branded a “semiannual physical” — included a CT scan that showed no cardiovascular abnormalities.

Representative Raskin’s April 10 letter demanding a comprehensive cognitive assessment and public release of results by April 24 was ignored. The White House has not confirmed whether any cognitive testing will be included in the May 26 exam, nor whether results will be made public.

Dr. Jonathan Reiner — cardiologist to former Vice President Dick Cheney, and a recurring voice in this series — has called for Congress to pass legislation requiring the White House physician to periodically certify a president’s fitness for office through annual physical, cognitive, and psychiatric evaluations.

No such legislation exists.

The May 26 physical will occur nine days after the China trip. The question of what it will reveal — and whether the public will ever see the results — is the immediate horizon of this story.

But there is a deeper problem, a structural one, that this series has been circling for fifteen installments without naming directly.

The Body That Was Never Built

The question I keep hearing — from readers, from colleagues, from my own family watching from Canada — is always the same: why can’t someone just get a second opinion?

The answer is that the American constitutional system is designed so that no one can.

Captain Sean Barbabella is a military physician assigned to the president. He serves at the president’s pleasure. His patient is the president. His medical reports are released — or not released — at the president’s discretion. When he writes “excellent health” and “fully fit,” there is no mechanism to challenge that assessment. No appeals process. No independent review. No second opinion. HIPAA protections apply to the president’s medical records the same as anyone else’s. Congress can ask. It cannot compel.

This was not supposed to be the case.

Section 4 of the 25th Amendment — ratified in 1967 — explicitly anticipated the problem. It provides two paths to declare a president unable to discharge his duties: the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet, or the vice president and a majority of “such other body as Congress may by law provide.”

Read that again. The Constitution told Congress to build an independent body — a commission, a panel, a mechanism outside the president’s own appointees — that could assess presidential capacity. The founders of the amendment understood that a Cabinet selected by the president for loyalty might not be willing to act against him. They provided a constitutional solution.

Congress never built it. In fifty-eight years, no Congress has established the “other body” that the 25th Amendment calls for. The escape hatch was written into the Constitution. No one installed the door.

On April 14, Jamie Raskin introduced a bill to finally do what the 25th Amendment has been waiting fifty-eight years for someone to do. (House Judiciary Committee Democrats: Ranking Member Raskin Introduces Legislation Establishing Independent Commission on Presidential Capacity) The Commission on Presidential Capacity would be a 17-member body: congressional leadership from both parties would each select four physicians and four psychiatrists. If requested by Congress, the commission would carry out a medical examination of the president. Fifty House Democrats signed on as co-sponsors.

It has zero chance of passing the current Congress.

Consider the irony. The same Republicans who subpoenaed Biden’s White House physician, who declared Biden’s cognitive decline “one of the greatest scandals in our nation’s history,” who had Chairman Jordan declare that a president who is not cognitively fit “isn’t fit for office” — those same Republicans will not vote to create the independent body that the 25th Amendment explicitly told them to create.

They built the oversight framework for Biden. They will not apply it to Trump.

Meanwhile, every American service member must pass a fitness-for-duty exam before serving. Those who handle nuclear weapons undergo an especially rigorous screening process, updated annually. The commander-in-chief — the person who orders the use of those weapons — is subject to no equivalent requirement. A lieutenant assigned to a missile silo undergoes more rigorous cognitive screening than the man who tells him to turn the key.

The 36 doctors who signed the Congressional Record statement are exactly the kind of professionals that courts routinely rely on for capacity evaluations. Forensic mental health specialists — independent of the interested parties, not engaged in treatment with the person being evaluated — are standard in legal proceedings across the country. Judges order them. Employers request them. The military requires them. But there is no mechanism to require the president to submit to one.

Three proposals are now on the table. The Raskin Commission: an independent body under the 25th Amendment. The Reiner certification mandate: requiring the existing White House physician to formally certify fitness through annual cognitive and psychiatric evaluations. And the broader academic proposal, championed by scholars of presidential fitness, for independent fitness-for-duty exams modeled on military requirements.

All three are dead on arrival. And even in the most optimistic Democratic scenario, they would remain so for this president.

Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-MD) introduced H.R. 8275 in April 2026 to create the independent Commission on Presidential Capacity called for (but never established) in Section 4 of the 25th Amendment.

Suppose the midterms deliver both chambers to the Democrats. The Raskin Commission bill passes the House and Senate. It then lands on the desk of the president it is designed to evaluate. He vetoes it. Override requires two-thirds of both chambers — a supermajority that does not exist in any foreseeable political configuration.

And even if, by some constitutional miracle, the commission were created and conducted its evaluation and found Trump unfit — Section 4 still requires the vice president’s concurrence to act on the finding. JD Vance would have to sign the declaration. The same Vance who has shown no inclination to break with the president on any matter of substance. The same Vance who would assume the presidency if the declaration succeeded — and who would have every political incentive to let the process play out on its own calendar rather than trigger a constitutional crisis that makes him look like a usurper.

Five gates, every one of them locked: pass the bill, survive the veto, override with two-thirds, conduct the evaluation, and persuade the vice president to sign. The mechanism was designed to be used. It was also designed in a way that makes it almost impossible to deploy against a sitting president who does not want to be evaluated.

The fire alarm has no wiring. And even if you wired it, the person it is meant to protect against holds the circuit breaker.

The only scenario in which the Raskin Commission has real teeth is if it is established prospectively — built now for the next president, the way it should have been built in 1967. Which requires a political moment in which neither party feels threatened by it. Which is to say: never.

The system doesn’t just lack the will to act. It lacks the structure to act. The physician who could report the truth works for the patient. The Cabinet that could invoke the amendment was selected for loyalty. The commission that could provide independence does not exist because the Congress that should have created it fifty-eight years ago never bothered — and the Congress that is being asked to create it now refuses because the president it would evaluate is their own. And even a future Congress that tried would find the veto pen and the vice president’s signature standing between the blueprint and the building.

Conclusion

If the president can confabulate two wars on camera, brag about identifying a bear the next day, and continue to hold sole authority over 5,000 nuclear warheads while 36 doctors call for his removal in the Congressional Record — and nothing changes — then the baseline problem is no longer a clinical observation.

It is a constitutional verdict.

The system has seen the evidence. The system has heard the doctors. The system has polled the public. The system has watched Congress plan around the decline.

The system has decided that none of this is sufficient to act.

And the reason is now clear: the system was never finished. The blueprint sits in the Constitution. The wiring was never installed. The circuit breaker belongs to the patient.

The president turns 80 on June 14.

His physical is May 26.

His doctor works for him.

And the man at the center of it all stands at a podium in Florida’s largest retirement community, tells a cheering crowd that he can identify a bear, and does not know that the test he is celebrating is the test designed to detect the condition he is exhibiting.

That is the baseline problem in its final form: not that we can’t detect the decline, but that detection was never the obstacle. The obstacle is that detection without a mechanism is observation without consequence — and fifty-eight years of constitutional neglect has ensured that observation is all we have.
