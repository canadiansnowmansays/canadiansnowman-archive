This series has spent eighty-seven parts documenting AI’s harms, contradictions, and governance failures largely from the outside — as a chronicle of what institutions did and didn’t do, what companies said and didn’t deliver. Part 88 covers something different: the first serious attempt this series has tracked to organise the diffuse, well-documented public anger into an actual political movement, built by people who already know how to run one.

As Matteo Wong reported for The Atlantic on August 28, 2026, a new organisation called Irreplaceable launched that day, explicitly modelled on the climate movement’s organising playbook and staffed by veterans of it. The name is a deliberate thesis statement — people are irreplaceable by machines — and the group’s founding logic is that the sprawling, often contradictory public anxiety this series has documented in its Capex Ledger companion thread (the 71 percent opposition to local data centres, the cross-partisan recall elections, the Gen Z activists this series covered in Capex Ledger Part XII) needs an organising structure to become anything more than background noise.

The Numbers Behind the Premise

Wong opens with a polling picture that should be familiar to readers of this series’ data-centre coverage: roughly three-quarters of Americans oppose a data centre being built in their area, three-quarters believe AI threatens their job security, and close to 70 percent think the technology is advancing too quickly. What Irreplaceable’s founders are betting on is that this genuine, widespread anxiety remains politically inert without an organisation translating it into sustained pressure — the same argument this series has made implicitly every time it documented a company or an administration absorbing criticism without changing course.

Irreplaceable’s executive director, Phil Aroneanu, co-founded the climate group 350.org, and campaign strategist Jeremy Ornstein previously organised with the Sunrise Movement. Their shared diagnosis of climate advocacy’s early failure is the intellectual foundation for Irreplaceable’s entire approach: Aroneanu told Wong that climate advocates spent years acting as policy wonks, trusting that evidence-based arguments about melting ice sheets would move receptive legislators, and it didn’t work. What eventually built power, in his account, was emotional and cultural organising — fossil-fuel divestment campaigns, pipeline protests, Greta Thunberg’s school strikes — rather than white papers.

Ornstein’s summary of the AI equivalent is blunt: “People get it. People have the intuition that if we create machines more powerful than people, that could go dangerously and terribly wrong.” Irreplaceable is explicitly choosing to organise around that intuition first and build policy specifics later, a sequencing decision this essay will return to as its central point of vulnerability.

The March That Learned From Its Own Mistakes

The clearest evidence Irreplaceable’s organisers know what they’re doing operationally is the contrast Wong draws between two demonstrations months apart. Earlier in 2026, “Stop the AI Race” organisers staged what Wong describes as a somewhat disorganized protest, where attendees struggled to chant in unison. By July 2026, with Ornstein advising, the same underlying coalition produced what was likely the largest anti-AI demonstration in San Francisco’s history — a march from OpenAI’s headquarters to Anthropic’s to Google’s, complete with chant sheets, a marching band, and a slushy truck deployed specifically to “freeze” AI.

That level of production values is not incidental. It is the visible signature of professional movement-building infrastructure being applied to a cause that, when this series began in January 2026, was still operating at the level of scattered local protests this series covered as isolated incidents rather than a coordinated campaign.

The overlap with existing climate infrastructure is both practical and thematic, and worth taking seriously as a force multiplier this series has not previously accounted for. The NAACP’s environmental-justice arm, the Sierra Club, and Earthjustice are already supporting local data-centre fights with lawsuits, model legislation, and organising support — the same groups whose organising muscle helped pass climate legislation are now redirecting a meaningful share of that capacity toward data centres, partly because the two fights share concrete overlap (data centres burn fossil fuels and strain electrical grids) and partly because both target the same underlying grievance: corporate power operating with minimal public accountability. Wong notes tangible local wins already accumulating from this redirected pressure — dozens of cancelled data centre projects, and every major AI company now publicly promising not to pass increased electricity costs onto local ratepayers, the same Ratepayer Protection Pledge language this series’ Capex Ledger thread already traced through Florida, Texas, and the NRSC’s own internal Ohio messaging strategy.

The Michigan Connection

Wong’s reporting surfaces a direct, named overlap with this series’ own coverage: William Lawrence, the Michigan progressive activist this series covered in Capex Ledger Part XI after he won a Democratic primary running against data centres and immediately turned the issue on Republican incumbent Tom Barrett, is a co-founder of the Sunrise Movement — the same organisation Ornstein came from. Abdul El-Sayed’s primary win in the same state, also built substantially on anti-data-centre messaging, confirms this is not an isolated candidate strategy but an emerging pattern with actual institutional roots in climate organising infrastructure.

This series treated Lawrence’s Michigan race as a discrete data point about cross-ideological coalition-building when it appeared in Part XI. Wong’s reporting reframes it as one visible node in a much larger, more deliberately constructed network this series had not yet fully mapped.

The Deliberate Choice to Polarize “Down-Up”

Aroneanu’s most analytically interesting move is his explicit framing of Irreplaceable’s target polarisation as vertical rather than horizontal — not left versus right, but what he calls “down-up.” Caroline Behringer, a communications strategist who worked on Obama’s Climate Action Plan and now runs the AI-focused polling firm Athena Insights, offers the clearest articulation of why this framing might actually fit the underlying sentiment better than a conventional left-right frame: opinions on specific AI applications vary widely, but the common thread across the political spectrum is that people don’t like the technology entering their lives without their consent or control. That is a genuinely different organising thesis than anything this series has previously catalogued from either party’s official platform, and it maps cleanly onto the cross-partisan recall elections and coalition-building this series documented in Capex Ledger Parts IX through XII — Trump voters and environmentalists in the same Michigan coalition, conservative and progressive candidates both running against data centres in the same states.

Aroneanu is notably unsentimental about the risk this strategy carries. When Wong pressed him on whether organising primarily through progressive infrastructure might polarise the issue along conventional partisan lines despite his stated ambitions, his answer was pragmatic rather than defensive: “Chances are, this issue becomes polarised. I think that political parties are really exceptionally good at generating negative partisanship,” and industry itself is likely to “split the base” deliberately. He is choosing to build power from the left first, hoping a conservative counterpart movement eventually emerges independently, rather than waiting to build a single unified coalition before acting — a sequencing decision this series’ own TACO framework would categorize as accepting short-term Obfuscation risk (being mischaracterized as a partisan vehicle) in exchange for faster Accommodate-forcing pressure on the industry.

Three Priorities, Not Yet a Platform

Irreplaceable has named three broad priority areas even while declining to commit to specific legislative language: mitigating automation’s economic disruption, protecting against government and corporate surveillance conducted through AI systems, and establishing industry oversight sufficient to prevent catastrophic failures — Wong’s examples include AI-powered hacking causing widespread blackouts and engineered pandemics, the same category of extreme risk this series’ Part 87 already covered through Bill Gates’s bioweapon-review proposal. These are not narrow concerns; taken together, they span labour economics, civil liberties, and existential risk management, which is either a sign of appropriately broad ambition for a genuinely general-purpose technology, or evidence that Irreplaceable has not yet done the harder work of prioritising among competing demands on a finite amount of political capital. Wong does not resolve this tension, and neither should this series pretend it has an easy answer — the honest position is that both readings are simultaneously true, and which one dominates will depend entirely on execution over the next several years.

Wong’s historical framing deserves direct engagement rather than a passing mention, because it is the strongest empirical case for Irreplaceable’s populist strategy succeeding where the climate movement’s own Green New Deal ultimately stalled. He points to the Tea Party, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s own political rise, MAGA, and election denialism as the most durable and consequential American political movements of the past fifteen years, and identifies their common fuel as outrage over an unfair economy and a political system perceived as rigged against ordinary people — not, notably, carefully argued policy papers. If that diagnosis is correct, Irreplaceable’s bet on emotional and cultural organising over technocratic argument is not a naive departure from how American politics actually works; it is closer to the only strategy Wong’s own examples suggest has reliably worked at all in the current political environment. This series should take that historical pattern seriously as a genuine strategic insight, even while noting that all four of Wong’s examples eventually calcified into instruments of one party or the other — precisely the “down-up not left-right” outcome Aroneanu says he wants to avoid, and precisely the outcome his own “chances are, this issue becomes polarised” concession suggests he expects anyway.

The Weakness Nobody Involved Denies

The single most important admission in Wong’s entire piece comes from Lawrence himself, and this series should treat it as the essay’s real thesis rather than a passing caveat: “We’ve aligned around saying no. Now, there is an important conversation that is yet to come, about — okay, so what do we want?” Irreplaceable has a stated three-stage timeline — build a coalition through the remainder of 2026, develop unified policy in 2027, implement an agenda by the end of 2029 — that Wong correctly identifies as fast by the standard of the Green New Deal’s decade-long gestation, and dangerously slow by the standard of frontier AI development this series has spent eighty-seven parts documenting in real time. This series’ own Part 87, on Bill Gates’s warnings published only two days before Wong’s piece, already registered the specific danger in exactly this kind of timeline: capability gaps this series has tracked compressing from decades to months make a three-year policy-development runway a genuine strategic risk, not merely an organisational inconvenience.

The group’s own published plan stretches that runway further than Wong’s summary does. Irreplaceable’s “Our Plan” page sets out five annual stages: build the base in 2026, convene a coalition of workers, parents, teachers, nurses, faith leaders and labour, consumer, child-safety and climate groups around a federal agenda in 2027, make AI a central campaign issue in 2028, push “a new President and Congress to act in the first hundred” days in 2029, and have “binding regulations on Big AI” on the books “by January 1, 2030.” The first stage has begun quickly. Protos reported on September 3, 2026 that the group was recruiting student protest leaders for walkouts planned at more than 100 colleges in October, and noted that its privacy page names an Education Fund and an Action Fund but discloses no donors. Protos also reported that Ornstein concurrently works for the Center for AI Safety, a link the outlet used to frame Irreplaceable as an effective-altruist project. Whoever is paying, a group whose central charge is that AI’s builders operate without public accountability will face the same question about its own money.

Wong’s own scepticism deserves to anchor this essay’s critical assessment rather than sit as a throwaway aside. Tech companies have already spent tens of millions of dollars in the run-up to the midterms — a figure this series’ Capex Ledger thread has already contextualized against Ohio’s NRSC memo and its plea for industry ad financing — and by the time Irreplaceable’s own policy platform matures in 2027, OpenAI, Anthropic, and an unknown number of Chinese labs may have already built and lost meaningful control of systems considerably more capable than anything currently deployed. Irreplaceable is, in Aroneanu’s own words when describing the sequencing logic, betting that “you have to show the benefits to people immediately. It’s gotta land in their pocketbooks almost immediately for it to have political salience.” That is a coherent theory of how to build durable political power. It is a much less obviously coherent theory of how to constrain a technology whose risk profile, by this series’ own repeated documentation, does not wait for coalitions to finish forming.

The money on the other side is better documented. A Public Citizen analysis published on June 30, 2026 found that Leading the Future, the pro-AI super PAC, had taken $75.1 million in contributions for the 2026 cycle by the end of the first quarter, $50 million of it from Andreessen Horowitz, while Meta had put $65 million toward state-level super PACs. Yet that money has started to look like a liability as well as an asset. The Next Web reported on October 1, 2026, following New York Times reporting by Theodore Schleifer and Mike Isaac the previous day, that OpenAI president Greg Brockman and his wife Anna had not delivered the second $25 million of a $50 million pledge to Leading the Future. Brockman had told OpenAI staff in a June Slack message, “We’ve donated $25m so far, and have no plans to donate more at this time,” citing concern that the super PAC kept “reflecting on the company.” Representative Greg Casar, who chairs the Congressional Progressive Caucus, claimed the result for the movement Irreplaceable wants to build. “The pressure is working,” he said, as Truthout reported on October 1, adding that progressives “have worked hard to make LTF as toxic as” AIPAC and that “Democrats need to stand with the people and reject AI billionaires’ money.” The episode is small next to the sums involved, and Brockman’s stated reason was the distraction to OpenAI rather than public opinion, but it shows that the industry’s political money is no longer free of cost for the people giving it.

The Incoherence Problem, Named Directly

Wong’s reporting is admirably honest about a structural weakness this series has implicitly documented without ever naming as bluntly: American anxiety about AI is not one coherent position but several incompatible ones held simultaneously by the same people. Wong personally observed attendees at a single anti-AI protest disagreeing with each other about water use and whether certain jobs would actually be better off automated. Americans want to stay ahead of China in AI development, per the polling this series’ Part 86 already examined sceptically, while also fearing AI becoming too powerful too quickly — two positions that pull toward opposite policy conclusions depending on which fear is weighted more heavily in a given month. And the backlash itself is substantially an American phenomenon; AI polls considerably better internationally, which Wong notes bodes poorly for any prospect of the kind of binding global framework Bill Gates argued for in this series’ immediately preceding part. A movement built to constrain a globally-developed, globally-deployed technology, organised almost entirely within one country’s borders, is solving at most half the actual governance problem this series has spent its entire run documenting.

Where This Leaves the Ledger

Irreplaceable’s own closing provocation, which Wong reports without fully endorsing, deserves to stand as this essay’s final note because it reframes a question this series has asked in fragments across eighty-seven parts into a single sentence: if AI is genuinely the general-purpose technology Silicon Valley claims it is — touching labour markets, surveillance, information integrity, and physical infrastructure simultaneously — then no single regulatory fix, whether a token tax, a cyberattack oversight body, or a wealth-redistribution scheme, addresses more than one facet of a problem that is, by the industry’s own preferred description of its product, total.

Major figures at both OpenAI and Anthropic have reportedly suggested a genuine AI revolution might require entirely new democratic and economic institutions to match its scale. Wong’s closing line is worth sitting with rather than resolving: perhaps that’s true, and perhaps the companies that have already demonstrated, across this series’ eighty-seven parts, exactly how much they are willing to tear down without consequence should not also be the ones entrusted with deciding what gets built in its place.
