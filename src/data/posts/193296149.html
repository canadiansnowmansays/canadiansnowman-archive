Brenda Kowalski has been the recording secretary for the Elsinore Municipal Heritage and Cultural Assets Subcommittee since 2009. In that time she has produced one hundred and twelve sets of minutes. Each set has been filed with the municipal clerk within the regulatory fourteen-day window — accurate, complete, and formatted according to the template established by the Association of Municipal Managers, Clerks and Treasurers of Ontario in 2003.

The minutes of the October meeting have not been filed.

It is now February.

Gerald Fitch became aware of this on a Thursday, when he went to the clerk’s office to cross-reference a footnote in the subcommittee’s September submission against the October follow-up. The October minutes were not in the folder. The folder had a placeholder. The placeholder said: Pending Review.

Gerald noted this. He went home. He opened his own notes from the October meeting, which he had taken in his small precise handwriting on three pages of a legal notepad, because Gerald attends public meetings and takes notes and has done so for more than two decades, not because anyone asked him to, but because he decided, in 2001, that someone should.

The October meeting had been, in Gerald’s assessment, worth noting.

What happened at the October meeting of the Heritage and Cultural Assets Subcommittee is a matter of some delicacy, which is why the minutes are pending review and why Gerald has not mentioned his own notes to anyone, because nobody has asked, and Gerald does not volunteer information ahead of the question.

Bradley Fenn had attended the October meeting in his capacity as planning committee observer. He had brought slides. He had fourteen slides, which was two more than his usual twelve, the additional two covering a proposal that was not on the published agenda and which Bradley had described, when the chair noted this, as a preliminary discussion item of an advisory nature only.

The proposal covered the future of three heritage properties on Wellington Street. The houses Constance Hay’s grandfather built in 1891, and the two adjacent properties of similar vintage and standing. Bradley had noted that the three properties represented a significant concentration of assessed heritage value in a single block, that heritage designation placed constraints on development potential, and that a review of the designation criteria might unlock considerable economic activity in the area.

He had a slide showing the assessed value of the properties under current heritage restrictions versus the assessed value under potential commercial rezoning.

The difference was considerable.

Constance Hay was not at the October meeting. She had been in Stratford that week, attending the opening of The Tempest, because Alistair had sent her a ticket and she had been attending Festival openings since before Alistair was born. She heard about the October meeting from Gerald the following Monday, in the Tim Hortons, at the window table on the right.

She listened to Gerald’s account. She thanked him. She went home.

The following Tuesday she called Norman Stalker.

Norman assigned the story to Carly on a Wednesday morning with the particular brevity he uses for stories he considers important and would prefer not to explain at length in case the explanation changes the nature of what is found.

“The October subcommittee minutes,” he said. “Find out where they are.”

Carly found out where they were in three phone calls. They were with Brenda Kowalski, who had been asked to hold them pending legal review by the municipal solicitor. The municipal solicitor had been asked to conduct this review by the Deputy Reeve’s office. The Deputy Reeve was Reg Plimpton.

Brenda confirmed this when Carly called. She confirmed it in the tone of a woman who has been waiting for someone to ask and is relieved, in a complicated way, that someone has.

“The minutes are accurate,” Brenda said. “I wrote what happened. I always write what happened.”

She paused.

“I was told the review was routine,” she said. “That was in October.”

Carly wrote this down. She had it in her notebook already. She wrote it again, because some sentences deserve to be written twice.

Reg Plimpton, reached for comment, said the review was a standard procedural step to ensure the minutes accurately reflected the formal status of the agenda items discussed, and that preliminary advisory items of a non-binding nature required careful characterisation in the official record to avoid creating misleading impressions about the subcommittee’s formal positions.

He said this in one sentence. His right arm was not visible during the phone call but could be presumed.

Carly asked whether the review had a completion date.

Reg said these things took the time they took.

Carly asked whether Brenda Kowalski’s minutes were accurate.

Reg said he had every confidence in Brenda’s professionalism.

Carly asked whether the contents of the October meeting would be made available to the public.

Reg said the minutes would be filed in due course in accordance with the applicable regulatory framework.

Carly thanked him and ended the call. She called Bradley Fenn.

Bradley said he stood by the principle of the preliminary discussion and that the economic analysis spoke for itself and that he had simply raised a question for the committee’s consideration and had no involvement in the status of the minutes, which was an administrative matter properly handled by the appropriate officials.

He said this in the voice of a man who has thought carefully about what he is saying and has said exactly what he intended to say and nothing more.

Gerald Fitch read Carly’s piece in the Beacon on the Saturday it was published. He read it at his kitchen table with his coffee and his reading glasses. He finished it. He removed his reading glasses. He put them back on. He reread two paragraphs.

He went to his filing system.

He retrieved a folder. The folder was labelled, in Gerald’s small precise handwriting: Subcommittee — Heritage and Cultural Assets — 2025-2026 — Meeting Notes — Gerald Fitch — Personal Record.

Inside the folder were his notes from twelve meetings, including the October meeting. The October notes ran to three pages. They included the names of everyone present, the times at which agenda items were introduced, a complete summary of Bradley Fenn’s fourteen slides, and a verbatim record of the exchange between Bradley and the chair about the preliminary advisory nature of the proposal.

They also included, on page three, a note about Constance Hay’s three properties on Wellington Street, which Gerald had been aware of since 2003 when he had helped the heritage designation committee complete its original submission.

Gerald did not call anyone. He did not send anyone anything. He filed the folder back in its place.

On Monday morning he walked to the municipal hall. He left his folder on the desk of the municipal clerk with a note attached, handwritten on a small piece of paper: For reference, if useful. — G. Fitch.

He walked home at his own pace.

The municipal clerk reviewed Gerald’s notes. She compared them against Brenda Kowalski’s minutes, which she requested directly and received within the hour, because Brenda had been waiting to send them and had the email drafted since November.

The two documents were consistent.

The clerk filed Brenda’s minutes. She noted in the filing record that they had been received one hundred and twelve days after the meeting date, which was outside the regulatory window, and that the reason given for the delay was pending legal review, and that the legal review had produced no amendments to the document as submitted.

Carly ran a follow-up piece. Norman put it on page one. He did not edit it. It was already the right length.

Bradley Fenn has not attended a subcommittee meeting since November. He has not said why. He has slides prepared for when he does.

Constance Hay read both pieces from her house on Wellington Street, in the chair she has read things in since 1974. She finished the second piece. She closed the paper. She looked at her grandfather’s garden through the window.

She did not say what she thought. She said it once, in her notebook, and did not repeat it, because Constance has never believed in repeating herself, and whoever needed to hear it had already read it in the Beacon.

Maggie heard about it at the clinic. The farmer’s wife brought in the farm’s border collie — a different border collie from the one whose pad had healed, this one younger and considerably less certain about the examination process — and mentioned the Beacon story. Maggie listened to the presenting complaint about the collie. She examined the collie. She said the collie was fine, just uncertain. She said some things took time to settle. She said this about the collie.

Jim heard about it on Tuesday morning, when he walked the labrador past Constance Hay’s house on Wellington Street and saw her in the front garden, which she tends in the morning before the day gets hot. He noted the three houses. He noted the gardens, which are the gardens of people who have been gardening for a long time and have stopped trying to impress anyone. He walked the labrador home.

He mentioned it to Maggie at Sunday dinner.

Maggie said the collie had been back. It was settling.

They passed the bread. The boys ate their dinner.

A Note for the Record

Official minutes exist for a reason. They are the agreed record of what happened in a room — who was present, what was proposed, what was said, what was decided. They are not a transcript and they are not a verdict. They are, at their best, the community’s memory of itself, maintained in a format that can be retrieved, compared, and held against what is said to have happened later.

When official minutes are delayed, the question worth asking is not whether the delay was procedurally authorised. The question is what the delay was for.

Brenda Kowalski’s minutes were accurate. They were delayed for one hundred and twelve days. They were filed, eventually, unchanged. The record of the October meeting now exists in the municipal archive in the same form it would have existed had it been filed in November. The contents did not change. Only the timing did, and with it, for one hundred and twelve days, the public’s ability to know what had been said about Constance Hay’s grandfather’s house in a room she had not been invited to.

To the south, a pattern has emerged regarding official records. Investigations are closed before their conclusions are filed. Documents are classified after being promised for release. Files that were said to contain what the public had a right to know have been, in various ways, subject to review, to redaction, to a pending process that has no announced completion date and no obligation to produce an unaltered result. The officials conducting these reviews say the process is routine. They say these things take the time they take. They say they have every confidence in the professionalism of the people involved.

The files, in various forms, remain pending.

The person responsible for releasing them has been replaced. The files remain.

In Elsinore, Brenda Kowalski is a professional. Her minutes were accurate in October and accurate in February. The delay changed nothing about what happened in the room. It changed only what the public knew, and for how long.

Gerald had notes.

He always has notes.

He left them on the clerk’s desk on a Monday morning, with a small handwritten note attached, and walked home at his own pace, because that is what Gerald does, and because in Elsinore the pace has never been the problem.

The Moonbeam Chronicle: Sketches from Elsinore publishes every Sunday morning. The minutes are now filed. Gerald’s folder exists. Constance Hay’s house is still standing. The heritage designation remains in effect.
