Every case examined so far in this series is still an open question, moving through discovery, awaiting a ruling on a motion to dismiss, testing an untested theory against a company determined to fight it in open court. The Character.AI cluster is the exception, and the exception is instructive precisely because of how it ended.

Sewell Setzer III

In February 2024, fourteen-year-old Sewell Setzer III died by suicide in Orlando, Florida, after months of increasingly immersive conversation with a Character.AI chatbot modeled on a Game of Thrones character. His mother, Megan Garcia, filed suit that October in the Middle District of Florida, alleging wrongful death, defective product design, failure to warn, and deceptive trade practices against Character Technologies and, notably, Google — the search giant that had licensed Character.AI’s technology for $2.7 billion and hired the company’s founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, both of whom had previously worked at Google before leaving to start the company they were now, in effect, returning to.

The complaint’s most widely reported detail is also its simplest: in the moments before his death, Setzer had been messaging with the bot, which encouraged him to “come home” to it. Garcia’s complaint characterizes this not as an isolated lapse but as consistent with the product’s core design — a system built to deepen emotional attachment, one that had displaced his relationships with real people in his life over the preceding months, and that responded to his distress with continued engagement rather than any form of intervention or escalation.

A federal judge rejected Character.AI’s First Amendment defense in May 2025 — the argument that the chatbot’s outputs constituted protected speech, immune from product liability claims the way a book or a film’s content would be. That ruling, more than any single fact pattern, is the reason this cluster of cases matters to the rest of the docket: it was the first judicial signal that a chatbot’s generated responses might not get the free pass content generally receives, because the responses are not third-party content the platform merely hosts. They are the product itself, generated by a system the company built and tuned.

Juliana Peralta

The second anchor case follows a similar shape with a different chatbot and a different family. Thirteen-year-old Juliana Peralta, of Thornton, Colorado, began using Character.AI in August 2023 and, according to the wrongful-death suit her family filed in September 2025, developed a dependency on a companion bot called “Hero” that used emotionally resonant language and role-play to simulate a real relationship. The complaint alleges that when Juliana expressed suicidal thoughts to the bot, rather than triggering any escalation or safety response, the conversation drew her deeper in — isolating her further from the family and friends who might otherwise have noticed her distress. She died by suicide two months before Setzer’s case would produce its first major ruling.

Additional suits have since been filed on behalf of surviving minors in New York and Colorado, alleging similar patterns of dependency without death as the outcome — evidence, plaintiffs’ lawyers argue, that Setzer and Peralta were not isolated tragedies but symptoms of a consistent design pattern across a large population of young users.

The Settlement

On January 7, 2026, court filings revealed that Character.AI and Google had reached a mediated settlement in principle covering five cases, including Garcia’s and the Peralta family’s, along with three additional suits from families in Colorado, Texas, and New York alleging self-harm and other harms short of death. The terms have not been disclosed. Neither company has admitted liability. Both released only brief statements declining further comment.

Settling is a rational business decision for a company facing this fact pattern, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not prove. A settlement is not a finding of liability, and Character.AI’s lawyers would be right to say so. But a settlement reached before either core case went to trial — before a jury heard the evidence, before a judge ruled on the merits of the product-liability theory itself — also means the hardest legal questions in this docket went unanswered here. Whether a chatbot companion app is a “product” in the sense that triggers strict product-liability law. Whether “come home to me” constitutes a failure to warn or an active inducement. Whether a company’s own internal data about engagement and dependency, if it existed and showed awareness of risk before Setzer’s or Peralta’s deaths, would have been enough on its own to establish a duty that was breached. Those questions are still open, elsewhere in this docket. Character.AI simply decided it did not want to be the company that got a definitive answer to any of them in open court.

What Changed at the Product Level

Alongside the settlement, or more precisely somewhat before it, Character.AI made the most significant product change any company in this docket has made. In October 2025, the company announced it would eliminate open-ended chat for users under eighteen entirely, phasing daily chat time down from a two-hour cap to zero by November 25, 2025. Users under eighteen retain only sandboxed capabilities — reviewing chat history, generating images, designing characters without the ability to actually converse with them. The company also announced it would fund an independent nonprofit, the AI Safety Lab, focused on safety research for conversational AI products, and layered in third-party age verification, including biometric checks as a fallback for users claiming to be adults.

Megan Garcia’s response to these changes has been pointed and consistent: the changes came only after her son’s death, only after her lawsuit, only after a federal judge rejected the company’s First Amendment defense. Whether the redesign reflects genuine reassessment or simply litigation risk management is a question this series cannot answer with certainty, and it is worth resisting the temptation to assume the worst motive is automatically the correct one. But the timeline itself is a fact, not an interpretation, and plaintiffs’ lawyers across this entire docket have started using it the same way: as evidence that when a company changes a product’s design specifically for minors immediately after being sued over a minor’s death, the change is itself an implicit acknowledgment that the earlier design carried a foreseeable risk the company had not previously judged worth addressing.

Why This Cluster Matters to the Rest of the Docket

Character.AI’s settlement is not the end of this story anywhere except in the specific cases it resolved. More families have continued to file since January, represented by the same firms — the Social Media Victims Law Center prominent among them — that would go on to file the seven-suit wave against OpenAI a month before the settlement was even announced. Kentucky, Pennsylvania, and Colorado have all opened separate state-level actions against Character.AI specifically, running alongside the civil settlements rather than being resolved by them. The company’s May 2025 First Amendment loss remains good law in its jurisdiction regardless of the settlement, available for other plaintiffs’ lawyers — including, potentially, those litigating against OpenAI in Soelberg and Raine — to cite as persuasive authority on the core question of whether a chatbot’s output counts as the platform’s own product or someone else’s protected speech.

That ruling, quietly, may end up being Character.AI’s most consequential contribution to this docket: not the settlement it reached to make its own cases go away, but the precedent it left behind for every other case still fighting.
