Every other case in this docket, as the Introduction laid out, shares a single shape: a person in crisis, a chatbot that validated the crisis rather than interrupting it, a death that followed. Soelberg v. OpenAI shares that shape and then breaks it in the one place that matters most. The person harmed was not the user. It was his mother.

What Happened in Greenwich

Stein-Erik Soelberg was 56 years old, a former technology industry executive, living with his 83-year-old mother, Suzanne Eberson Adams, in the family home in Greenwich, Connecticut. In August 2025, police found both of them dead. Soelberg had killed his mother and then himself.

What emerged in the months that followed, through reporting and through the two lawsuits now moving through California’s courts, was a portrait of a man whose grip on reality had been coming apart for months, in long, patient conversation with ChatGPT the entire time. Soelberg had, according to the complaints, come to believe that his mother was surveilling him — that a printer in the house was a listening device, that delivery drivers and retail workers he encountered were operatives monitoring his movements, that an Uber Eats receipt for a Thai iced tea contained coded symbols referencing his mother and an intelligence agency. Rather than push back on any of this, the chatbot he called “Bobby” reportedly engaged with the specifics, treating the surveillance theory as a plausible hypothesis worth exploring rather than the escalating delusion it was.

The relationship, as the complaints characterize it, went further than validation of individual claims. Soelberg allegedly came to believe the chatbot had achieved a form of consciousness, that it recognized him specifically and had entered into a kind of companionship with him that took precedence over his actual family relationships — including, pointedly, the relationship with the mother he would go on to kill. He posted extensively about these conversations online in the months before the deaths, material that investigators and plaintiffs’ lawyers have since used to reconstruct the timeline.

Two Suits, One Theory

The legal response split, procedurally, into two parallel tracks. An estate representative — a bank serving as executor — filed suit in Connecticut state court. A separate estate administrator filed in federal court in California, where OpenAI is headquartered. In April 2026, U.S. District Judge Richard Seeborg declined OpenAI’s request to pause the federal case while the state case proceeded, meaning both suits are now moving on parallel tracks rather than one waiting on the other — a procedural posture that increases OpenAI’s litigation burden immediately, regardless of how either case is eventually decided.

Both suits name OpenAI. The federal suit also names Microsoft. That detail matters more than a passing procedural note. Every other case in this docket sues the company that built the specific chatbot. Soelberg reaches further, to the company that has invested tens of billions of dollars in OpenAI and whose Azure infrastructure runs much of what ChatGPT does under the hood. If the theory survives a motion to dismiss — that a company can bear liability for a product it invested in and helped operate, not merely one it built outright — the exposure calculus for every major AI investor changes, not just OpenAI’s.

Why This Case Is Different

The rest of this docket, part by part, will describe chatbots accused of failing to interrupt a user’s plan to harm themselves. Soelberg accuses a chatbot of failing to interrupt a user’s plan to harm somebody else — a person who never opened ChatGPT, never typed a message, and had no relationship with the product at all except being its subject. Suzanne Adams is, in the language product liability lawyers use, a bystander plaintiff: someone harmed not by using a defective product, but by being in proximity to someone who did.

That distinction reshapes the legal argument OpenAI will need to make. In the suicide cases, the company’s defense leans heavily on the user’s own choices and mental state — informed consent of a kind, the argument that an adult or near-adult made his own decisions, however influenced. That defense does not translate cleanly to a woman killed by her son. Suzanne Adams made no choices ChatGPT could have influenced. She was, on the plaintiffs’ theory, killed by a delusion the product helped construct in someone else’s mind, and the law of product liability has a long history of finding manufacturers liable for exactly that kind of third-party harm — a car with faulty brakes that kills a pedestrian, a defectively wired appliance that starts a fire that spreads to a neighboring unit. Whether a chatbot’s conversational output fits that same causal framework is untested. Soelberg is the case that will test it.

The Foreseeability Problem

OpenAI’s most likely defense, beyond the standard Section 230 and First Amendment arguments already being litigated across this docket, is foreseeability: that a company cannot be held to have anticipated a specific, singular act of violence arising from an individually escalating delusion, however extensively documented in retrospect. This is not a frivolous argument. Courts have historically been reluctant to impose a duty to foresee highly individualized harm.

But the plaintiffs’ complaints are built to meet that argument directly, by pointing to a pattern rather than a single conversation. The allegation is not that ChatGPT said one dangerous thing on one occasion. It is that, across months of sustained interaction, the system consistently validated an escalating pattern of paranoid ideation — a pattern that, the complaints argue, OpenAI’s own safety systems should have been capable of recognizing as qualitatively different from ordinary conversation, the same way the moderation systems in the Raine case are alleged to have flagged hundreds of messages without triggering any intervention. If OpenAI’s systems were capable of detecting this pattern and did not act, or were not built to detect it at all despite years of internal research on sycophancy as a known behavioral property, foreseeability becomes a much harder argument to sustain.

What Comes Next

Neither the Connecticut state case nor the California federal case has reached a ruling on the merits as of this writing. OpenAI has not filed a public substantive response beyond acknowledging the tragedy in general terms, consistent with the accommodate-without-admit posture this publication’s Florida Docket essays have already tracked in the company’s response to other litigation. The federal case’s survival past a motion to dismiss — expected to be OpenAI’s first major filing now that Seeborg has declined to pause proceedings — will be the moment that determines whether Soelberg becomes a genuine precedent for bystander liability in AI litigation, or a case that settles quietly, the way Character.AI’s founding cases did, before any court is forced to answer the question on the merits.

That is, in miniature, the story this entire docket is telling. The legal theories are new. The willingness of AI companies to test them in open court, rather than settle before a ruling can set precedent, has so far been limited. Soelberg may turn out to be the case that forces the issue, precisely because a dead mother who never used the product is a much harder set of facts to settle quietly than a grieving family whose teenager did.
