The Terrorism Confinement Center (CECOT) in El Salvador. Built to hold 40,000 inmates, it has become the centerpiece of President Bukele’s anti-gang campaign — and, in 2025, a destination for U.S. deportees.

Three days after ICE agents stopped Kilmar Abrego Garcia on a Maryland road, he was on a plane. Not to a detention centre. Not to a courtroom. To El Salvador — the one country on earth that a federal judge had specifically prohibited the United States government from sending him to.

He was one of 238 men on the flight. As the Economic Policy Institute documented, three-quarters of them had no criminal record. They were construction workers, day labourers, fathers, brothers — men whose primary offence, in most cases, was being present in the United States without authorization. They were flown to El Salvador under an arrangement between the Trump administration and President Nayib Bukele, an arrangement in which the United States paid El Salvador to receive and imprison deportees. The arrangement had no public price tag. The terms were not disclosed to Congress. The men on the plane had no lawyers present, no hearing scheduled, no opportunity to contest what was happening to them. They were, in the language of the administration, being “removed.” In the language of the law, at least in Abrego Garcia’s case, they were being disappeared.

President Nayib Bukele with Donald Trump. Under their arrangement, the U.S. paid El Salvador to accept and imprison deportees — including men with no criminal records.

On March 15, 2025, Kilmar Abrego Garcia called his wife Jennifer from a phone he would not have access to for much longer. He told her he was being taken to the Terrorism Confinement Center — CECOT — in El Salvador. As the Nashville Banner’s timeline documented, it is one of the largest and most brutal prisons in the Western Hemisphere, built by Bukele as the centrepiece of his war on gangs. CECOT — Centro de Confinamiento del Terrorismo, the Center for the Confinement of Terrorism — is a name that tells you everything about its purpose and nothing about its reality. It is not a correctional facility. It is not a place designed to rehabilitate or even to punish in any juridical sense. It is a warehouse for human beings, built to hold 40,000 inmates in conditions that multiple international organisations have described as constituting torture. The U.S. government was paying El Salvador to imprison these men. Abrego Garcia had been convicted of no crime.

What happened when he arrived is documented in a court filing from July 2025, one of the first detailed accounts from inside CECOT’s walls. Abrego Garcia is the only one of the 200-plus deportees to have been released, which makes his testimony unique and irreplaceable — and which also means, as a matter of evidentiary discipline, that it cannot be independently corroborated by another firsthand account.

A deportation flight similar to the one that carried Kilmar Abrego Garcia and 237 other men to El Salvador in March 2025. Many had no criminal record.

When the plane landed, as The Guardian reported in its detailed account of the filing, guards grabbed him and pushed him down the stairs head-first. He was shoved toward a bus, forcibly seated, fitted with a second set of chains and handcuffs, and repeatedly hit by officers when he attempted to raise his head. The ordeal was filmed.

At CECOT, a prison official greeted the new arrivals with words that have the quality of a line from a screenplay but were, according to the filing, spoken in earnest: “Welcome to CECOT. Whoever enters here doesn’t leave.”

He was forced to strip. He was issued prison clothing. He was kicked in the legs with boots and struck on his head and arms to make him change faster. He was frog-marched to a cell while guards hit him with wooden batons along the way. The next day, according to TIME’s account of the court filing, he had visible bruises and lumps covering his body.

That first night, and for nights afterward, Abrego Garcia and twenty other Salvadorans in his cell were forced to kneel from approximately 9:00 PM to 6:00 AM. Guards struck anyone who fell from exhaustion. He was denied access to a bathroom. He soiled himself. The cell had no windows. Bright lights remained on twenty-four hours a day. There were no mattresses — only metal bunks. As The Hill reported, screams from nearby cells rang out through the night without any response from prison guards.

Prison officials told him, repeatedly, that they would transfer him to cells containing gang members who, they assured him, would “tear him apart.” He observed prisoners in nearby cells violently harm each other with no intervention from guards. He suffered what his lawyers described as “severe beatings, severe sleep deprivation, inadequate nutrition, and psychological torture.”

In his first two weeks, he lost thirty-one pounds.

Inside a high-security prison block. Abrego Garcia described windowless cells with constant bright lights, metal bunks, no mattresses, forced kneeling for hours, and screams echoing through the night.

On April 9, before he was transferred out of CECOT to a lower-security facility called Centro Industrial, he was forced to appear in what he believed was a staged photograph — posed with mattresses and better food, images apparently designed to document improved conditions that did not exist in the cell where he had actually been held.

These allegations have not been independently verified. Bukele disputed the claims, posting a video he said showed Abrego Garcia eating, working at a fish farm, and holding a parrot as part of a “mental wellness activity.” The video appeared to have been shot at Centro Industrial — the lower-security facility — not at CECOT itself, where the abuse allegedly occurred. “If he’d been tortured, sleep-deprived, and starved, why does he look so well in every picture?” Bukele wrote. The conditions described in the filing are, however, consistent with patterns documented by Human Rights Watch and the ACLU across CECOT’s broader detainee population. Abrego Garcia is the only person to have come out and spoken. The other 200-plus deportees remain inside, and their stories — whatever they are — remain untold.

Meanwhile, back in the United States, the legal machinery was grinding forward. On March 24, nine days after Abrego Garcia arrived in El Salvador, his wife Jennifer, through counsel, filed a complaint in federal district court in Maryland challenging the deportation. The case was assigned to Judge Paula Xinis. On March 31, 2025, as the Nashville Banner documented, the government filed a response admitting that Abrego Garcia’s deportation to El Salvador was an “administrative error.” An error. An oversight. They conceded it should not have happened. But they opposed bringing him back, citing his “danger to the community” and arguing that returning him would harm the public interest by preventing the president from “implementing a unified course of conduct for the United States’ foreign affairs.”

The government of the United States had illegally deported a man, imprisoned him in a torture facility at taxpayer expense, admitted it was a mistake, and then argued that fixing the mistake would inconvenience the president’s foreign policy. Read that sentence again, because it is the sentence that the entire case turns on. The government conceded the illegality and then argued that the illegality should stand because correcting it would be diplomatically awkward.

A DOJ attorney named Erez Reuveni appeared before Judge Xinis on April 4, 2025. Xinis asked him: on what basis was Abrego Garcia being held? Why was he in CECOT, of all places?

“I don’t know,” Reuveni said. “That information has not been given to me. I don’t know. We have nothing to say on the merits. We concede he should not have been removed to El Salvador.”

There it is. The government’s own lawyer, standing in a federal courtroom, telling a federal judge that he had nothing to say on the merits of his own government’s actions. Not “we disagree with the characterisation.” Not “we believe the deportation was justified.” Nothing. We have nothing. We concede. This is the sound of a government that knows it has broken the law and cannot explain why.

On that same day, Judge Xinis ordered the government to facilitate and effectuate Abrego Garcia’s return to the United States by 11:59 PM on Monday, April 7.

The government appealed. The Fourth Circuit denied the stay. The government went to the Supreme Court. And that is where, remarkably, the story turned.
