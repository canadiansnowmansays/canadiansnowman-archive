There is a number, and the number was supposed to be two hundred dollars.

Two hundred is a round number, and round numbers are supposed to matter. They are where buyers pause to catch their breath, where the chartists draw their lines, where a rational market stops to ask itself whether it has come too far. So I sat down to write about the moment SpaceX approached $200, expecting to describe a stock testing its ceiling.

By the time I had finished a paragraph, it was trading at $210.

That is the detail worth pinning to the wall. As I write this on the evening of June 15, a company that sold its shares to the public seventy-two hours ago — at $135 on Friday — is changing hands in after-hours trading above $208. That is a gain of better than fifty percent in three sessions, two of which the market was closed. And it did not pause at $200. It did not test the level, or probe it, or respect it. It went through the supposed psychological barrier the way a husky goes through a screen door it has decided is not really there. The stock has now climbed past every price target that every analyst on Wall Street published, including the highest one.

A price that ignores a round number is telling you something. It is telling you there is nothing underneath it holding it up — and nothing overhead holding it down.

I should disclose, in the spirit of the open kimono this newsletter has always favoured, that I felt it too. The pull. I looked at the green candles marching up the right side of the chart and I heard a small voice — the same voice that has separated more men from more money than any confidence trickster in history — say the moon is the limit. I want to be honest about that voice, because the entire purpose of this essay is to take it apart, and a man cannot credibly dissect a thing he pretends never to have felt.

The voice is wrong. It is always wrong. But understanding why it is wrong this particular time requires understanding what is actually being sold here, which is not rockets.

I. The Trick, Performed in Plain Sight

Let us start with the mechanics, because the mechanics are the whole magic act, and a magic act explained is a magic act defeated.

SpaceX sold 555.6 million shares at $135, raising on the order of $75 billion and arriving at the public markets with a valuation around $1.77 trillion — as multiple outlets reported, the largest share sale in the history of the species. A staggering number. And yet the relevant number is a different and much smaller one: the free float, the portion of the company actually available to trade, sits at roughly four percent.

Four percent. Let that settle. Ninety-six percent of the company is locked up — held by insiders under a 180-day restriction, held by funds, held by everyone who is not you. What sloshes around the public market is a thin film of stock on top of an ocean of locked value.

Now add the second ingredient. The stock is barely days old, yet a great mechanical herd of index funds is already obligated to buy it — just not quite yet. Under fast-track rules rewritten this spring expressly to accommodate a company of this size, FTSE Russell will add SpaceX to its major US indices after the close on June 26, and MSCI follows on June 29. The funds tracking those benchmarks do not think, do not value, do not blink; they will hold the stock simply because it has joined the index, and they will buy it regardless of price, because their entire reason for existing is to mirror the benchmark rather than to judge it.

And here is the elegant cruelty of the thing: everyone knows the date. The forced buying is not a surprise lurking somewhere in the future — it is a scheduled event, printed on the calendar a fortnight in advance. So the crowd front-runs it. A good part of the squeeze you are watching this week is simply a wager that the index funds will be made to pay more at the end of the month than you paid today.

So here is the trick, performed in full view of an audience that paid to see it: take a colossal company, release only a sliver of it to the public, then announce to the world the precise date on which a herd of price-blind buyers will be required to purchase that sliver. A small amount of stock, enormous forced demand, and a countdown clock everyone can read. There is a word for what happens to a price under those conditions, and the word is up.

The float was not thin by accident. It was thin by design. That is the part worth sitting with.

II. The Flywheel

What the engineering sets in motion, human nature finishes.

The index funds will be made to buy. The thin float cannot supply them without the price rising, and everyone knows it is coming. The price rises now, in advance. Retail investors, watching from their phones on the subway, see the price rising. They buy. Their buying makes the price rise further, which confirms — retroactively, and falsely — the wisdom of everyone who bought before them. Each green candle is a character witness for the last green candle. The thing feeds itself.

This is a flywheel, and flywheels are wonderful machines right up until you remember that they store energy rather than create it. Nothing about this loop has anything to do with how many Falcon 9s flew last quarter or how many Starlink subscriptions were sold in Manitoba. It is pure momentum, dressed in the costume of conviction.

You can spot the moment sentiment took the wheel from valuation by listening to how the bulls talk. A week ago they spoke in multiples — revenue projections, EBITDA, sum-of-the-parts breakdowns of telecom and satellites and the speculative orbital-data-centre business. Now they speak in astronomy. To the moon. When the argument for a stock migrates from the spreadsheet to the solar system, you are no longer looking at an investment thesis. You are looking at a mood.

III. The Product Was Never the Rocket

Here is the turn, and it is the part I most want you to carry out of this essay.

SpaceX sells launches. It sells satellite internet. These are real businesses with real revenue, and only a churl would deny the engineering genius that lands a booster upright on a barge in the Atlantic. But the IPO — the financial event of the last few days — sold something else entirely. It sold scarcity. Manufactured, engineered, deliberately rationed scarcity, packaged as a once-in-a-generation opportunity and timed to the minute.

This is not a new product. It is the only product. Consider the catalogue. The Cybertruck with its years-long waitlist and its million-deep reservation book. The “funding secured” tweet that conjured a buyout out of three words and a number. The Mars colony that has been precisely seven years away for fifteen consecutive years. The Boring Company, the flamethrower, the hyperloop. In every case the mechanism is identical: create the impression of scarce access to an extraordinary future, and let human desire do the rest. Scarcity manufactures desire; desire manufactures a premium; the premium is the point.

The thin float is that same trick rendered in financial instruments. Four percent of the company is the Cybertruck waitlist with a ticker symbol. And the man at the centre of it has just become, by most reckonings, the first trillionaire in history — a milestone achieved not by selling more rockets last Tuesday, but by selling the story of the rockets to a market primed to buy stories.

A Canadian watching this from the cheap seats — and I will cop to watching from the cheap seats, a few thousand miles north of the launch pad — cannot help but admire the artistry even while declining to pay for the ticket.

IV. The Three Doors

A squeeze does not die of altitude. No stock ever rolled over because it got the nosebleeds. A squeeze dies the moment supply walks through the door, and the unsettling thing about this particular squeeze is that there are three doors, and all three are already on the calendar with the dates filled in.

The first door is the overallotment. As of June 15 — today — the underwriters’ option to sell an additional 83.3 million shares at the offering price becomes exercisable, and stays open for thirty days. That is fresh supply, available at $135, into a market paying over $200. You do not need a degree in market microstructure to imagine what an underwriter holding shares he can sell at $135 does when the stock is at $210.

The second door is earnings. The first quarterly report as a public company is expected in late July or early August, and it will be the first moment the market is forced to weigh the story against an actual income statement. As Morningstar observed, even generous assumptions struggle to justify the current valuation — only the most optimistic moonshot scenario gets you anywhere near the offering price on fundamentals, never mind the price today. Earnings are where the spreadsheet gets to speak again, and the spreadsheet has been very quiet lately.

The third door, furthest out, is the lockup. In roughly 180 days the insiders — that ninety-six percent — begin to be free to sell. The film of stock on top of the ocean gets a great deal thicker.

And there is a quieter trap folded into the very event the crowd is betting on. The forced index buying arrives on June 26 and 29 — but a scheduled catalyst that everyone can see is a catalyst everyone can sell into. The classic move on a telegraphed event is to buy the rumour and sell the news: the front-runners who bought this week in anticipation of the index funds may well be handing their shares to those index funds at the end of the month and walking away. The forced buying is real, but it may arrive to find the early crowd already heading for the exits. Demand and the urge to take profit can collide on the very same calendar square.

None of these doors guarantees a reversal tomorrow. The squeeze may well clear $200 and run; engineered scarcity plus forced buying plus crowd psychology is a powerful cocktail, and parabolas can stay parabolic longer than the prudent stay solvent. But the difference between a sober observer and a mark is not that the observer can predict the day the music stops. It is that the observer can see the doors, and knows that the absence of a floor is the natural consequence of a manufactured ceiling.

V. The Number, Revisited

So we return to the number — to two hundred dollars, the round figure that was meant to mean something and didn’t, and to the small voice that took its breaching as proof the moon is now merely a way station on the road up.

The voice is making a claim, and it is worth being precise about what it is claiming. It is not claiming anything about SpaceX’s engineering, or Starlink’s subscriber growth, or the commercial viability of Starship. It is claiming something about the behaviour of a crowd — that the crowd will keep buying, that the next person will pay more than you did, that the flywheel will spin a while longer. And crowds, unlike companies, have a shelf life measured not in years but in catalysts. There are three catalysts on the calendar.

“The moon is the limit” is therefore not a statement about a rocket company at all. It never is. It is a statement about other people’s fear of missing out, repeated back to oneself in the first person until it feels like analysis. It is the most expensive sentence in the English language, and it has been bankrupting confident men since the Dutch ran out of tulips.

I felt the pull. I have told you that honestly. And the only thing within the control of a small investor watching a trillionaire’s manufactured mania from a townhouse in Toronto — the only thing — is whether he lets someone else’s FOMO become his own buy order at the top of a squeeze he can see the machinery of.

The moon is not the limit. The moon is the marketing. The limit, as always, is the moment the doors open.
