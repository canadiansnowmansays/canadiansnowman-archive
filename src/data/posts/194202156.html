China calls itself Zhōngguó. The claim is older than the century. The evidence is not.

This section started with a sentence, not a war. On March 10, 2026, I published The Quiet War: “The 141 Pages” — an essay on a Chinese Five-Year Plan for artificial intelligence, robotics, quantum computing, rare earths, and semiconductors that almost nobody in the West had read. I did not know it was the first essay of a major section. I knew it was the first essay in one series, as The Quiet War was conceived as six parts from the beginning, each building toward the same conclusion: that China is winning a strategic competition the United States is too distracted to see.

What I did not know was that The Quiet War would need neighbours. That Formosa — an eleven-part series on Taiwan I had already written — belonged in the same house. That both of them pointed toward a history that needed telling, and toward an older network of trade routes that explained how China had learned, the first time, to sit at the centre of the world. The section arrived after the first series was already underway, because the work has a way of telling you what it wants to become.

China calls itself Zhōngguó — the Middle Kingdom — a name that has carried the weight of civilisational self-perception for millennia. It is not a boast. It is a cosmology. For most of recorded history, China was, by its own reckoning and often by everyone else's, the centre of the known world. Then it wasn't. The nineteenth century broke that self-image against gunboats and unequal treaties; the twentieth spent itself on revolution, famine, and reform trying to put it back together. The twenty-first published a 141-page plan.

What This Section Is

Why does a country that has fought no war since 1979 spend billions preparing to invade its own breakaway province? What does it mean to win a technological race nobody in the country you’re racing has noticed is happening? How does a civilisation that measures time in dynasties think about a hundred-year plan? And what does it cost, at the scale of a single friendship, to live inside a system built for that kind of patience?

These are not abstract questions. They are the subject of a 141-page plan, a live-fire naval blockade drill, a house cleaner in Hsinchu who spends her weekends training to evacuate casualties, and a friend in Shenzhen who simply stopped replying. This section watches all of it — with the Canadian outsider’s distance, and the discomfort of someone who keeps going back anyway.

The Middle Kingdom contains four sub-series, listed in reverse chronological order — the present first, then the disputed territory, then the history that explains both.

How China became the country that published those 141 pages. From Zheng He's treasure fleets at the height of Ming power, through the wreckage of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, to Deng's pivot, Tiananmen's shadow, and Xi's consolidation — the foundation the rest of this section rests on. Silk Road: A History

Before there was a Middle Kingdom to defend, there was a Middle Kingdom to reach. Eighteen essays trace the routes, the cities, and the accidents of geography that connected China to Rome, Samarkand to Venice, centuries before anyone called it a network — and the lesson, repeated ever since, that connective infrastructure produces effects its builders neither anticipated nor controlled. Click here for this sub-series.

The reverse chronology is deliberate. The Quiet War tells you what China is building. Formosa tells you what China says it cannot lose. Silk Road and The Long Return tell you why — thirty-four parts between them, from the caravans of the second century B.C. to Zheng He's treasure fleets of 1421 to the 141 pages of 2026. Readers who arrive at the history last will understand it differently — and more urgently — than if they had encountered it first. The section closes with The Price of the Bargain — a postscript on what the Long Return costs at the scale of a single life.
