Update — May 25, 2026: On the morning of this update, The Atlantic published "I Now Believe Our National Debt Is a Problem" by Jared Bernstein, who served as chair of President Biden's Council of Economic Advisers. Bernstein's confession is worth reading alongside Blankfein's calm alarm, because they are mirror images of the same failure seen from opposite sides of the aisle. Blankfein, representing the financial establishment, describes the mechanics of default and says polarization prevents the fix. Bernstein, representing the Democratic policy establishment, admits he spent most of his career dismissing debt concerns — and now concedes the country is approaching a debt spiral. His arithmetic confirms Blankfein's intuition: when the interest rate on the debt exceeds the growth rate of the economy, every year digs the hole deeper. Between them, they have given us the bipartisan diagnosis. Bernstein even supplies the bipartisan epitaph, borrowing from Saint Augustine: "God, guide us toward fiscal sustainability… just not quite yet." The grain silo has two doors. Both are open.

In the spring of 2026, a Florida company licensed by the Trump Organization collected $59 million from approximately 590,000 Americans who each paid $100 for a gold-encased, flag-bearing mobile phone promised to be proudly made in the United States. The delivery date slipped from late summer 2025 to December, then to 2026, then to the land beyond calendars. The “Made in USA” commitment vanished from the promotional materials like a campaign pledge on November 5th. A reporter from 404 Media noticed recurring charges appearing on his credit card. Then, in April 2026, the company quietly updated its terms and conditions. The $100 deposit was now a “conditional opportunity” — not a binding sales contract.

Five hundred and ninety thousand people. Fifty-nine million dollars. A conditional opportunity.

I begin with the Trump phone not because it is the most consequential grift of this era — it is not even close — but because it is the most distilled. Crypto schemes, the ballroom, the reflecting pool, the meme coins: the grift ecosystem around this presidency is vast and tangled. But the phone is clean. People wanted a thing. They paid for a thing. They were told they had bought a thing. And then the thing became, retroactively, a conditional opportunity. That is, in miniature, the story of American fiscal governance in the twenty-first century.

On his podcast in late spring 2026, David Frum sat down in New York with Lloyd Blankfein — the former CEO of Goldman Sachs, the kid from East New York public housing who grew up to run the most symbolically loaded institution in American capitalism, who led it through the 2008 financial crisis, repaid TARP’s $10 billion inside eight months and returned $1.41 billion in profit to taxpayers, and who has now published a bestselling memoir called Streetwise. What followed was one of the more illuminating conversations I have encountered about the fiscal condition of the United States, precisely because Blankfein was not panicking. The man who spent his professional life reading markets and managing risk sat in a chair and described, in the calm cadence of someone who has spent forty years disciplining his emotional responses to incoming data, a situation that should terrify everyone listening.

Five trillion dollars comes in. Seven trillion goes out. The gap, in the last fiscal year, was $1.8 trillion. In the current year, it is on its way to two trillion. And nobody in Washington appears to regard this as a problem worth getting out of bed five minutes early to think about.

Blankfein reached for Hemingway — or rather for the line commonly attributed to Hemingway — to frame the risk. How does a country go bankrupt? Slowly, then all at once. This is not a metaphor. It is a mechanical description of how sovereign debt crises actually work, and Blankfein, to his great credit, explained the mechanism plainly.

The United States borrows in dollars and prints dollars. A technical default — simply refusing to pay — is almost impossible because the printing presses are always available. The question is not whether creditors get their dollars back. They will get their dollars back. The question is what those dollars will be worth. America defaults not by refusing to pay but by inflating the currency until the purchasing power of the repaid dollars no longer resembles the purchasing power of the dollars that were lent. It is like borrowing your neighbour’s lawnmower and returning it with the blade removed. Technically, you returned the lawnmower.

And who are these creditors? Blankfein was blunt. Foreign governments hold their reserves in dollars because there is no alternative of comparable size, liquidity, and reliability. Not the Chinese yuan, which is a managed currency under central command. Not the euro, which is not large enough and carries its own structural fractures. Not Swiss francs — there are simply not enough of them. Not Bitcoin. Not gold. Not art. The dollar is the reserve currency because the American economy is the world’s largest, and — here is the cruel irony — the very size of the American debt creates the liquidity that makes the market for that debt function. The debtor sustains the system by being an ever-larger debtor.

So the creditors keep lending, not because they trust American fiscal discipline, but because they have nowhere else to go. Slowly, slowly, slowly. And then one day the Treasury holds an auction and nobody shows up. Or they show up, but only at yields that price in the inflationary discount they now expect. And at that point the deficit itself begins to compound: higher interest rates mean larger interest payments, which mean larger deficits, which mean more borrowing, which mean higher interest rates. The virtuous cycle becomes the vicious cycle. The grain silo is not just empty — it is on fire.

That fire is no longer hypothetical. Last week, 30-year Treasury bond yields hit 5.12 percent — the highest since 2007, up from a low of 1 percent in 2020. Net interest payments by the federal government now exceed the defence budget. Read that again. The cost of having borrowed is now larger than the cost of defending the country. The compound spiral Blankfein described is not a future risk. It is the present condition. And the Congressional Budget Office projects publicly held debt to keep growing and hit 175 percent of GDP by 2056.

Morgan Stanley’s chief economic strategist, Ellen Zentner, said clients constantly ask her whether the federal government’s indebtedness is on a sustainable path. Her answer was four words: “We are not.”

The auction nobody attends. This lands the Blankfein warning about failed Treasury auctions. The stacks of cash sitting unclaimed next to an empty room is chilling in context

Blankfein used that grain silo image repeatedly, and it deserves to be the central metaphor of any honest appraisal of current American fiscal policy. The postwar model that this series has tracked — the one encoded in the bipartisan consensus from roughly 1945 to 1980 — was built on a simple principle: in good times, you store the grain. You run surpluses or modest deficits. You allow the economy to grow faster than the debt. You build the cushion so that when the crisis comes — the recession, the war, the pandemic — you have the fiscal space to respond without destabilizing the currency.

The 2008 financial crisis required massive spending. Of course it did. The 2020 pandemic required massive spending. Of course it did. But what the United States is doing now — running deficits of nearly $2 trillion in a period of reasonable prosperity, with no external shock, no recession, no world war — is the fiscal equivalent of eating the seed corn while the sun is shining. It is not preparing for the crisis. It is manufacturing the conditions under which the next crisis will be unmanageable.

And what are both parties proposing as a response? Targeted tax relief. No tax on tips. No tax on seniors. Highly specific carve-outs designed to buy the gratitude of specific voter blocs at the lowest possible political cost. Blankfein, who practised tax law before turning to finance, was characteristically dry about this: a tax expenditure is the same as writing a cheque from revenue. One simply sounds better in a campaign advertisement.

And the much-heralded mechanism for fiscal discipline? Elon Musk's deficit commission produced $1 billion in durable savings — roughly one-tenth of one percent of what Musk promised. One billion against a two-trillion-dollar annual gap. That is not a rounding error. It is a confession.

There was a moment in the interview that stopped me — a moment more revealing, in its way, than any of the fiscal arithmetic.

Blankfein said that when he was growing up in the East New York projects in the 1950s and 1960s, he thought he was middle class. He found out later he was not. But the important thing is that he thought he was, and that 85 percent of the country thought the same thing. That shared self-perception — not the reality of equal outcomes, but the belief in a broadly shared condition — was, he said, one of the geniuses of American culture.

That belief is what this series has been tracking the dissolution of. The postwar promise was not a guarantee of equal wealth. It was a guarantee of sufficient dignity, sufficient security, sufficient mobility that most Americans could look at their lives and say: I am middle class. I have enough. My children will have more. And it was underwritten not by sentiment but by policy — by a tax code, a regulatory structure, a social insurance system, and a fiscal discipline that together maintained the conditions under which that belief could be sustained.

Blankfein's K: one road up, one road down, one figure at the junction.

What Blankfein described as the “K-shaped economy” is the architectural evidence of that promise’s collapse. Those with assets — stocks, property, portfolio exposure to the hyperscaler investment boom — are riding the upper arm of the K, watching their wealth inflate in real time. Those without assets are riding the lower arm, contending with the accumulated residue of prior inflation, unable to buy homes, unable to form families, watching the economy that is “doing well” from a distance that grows every quarter.

Beliefs do not have to be true to motivate action, Frum observed. False beliefs can be as destructive as true beliefs. If enough people believe they are being ripped off, the consequences are the same as if they are being ripped off in reality. But here is the thing about the K economy: the people on the lower arm are not holding a false belief. They are observing their own condition accurately. The economy is doing well. It is simply not doing well for them.

The rise in Treasury yields has made this visible in the most intimate way possible. Higher yields have pushed up mortgage rates, hitting Americans where they live — literally — and making the abstraction of budget deficits suddenly personal. The deficit is no longer a number in a Congressional Budget Office report. It is the reason you cannot afford the house.

A Canadian watching this conversation is struck by a particular irony. Blankfein — the consummate markets professional, the man who ran Goldman Sachs through the worst financial crisis in eighty years — said that the deficit is not his single biggest concern. Polarization is. Not because the deficit does not matter, but because polarization is the mechanism by which the deficit becomes unsolvable. A polarized society cannot form the consensus necessary to impose fiscal discipline. It cannot agree on what counts as a problem, let alone agree on the sacrifice required to address it. And so the grain silo empties, and no one can agree on whether the silo is empty, or whether silos matter, or whether the concept of grain storage is a talking point manufactured by the other party.

This is the doom loop that Thirty Seven Trillion has been documenting from the beginning. It is not merely that Republicans cut taxes and Democrats increase spending, though both statements are true in their crude outlines. It is that the political system itself has lost the capacity for the kind of bipartisan fiscal stewardship that characterized the postwar era — the era when Eisenhower maintained the New Deal's social insurance framework and top marginal tax rates above 90 percent, when Nixon created the EPA, when Clinton balanced the budget with a Republican Congress. Those acts of institutional self-discipline required a political culture in which the other party was an opponent, not an enemy, and in which the long-term stability of the fiscal order was understood by both sides as a shared interest superseding any electoral cycle.

That culture is gone. In its place we have conditional opportunities.

Oddly, Americans were more alarmed about deficits in 2011, when the debt-to-GDP ratio was only 66 percent and the economy actually needed deficit spending to recover. Now, at 100 percent — with the compound interest spiral already turning — the milestone has lost its power to frighten. A Boston University economist recently calculated that the United States is in worse fiscal shape than Italy once you include Social Security, Medicare, and other obligations that do not appear in the official debt figures. “We don’t have any grown-ups in the room in Washington,” he said. “Nobody picks it up and says, ‘You have a problem.’”

Nobody picks it up. That is Blankfein’s grain silo, restated by an academic who has run the numbers and arrived at the same place by a different route. The silo is emptying. Everyone can see it. Nobody will say so in a room where saying so has consequences.

Blankfein ended on optimism, as markets people must. America deals with its problems. It looks terrible, it is sometimes terrible, but it deals with its problems and moves on, better than any other system. This is not an unreasonable view. It is the view of a man who has watched the American system absorb enormous shocks and recover from them within his own lifetime, and who has a professional incentive — and perhaps a psychological need — to believe that the pattern will hold.

But there is a difference between optimism and complacency, and the distance between them is measured in the gap between recognizing a problem and doing something about it. Blankfein recognizes the problem. He told Frum he is in the top decile of deficit worriers. He knows the grain silo is emptying. He knows the tipping point does not advertise itself in advance, does not give you ten minutes’ notice before it arrives.

And yet.

The markets are calm. Wall Street is happy. The economy, for those on the upper arm of the K, is spectacular. The next auction will be financed. The dollar will remain the reserve currency for another quarter, another year, another decade — because there is no alternative, and because the world’s creditors have nowhere else to go.

Slowly. Slowly. Slowly.

And then all at once.
