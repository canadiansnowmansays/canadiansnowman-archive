Postmark: A contested position, eastern Ukraine. Russian soldiers, hands raised, surrendering. No Ukrainian captors in sight — only the machines, and a pilot watching from miles away.

Part VI of this series was called Robots Do Not Bleed, and it took its title from the grim arithmetic driving robots onto the battlefield: a machine can be sent where a person cannot, lost where a person cannot be, spent where a person must not be. When I drafted it, the machine-on-machine war was mostly a projection — a line on a trend, a thing that was coming. This postcard is the sequel, and its occasion is that the projection has arrived. Two thresholds were crossed in the space of a few months, each a first in the history of warfare, and together they mark the moment the robot war stopped being a forecast and became a fact you can date.

I want to report this carefully, because it is the darkest territory this series visits, and the temptation to either thrill or despair must be resisted in equal measure. The wonder this series is pledged to is real here too — the engineering is genuinely astonishing — but it is wonder with a body count attached, and the honest postcard holds both.

The position that fell to no one

The first threshold: in the spring, as Al Jazeera reported, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that for the first time in the history of the war, an enemy position had been taken exclusively by unmanned platforms — ground robots and aerial drones, with no infantry involved and, in his telling, no losses on the Ukrainian side. Footage circulated of Russian soldiers emerging with their hands raised, following shouted orders, surrendering — to no one. To machines. The captors were a pilot and his systems, watching and directing from a position miles behind the line.

As CNN reported, in a dispatch whose headline — “Robots don’t bleed” — landed on my screen as a direct echo of the postcard I had already written, the commander of the Ukrainian unit that ran an earlier version of this mission described taking a position without a single shot fired. His name is Mykola Zinkevych; his call sign is “Makar”; he commands a robotic-strike unit in Ukraine’s Third Separate Assault Brigade. A former infantryman now in charge of machines, he told CNN that if he had heard himself talking this way in 2022, he would have assumed the speaker was a madman — that it was all science fiction. It is not science fiction now. It is his Tuesday.

The strategic logic he offered is the same cold arithmetic Part VI identified, stated by a man who lives inside it: Ukraine will never have more personnel than Russia, will never win on numbers, and so must find its advantage in technology instead. Robots don’t bleed, and a country with fewer people to bleed reaches for them first. There is a data point from that reporting that has stayed with me: a single Ukrainian ground robot with a mounted machine gun reportedly held off a Russian advance for forty-five days, needing only light maintenance and a battery change every couple of days. Forty-five days of holding a position that would have cost how many human lives to hold the old way. That is the promise and the horror of the thing in one statistic.

The drone that killed a drone

The second threshold is subtler but, to my mind, more consequential, because it marks the war turning inward on itself — machines fighting machines with the humans a step further removed.

As the Atlantic Council documented in a detailed study of the naval war, in April an elite Ukrainian unit destroyed a Russian Shahed attack drone using an interceptor drone launched from an unmanned surface vessel — a robot boat launching a robot interceptor to kill a robot aircraft. It was, the study noted, the first engagement of its kind in the new era of drone-on-drone warfare. Read that sequence again and notice where the human is: not in the boat, not in the interceptor, not in the target. The human is on shore, watching a screen, having set in motion a chain of machines that found, closed with, and destroyed another machine. This is the moment warfare’s oldest structure — human against human, however mediated — quietly acquired a new layer, in which the immediate combatants on both sides are robots, and the humans recede to the roles of designer, launcher, and witness.

And the naval robots are proliferating outward from Ukraine into the wider world’s arsenals, which is how you know a threshold has truly been crossed. As Defense News reported, American special forces used a Ukrainian-designed Magura sea drone to sink a target ship during an exercise off the Philippines at the end of June — the first use of the technology in the Indo-Pacific, and no accident of curiosity: a cheap, expendable robot boat that can carry attack drones within striking range is precisely the weapon the United States covets as it contemplates the vast distances of a possible Pacific war. The Ukrainian battlefield has become the world’s R&D lab for machine warfare, and the designs proven in the Black Sea are already migrating to the Pacific. What is invented in one robot war is inherited by the next.

The scale is the part that turns a series of firsts into a transformation. Ukraine’s drone production reportedly approached five million units in a single recent year, and the global count of drone-strike events has climbed from a few hundred in 2018 to well over forty thousand — the overwhelming majority of them in this one war. Thousands of ground robots now crawl through the gray zone along the eastern front, most still hauling supplies and evacuating the wounded, but a growing minority fitted with turrets and guns. A threshold crossed once is a milestone; a threshold crossed five million times is an industry. The robot war is not a demonstration any longer. It is the largest manufacturing effort of the conflict, and its products are learning.

The line every operator draws

Here is where the postcard turns from what the machines can do to the question that actually matters, and where I find, to my genuine surprise, a thread of something like hope in the darkest of this series’ territories.

The men and women running these systems — the ones with the most reason to want more automation, the ones whose lives the machines are saving — are, very nearly to a person, the most insistent that a human being must remain in the loop. Zinkevych, the commander who is “all in” on robots, drew the line himself, and drew it hard: the final decision, he told CNN, must always be made by a human. Would you entrust weapons to artificial intelligence, he asked — how can you be sure it will tell friend from foe, that there won’t be a malfunction, that something won’t go wrong? This is not a peace activist talking. This is the man whose job is killing with machines, saying that the one thing he will not hand to the machine is the choice of whom to kill.

The military analysts studying the war report the same pattern structurally. As strategic researchers have documented, Ukraine’s actual deployment of autonomy remains partial: AI has largely taken over drone-footage analysis, target recognition, tracking, and last-mile navigation — the perception tasks — while the decision to engage a target still, as a matter of doctrine and practice, runs through a human. The machines have been handed the eyes and the hands. The trigger, mostly, is still human. That distinction — perception automated, decision reserved — is the fragile, essential membrane between the war we have and the war we fear, and it is being held, for now, by the professional judgment of the people at the controls rather than by any binding law.

The membrane and its enemies

But I would be failing this series’ commitment to honesty if I let the story rest on that reassurance, because the membrane is under pressure from three directions at once, and naming them is the responsibility of the postcard.

The first pressure is technical: electronic warfare. Both sides jam each other’s communications relentlessly, and the standard engineering answer to jamming is autonomy — a drone that loses its link and must complete its mission alone. The reporting is candid that AI-assisted targeting increasingly “takes over” in the final seconds precisely when jamming severs the human connection. Every advance in jamming is thus an argument for handing the machine more of the decision, and the membrane thins a little each time. The Russian side has reportedly already fielded loitering munitions with onboard chips that identify targets autonomously once the operator link is gone; in one reported May 2025 incident, several such drones broke from their planned mission, formed a holding pattern on their own, and coordinated attacks. Whether or not every detail of that account holds, it describes the exact failure mode the operators fear.

The second pressure is legal and it is a vacuum. As researchers studying autonomous weapons have concluded, existing international law is insufficient to govern machines that can select and kill targets on their own. The first recorded use of a lethal autonomous weapon acting without human intervention was not in Ukraine but in Libya in 2020, where a UN report described a Turkish-made loitering munition hunting down retreating fighters in fully autonomous mode. That was five years ago, and the international community has since held meeting after meeting on regulating lethal autonomous weapons systems without producing a binding regime. The technology is sprinting; the law is holding conferences.

The third pressure connects this postcard directly to the parent series, and it is the one that gives me a strange, cold comfort. As has been reported, when the Pentagon sought to amend the terms of use for its AI systems to permit their integration into fully autonomous weapons, Anthropic’s chief executive declined, on the ground that frontier AI systems are simply not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons. Hold that sentence next to Zinkevych’s. The commander on the Ukrainian front line and the AI executive in San Francisco — men who could not occupy more different positions — arrived at the same judgment: not yet, not this, not the final decision. When the people building the intelligence and the people wielding the weapons both say the machine cannot be trusted with the choice to kill, that convergence is the strongest safety mechanism currently in existence. It is also, being merely a convergence of judgment rather than a law, only as durable as the judgment holds.

What the robot war asks

From the balcony, the robot war forces a question this series has circled since Part VI, and can now state at full weight.

We have spent this series marvelling at machines that dance, that fold laundry, that haul cargo, that resemble us — and insisting, each time, that the wonder is real and worth defending. The battlefield is where that insistence meets its hardest test, because the very capabilities that make a home robot useful — perception, coordination, autonomy, the ability to act when the human link fails — are the capabilities that make a weapon terrible. There is no bright line in the engineering between the machine that catches a falling glass and the machine that catches a falling soldier in its sights. The force-feedback that lets a robot dance a Drunken Fist, as Part IX observed, is the same force-feedback that steadies a mounted gun. The technology does not know which war it is fighting. Only the humans do, and only for as long as they keep the decision.

A Canadian reads this with the particular unease of a country that has always been a middle power in a world of larger armies — a country whose security has depended less on the size of its own forces than on the rules that restrain everyone’s. The robot war is, among other things, a stress test of whether rules can restrain a technology that is cheap, decentralized, dual-use, and improving faster than any treaty process can track. Chemical weapons, the analysts remind us, were eventually brought under imperfect but real international control after they horrified the world. The open question of our decade is whether autonomous weapons will be brought under control before they horrify us, or only after — and whether the membrane the operators are holding by hand today can be turned into something more durable than one commander’s conscience before the pressures tear it.

The position fell to no one. The drone killed a drone. The machines are here, on the battlefield, doing the oldest work in the newest way, and the humans who command them are telling us, with rare unanimity, exactly where the line must hold. Whether we listen — whether we build the law before we need it rather than after — is not a question the machines can answer. It is, still, for now, ours.

The future keeps sending postcards. This one came from the front, and it does not bleed, and that is precisely the problem.
