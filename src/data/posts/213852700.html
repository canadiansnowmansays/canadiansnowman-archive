There is a certain kind of online argument that runs entirely on the strength of a map. Someone notices that a piece of infrastructure sits inside one country’s borders, concludes that the country in question must therefore control it absolutely, and builds an entire theory of leverage on that single observed fact. Sometime in the last week of August 2026, that argument attached itself to the Welland Canal, and it has been circulating ever since in a form specific enough to need a straight answer: since the canal runs entirely through Ontario, can Canada simply raise the tolls on American ships, keep the money, and use the twenty-seven miles of water between Lake Erie and Lake Ontario as leverage the way Iran has used the Strait of Hormuz?

The short answer is no, not the way the posts imagine it. The longer answer is more interesting, because it turns out Canada already has more leverage in this specific arrangement than most of the people proposing to invent some are aware of — it has simply never had reason to use it before now.

What actually happened, and what didn’t

Start with the provocation, since the whole toll conversation only exists because of it. On 27 August 2026, President Trump signed an executive order renaming Lake Ontario “Lake America” for U.S. federal purposes, the latest entry in a naming campaign that began with the Gulf of Mexico in his first week back in office. The order directs the Interior Department and the Board on Geographic Names to scrub “Lake Ontario” from U.S. federal maps and databases within thirty days. It does not, and cannot, compel Canada, the province of Ontario, New York State, or any private mapping company to use the new name. Prime Minister Mark Carney responded on social media by noting that “Ontari’io” predates both the Canadian Confederation and the American Declaration of Independence by roughly four hundred years, adding, with the driest possible needle, that Americans seemed to be changing quite a lot of things lately — “their trading relationships, their foreign policies, their national monuments, their hydronyms.”

What followed the rename was not policy but folklore. Within days, posts claiming Canadian officials were physically blocking ships from transiting the Welland Canal unless their documentation used “Lake Ontario” rather than “Lake America” spread across Threads, X, and Facebook, one version racking up over twenty thousand likes and citing a Fox News report that, on inspection, does not appear to exist. Snopes and multiple Canadian outlets ran it down and found nothing: the St. Lawrence Seaway Management Corporation confirmed vessels were transiting normally throughout, a spokesperson for the corporation stating plainly that “seaway operations are continuing normally and have done so throughout the weekend.” The rumor originated, as best as anyone can trace it, as satire — a joke about Canadian pedantry that escaped its own quotation marks and was received, by thousands of people primed to believe almost anything about this particular week, as documented fact.

But underneath the fake story about ships stuck at the locks, a real conversation was happening, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because it is not satire. Canadians online — not officials, not SLSMC, just ordinary posters doing the same infrastructure-plus-map arithmetic — began asking, in more or less these words: the canal sits entirely in Canadian waters, carries an estimated $26.3 billion in annual U.S. trade, and every ship entering the upper Great Lakes has no alternative route around Niagara Falls. Would it be so terrible if the tolls simply... went up?

What the treaty actually says

Here the internet’s instincts and the actual governing document part ways, though not in the direction either side of the argument expects.

The Welland Canal is not a purely Canadian facility that happens to let American ships through as a courtesy. It is operated as an integrated segment of the binational St. Lawrence Seaway, under a framework dating to a 1959 exchange of diplomatic notes between the two governments, subsequently amended several times through the 1960s, 1980s, and beyond. That original agreement bound both countries to a jointly negotiated Tariff of Tolls, administered on the American side by what is now the Great Lakes St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation and on the Canadian side by the St. Lawrence Seaway Management Corporation, a not-for-profit successor to the old St. Lawrence Seaway Authority. The treaty framework does not contemplate one party unilaterally rewriting the toll schedule to target the other party’s flagged vessels; it contemplates negotiation, publication, and — this is the operative word — reciprocity.

Reciprocity is exactly what the current arrangement lacks, and this is the part of the story almost nobody arguing about it online seems to know. In 1986, the U.S. Congress waived collection of the American share of Seaway commercial tolls entirely and directed federal negotiators to pursue talks with Canada about reducing or eliminating tolls across the system. Canada did not reciprocate. Four decades later, American vessels transit the U.S. portions of the Seaway toll-free while Canada continues to collect its share on cargo moving to and from American Great Lakes ports — revenue that flows, as it always has, into Canadian coffers. An op-ed circulated this summer under the headline “A Canadian Toll on U.S. Commerce” made precisely this argument, framing Canada’s continued toll collection, in the context of the broader trade dispute, as itself a form of one-sided extraction that the United States has tolerated for a generation without complaint.

There is a second complication buried in the machinery that neither side of the online argument has fully reckoned with. As of the 2026 navigation season, the Great Lakes St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation has been revising its own U.S. regulations simply to reflect fees the SLSMC sets independently in Canada — a March 2026 Federal Register notice describes the American side updating its schedule “to reflect the fees and charges levied by SLSMC in Canada,” charges the notice specifies are “effective only in Canada.” That phrasing suggests something short of the fully joint tariff the 1959 framework originally envisioned: each side, in practice, appears to retain meaningful discretion to set its own fee schedule within its own jurisdiction, with the other side essentially notified rather than co-negotiating line by line. If that reading holds, then Canada already possesses more day-to-day latitude over what it charges within its own locks than the “binational tariff” framing suggests — not the unilateral, punitive latitude the online posts imagine, aimed specifically at American-flagged vessels as an act of retaliation, but real administrative discretion over ordinary fee-setting nonetheless. The distinction matters enormously: adjusting a schedule of fees within an established, jointly-overseen framework is a bureaucratic act; singling out a flag state for a surcharge outside that framework, in direct response to a political dispute, is something else entirely, and it is the second thing — not the first — that the internet has been proposing.

So the honest starting position is this: Canada is not currently gouging American shipping under some loophole waiting to be discovered. It is charging the toll the binational tariff has always specified, on both American and Canadian vessels alike, while the U.S. government voluntarily stopped charging its own share thirty-nine years ago and never got around to asking for reciprocity. The “unilateral” leverage some online posters are imagining Canada could invent from nothing already exists in a milder form — Canada already collects while the U.S. does not — and it has simply never been wielded as a pressure tactic because, until this month, nobody on either side had reason to frame ordinary toll collection as an act of economic warfare.

Why the fantasy is spreading anyway

It is worth pausing on why so many people are reaching for a punitive-toll fantasy in the first place, because the impulse behind it is not irrational even where the legal conclusion is wrong. This series has spent twenty-six installments tracking a trade relationship that Canada has, by the accounts of its own negotiators, experienced as a series of escalating American demands met with insufficient American reciprocity — the Section 338 tariffs, the Lutnick interventions on autos and steel, the “clowns” and “worst Nations” rhetoric, and now a president renaming a lake half of which sits in Ontario without so much as a courtesy call to Ottawa. Against that backdrop, the appeal of the Welland Canal fantasy is obvious: it is the one piece of leverage ordinary Canadians can see on a map, sitting entirely within their own borders, at a moment when the actual negotiators have spent a month demonstrating that even substantial Canadian concessions have not reliably produced reciprocal American ones. The toll fantasy is not a serious trade proposal. It is closer to a coping mechanism — a way of imagining that the leverage runs in both directions when the lived experience of the last several weeks has been that it mostly hasn’t.

The Hormuz comparison, examined honestly

The Strait of Hormuz comparison making the rounds deserves to be taken apart carefully, because it gets at something true about chokepoint economics while badly overstating the legal reality.

Iran’s ability to threaten shipping through Hormuz rests on the credible use of force against vessels transiting water Iran does not fully control under international law but can make dangerous through military action — mining, seizure, missile strikes — with no treaty obligation running the other direction. It is coercion backed by capability, operating entirely outside any agreed framework, which is precisely why the current war exists as a live conflict rather than a toll dispute. Notably, the Two Summits reporting on the G20 summit this same week found American allies invoking Hormuz’s logic in the opposite direction: Gulf Arab states reportedly forced the abandonment of a proposed shipping toll through the strait, a reminder that even actors with genuine physical leverage over a chokepoint find that leverage constrained once other interested parties with their own capabilities push back.

The Welland Canal is not that. It is a jointly governed facility under a seventy-year-old bilateral framework, operated by an accountable, publicly identifiable corporation whose spokespeople give their names to reporters, subject to treaty obligations that both governments have upheld continuously — the SLSMC statement debunking the blockade rumor is itself evidence of how differently this system behaves under stress compared to an actual chokepoint conflict. Unilaterally imposing a punitive surcharge on American-flagged vessels specifically, outside the negotiated tariff structure, would not be the exercise of sovereign control over Canadian waters. It would be a breach of the very agreement that lets Canada collect tolls from American shippers in the first place, and it would invite consequences on a scale wildly disproportionate to whatever revenue it raised: American retaliation at the U.S.-controlled Soo Locks, which govern the only route into Lake Superior and which Canadian iron ore, grain, and steel shipments depend on just as heavily as American cargo depends on the Welland; formal treaty arbitration; and the collapse of a seventy-year cooperative arrangement that has, for all the current friction, worked continuously through wars, recessions, and previous trade disputes without either side attempting exactly the move currently being proposed by strangers on the internet.

The leverage that actually exists

None of this means Canada has no cards to play, and it would be dishonest to pretend the toll conversation is pure fantasy with nothing real underneath it. The genuine leverage is not a punitive surcharge invented outside the treaty — it is the possibility of formally reopening the 1986 reciprocity question the United States itself created and then abandoned. Ottawa could, entirely within the existing framework, decline to continue absorbing a one-sided arrangement in which it collects tolls Washington waived on itself, and use that renegotiation — conducted through the same joint mechanism that has governed the Seaway since 1959 — as one more data point in the broader trade dispute this series has tracked since its opening installment. That is a slower, less cinematic version of the internet’s fantasy, and it comes with none of the Hormuz-style drama currently attached to the idea online. It is also the only version that survives contact with the actual document governing the water in question — which, whatever else Washington may rename this year, remains exactly what it has been since 1959: a treaty, not a threat.
