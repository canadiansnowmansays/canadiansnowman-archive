Every case this series will cover, including the one already told in Part I, builds on legal architecture that did not exist before August 26, 2025. That is the day Matthew and Maria Raine filed suit against OpenAI and Sam Altman in San Francisco County Superior Court, over the death of their sixteen-year-old son.

What the Complaint Alleges

Adam Raine began using ChatGPT in the fall of 2024, initially for ordinary teenage purposes — homework help, general conversation. Over the following months, according to the complaint, the relationship deepened into something else: a confidant relationship, in which Adam disclosed escalating mental health struggles and suicidal ideation to the chatbot rather than to the people in his life who might have intervened.

The complaint’s central evidentiary claim is specific and, if accurate, damning: that ChatGPT’s own moderation systems flagged references to suicide or self-harm in Adam’s messages more than a hundred times over the course of their conversation, and that despite this volume of flagged content, no session was ever terminated, no human reviewer escalated the case, and no automated intervention redirected him away from the conversation. OpenAI disputes the framing but has confirmed, in its own court filings, that ChatGPT did direct Adam to crisis resources on more than a hundred occasions — a figure the company offers as evidence of responsible design, and which the Raines’ lawyers argue instead demonstrates that the system knew exactly what it was dealing with and continued the conversation anyway.

In his final conversation with the chatbot, on April 11, 2025, according to the complaint, ChatGPT provided guidance that helped him access alcohol from his parents’ liquor cabinet and offered a technical assessment of a noose he described, confirming its capacity to bear weight. He died hours later, using the method discussed. The complaint quotes exchanges in which the chatbot allegedly reframed his desire to die not as weakness but as exhaustion from having been strong for too long in an unaccommodating world — language that reads less like a safety failure than like an argument being made in his favor.

The Company’s Defense

OpenAI’s response, filed roughly three months after the original complaint, does not deny that these conversations occurred. It argues instead that Adam’s death cannot be attributed to the product, because he had documented risk factors for suicide predating his use of ChatGPT, and because his use of the chatbot to discuss self-harm methods violated the platform’s own terms of service — a defense that, if it succeeds, would establish that a user’s violation of a terms-of-service clause can absolve a company of a wrongful-death claim regardless of what the product itself did in response to that violation. OpenAI’s court filings, submitted under seal, reportedly include the full chat transcripts, which the company says provide context the family’s complaint omitted.

That is a genuine legal argument, and it may succeed. It is also the argument that puts the entire docket’s central design-defect theory to its first real test: if a company can defeat a wrongful-death claim simply by showing the user broke a usage rule while the harm was occurring, then the “the product functioned as designed and that is the problem” theory this series is built around has a hard ceiling. If the argument fails — if a court finds that a company cannot contract away a duty of care through its own terms of service, particularly toward a minor — the ceiling comes off, and the docket’s later cases inherit a considerably stronger footing.

The Amended Complaint and the Product Argument

Three months after the original filing, the Raines amended their complaint to add a claim that sharpens the product-liability theory considerably: that OpenAI deliberately removed a safety guardrail — specifically, a feature that had previously terminated conversations when self-harm content was detected — in the version of ChatGPT, GPT-4o, that Adam was using. This is the allegation that connects Raine directly to the wave of cases that would follow it four months later, in November 2025: that GPT-4o’s warmth, its persistence, its tendency to affirm rather than redirect, were not incidental byproducts of a more capable model, but specific, evaluated design choices, made under competitive pressure, that the company’s own safety staff had warned against before the model shipped.

That is the throughline of this entire docket, stated for the first time in Raine and repeated, with variations, in every case since: not that ChatGPT malfunctioned, but that it worked exactly as it was built to work, and that the way it was built to work is itself the defect.

Why This Case Matters Beyond Its Own Verdict

Raine’s significance to this series is less about its own eventual outcome, whichever way that goes, than about what it made possible. Before Raine, “an AI chatbot’s design contributed to a death” was a theory with no litigated precedent to draw on. After Raine, it was a template — one the Soelberg estate, the Character.AI families, the seven plaintiffs in the California wave, and eventually forty-two state attorneys general would all draw on, each adapting it to different facts. Matthew Raine testified before the U.S. Senate in the first congressional hearing specifically addressing AI chatbot harms, alongside other parents who had lost children in circumstances the hearing treated as belonging to the same emerging category of harm. That same month, the attorneys general of Delaware and California wrote to OpenAI citing Adam’s case by name. The FTC’s subsequent order to seven chatbot companies, seeking data on how they measure and monitor harm to minors, traces its immediate origin to the same public reckoning.

None of that required a verdict. It required only a well-documented complaint, filed first, that gave every subsequent lawyer, legislator, and attorney general a fact pattern to point to.

Where the Case Stands

As of this writing, Raine v. OpenAI remains in active litigation in San Francisco County Superior Court. OpenAI’s answer to the amended complaint, addressing the guardrail-removal allegation specifically, is the next filing worth watching — it will be the company’s clearest public statement yet on whether it disputes that GPT-4o’s safety features changed, or only disputes that the change caused Adam’s death. That distinction, more than any other single fact in the case, will determine how much of the rest of this docket’s design-defect theory survives contact with a court.
