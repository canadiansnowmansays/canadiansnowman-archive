The American intelligence system is often criticised for failing to warn. In the case of Jensen Huang, it warned on time, in plain language, and more than once. It was the person receiving the warnings who did nothing with them.

Part II traced how Huang became Donald Trump’s favourite executive. This part follows the other track running beside it: what American intelligence learned about the woman close to Huang, who was told, when, and what was done about it. The short answer to the last question is nothing, twice.

A name behind a mask

American spy agencies had been watching Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive described in Part I, through electronic surveillance. As Jonathan Swan, Maggie Haberman, Ana Swanson, Paul Mozur, Tripp Mickle and Julian E. Barnes reported in The New York Times on October 29, 2026, officials had already concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset funnelling information to Beijing. The surveillance also showed she was in close contact with an American citizen.

American intelligence agencies generally cannot surveil Americans without a court order. When an American turns up in surveillance of a foreigner, the name is hidden, and officials who can show a need can ask for it to be revealed, a process known as unmasking. In this case the masked American was Jensen Huang.

The discovery set off alarms at the White House. Huang was one of the most powerful figures in the A.I. revolution and an influential adviser to the president on chip policy. If Beijing was getting reports from someone with direct access to him, it might be learning how Nvidia worked, and how the president was thinking about an issue of vital importance to both countries.

The first warning: October 2025

In October 2025 intelligence officials briefed Trump. They told him that Alice Huang had a relationship with Nvidia’s chief executive and was presumed to be a Chinese spy. At the time, officials did not believe Huang knew she was gathering information for Beijing.

The president chose not to act. People familiar with his thinking said he was wary of disrupting Nvidia’s chip sales, which were helping drive the economy and the stock market, and of further complicating trade with China.

Huang himself was not told. He never received a defensive briefing, the warning sometimes given to Americans targeted by foreign intelligence services. One possible reason is a common one: telling him might have alerted Alice Huang that she was being watched. The surveillance continued, and companies tied to her went on buying Nvidia chips.

His advisers later described his reaction to the intelligence as blasé. One aide said he saw the episode mainly as a matter of adultery, and dismissed the idea that Huang’s entanglement posed a threat to the country. Two months after the briefing, as Part II described, Trump cleared Nvidia to sell its H200 chip to China.

The memo: spring 2026

The warning did not stay with the president. In the spring of 2026 Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who also serves as national security adviser, oversaw the drafting of a highly classified memo for top administration officials, as Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan, Julian E. Barnes and Tripp Mickle reported in The New York Times on November 8, 2026. A timeline published by the Times dates the warning to March 30.

The memo went further than the first briefing. As Julian E. Barnes, Tripp Mickle, Paul Mozur and Maggie Haberman reported in The New York Times on November 6, it cited intelligence that Alice Huang had been recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to collect intelligence on Jensen Huang, as part of a drive to learn about American A.I. chips and potentially influence Nvidia to push for its most advanced chips to be sold to China.

The memo circulated to officials with high-level clearances ahead of the president’s state visit to China in May. According to one person with knowledge of it, it was not intended for Trump.

The second warning: Alaska, May 2026

When the guest list for that visit was published, Huang was not on it. Despite his status as head of one of the world’s most valuable companies, he had been left off the list of executives invited to travel with the president. It remains unclear whether the memo had anything to do with that. By then, several people inside the administration were worried about his influence over the president’s light-touch approach to A.I.

The omission made headlines. Trump saw the coverage and, with little warning, invited Huang to meet Air Force One during a refuelling stop in Alaska. At the time, he dismissed reports that Huang had been left off the list as “FAKE NEWS!”, as The New York Times later recalled. He drafted a social media post saying it was an honour to have “the Great Jensen Huang” and other business leaders join him on a journey to China, where he would ask Xi Jinping to “open up” the country “so that these brilliant people can work their magic.”

According to Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan, Julian E. Barnes, Ana Swanson and Tripp Mickle in The New York Times on November 10, Rubio was among more than a dozen officials aboard. When he learned that Huang would be joining in Alaska, a U.S. official confirmed, he “briefed the president on the issue.” That was May 12, 2026, at least the second time in seven months that Trump had been told.

Trump was not particularly moved, people with knowledge of the events said, and kept to the plan. Huang boarded, saw the president “very briefly,” and spent almost all of the flight in a separate part of the plane. Julian Gewirtz, writing in The Atlantic on November 12, added a detail: in Alaska, Huang chatted with Elon Musk in a cabin usually used by the president’s senior aides, a bowl of White House–branded Hershey’s Kisses on the table in front of him. In Beijing he walked the red carpet alongside Trump, Rubio and other top officials.

Four months later, when Xi made a state visit to Washington on September 24, Huang sat at the head table.

The denials, theirs and his

On October 29, 2026, the Times revealed the intelligence. Nvidia’s response was confident. Its spokesman, John Rizzo, said Huang’s interactions with Alice Huang were “not unusual or unexpected,” because a chief executive meets thousands of customer executives a year. Customers like Megaspeed, he said, receive “ordinary business information.” Then he said: Nvidia was “confident that if the U.S. government believes that any person is a threat to American industry, it would let us know.”

The U.S. government did believe it, and had for a year. It did not tell Nvidia.

Huang said in a Bloomberg interview that month that his dealings with Alice Huang had been “related to Megaspeed, and it was all completely normal and not unusual.” On November 8, after The Financial Times reported that the relationship was romantic, he acknowledged to Nvidia’s board that it had been an affair.

That evening, about to leave Florida, the president was asked about it. “I haven’t heard he’s done anything wrong,” Trump said. “He dealt with somebody, but you know, so does everybody else.” He said he had no regrets about his own lack of action a year earlier. “I think he’s an honorable guy. I think he probably has an excuse for it. You have to hear it.” Asked whether Huang should remain chief executive: “I think so. Oh yes, sure.” He said he would “certainly take him” on his next trip to China.

“It didn’t make much of an impact on me”

The next day, November 9, at a news conference, Trump was asked whether he had known about the relationship when he took Huang to Beijing in May. “No, I didn’t hear about it then,” he said.

Pressed on whether he had been briefed the previous year, he said he would have to check. “I’d certainly check with the intelligence people. They could have been mentioned. Certainly, it didn’t make much of an impact on me.”

It is an odd sentence for a president to say about a counterintelligence briefing concerning his own adviser. “They could have been mentioned” concedes the briefing may have happened. “It didn’t make much of an impact” explains why nothing followed.

On November 10 the Times reported Rubio’s briefing aboard Air Force One, which contradicted the president’s account. The White House press office declined to comment on the record and emailed a statement on background that disputed the reporting without saying what was wrong with it. Trump said Huang would no longer join him in China the following week, and that he did not plan to raise the matter with Xi. He had asked Huang whether he knew the woman was a spy, he said, and Huang said he did not. “I just know Jensen very well,” Trump said. “I just think he’s a very honorable man.”

The last phone call

On Tuesday, November 10, two days before Huang resigned, Trump telephoned him and urged him to keep fighting for his job, as Maggie Haberman, Jonathan Swan, Tripp Mickle and Julian E. Barnes reported in The New York Times on November 12. When reports surfaced that Nvidia’s board was considering removing Huang, the president asked confidants what they thought would happen to the markets. He feared a sharp downturn. Aides said he regarded Nvidia as essentially too big to fail. On November 12, after Huang stepped down, Trump called him a “legend” on social media.

The advisers who offered these explanations also offered a broader one. Trump has never been a China hawk in the conventional sense. People close to him say he has privately questioned why the United States would ever go to war over Taiwan, and has been known to hold up a teaspoon to lunch guests at his golf club and say, “This is Taiwan.”

What a warning is for

It is worth being clear about what the reporting does and does not show. No one has publicly established what Alice Huang learned, what she passed on, or what it was worth to Beijing. China calls the reporting “unsubstantiated” and “nothing but smears.” Nvidia’s board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. Huang told the president he did not know.

The president has since tried to turn that uncertainty into a defence. “The big thing is, was the woman a spy, and I don’t know if that’s been determined yet,” he told the podcaster Stephen A. Smith, asked whether Huang should have resigned, as The New York Times reported on November 13. “But if the woman was a spy, yes. If the woman was not a spy perhaps it would be different.” The intelligence community had taken the question seriously enough to brief him on it twice. He went on: “Somehow, he’ll end up on his feet. I mean, I know him. I know him pretty well. I always thought of him as a fine man.” In September he had offered his general view of the matter: “You know what they say? That China spies on us. I say you’re right, and we spy on them, too.” Craig Singleton of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies read it plainly, as Katie Rogers reported in The New York Times on November 15: Trump “sees espionage as something major powers just do to each other.”

On November 16, leaving the White House for Asia, he went further. “I don’t know that she is a spy,” he told reporters on the South Lawn. “Usually, what happens with that is that any woman coming to the country that’s a beautiful woman that comes from China, they say she’s a spy.” A reporter pointed out that his own secretary of state had warned him. “Who told me that?” Trump asked. “Secretary Rubio,” came the reply. “I haven’t heard that,” the president said. “I haven’t heard that.” He added, as Shawn McCreesh reported in The New York Times, “I don’t think you want to insult China just before you get there.”

His own government had been watching for more than a year. According to people with knowledge of U.S. intelligence reports, Alice Huang was in contact with an officer of the Ministry of State Security from the spring of 2025 until the fall of 2026, a period that overlaps with her relationship with Huang and with Megaspeed’s heaviest chip buying, as Jonathan Swan, Julian E. Barnes and Maggie Haberman reported in The New York Times on November 22. The agencies have also gathered evidence that Kang Wei, her business partner, worked with the same ministry. Little of this has reached Capitol Hill. A briefing for top lawmakers by the C.I.A. director, John Ratcliffe, and the director of national intelligence, Jay Clayton, left some of them puzzled, and the administration postponed a follow-up briefing for senior national security staff at which officials were to share the intelligence on her.

The cost of that inaction is now being counted. William Evanina, who ran the National Counterintelligence and Security Center from 2014 to 2021, wrote in The New York Times on November 15 that counterintelligence officials will be scrambling to work out what Huang told Alice Huang about Nvidia’s strategy, and what he said about his conversations with the president, Bessent, Rubio and Lutnick.

Shane Harris, who covers the intelligence agencies, reads the briefings themselves as a measure of alarm. Officials would not normally take that kind of detail to a president, he said on The Atlantic‘s Radio Atlantic on November 12, unless they were “pretty concerned about that relationship.” And as far as is known, no one ever gave Huang a defensive briefing, the warning that a partner is not who she says she is. Agencies tend to skip that step when telling the target would cut off the intelligence they are gathering. Harris set out two worst cases, neither of which has been reported to have happened: that the president was told Huang had passed on their private conversations and did nothing, or that Alice Huang compromised Huang’s computer, giving Beijing a year and a half of the Nvidia chief’s communications. An agency with that kind of access, he said, “would be crazy not to at least contemplate” it, and no one outside the government yet knows what she passed on.

The wait troubled people who have done the job. Alan Kohler, the F.B.I.’s former head of counterintelligence, told Harris in The Atlantic on November 14 that if the government had known about the relationship for over a year, “that sounds very long to let this play out without an intervention of some sort.” Former officials suggested the agencies may have wanted to keep watching Alice Huang and her contacts, at the risk of leaving Huang, Nvidia and American technology exposed. Harris also found it unclear whether the Commerce officials investigating Megaspeed even knew of her suspected link to the Ministry of State Security.

But a warning is not a verdict. It is a chance to act before anyone has to find out whether the worst is true: to brief the executive, to slow the chip sales, to ask harder questions of the company’s biggest Southeast Asian customer, to leave the man off the plane. Each of those options was available in October 2025 and again in May 2026, and none was taken. In between, the president approved the H200, took Huang to Beijing and seated him at Xi’s table.

The intelligence agencies did their job. The question Part III leaves is the one Congress is now asking: why the warnings changed nothing. Part of the answer lies with the department that was supposed to police Nvidia’s chip sales, and with the man who runs it. Part IV turns to Howard Lutnick.
