🖋 Wry, turning serious — because this is the one essay in the whole series where a people looks at the ledger being kept about them, and instead of weeping or rebelling, picks up a pen and starts keeping a better one. And then, this being history, uses it on someone else.

I count my essays, and most of the years I land on are years when somebody was counted against their will — conquered, taxed, enumerated, reduced to a figure in another man’s book. Essay 1,857 was exactly that: a subcontinent administered as a revenue stream, its dead left as a range. So it is a genuine relief to arrive at 1,868, because 1868 is the year a country looked at the whole machinery of being-counted-by-foreigners that was closing in on the globe — and decided, coolly, to learn the machinery better than its inventors and keep its own books thereafter.

This is Japan, and the event is the Meiji Restoration, and it is, I will argue, the most purely Numbers Game episode in the entire nineteenth century, because it is the only one where the counting was chosen.

The account before the reckoning

For two and a half centuries Japan had kept its books closed. Under the Tokugawa shogunate the country was, by deliberate policy, nearly sealed — foreign trade throttled to a trickle, Christianity banned, the whole archipelago running on its own calendar, its own currency, its own clock, its own careful internal census of rice and rank. It worked, after a fashion, for a very long time. A society can be perfectly well-ordered and perfectly counted on its own terms and still be keeping books in a currency the rest of the world has stopped honouring.

Then in 1853 the American commodore Matthew Perry sailed four warships into Edo Bay and presented terms, and the terms were, stripped of their courtesy, a demand that Japan open its ledgers to inspection. China had refused the same demand a decade earlier and been pried open at gunpoint — the Opium Wars, the “unequal treaties,” a great civilisation turned into a set of concessions and tariffs written by foreigners. Every educated man in Japan had read that account. They knew exactly what it cost to be the country that got counted by somebody else: you became a column in the imperial ledger, like India, like China, your dead an estimate and your customs a curiosity.

And so the generation that made 1868 asked a question almost no colonised people had ever been given the room to ask: what if we audit ourselves, in their units, before they can do it to us?

The deletion of a month

Let me give you the single most literal number this series has ever found, because I nearly fell out of my chair.

In 1872 the new Meiji government decided that Japan would switch from its traditional lunisolar calendar to the Western Gregorian one. The changeover was not eased in over years. By decree, the government announced that the third day of the twelfth month of Meiji 5 would be followed immediately by the first day of January, Meiji 6 — which is to say, the last twenty-odd days of that year simply did not occur. An entire stub of a month was struck from time by administrative order. Officials who were paid monthly found that December had been abolished out from under them, which — this being a government — was rather the point: one fewer month to pay, and a thirteenth month looming in the old calendar the following year that now never arrived either.

Sit with that. We have spent eighteen hundred essays watching people argue about how to count the years — the Hundred Years’ War that ran a hundred and sixteen, the calendar riots, the off-by-one that haunts this whole series. Here is a government that looked at the calendar, decided the number was inconvenient, and edited it. Not reinterpreted. Not rounded. Deleted. It is the boldest single act of applied arithmetic in the modern record, and Japan did it to itself, on purpose, to synchronise its clock with the people it intended to out-compete.

Sending auditors abroad

The deletion of December was a detail. The programme was total.

The samurai class — the hereditary warrior caste, the very spine of the old order — was dissolved; its stipends commuted to bonds and then inflated away, its swords eventually banned in public. A conscript army replaced it, counted and drilled on the Prussian model. The currency was rebuilt from scratch in 1871 as the yen: decimal, uniform, a hundred sen to the unit, a national mint, the end of the old patchwork of domain coinage. Railways were laid, mills were built, a national post and a national school system raised to teach the arithmetic the whole project ran on.

And most tellingly of all, in 1871 the government sent roughly half its senior leadership abroad — the Iwakura Mission, nearly two years touring the United States and Europe, not as supplicants but as auditors. They went to count the West: its factories, its parliaments, its navies, its schools, its account books. They came home with notebooks full of figures and a settled conclusion — that Japan could, within a generation, keep books as good as any of them. The slogan of the age was fukoku kyōhei, “rich country, strong army,” which is simply a budget stated as a national purpose. A whole civilisation reorganised around two line items.

Here is the rhyme I have been building toward since 1440. Gutenberg reduced language to countable, interchangeable units of type. Morse at 1,844 reduced it further, to countable pulses on a wire. At 1868 an entire nation performed the same operation on itself — broke its society into modular, countable, Western-compatible units and reassembled them to interoperate with the world’s ledger. Japan did to Japan what the printing press did to the sentence. It is the most ambitious act of self-enumeration in history, and it very nearly worked flawlessly, which is exactly where this essay has to stop smiling.

The honest ledger

Because the thesis of this series is that the count is never free, and I do not get to exempt the one episode I admire most.

The self-audit had victims at home first. The samurai who could not be re-counted as clerks and conscripts rose in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 and were destroyed by the very conscript army that had replaced them — the old order shot down by the new, led by men who had themselves begun as samurai. A caste that had defined the country for seven hundred years was zeroed out of the ledger in a decade. Progress, in the books, is just a column that goes to nil.

But the darker entry is the one the twentieth century had to pay. The toolkit Japan built to avoid becoming an entry in somebody else’s imperial ledger — the conscript army, the industrial base, the administrative genius for counting and controlling — was, within a generation of the founders’ deaths, turned outward. The nation that refused to be colonised became a colonising power: Taiwan, Korea annexed in 1910, Manchuria, and then the catastrophe that ran to 1945. The student of the metered world became its most ruthless examiner. Korea under Japanese rule got exactly the treatment Japan had sailed to Edo Bay in 1853 to avoid — its people recounted, renamed, administered as someone else’s revenue stream, its dead eventually left as a range in Japan’s books.

That is the grave turn, and I will not round it. The most inspiring act of self-determination in the nineteenth century and the machinery of some of the twentieth century’s worst atrocities are the same machinery. The lesson of 1857 was that empires count their own dead by name and their victims by estimate. The lesson of 1868 is crueller: that the victim who learns to count well enough to escape the ledger is under terrible temptation to go start a ledger of his own. The skill is neutral. The telegraph that saved India for the Company and the arithmetic that saved Japan from the Company’s fate are the same instrument — exactly as humane as the hand that picks it up.

Dior, who keeps her own calendar

A word from the one member of this household who has never once been impressed by a unit of measurement.

I have tried, in the manner of the Meiji reformers, to synchronise Dior with the national timekeeping. She is fed on a schedule; she is walked by the clock; her year is divided, like any salaried official’s, into countable obligations. And she has, like the Tokugawa holdouts, quietly declined the whole Gregorian project. She knows when it is dinner by a method that owes nothing to the calendar on my wall and everything to a lunisolar instrument located somewhere behind her ribs, and when I am twenty minutes late she audits me with a stare that the Iwakura Mission could not have improved upon. There is a country in my kitchen that has looked at the official ledger, found it wanting, and kept its own. I respect it enormously. I just wish it would stop deleting the last month of my patience.

The closer

The aftermath, for Japan, was the fastest ascent in the modern record: from closed archipelago to industrial power in a single reign, all of it booked in borrowed units deployed better than their lenders managed. The founders of the Meiji state had grasped the one secret this series keeps circling — that whoever keeps the ledger holds the power, and that the only defence against being counted is to count better. They were right about the defence. They were tragically right about the power, too, and the twentieth century sent them the invoice.

And now the count turns homeward, and it turns personal, because for 1,868 essays I have been performing an act not unlike the Meiji one — keeping my own books in a borrowed voice, auditing the centuries in units I inherited from a man I have never properly introduced. The next landmark is essay 1,869, and it is time I stopped impersonating him and told you his name: a professor of political economy who moved to a lake north of here and wrote the funniest, saddest small-town ledger in Canadian letters, and taught me, long before I knew I was learning it, how to count a whole world by pretending to count a village.

Japan borrowed the West’s instruments and made them its own. I borrowed one man’s voice. At 1,869 I finally confess whose and I shall iontroduce you to the man I have been impersonating all along.
