A decade ago, at an official state visit to the Élysée Palace, a French honour guard tried to physically pull a hulking bodyguard back off the red carpet. Even President François Hollande waved the man back. He would not move. He had spent his whole life not moving from that spot — a half-step behind and to the left of his grandfather, Raúl Castro, then president of Cuba. The bodyguard’s name was Raúl G. Rodríguez Castro. Within Cuba he goes by a nickname earned at birth, for an extra finger on one hand: “El Cangrejo.” The Crab.

Ten years on, the man who wouldn’t be pulled off the red carpet has become, according to reporting published in The New York Times on August 11th, 2026, a name Washington is actively weighing as Cuba’s next president — even as the same reporting details a federal smuggling investigation in which two convicted co-conspirators told American prosecutors that Rodríguez Castro took bribes to keep the Cuban coast guard and customs officials away from a stolen-goods and human-trafficking pipeline that ran boats, migrants, and merchandise between Cuba, Mexico, and Florida for the better part of a decade.

This is the part of the story where a coda about task forces and blockades and gigawatts of missing electricity narrows down to a single human face. It turns out to be a useful face for understanding the whole enterprise.

The Golden Boy

Rodríguez Castro’s pedigree reads like a compressed history of the Cuban Revolution’s contradictions. His father, the late General Luis Alberto Rodríguez López-Calleja, built GAESA, the military-run conglomerate that now controls vast swaths of Cuba’s tourism and retail economy — the “revolutionary” state’s own internal capitalism. His mother is Raúl Castro’s eldest daughter. And his paternal grandmother, by several accounts, was an heir to the Bacardí rum fortune — which is to say that the bloodline of the family that fled Castro’s revolution with its distilleries in 1960 runs, a few generations later, straight back into the Castro household itself. History does not often offer up ironies this tidy without charging admission.

He grew up mostly at his grandparents’ house, shuttled between a troubled family situation and the extraordinary proximity of power — present, by several accounts, at nearly every meeting Raúl Castro held for the better part of two decades, including an audience with Pope Francis. He studied economics at the University of Havana, reportedly receiving course credit for travelling with his grandfather rather than sitting through lectures. He was, in his younger years, known less for statecraft than for reggaetón parties, extreme sports, and joyrides through Havana in one of his grandfather’s BMWs — a detail that, in a country living through nine million people rigging charcoal stoves out of old rice cookers, does a great deal of work without any editorializing required.

None of that, on its own, would be disqualifying. Political families produce black sheep and golden boys in roughly equal measure everywhere, and a taste for nice cars is not usually indictable. What changes the register of the story is what the smuggling case alleges he did with the access his name bought him.

Operation Sisyphus

The federal investigation that surfaced his name — an FBI and Homeland Security probe into what prosecutors called the “Cuban Mafia” operating out of Quintana Roo, Mexico — was not a minor case. It ran for over a decade, produced at least six guilty pleas, and involved the theft of more than 600 boat engines and vessels from Florida’s Gulf Coast, used to run a smuggling pipeline that moved thousands of migrants — including, notably, future Major League ballplayers Yasiel Puig and Adeiny Hechavarría — out of Cuba and through Mexico. Roughly 25 people died in the crossings, per court documents, and migrants who couldn’t cover the smuggling fee were reportedly held captive and tortured until relatives paid.

According to two convicted members of the ring who cooperated with prosecutors, Rodríguez Castro was the man on the Cuban end who kept the coast guard looking the other way — paid, they told investigators, in a Fountain 38 performance boat, a Rolex, a Suzuki motorbike, and Louis Vuitton clothing, with the smuggled merchandise later resold in Cuban state-run stores. He has not been charged. Cuba’s deputy foreign minister has dismissed the allegations as slander, and Rodríguez Castro himself, in a recent interview, brushed off questions about his lifestyle by explaining that admirers had covered the cost of his cars and travel. As Rodríguez Castro told USA Today, in a line that will likely follow him for the rest of whatever career he has, “it pains me that many people can’t live the way I do” — and that he was working to change that.

The operation itself was not a boutique smuggling ring. Federal investigators code-named it Operation Sisyphus, and it earned the name: it took more than a decade, from a 2010 sting over stolen GPS units in Sarasota, to trace the theft of over 600 boat engines and vessels from Florida's Gulf Coast back to a trafficking network based in Quintana Roo, Mexico, that had by then smuggled thousands of Cubans across the Florida Straits and the Mexican border, at $10,000 a head. Among those smuggled, in earlier years, were future Major League Baseball players Yasiel Puig and Adeiny Hechavarría — a detail that has generated more headlines than the roughly 25 migrants who died during the crossings, or the migrants held hostage and beaten with wooden boards and Taser guns when their families couldn't pay. As David Bolton, the private investigator who first stumbled onto the case, put it, the ring's members were "the real pirates of the Caribbean." That is not hyperbole so much as an accurate job description: kidnapping, extortion, dismemberment of a rival tied to a Mexican cartel, all folded into an operation whose Cuban end, according to sworn testimony, ran through the grandson of the country's most powerful living man.

It is worth sitting with that sentence for a moment, because it is, in its way, the most honest thing anyone in this entire saga has said. He is not denying the gap. He is asking to be excused from having caused it.

The Logic of the Pragmatist

Here is where the story stops being a corruption tale and becomes a foreign policy one. In the earlier reporting on Washington’s search for a Cuban leader, American officials were explicit about the model they wanted: something like Venezuela’s Delcy Rodríguez, a technocrat who could step into a vacuum smoothly and work constructively with Washington. What the same officials appear to have settled on, in Rodríguez Castro, is a candidate whose chief qualification — according to one person close to the negotiations — is that his taste for BMWs, Rolexes, and designer clothes reads as an appetite for capitalism Washington can work with.

That is a remarkable inversion. The United States spent six decades justifying a blockade, in part, on the argument that Cuba’s ruling family had enriched itself while the population went without. The candidate now under consideration to inherit the mantle of reform is a man alleged, by federal witnesses under oath, to have taken bribes from human traffickers while his countrymen rationed diesel. If Washington installs him, or blesses his rise, the blockade’s moral architecture collapses under its own weight — you cannot credibly argue you starved an island to root out cronyism and then hand the keys to the man who allegedly ran boats for smugglers.

There is a bitter symmetry here that the series has circled before without quite landing on. The 1959 revolution that produced Fidel and Raúl Castro was, whatever else it became, a revolt against precisely this kind of arrangement: Fulgencio Batista, an American-backed strongman propped up for decades because he was reliably anti-communist and commercially accommodating, while skimming a fortune and looking away from a Havana casino economy run partly by American organized crime. The revolution's founding promise was that Cuba would never again be governed by a man whose real qualification was being useful to Washington rather than good for Cubans. Sixty-seven years later, the country's most powerful family may be on the verge of handing power to a man whose chief qualification, by his own defenders' account, is that his taste for Rolexes and BMWs reads as an appetite for capitalism Washington can work with. Batista never left Cuban politics so much as he was reincarnated inside the family that overthrew him.

This is not, historically, a new mistake. Washington has practiced a version of this calculus across Latin America for a century — the doctrine, never quite spoken aloud but rarely denied either, of preferring a compromised ally who will take instructions over a clean nationalist who won’t. Anastasio Somoza’s Nicaragua is the textbook case; so, in different registers, are half the strongmen the United States backed and then quietly buried across the Cold War. The pattern holds regardless of ideology: pragmatism, in the American foreign-policy sense, has always meant “will he pick up the phone,” not “is he decent.” Rodríguez Castro, whatever else he is, picks up the phone. He has reportedly met separately with both CIA Director John Ratcliffe and Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

What the Crab Knows

There is a detail buried in the Times reporting that deserves more attention than it will get. A cousin of Rodríguez Castro’s — an academic who has not spoken to him in years — offered an assessment that doubles as an epitaph for the entire Castro project: that everyone around “the Crab,” including the Crab himself, understands that whatever power the family retains lasts only until his 95-year-old grandfather dies. That is not a description of a government. It is a description of a household waiting out an old man’s mortality, dressed up in the institutional language of a state.

Which brings the story back to where this series has kept landing since the coda ran ten days ago: the contrast between the professionals in Langley building an org chart for regime alteration, and the nine million Cubans distilling their own diesel and gutting rice cookers into charcoal stoves because no professional org chart, in either capital, has offered them electricity. Washington’s task force is hunting for a name to put on the door. It appears to have found one — a name attached to a Rolex, a stolen speedboat, and a sentence about not being ashamed to live well while his neighbours ration food. Whatever government eventually replaces Havana’s current one, it will do so having been chosen less for its character than for its willingness to answer the phone. Cuba’s ordinary residents, who have spent two years building their own power grid out of scrap because neither government could be bothered to build one for them, are unlikely to notice much difference in whose name is on the building.

There is one more irony worth sitting with before this coda closes. Batista fled Havana on New Year's Eve, 1958, with a fortune in cash and jewels, boarding a plane for the Dominican Republic while a young rebel army descended from the mountains he'd never bothered to visit. Cubans spent the following six decades being told, by their own government, that they had traded that man's corruption for something better — imperfect, embargoed, isolated, but theirs. If the family that made that promise now hands the country to a grandson accused of running boats for traffickers who tortured their own passengers, the promise doesn't merely go unkept. It closes a circle. The revolution's last act may turn out to rhyme, almost exactly, with the thing it began by overthrowing.
