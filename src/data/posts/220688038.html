At a little before nine o’clock on the evening of Thursday, October 15, 2026, I sat in Toronto watching a livestream from a SpaceX rocket test site in McGregor, Texas, where a white two-seat coupé drove up a curved ramp, kept going until it was upside down, hung there, and then, with a hiss of compressed air, rolled itself back onto its wheels and drove off.

That was the first demonstration. In the second, the car leapt a line of parked Tesla vehicles, a Semi among them, spinning as it went. In the third it reached 60 miles an hour in 0.81 seconds. In the fourth it climbed straight up, rolled, and landed. The audience had been handed earplugs on arrival and warned to expect the roar of a Raptor engine. Nobody, as far as I could tell, had been warned to expect a car that does not need a road.

This was the long-promised second-generation Tesla Roadster, revealed nine years after it was first shown and eight years after it was supposed to be in customers’ garages. It was, by any measure, the most remarkable automotive demonstration I have ever seen. It was also the most carefully arranged, and the arrangement is the story.

What Was Shown

Begin with what Tesla claims, because the claims are extraordinary. As Harvinder Sidhu reported for paultan.org on October 16, 2026, the car carries two carbon-overwrapped pressure vessels holding about 550 pounds of compressed air, feeding ten nozzles: four main thrusters in the rear diffuser and a ring of attitude-control jets around the body. Tesla says they produce about 18,000 pounds of thrust, enough to hold up more than 17,600 pounds against gravity. Underneath, a bank of fans pulls air out from beneath the car. Tyres alone give about 1.26 g of grip; Musk said the fans add up to 2 g, which is how a car stays on a ceiling.

The arithmetic of that 0.81-second run checks out: 0 to 60 mph in that time means an average of about 3.4 g, more than tyres alone can manage. The fans press the car into the ground and the air jets shove it forward; it is a car that gets round physics by bringing a second propulsion system along. Tesla’s own web page is honest about this, under the heading “Beyond The Limits of Physics.”

There are caveats, and the trade press supplied them. The 0.81-second figure came from a single live run that, Sidhu noted, was cut short for lack of space on the pad; a recorded run showed a full second. As Darius Popa wrote for The Next Web on October 16, 2026, the number “has not been independently verified.” Top speed was given as over 230 mph, with Musk aiming for Mach 0.5, roughly 380 mph. That is, for the record, slower than the 250 mph promised in 2017.

None of which made it any less astonishing to watch. But astonishment is the purpose of a stunt, and the question worth asking about any stunt is who it was staged for, and what it was staged to conceal.

The Empty Seat

Here is the detail I noticed first, and that stayed with me after the livestream ended: there was no one in the car.

Musk was candid about it. “It is technically autonomous for this demo,” he told the audience, “to make sure we don’t kill anyone.” During one run, the driver’s seat held Starman, the spacesuited mannequin that rode the first Roadster into orbit on a Falcon Heavy in 2018. Tesla felt obliged to clarify that it was “not a real human.”

Then Musk said the thing that turns a stunt into a contradiction. As Byron Hurd reported for The Drive on October 15, 2026, Musk confirmed that the Roadster will remain human-driven, and that it will be the only Tesla to be so going forward.

Sit with that for a moment. Tesla has just told the world that every other car it makes is headed for a future without a steering wheel, the Cybercab future, the one this series examined in Part XVI. The Roadster is to be the exception: the last car Tesla builds for a person who wants to drive. Tesla’s web page calls it “A True Pilot’s Car.” And on the night it was revealed, the true pilot was a mannequin, and the car was driven by software.

Even the “manual” part bears inspection. The yoke, Tesla says, “is designed specifically for steer by wire.” Sidhu reported that braking and throttle are by-wire too. There is no mechanical connection between the driver’s hands and the front wheels; every input passes through a computer that decides what to do with it. The last manually driven Tesla is manual in roughly the way a video game is manual.

And then there was the line that, had it been uttered by the chief executive of any other carmaker, would have led the evening news. The Roadster, Musk said, is “not necessarily a safety-optimised car.”

That is a curious thing to say in a year when, as Keith Bradsher and Jack Ewing reported for the New York Times on August 21, 2026, in “In China’s Biggest Car Recall, Tesla and 8 Others Will Address Door Safety,” China ordered its largest car recall ever, 4.3 million vehicles from nine manufacturers, almost three million of them Teslas, over retractable door handles that occupants and rescuers could not find or operate after crashes. It is more curious still while Tesla contests a lawsuit brought by the parents of two college students who died inside a burning Cybertruck in Piedmont, California, in 2024, unable, the suit claims, to get out because the electronic doors failed. Tesla argues the victims’ own negligence caused their deaths; a jury trial is scheduled for 2027.

The company that cannot yet guarantee you can open its doors after a crash is now selling a car that flies, and telling you in advance that safety was not the priority.

Three Cars Wearing One Name

The livestream showed one car. Tesla’s Canadian order page, live by the end of the evening, offers three.

The base model is called Performance: “road ready with seating for two,” with active aerodynamics and the suction fans. Then come two “Augments.” Thrust, a “Limited Release,” adds the rear thrusters that “propel Roadster from 0-100 in under 1 second” (km/h, this being the Canadian site), one car per customer, with “availability subject to legal requirements and applicable laws in each region.” And Rocket, “Invite Only,” which is “air ready with seating for one,” with thrusters around the whole perimeter. “So yes,” the page says, “it can fly.” Beneath that, in smaller type: “Note: Not approved for public road use.”

So the car that drove upside down and leapt the Semi is, by Tesla’s own description, a one-seat, invitation-only machine you may not drive on a public road. Footnote 1 on the page, attached to the upside-down suction claim and the flight-stabilising thrusters, reads: “Available with the Rocket trim only, which is not available in all regions.” A second line, directly beneath it, says the features and specifications on the page “are reflective of our Thrust augment only.” The footnote and the line below it cannot both be true, and Tesla printed them one above the other.

Asked whether the car was street legal, Musk replied, as Business Insider reported, “Yeah, depends on your street.”

Paid For Before It Exists

No price was announced for any of the three. What Tesla published instead was a payment schedule.

In Canada, the Performance model requires C$7,000 today, “fully refundable,” followed by C$63,000 within ten days. The Thrust model requires C$7,000 today and C$343,000 within ten days. In American dollars, as The Next Web reported, that is a $50,000 deposit for Performance and $250,000 for Thrust. “Reservations are not confirmed until payment has been received,” the fine print says, and “the reservation may be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days. No refunds will be given for changes.” Production, Musk said, begins in 2028. “Actually,” he added. “For real.”

Those two numbers, $50,000 and $250,000, should be familiar. They are exactly what Tesla charged in November 2017, when it took $50,000 deposits on the standard Roadster and required the full $250,000 up front for a “Founders Series.” Those customers were promised delivery in 2020. As Fred Lambert reported for Electrek on September 20, 2026, the car has since been delayed at least eight times; the first depositors are still waiting; and those who tried to cancel last year had trouble getting their money back. Tesla initially kept $5,000 from some cancellations, including one by the technology reviewer Marques Brownlee, and Sam Altman said he was ghosted when he asked for a refund.

On Thursday night, so far as I could find, nothing was said about them at all.

In Part XIV, I called this the discount rate on belief: the market pays for the promise rather than the balance sheet. The Roadster reservation scheme turns that idea into a payment schedule. A Thrust buyer hands over a third of a million Canadian dollars, interest-free, for a car whose price has not been set, whose road legality depends, in the words of its creator, on your street, and whose production date has already slipped by eight years. The first instalment is refundable. The page does not say what happens to the rest.

The Halo and the Slump

Why build it at all? The most useful answer came hours before the event, from Jack Ewing in the New York Times on October 15, 2026, in a piece headlined “Tesla Leads a Rush to Sell Electric Cars That Cost More Than $100,000.”

Ewing’s point is that Western carmakers, unable to compete with China on affordable electric vehicles, are retreating upmarket, and that the upmarket is a dangerous place to be. Porsche’s electric Taycan, starting at $112,000, saw global sales slump 31 per cent in the first nine months of 2026. Lucid’s sales fell 7 per cent in the third quarter. Lamborghini has postponed its electric car altogether. Musk himself, Ewing noted, has acknowledged that the Roadster is unlikely to generate much revenue, and some analysts have asked why Tesla is spending money on it while sales of the Model 3 and Model Y are slipping.

That is the halo-car argument, and it has a history. On July 15, 2026, in “The American E.V. Has Been Crushed. Will It Take the U.S. Auto Industry With It?”, Matthew Shaer recalled in the New York Times Magazine that the first Roadster, in 2008, was “effectively a Lotus Elise” with an electric motor, a car Musk later admitted “didn’t really work,” sold for the equivalent of $150,000 in today’s money. It was, Shaer wrote, a statement piece: an advertisement for the idea of the electric car. It worked. The Model S followed.

The new Roadster is a statement piece too, but the statement has changed. The first one said an electric car could be desirable. This one says Tesla is not merely a car company. Gene Munster of Deepwater Asset Management, as Deepti Sri reported for Stocktwits on October 15, 2026, called it an “engineering flex with no commercial value beyond embarrassing traditional car makers.” The market, as is its custom, applauded anyway. Tesla shares fell more than 1 per cent on Thursday to $362.44, Sri reported, then rose nearly 5 per cent in premarket trading on Friday, October 16, 2026, as Fiona Craig reported for Yahoo Finance.

A Rocket in the Roadster

Munster’s real point came in his second sentence. The car, he said, was a “mashup of the two tech teams,” and Musk “can’t wait” to bring Tesla and SpaceX together.

Consider the evidence assembled on one evening. The event was held at a SpaceX facility rather than a Tesla one. The propulsion system is SpaceX technology, built around SpaceX’s pressure vessels. The top trim is named Rocket. Musk shared the livestream with the words “Tesla x SpaceX.” The opening segment, by a fan account’s own timestamp, was a “Tesla + SpaceX collab preview.” Tesla’s web page puts it with admirable economy: “We already put Roadster in a rocket. Now we put a rocket in Roadster.” And, as Engadget‘s live blog noted on the afternoon of October 15, 2026, Barclays had suggested a Tesla–SpaceX merger announcement could come later this month.

In Part XIII, I argued that a stock-swap merger of Tesla into SpaceX would solve Musk’s governance problem at a stroke, folding the Tesla board’s fiduciary awkwardness into a company where his Class B shares carry 82 per cent of the votes. In Part XIX, I argued that the compensation milestones on both sides looked designed to be missed. Thursday night supplied the marketing. A merger is easier to sell to shareholders when the product already looks like both companies at once.

Snake River

Near the end of the presentation, Musk teased a future demonstration: a jump across Snake River Canyon in Idaho.

Readers of a certain age will remember the original. On September 8, 1974, Evel Knievel climbed into a steam-powered rocket called the Skycycle X-2 and launched himself at the canyon before a crowd of thousands and a closed-circuit television audience. The parachute deployed early. The rocket drifted back and came down at the bottom of the canyon, on the launch side, a few feet from the river. Knievel survived. The jump did not succeed, and it remains one of the most famous failures in American showmanship.

It is a revealing choice of reference. Knievel’s genius was never the landing; it was the build-up, the television rights, the merchandise, the crowd. The event was the product. “Success is not guaranteed,” Musk said on Thursday, and he deserves credit for saying so. He also said, of the live audience: “We need an audience to prove that it’s not AI.” That is perhaps the most honest sentence of the night. In 2026, a car flying over a Semi looks fake unless people are there to see it in person.

What stays with me is not the flight. It is the empty seat, and what it implies. Tesla has promised that every car it sells in future will drive itself, and that one, the Roadster, will be kept for people who still want to drive. Then it demonstrated that car with a mannequin in the seat and software in control, because a human being might have died. It asked for a quarter of a million dollars per car before naming a price. It said its road legality depends on the street. And it chose, as the next spectacle, a canyon best known for the man who did not make it across.

Stephen Leacock once wrote of a man who “flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions.” He did not foresee a car that could do the same, with nobody in it, and a waiting list to buy one.
