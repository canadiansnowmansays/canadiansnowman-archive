by Stephen Gordon

The count arrives at 1,543, and it concerns the most polite catastrophe in the history of thought. No one marched. No one was burned — not in 1543; the burnings this book would eventually feed came later, and fell on other men. A dying church official in a small town on the Baltic was handed a printed book, and the book contained tables of numbers, and the tables, worked through honestly, removed the Earth from the centre of the universe and everyone you have ever loved along with it. He died within hours or days of touching it, depending on which account you trust, and this series has taught you not to trust any of them completely. The story is that Nicolaus Copernicus received the first printed copy of De Revolutionibus Orbium Coelestium on his deathbed, on May 24, 1543. The story comes to us from a friend’s letter written afterward, which is exactly the provenance of the Wittenberg door, and I have decided to enjoy it in exactly the same way: the deathbed is disputed. The book is not.

Let me tell you about the man, because he is the strangest figure this series has yet admitted: a revolutionary with no interest whatsoever in revolting.

Copernicus was a canon of the cathedral chapter at Frauenburg — a church administrator, essentially, in the far north of Poland, with a medical degree, a law doctorate, and a sideline in currency reform, which I mention because it means the man who moved the Earth spent his working days worrying about debased coinage, and there is a version of this essay that is entirely about that: he understood, professionally, what happens when the official number and the true number drift apart. He watched governments do it to money. And then, in his spare decades — he worked on the manuscript for something like thirty years and showed it to almost no one — he concluded that astronomy had done it to the sky.

I promised you the coinage sideline mattered, and here is why: Copernicus did not merely worry about debased currency, he wrote a treatise on it, and in it he stated, decades before the Englishman whose name got attached to the principle, the law that bad money drives out good. When a kingdom circulates debased coin alongside honest coin at the same face value, the honest coin vanishes — hoarded, melted, exported — until only the debased remains. Now hold that law up to the epicycles, because I do not believe the same mind produced both by coincidence. What is an epicycle but debased currency of explanation? Each one is stamped with the full face value of truth, each one passes, each one is a little further from the metal, and the honest explanation — the one that would cost the realm its centre — is driven out of circulation precisely because the cheap ones still spend. Copernicus is the only figure in this series who wrote the theory of the corrupted ledger and the correction of the grandest ledger there was, and history has filed the two achievements in separate drawers, and this essay’s small service is to put them back on the same desk where he kept them.

Here is the accounting problem he inherited. The reigning model of the heavens, refined since Ptolemy, held the Earth still at the centre while everything else wheeled around it. And the model worked, more or less, in the only sense that mattered to its users: it predicted. But it predicted the way a desperate bookkeeper balances a failing firm — with adjustments. Every planet that misbehaved got an epicycle, a circle riding on a circle; every epicycle that misbehaved got another. The system was not wrong so much as it was maintained, in the way this series has taught you to hear that word: a structure of special pleadings, each one added honestly, the whole assembly groaning. Nobody had cooked the books. The books had simply been kept, for fourteen centuries, on the assumption that the counting-house could not possibly be the thing that was moving.

You will recognize that assumption. Essay 1,522 was about eighteen sailors who kept a flawless log and came home a day wrong because they had not accounted for their own motion. Copernicus’s proposal was the same discovery, scaled up from a ship to a planet: the observers are aboard something. The Earth is the Victoria. Put the Sun at the centre, set the Earth moving around it like everything else, and whole shelves of epicycles become unnecessary — not because the arithmetic changed, but because the arithmetic finally included the accountant.

Now I must be honest with you about the numbers, because this is the Numbers Game and honesty about numbers is the entire franchise. The new model was not, at first, more accurate. This surprises everyone and it should be said plainly: Copernicus kept the old obsession with perfect circles, and so he needed his own small epicycles, and for decades a competent astrologer casting your horoscope got answers of about the same quality from either system. If this had been a contest of prediction, 1543 would be a footnote. It was a contest of honesty. The old ledger balanced by treating the Earth as a special client — the one account that never moved, around which all other accounts were forced to reconcile at whatever cost in complication. The new ledger fired the client. It demoted us from proprietor to entry — one planet among the receivables, third from the Sun, subject to the same audit as everything else. The numbers were not yet better. They were, for the first time, disinterested. And disinterested numbers, this series has found in every century it has visited, eventually win, because they are the only kind that improve.

The book itself had a guardian angel of the nervous variety. Copernicus, dying, had entrusted the printing to others, and a Lutheran clergyman named Andreas Osiander, supervising the press at Nuremberg, quietly inserted an unsigned preface assuring readers that none of this need be taken as true — merely as a convenient device for calculation. He did not sign it, and Copernicus, by every account, never authorized it, and for decades many readers assumed the disclaimer was the author’s own. I want you to sit with what Osiander did, because it is a move this series meets constantly in its modern coverage: he did not suppress the number. He reframed it. The figure stands, the finding stands, but a paragraph is affixed explaining that it is not the kind of number that means anything — a hypothesis, a model, an exercise. Every institution that has ever received an unwelcome audit has reached for Osiander’s preface. It bought the book seventy years of quiet circulation, which the historians count as a mercy and I count as the world’s most successful editorial note, and both of us are right.

The quiet ended in 1616, when the Galileo affair — a story this series is saving, with some dread, for a later number — finally made the book dangerous, and De Revolutionibus went onto the Index of Forbidden Books. But note the entry, because even the censorship was an accounting exercise: the book was not banned outright. It was suspended until corrected, and when the correction came, it amounted to roughly ten passages — the censors went through the great demotion of mankind line by line and found that it could be rendered acceptable by amending about ten sentences, chiefly the ones asserting the model was true rather than convenient. Osiander’s preface, in other words, was retrofitted onto the text by decree, one qualification at a time. They itemized. Somewhere, I like to think, the ghost of the man from essay 1,517 permitted himself a single dry laugh: the institution that had answered his ninety-five items with a demand for blanket recantation had learned, a century on, to dispute a book item by item — and used the skill to soften a truth rather than to answer a challenge. The format is neutral. The books balance either way. That is the coldest sentence in this essay and I am leaving it in.

And the year itself was showing off. In that same 1543, a twenty-eight-year-old anatomist named Andreas Vesalius published De Humani Corporis Fabrica, built on the scandalous practice of actually dissecting human bodies and actually drawing what he found, which contradicted Galen at point after point. Fourteen centuries of inherited authority about the heavens, and fourteen centuries of inherited authority about the body, and both sets of books were reopened for audit in the same publishing season. Outward and inward at once. If the Numbers Game gave out a Year of the Year award, 1543 would be on the shortlist, and it would lose to a year I will get to eventually, and the loss would be contested, and that is what award shows are for.

The demotion, of course, took generations to feel. That is the part I keep circling. Nothing in the street changed on May 24, 1543. Bread cost what it cost. The Sun, with magnificent indifference to the new paperwork, went on appearing to rise. The Earth had been moving at ferocious speed the entire time — it did not start moving when Copernicus said so, any more than the Victoria‘s day went missing at the moment the shore party heard the word “Thursday.” The number does not create the fact. The number discloses it, and there is always a lag between disclosure and digestion, and in that lag live all the Osianders, explaining that the figure is merely a device. We are, I would argue, living inside several such lags right now, on subjects I write about under other datelines, where the tables have been published and the prefaces are still doing their soothing work. The audit is in. The client disputes the audit. The client is a planet’s worth of us. It has happened before.

Dior received the news of her demotion this morning on the lakeshore and has declined to recognize the finding. Her position, as I understand it from the set of her ears, is that centrality is not a matter of astronomy but of arrangement: the household provably orbits her — meals arrive on her schedule, walks occur at her insistence, and I have reorganized an entire morning, this very morning, around her refusal to pass a particular squirrel. Copernicus, she notes, never met her. His sample was incomplete. I have reviewed her arithmetic and I cannot fault it; the man moved the Earth, but he never had a husky, and his model accounts for everything except who is actually in charge.

The count moves on, as it does. The next landmark on this stretch is essay 1,582, and I can promise you the strangest entry the series has yet attempted, because it is about the year the books of time themselves were corrected — the year a pope audited the calendar, found it ten days in arrears, and simply deleted them. People went to bed on a Thursday and woke up eleven days later, by decree, having aged one night. Some of them, we are told, wanted the days back.

Copernicus found the error and apologized for it in advance. Osiander explained it away. The Earth kept moving, unbothered, exactly as it had while everyone was wrong.

See you at 1,582, when the missing days go missing on purpose.
