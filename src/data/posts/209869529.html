Somewhere in Waterloo, or perhaps a boardroom in Vancouver, a Telus executive signed a memorandum of understanding with a company that had, at the time, six satellites in orbit.

Six. Not six hundred. Six.

That company is AST SpaceMobile, and the six satellites belong to a strategy that looks, on paper, almost suicidal against Starlink’s six hundred and fifty. But strategy in this business isn’t only about who launches the most hardware fastest. It’s also about what each satellite can actually do once it’s up there — and on that question, AST is betting the entire company that quality beats quantity, patience beats speed, and that being second to market doesn’t matter if you’re first to deliver something Starlink physically cannot.

The two philosophies in orbit

Starlink’s approach to direct-to-cell is, in the truest SpaceX tradition, brute force done beautifully. Small, cheap, mass-manufactured satellites, launched by the hundreds on rockets SpaceX already owns, connecting to ordinary phones using spectrum borrowed from whichever carrier signed the deal. It is a coverage strategy first and a bandwidth strategy second: SpaceX can blanket a continent in “good enough” connectivity faster than anyone alive, but the current generation is genuinely constrained to text messages, basic data, and voice calls routed through apps like WhatsApp — a supplemental service for dead zones, not yet a replacement for your home network.

AST SpaceMobile has made the opposite bet. Its BlueBird satellites carry deployable antennas the size of a tennis court — 64 square metres per satellite, roughly a hundred times the individual capacity of a Starlink direct-to-cell satellite. Building something that large and that precise is a genuinely harder manufacturing problem, and AST doesn’t have its own rockets the way SpaceX does, so it’s dependent on other companies’ launch schedules for every satellite it puts up. The payoff, if it works, is real broadband — not a text-message lifeline, but something that could plausibly deliver premium mobile service from orbit, competitive with what a terrestrial tower offers today.

The bet each company is making on the other’s weakness is almost symmetrical. AST is betting that once Starlink’s carriers realize their customers want more than SMS, they’ll switch allegiance to a network that was built for real bandwidth from the start. SpaceX is betting that global coverage arrives so fast, and becomes so entrenched, that by the time AST’s superior architecture is fully deployed, nobody will bother switching. Whoever is right doesn’t just win a technology argument. They win the wholesale relationship with every carrier still deciding which horse to back — which is exactly why Bell and Telus, Canada’s two largest carriers after Rogers, have both aligned with AST rather than Starlink.

It’s worth noting that AST isn’t only playing in Canada. The company has also signed with Verizon and AT&T in the US — sitting, in effect, on the opposite side of the American story from Part I, where SpaceX is the one trying to break in as a retail competitor. AST has additionally struck a deal with Saudi Arabia’s STC, giving it a genuine foothold in a market SpaceX hasn’t yet cracked. None of this makes AST the favourite. It has launched only a handful of its planned satellites, against a target of roughly sixty in 2026 alone, and every one of those launches depends on a rocket AST doesn’t own. But it does mean the “quality over quantity” bet has real institutional backing, in more than one country, from carriers with every incentive to want a genuine alternative to a SpaceX-dominated market.

A tale of two Canadas

This is the part of the story that hits closest to home, because it’s playing out inside my own household. Rogers signed with SpaceX in 2023 and has Rogers Satellite live nationwide today. Telus — the company behind my own Koodo plan — has instead signed a memorandum of understanding with AST SpaceMobile, joining Bell, which struck its own AST partnership the year before. Two of Canada’s three national carriers are betting on the company with six satellites in orbit; the third is riding on the company with six hundred and fifty.

Neither bet is obviously foolish. Rogers gets to market first, with a service that already works, however limited, and a first-mover advantage in a country large enough that “we have some coverage everywhere” beats “we might eventually have excellent coverage somewhere.” Bell and Telus are betting that when AST’s constellation matures, “eventually excellent everywhere” beats “adequate everywhere,” and that Canadian customers — used to paying premium prices for premium service — will pay for the difference.

What this means in practice is that Canada, almost by accident, has become a live experiment in exactly the question this series keeps circling: does speed of deployment or quality of service win the direct-to-cell race? Nobody designed it as a controlled test. Three carriers just made three different bets, and the results will show up in customer churn data years before any regulator or industry analyst renders a verdict.

Lynk, and the art of hedging your bets

There’s a third satellite company worth naming, and Rogers’ relationship with it tells you something important about how carriers actually think about this technology. Rogers didn’t just sign with SpaceX. It also signed with Lynk Global, a smaller operator that has quietly built commercial agreements with more than 25 mobile network operators across more than 40 countries — Vodafone Ghana, 2degrees in New Zealand, Globe Telecom in the Philippines, and dozens more. Lynk remains, for now, a messaging-only player rather than a true broadband competitor, but its footprint is enormous, and its patent position is genuinely strong; the company describes itself, not inaccurately, as the world’s only patented, proven, commercially licensed, and operational satellite-to-mobile system.

Why would Rogers sign with two competing satellite companies at once? Because a carrier’s job isn’t to bet on a winner. It’s to make sure it isn’t left without a seat when the music stops. Signing multiple satellite partners costs a carrier almost nothing beyond a modest wholesale commitment, and it means that whichever technology matures fastest, or proves most reliable, or wins the regulatory race in a given country, the carrier already has a relationship in place rather than starting from zero. Rogers hedged. So, in effect, did Bell and Telus, just with a different primary partner.

Veon, and the leverage of staying uncommitted

The clearest illustration of this hedging logic at a global scale is Veon, the telecoms group with operations across Kazakhstan, Ukraine, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Uzbekistan. In late 2025, Veon signed what both companies called Starlink’s largest direct-to-cell deal yet — access to more than 150 million potential customers, launching first through Beeline in Kazakhstan and Kyivstar in Ukraine. It was covered, reasonably, as a landmark SpaceX win.

What got less attention is what Veon’s own executives said about it. The deal is explicitly non-exclusive. Veon’s CEO told Reuters the company was simultaneously in discussions with Amazon’s Project Kuiper, AST SpaceMobile, and Eutelsat OneWeb — the full field of direct-to-device competitors, all being courted at once. Kyivstar’s business development director put it more bluntly: there will be more announced soon, and the company has no interest in waiting until 2027 or 2028 to develop the business today.

This is the wholesale carrier’s version of exactly the retention-desk leverage that Canadian wireless customers use against Rogers, Bell, and Telus. A carrier that plays multiple satellite operators against each other extracts better terms than one that commits early and exclusively. It is, in miniature, the same dynamic this series keeps returning to: whoever holds the scarce resource — spectrum on one side of the table, a customer relationship on the other — gets to make the other side compete for the privilege of serving them.

Amazon and Eutelsat, waiting in the wings

Two more names belong in this field, even though neither has SpaceX’s head start. Amazon’s Project Kuiper and Eutelsat’s OneWeb are both advancing their own satellite constellations, with initial commercial direct-to-device launches anticipated through 2026 and beyond. Neither has anything close to Starlink’s deployed capacity yet, and neither has AST’s premium-bandwidth architecture. But both have something else that matters in this business: extremely deep pockets and no interest in ceding a market this large to two competitors before the technology has even fully matured. Amazon in particular has the logistics and capital discipline to become a serious third option once Kuiper reaches meaningful scale — worth watching for a future instalment in this series, once its commercial deals start appearing.

It’s also worth pausing on why four or five well-funded companies are willing to spend billions chasing what is, for now, a text-messaging feature. The prize isn’t the current product. It’s the option value of being the default satellite layer once direct-to-device evolves from a dead-zone backstop into genuine broadband — at which point the winner isn’t selling a feature to a carrier anymore. It’s selling connectivity to every device on the planet that doesn’t happen to be standing under a cell tower, which, depending on how you count it, is most of the planet’s surface. Nobody in this field is racing for 2026’s market. They’re racing for whichever year direct-to-device stops being a novelty and starts being infrastructure.

The new names on Starlink’s own list

None of this competition has slowed Starlink’s own signing pace. On its second-quarter 2026 earnings call, Gwynne Shotwell noted that the company had “launched the new Starlink Mobile partnerships with international carriers, including SoftBank, NTT Docomo, and Spark New Zealand” — SoftBank and NTT Docomo adding a second and third Japanese carrier alongside the existing KDDI relationship, and Spark joining One NZ as a second New Zealand partner. Whatever else is true about the competitive field, Starlink is still adding carriers faster than anyone else in this race, in country after country, largely because it can point to a service that already exists rather than one still being built.

What the home front reveals

There’s a final, sharper irony worth sitting with, because it complicates the tidy story Part I told about SpaceX’s international strategy. Abroad, SpaceX is winning easy, fast partnerships precisely because it isn’t the incumbent’s rival — it’s a vendor offering carriers a feature their competitors don’t have. At home, where SpaceX wants to become the incumbent’s rival directly, the reception has been the opposite. All three major American carriers have publicly declined to offer SpaceX an MVNO-style deal on their own spectrum; AT&T’s and T-Mobile’s CEOs said so explicitly on their own Q1 2026 earnings calls. In direct response, AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile announced in May 2026 that they were forming a joint venture to pool their own spectrum for a competing direct-to-device push — a defensive alliance between three companies that don’t otherwise cooperate on much of anything, formed for the specific purpose of keeping Starlink from becoming a fourth national carrier on favourable terms.

Put the whole field together and a pattern comes into focus. Where SpaceX is a supplier, carriers compete to sign it, hedge with its rivals, and use the resulting competition to extract better wholesale terms — exactly the dynamic Veon is running today. Where SpaceX is a competitor, carriers close ranks against it instead. The company’s global expansion isn’t one strategy wearing different disguises. It’s genuinely two different games, playing out on two different boards, and the field of competitors — AST, Lynk, Kuiper, OneWeb — exists almost entirely on one of those boards and barely touches the other.
