Asked on Wednesday, September 23, 2026, whether he intended to seek a second term, Mark Carney gave the answer of a man who has checked his watch and found it running slow. “I just got here!” he told Matina Stevis-Gridneff and Sydney Ember of The New York Times, in an interview published September 24, 2026.

It is a charming line, and it is not quite true. Carney has been prime minister since March 2025. He has fought and won a general election, turned a minority into a majority without calling another one, walked away from trade talks with Washington, and told audiences at Davos in January 2026 and at the United Nations in September 2026 that the old order is finished. By the standards of most prime ministers, he did not just get here. He has been here long enough to have rearranged the furniture.

But the line is revealing all the same, because underneath the modesty sits a serious admission. The project Carney has set for himself, breaking a century of Canadian economic dependence on the United States, cannot be finished in the time he has been given. He said as much. He said he would consider running again if the job was not done, and he was frank that it would not be done soon. The first three parts of this series looked at what Carney says (Part I), what the numbers say (Part II), and what he is quietly preparing for (Part III). This one looks at the thing that governs all three: the calendar.

The Clock

Carney’s own estimate is the place to start. The major infrastructure projects at the heart of his strategy, he told the Times, will take roughly eighteen months to two years before they begin to lift growth in a way ordinary Canadians can feel. Some of the benefits, he conceded, will land beyond the political cycle.

The flagship example is Churchill Falls. On Monday, August 17, 2026, Carney stood on a pier at the St. John’s Port Authority alongside Newfoundland and Labrador Premier Tony Wakeham and Quebec Premier Christine Fréchette to announce a new energy agreement between the two provinces, backed by Ottawa. As The Globe and Mail reported, the provincial utilities are eyeing up to 14,000 megawatts of new and existing hydroelectric capacity in Labrador and along the Churchill River, and Carney described it as the largest clean energy investment in North America.

Anyone who knows the history of Churchill Falls will appreciate the irony of it being cast as the fast track. The original 1969 contract between Newfoundland and Hydro-Québec has been a byword for grievance in St. John’s for more than half a century, and the negotiations to replace it have lurched along for years, including a bruising moment in May 2026 when a Newfoundland and Labrador review committee published a critical report on the earlier memorandum of understanding. That the deal exists at all is an achievement. That it will be generating material growth before the next federal election is a hope.

And this is the difficulty with building a sovereign economy rather than merely declaring one. A speech takes a morning. A dam takes a decade. Carney, a former central banker, understands lags better than most politicians alive; he spent his career explaining to impatient finance ministers that a rate cut today shows up in the economy a year and a half from now. He is now in the position of the finance minister, waiting on a lag he cannot shorten, and answerable to voters who do not read monetary policy reports.

The Bridge

What fills the gap between the announcement and the payoff is money. Carney told the Times his government is taking short-term stimulus measures to carry Canadians through, including billions of dollars in employment insurance support and relief for small and medium-sized businesses hit by American tariffs. He was candid that the interval would bring headwinds from tariffs and from general uncertainty.

The obvious objection is inflation. Pour stimulus into an economy while tariffs are already pushing up import costs and you risk feeding the very price pressures that have made the trade war politically poisonous in the United States. Carney’s answer was that inflation pressures in Canada are much lower than in other jurisdictions, and that Canada’s fiscal position is much stronger.

The second half of that answer holds up well. As Part II of this series set out, and as this writer’s Thirty Seven Trillion series has documented at length on the American side, Canada’s debt-to-GDP position is far more comfortable than Washington’s, and the Bank of Canada has had more room to cut than the Federal Reserve. A country with that balance sheet can afford to borrow to buy time. That is precisely what balance sheets are for.

The first half is shakier. Part II found that inflation was the one measure on which Canada was not clearly winning, with a spike in May 2026 that no honest reading could wave away. “Much less than some other jurisdictions” may be true on a given month’s print, but it is the kind of claim that ages quickly. If the stimulus bridge is long enough to reach the far bank, it will also be long enough for prices to become the story. A prime minister who spent seven years at the Bank of England defending an inflation target knows this better than his critics do. Whether he says so in public is another matter.

The Fuel

Stimulus buys economic time. What buys political time is patience, and at the moment Carney has an extraordinary supply of it.

The Times reported that, as of mid-September 2026, Carney is by at least one measure the most popular prime minister in Canadian history, with one poll putting his approval as high as 70 percent. Roughly three in four Canadians backed his decision in August 2026 to walk away from trade talks with the United States. Carney’s own reading of the national mood was that Canadians have moved past the shock of the trade war and into what he called a “roll up the sleeves” frame of mind.

It would be churlish to deny the achievement. Canadians do not, as a rule, adore their prime ministers. They tolerate them, grumble about them, and eventually dispose of them. Seventy percent is a number more often associated with wartime leaders and freshly crowned monarchs than with a Liberal who spent his first year selling Canadians on major projects, private investment in airports and spending restraint.

But popularity is a depreciating asset, and Canadian political history offers no shortage of reminders. Brian Mulroney won 211 seats in 1984, the largest majority in the country’s history to that point. Nine years later his party was reduced to two. The public that cheers a leader for standing up to the Americans is the same public that will, eighteen months later, be asking why the grocery bill is still climbing and the promised dam is still a construction site. The Times noted the tension in its own reporting: the same polls that show Canadians behind Carney also record real anxiety about the economic cost of the fight.

The honest way to put it is that Carney is spending his popularity the way he is spending the stimulus money. He is borrowing against the future, confident the investment will pay off before the bill comes due. It is a reasonable bet. It is also, unmistakably, a bet.

The Majority He Built

One variable Carney has removed from the equation is Parliament. When he won the April 2025 election, the Liberals finished with 169 seats, three short of a majority. Over the following year, five opposition MPs crossed the floor to join them, and on April 13, 2026, the Liberals swept three by-elections in University-Rosedale, Scarborough Southwest and Terrebonne. As The Hill Times reported, that brought the caucus to 174 seats and made Carney the first Canadian prime minister to win a majority through floor crossing.

The opposition’s complaints about how that majority was assembled are loud and, in places, fair. Voters in those ridings did not elect Liberals. But the practical effect is that Carney’s constraint is no longer the House of Commons. Nobody can bring him down on a confidence vote over the next budget. The only people who can stop the programme now are the voters, and they do not get their say until October 2029 at the earliest under Canada’s fixed-date rules, with April 2030 as the outer limit.

This is why his refusal to entertain an early election is more interesting than it looks. A leader sitting at 70 percent approval, with a hostile American administration as a ready-made ballot question, would in most eras be sprinting to Rideau Hall to cash in. Carney waved the idea away. His argument, in effect, was that he has a majority and a large job to do, and that an election would be a distraction from it.

It may also be a calculation. An election in the autumn of 2026 would convert today’s popularity into four more years. But it would also define the next campaign around the trade war at its most painful, and it would hand his opponents the argument that he called a vote to escape the consequences of his own strategy before they arrived. Waiting keeps his options open and his timeline intact. It also means that if the eighteen months turn into thirty, he will face the voters at exactly the moment the bill is largest.

Two Clocks

There is a second calendar running alongside Carney’s, and it may matter more than his own.

Donald Trump’s term ends in January 2029. His party faces the midterms in November 2026, six weeks from the date of this interview. Canada’s next federal election falls due in the autumn of 2029. Put those dates side by side and the shape of Carney’s strategy comes into focus. He is not trying to beat Trump. He is trying to outlast him, building enough alternative capacity (European and Asian trade relationships, domestic energy, non-American defence procurement, the diversification plans described in Part III) that by the time Washington’s weather changes, Canada no longer needs a sunny day in Washington to function.

The American approach to Canada throughout this dispute has depended heavily on the assumption that the smaller party will run out of patience first. Tariff threats, abrupt demands at the negotiating table and the occasional musing about annexation all work on the same principle: squeeze until the other side decides a bad deal today is better than an uncertain fight tomorrow. Carney walking away from the table in August 2026, with three quarters of the country behind him, was a direct refusal of that premise. What the Times interview adds is his acknowledgement of what the refusal costs. He is not claiming the fight will be short. He is claiming Canadians can stand it being long.

What Sovereignty Takes

This series takes its name from a five-word answer Carney gave in September 2026, when asked what a Mexican deal with Trump might mean for Canada: it means we’re a sovereign country. Parts I through III examined what that sovereignty looks like on a podium, on a spreadsheet and in a contingency plan. This part arrives at a less glamorous conclusion. Sovereignty, in the sense Carney means it, is not a declaration. It is a construction project, and construction projects are measured in years.

The Canadian public has given him a mandate, a majority and a remarkable reservoir of goodwill. What it has not given him, because no public ever can, is a guarantee that the goodwill will still be there when the dam is finished. Carney’s own words describe the problem better than any critic could: “You don’t transform the economy overnight.” The question for the next eighteen months is not whether he is right about the destination. It is whether a country that is, for now, rolling up its sleeves will still be willing to keep them rolled up in the spring of 2028, when the tariffs are still biting, the stimulus is running thin and the payoff is still just around the corner.

Canadians are said to be a patient people, a reputation earned mostly by waiting out winters and queuing politely. Carney is about to find out whether it extends to prime ministers.
