Stephen Leacock, in his 1911 burlesque Gertrude the Governess, gave us Lord Ronald, who “flung himself upon his horse and rode madly off in all directions.” It is the finest description of political incoherence in the Canadian canon, and for a long while I assumed it described Elon Musk.

Consider the record. In May 2025, Musk said that, where political spending was concerned, he was going to do a lot less in the future. On October 12, 2026, as Theodore Schleifer reported for The New York Times, his representatives disclosed that he had poured more than $276 million into Republican groups and his own super PAC since the start of July alone, making him by far the largest known donor of the midterm cycle. In between, he ran a federal department, quarrelled with the President of the United States in public, accused him of appearing in the Epstein files, announced a third party, and then turned up at the White House for dinner.

That is a great deal of riding. But having spent twenty-odd essays in this series watching how Musk builds and defends his portfolio of power, I have come to think Lord Ronald is the wrong comparison. Musk has not ridden off in all directions. He has ridden, every time, toward wherever the stack was safest and away from whoever had wounded him most recently. The swings are real. So is the constant.

Here are the six swings.

Swing One: The Kindness Party

It is easy to forget now, but for most of his adult life Musk was a fairly conventional Silicon Valley Democrat. He said in May 2022 that he had voted overwhelmingly for Democrats and might never have voted Republican at all. He backed Barack Obama, voted for Hillary Clinton in 2016, and, as Rolling Stone noted, voted for Joe Biden in 2020.

He did flirt with the first Trump administration, sitting on two presidential business advisory councils in early 2017. But he resigned from both in June 2017 when Trump withdrew the United States from the Paris climate agreement. Hold on to that moment. It is the last time in this story that Musk walked away from Donald Trump over a matter of policy principle that cost him something and gained his companies nothing.

Swing Two: The Invitation That Never Came

The first crack had a date and an address. On August 5, 2021, President Biden hosted an event on the South Lawn to celebrate the electric-vehicle ambitions of American carmakers. General Motors, Ford and Stellantis sent executives. The United Auto Workers was there. Tesla, which at the time accounted for roughly three-quarters of all American EV sales according to Insider, was not invited.

Musk’s response on Twitter was mild by his later standards: it did seem odd, he wrote, that Tesla had been left out. Asked whether the omission had anything to do with Tesla’s non-union workforce, White House press secretary Jen Psaki pointedly invited reporters to draw their own conclusions.

The wound did not close. More than two years later, on December 24, 2023, Musk was still raising it, complaining that the White House had credited GM with leading the electric revolution in a quarter when, by his count, GM delivered 26 electric cars and Tesla delivered some 300,000. The grievance was compounded by policy: Democrats were proposing extra tax credits for union-made electric vehicles, which Tesla, the one major American EV maker without a union, could never claim.

What makes this episode worth more than a footnote is that the other side has since conceded the point. As Fox News reported, Kamala Harris writes in her memoir 107 Days that excluding Musk was a big mistake, and that Musk never forgave it. GM’s own chief executive, Mary Barra, told the 2025 DealBook Summit that she had privately told Biden that Tesla deserved more credit, as Carscoops recounted.

There was a backdrop, too. In May 2020 Musk had already fought Alameda County, California, over pandemic orders that kept his Fremont factory closed, and in 2021 he moved Tesla’s headquarters to Austin. The Democratic state that had made him rich was beginning to feel, to him, like a landlord who had changed the locks.

Swing Three: Spring 2022

The decisive turn came in a cluster of weeks in the spring of 2022, and the cluster is the most revealing thing in this whole story.

In April 2022 Musk agreed to buy Twitter. On April 29, 2022, he posted that he had strongly supported Obama but that the Democratic Party had been hijacked by extremists. On May 16, 2022, at the All-In Summit in Miami, he described himself as a moderate who had historically voted Democrat but would vote Republican this time, and complained, as CNBC reported, that the Democrats were overly controlled by unions and trial lawyers. On May 18, 2022, came the declaration: he had voted Democrat because they were mostly the kindness party, but they had become the party of division and hate, so he would vote Republican. He added, with a popcorn emoji, that the reader should now watch the dirty-tricks campaign against him unfold.

That same April, in Los Angeles County Superior Court, his daughter Vivian filed a petition to change her name and legal gender. She was turning eighteen. Her filing stated, in effect, that she no longer wished to be related to her biological father in any way. The court granted the change in June 2022.

Musk did not connect these events publicly at the time, and an essayist should be careful about connecting them for him. But Musk eventually connected them himself. In a July 2024 interview with Jordan Peterson, reported by The National Desk, he said he had been tricked into signing documents for his child’s care during the pandemic, used her former name, declared that the child he knew had been killed by the “woke mind virus,” and said he had vowed to destroy it.

Whatever one thinks of those words, and Vivian has made very clear what she thinks of them, they tell us how Musk understood his own politics: not as a conversion of ideas but as retaliation for a loss. The union snub was an insult to his company. The estrangement was an injury to his family. In both cases, the party he had voted for became, in his telling, the party that had done something to him.

Notice, too, what the May 18 post left out. He did not say what the Republicans had become. He did not need to. The kindness party had simply lost a customer, and in Musk’s world a customer who leaves is a verdict on the shop, never on the customer.

Swing Four: Butler and the Final Straw

For two years after that, Musk moved rightward mostly online, turning Twitter into X and his own feed into the platform’s loudest political channel. The formal leap came on July 13, 2024, minutes after the attempt on Trump’s life in Butler, Pennsylvania, when Musk posted that he fully endorsed him.

Three days later he announced that SpaceX would leave Hawthorne, California, for Starbase, Texas, and X would leave San Francisco for Austin. The trigger, he said, was the “final straw”: a California law signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on July 15, 2024, barring school districts from requiring teachers to tell parents when a student changes gender identity. As Euronews reported, The Wall Street Journal had meanwhile reported plans for Musk to give around $45 million a month to a pro-Trump super PAC.

Note the fusion. The personal injury of 2022 became, in 2024, a corporate relocation and a political endowment in the same week. Musk ended the 2024 cycle having laid out roughly $290 million to return Trump to power, much of it through America PAC, the canvassing operation he founded that year and the $1-million voter sweepstakes that drew legal challenges.

Swing Five: One Hundred and Thirty Days

Then the banker became an employee. From January 20, 2025, Musk ran the Department of Government Efficiency as a special government employee, a status that under federal ethics rules allowed him no more than 130 days of service a year. DOGE gutted agencies, froze grants, and made Musk the most visible and most resented figure in Washington. Tesla showrooms drew protesters. In March 2025, Trump bought a Tesla on the White House driveway as a show of solidarity.

The solidarity lasted until the clock ran out. On May 30, 2025, Trump handed Musk a golden key in the Oval Office and praised him lavishly, as NBC Los Angeles reported. But by then Musk had already broken ranks over the “One Big Beautiful Bill,” telling CBS he was disappointed that it would widen the deficit and undermine DOGE’s work.

Within a week the breakup was spectacular. Musk called the bill an abomination, then escalated to the claim that Trump was in the Epstein files. Trump replied by suggesting that the government could save billions by cutting Musk’s subsidies and contracts. By July 1, 2025, Trump was musing that DOGE was a monster that might have to go back and eat Elon, and that without subsidies Musk might have to head home to South Africa. On July 5, 2025, the day after Trump signed the bill, Musk announced the America Party, which would, as The Kyiv Independent noted, give Americans back their freedom from a one-party system of waste and graft.

Two motives were in play, and they deserve to be separated. The stated one was fiscal: Musk had spent four months cutting spending and the bill would add trillions to the debt. The unstated one was commercial: the bill ended the federal consumer tax credit for electric vehicles, which Trump himself identified as the real source of Musk’s anger. The two are not mutually exclusive. But it is worth noticing which motive survived the next fifteen months, and which did not.

Swing Six: The Return of the Banker

The thaw began at a funeral. On September 21, 2025, Musk and Trump shook hands at the memorial service for Charlie Kirk. On November 18, 2025, Musk sat down at the White House state dinner for Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, the pair’s second joint appearance since the feud, according to Al-Monitor, carrying the Reuters report. In the weeks before October 12, 2026, they appeared together three times in two weeks, and on October 8, 2026, the President called Musk “our modern-day Thomas Edison.” The America Party, meanwhile, has become one of those Musk announcements best remembered as a threat rather than an institution.

It takes two to reconcile, and it is worth asking what Trump got out of it. The obvious answer is money, and money is certainly most of it: the Times reports that Trump’s own political advisers asked Musk this spring to rebuild America PAC’s 2024 field operation, at a moment when Republicans were heading into a hostile midterm environment. But money alone undersells Trump. A Republican Senate is his firewall; losing it, along with the House, would mean subpoenas, hearings and perhaps another impeachment. A Musk funding Republicans is also a Musk not funding the America Party, not bankrolling primary challengers against loyalists, and not repeating the Epstein accusation to his enormous following on X. Then there is leverage of the ordinary governmental kind. Washington needs SpaceX to launch its satellites, Starlink to connect its soldiers, and American AI companies to keep pace with China; a president cannot easily freeze out the man who owns so much of that hardware. Trump forgives readily when forgiveness pays, and rarely when it does not. Here it paid three ways at once: in cash, in silence, and in rockets.

And then the money. According to the Times, Musk’s summer giving breaks down roughly as follows:

  • $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund, the main super PAC defending the Republican Senate majority, more than its Democratic rival raised from all donors that quarter

  • More than $76 million to America PAC, which has deployed nearly 3,000 paid canvassers and claims some 6.5 million doors knocked

  • $20 million to the Congressional Leadership Fund

  • $10 million to Lone Star Liberty PAC, backing Ken Paxton for the Senate in Texas

  • $10 million to the Republican Governors Association

  • $2 million to the Republican Attorneys General Association

His cumulative federal giving for the 2025–26 cycle is about $366 million, already well past the 2024 total, before counting donations to groups that do not disclose their donors.

Two details stand out. The first is where the money is aimed. More than a third of America PAC’s disclosed spending has gone into the Texas Senate race, where Paxton, the state attorney general who has investigated Musk’s critics, faces James Talarico. The canvassing scripts and door hangers lean hard on transgender issues; one leaflet accuses Talarico of a “radical trans agenda.” The injury of spring 2022 is still the operation’s emotional engine.

The second detail is how quiet Musk has been. The Times found only seven direct posts on X this year about candidates in competitive races. There are no rallies, no oversized cheques. Decisions are delegated to his adviser Chris Young. Friends say he rarely mentions the midterms. The man who once campaigned in Pennsylvania in person is now writing nine-figure cheques at arm’s length, and America PAC says it plans to stay active into 2028.

The likeliest explanation for the silence is not indifference but arithmetic. The last time Musk made himself the face of a political project, Tesla paid for it. Showrooms were picketed through the spring of 2025, cars were vandalised, and buyers in Europe and on both American coasts walked away. As Bluewin reported on January 28, 2026, Tesla delivered about 1.64 million vehicles in 2025, down 8.5 per cent and its second straight annual decline, with Musk’s politics cited among the reasons some customers turned away. Analysts said much the same in real time. A man who has watched his car company’s customers boycott him does not need a third lesson. Writing cheques to super PACs is invisible to the average Tesla buyer; posting about Talarico fifty times a day is not. The new Musk has learned to separate his money from his face.

Which brings us to the Senate Leadership Fund, the same party apparatus that passed the bill Musk called an abomination. He has now given that apparatus $158 million. Whatever happened to the deficit hawk of June 2025, he does not appear to have attended the reconciliation.

The Constant

Lay the six swings side by side and a pattern emerges that is more interesting than incoherence.

Each move away from a party followed a perceived wound. The Biden snub injured his company’s standing. The union-made tax credits threatened its margins. The estrangement from his daughter, which Musk himself blamed on progressive ideology, was a wound to his family that he chose to treat as a political act. The California law reopened it, and he responded by moving two headquarters and endowing a campaign. The One Big Beautiful Bill stripped the EV credit and, not incidentally, made his DOGE work look like theatre.

And each move back toward Trump followed a reminder of exposure. When Trump answered the Epstein jab by threatening federal contracts and subsidies, he was threatening the foundation of the stack: SpaceX launches, Starlink deals, Pentagon work, regulatory approvals for robotaxis and rockets. Earlier parts of this series have catalogued just how much of Musk’s empire runs through Washington; Part XX followed him back into the Pentagon itself. A man who owns that much federally entangled infrastructure cannot afford to remain the President’s enemy for long. The reconciliation was not a change of heart. It was risk management.

This is not to say Musk’s grievances were invented. The 2021 snub was real, and Democrats now admit it was a blunder. His concern about the deficit was real enough to say aloud on national television. There are people of genuine conviction on every side of the arguments he has joined. But conviction tends to be sticky. It does not usually travel from “utterly insane and destructive” to $158 million for the same party’s Senate arm inside fifteen months, and it does not usually go quiet at the precise moment it is spending the most.

So the answer to the question many readers ask me, how someone so brilliant can align himself with this administration, may be simpler and sadder than a theory of ideological capture. Musk’s politics are not a philosophy. They are a ledger. On one side, the slights; on the other, the exposure. Whichever column is heavier on a given day decides which way he rides.

Canada gave Musk his first citizenship, a fact I have noted before in this series with a mixture of pride and embarrassment. Leacock, who knew a thing or two about men of large ambition, would have recognised him at once. Not as Lord Ronald, galloping in all directions, but as one of the bankers of his Arcadian Adventures with the Idle Rich, convinced that his interests and the public good were the same thing, and generous with both parties, in turn, for exactly that reason.
