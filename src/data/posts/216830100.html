I. A Water Polo Match

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has a metaphor he likes for the US-China relationship, and he has used it often enough this year that it now counts as an official framing rather than an offhand line. The two countries, he said in a Fox Business interview in July, are playing “a water polo match” — leaders hitting the ball back and forth in full view, while underneath the surface, out of the cameras’ reach, “the Chinese were doing a lot of kicking.” If it comes to that, he added, “we’ll kick back.” China’s state-run China Daily ran an editorial in August agreeing that the metaphor was apt while insisting Bessent had the direction backwards — that Washington, not Beijing, was the one instigating the kicking. Both governments, in other words, agree on the choreography underneath the choreography. They just disagree about whose foot started it.

That admission — from the man who leads America’s negotiations with China, on television, months before Xi Jinping’s plane touches down — is the thread this essay pulls on, because it complicates almost everything the first five parts of this series have described. Parts I through V have largely been about the performance: honour guards rehearsing, a ballroom half-built, an AI dialogue folded into a single Sunday meeting, a $14 billion arms package sat on as leverage, Robert Kaplan’s warning that all of this ambiguity carries more risk than either government seems willing to price in. What Ana Swanson reported for the New York Times on 21 September 2026, three days before the summit, is that alongside all of that theatre, a genuinely separate and much less scripted contest has been running continuously since the two leaders shook hands in Busan last October — one that doesn’t pause for state dinners.

II. What the Truce Actually Covers

The Busan agreement, reached a year ago, restored some order to a relationship that had been spinning toward open economic conflict: Trump walked back a set of punishing tariffs, and Beijing promised to keep exporting the critical minerals it had cut off in retaliation. It was, by the standards of this relationship, a genuine de-escalation. It was not, as the intervening eleven months have made clear, a ceasefire in the fuller sense of the word.

American officials say China’s mineral exports — the inputs that go into cars, semiconductors, and power tools — remain more restricted than promised, leaving manufacturers uncertain about supply. China’s purchases of American farm goods lag the pledged levels, a shortfall this series flagged in Part I when it put a number on the gap: $3.9 billion delivered against a $17 billion commitment. And since March, China’s State Council has issued a string of decrees expanding Beijing’s authority over foreign companies operating inside the country — rules restricting unauthorised supply-chain audits (the audits American firms need to run to comply with forced-labour law), penalising compliance with foreign sanctions on Chinese firms, and tightening oversight of outbound investment and mineral exports. American firms now face a genuine bind: comply with US law on supply-chain verification, and risk penalties inside China for doing it.

Washington’s side of the ledger is no shorter. The administration has kept adding Chinese technology — routers, humanoid robots — to the list of things barred from the American market, and has accused Chinese firms of what it calls “industrial distillation,” a formal label for the copying of American AI models that this series’ Part III readers will recognise as the same underlying grievance behind the Trump administration’s “aggressive, malicious and targeted” accusation reported earlier this month. In June, a slate of major Chinese firms was added to a list of designated military companies; in July, dozens more went onto a forced-labour trade blacklist. Beijing answered by sanctioning seven American firms that help companies verify their own supply chains — including testing for Xinjiang-grown cotton, banned under US law over forced-labour concerns — which only tightens the same bind from the other direction.

None of this, on its own, is shocking. Great-power trading relationships run on friction; that’s not news. What makes it worth a full essay is the deliberate quality Swanson’s reporting assigns to it: officials describe these moves as calibrated specifically to stay under Trump’s notice, small enough individually to avoid disrupting the leader-level rapport, while adding up, collectively, to a running contest neither side is willing to name in public. Ryan Haas, who directs the John L. Thornton China Center at Brookings, put it in language that belongs in this series’ TACO glossary alongside “obfuscate”: there is, he said at a Brookings panel last week, “still room for shin-kicking under the table,” but “there are limits to how hard the kicking can occur.” Mira Rapp-Hooper, also at Brookings, called the whole arrangement “an extremely unique way to handle a great power relationship” — testing and probing on both sides, continuously, while the leader-level relationship stays deliberately insulated from the results.

III. The Deadline Nobody’s Discussing Out Loud

The truce itself has an expiration date — 30 November 2026 — and the question of whether, and for how long, it gets extended is, by most accounts, the actual centre of gravity for this week’s meetings, more than any single deliverable on AI or Taiwan. China wants a longer extension, for the obvious reason that longer commitments reduce uncertainty and buy Beijing more room to manage its own compliance on its own schedule. The United States, according to people familiar with the negotiations, has proposed extending it for just six months — deliberately keeping China on what Swanson’s sourcing describes as “a tighter leash,” with more frequent renegotiation points built in specifically so Washington can keep pressing on commitments China hasn’t fully met.

Sara Schuman, a former US trade official now at Beacon Global Strategies, frames the mineral-export question as the real lever inside that negotiation: “My expectation is that there’s a direct correlation between what China offers on critical minerals in this meeting and whether or not the truce is extended, and for how long.” That is a considerably more mechanical, transactional picture of the summit than the one the honour guard and the state dinner are built to project — and it is worth sitting with the fact that, as of this week, US officials still won’t confirm whether the truce gets extended at all, only that it’s “on the agenda.”

By Monday, the agenda had firmed up considerably. Ana Swanson’s follow-up reporting for the New York Times on 22 September 2026 says the Trump administration is now “widely expected” to extend the truce, sanctions pause on thousands of Chinese companies included — the clearest signal yet that Washington’s six-month “tighter leash” framing is the likely outcome rather than one option among several. That’s a genuine data point, not just more choreography, but it resolves only the truce’s headline question. The rare-earth deadline eleven days later, and the mineral-by-mineral compliance fight underneath both deadlines, remain exactly as unresolved as they were before.

One further card sits on the table that neither Swanson piece mentions directly, and it belongs in this ledger because it may be the single largest source of Chinese leverage walking into this summit: oil. China has, for reasons even close observers struggle to fully explain, cut its oil imports by roughly half this year — a move large enough, on its own, to have kept global crude prices well below the $150-to-$200-a-barrel spike most analysts expected once the Iran war began driving up energy costs. Rogé Karma, an economics reporter for The Atlantic, made the point plainly on Radio Atlantic’s 22 September episode: that restraint buys China real international goodwill, and gives Beijing a lever specifically calibrated to Washington’s midterm anxieties, since a sudden Chinese return to normal oil buying would send prices — and voter frustration — climbing right before November. It is, in its own way, the same shin-kicking logic as the mineral fight, played out on a market too large for either government to fully control, which makes it a strange kind of leverage: real, currently favourable to Washington, and entirely reversible the moment Beijing decides differently.

IV. The Agenda That Isn’t the Agenda

Swanson’s reporting also lists what officials say the two governments are actually hoping to announce this week, and the list is worth reading against everything the first three sections of this essay just described, because the gap between the two is the whole point. Both countries are reportedly interested in progress on a new “board of trade” that would lower tariffs on roughly $30 billion of goods on each side — a genuinely substantial figure, if it materialises, and one that would mark the first concrete structural change to the relationship since Busan rather than another rolling extension of the status quo. Analysts are also watching for movement on AI safety, the Iran war’s economic spillover, and — cautiously, without much expectation of a breakthrough — Taiwan and Chinese investment in the United States.

Notice what’s missing from that list: any of the mineral restrictions, the State Council decrees, the “industrial distillation” accusation, or the dueling blacklists that occupy the previous two sections of this essay. Those items aren’t on the public agenda because naming them publicly would require someone to admit the shin-kicking is happening, and neither government particularly wants to be the one to say so on camera, in the week they’re both trying to project stability. An administration official, briefing reporters on Friday, would only say that China’s underperformance on rare earths “would be discussed” — folded into the AI-safety and fentanyl conversation rather than given its own line item. That’s the pattern this series keeps finding: the disputes that could actually derail the relationship get handled as asides inside meetings whose headline purpose is something else entirely, while the genuinely soluble, mutually beneficial items — a tariff-reducing trade board, a notification mechanism for AI incidents — get the press conference.

Pierson's reporting names the strategy outright: analysts told him Xi has calculated that the best way to manage Trump is "high-profile summits rich in optics but thin in substance," on the theory that a president enticed by pageantry is less likely to do something that would derail the relationship — like releasing that frozen $14 billion Taiwan arms package. That isn't a byproduct of how this summit was planned. It's the plan.

V. The Miscalculation Nobody Thinks Is Coming, Specifically

This is the part of Swanson’s reporting that belongs in direct conversation with Part V of this series. Geoffrey Gertz, a senior fellow at the Center for a New American Security, told Swanson there is “a real risk” in the coming months that one side unintentionally crosses what the other considers a red line — not through a deliberate escalation, but through the ordinary accumulation of tit-for-tat moves each government considers proportionate and calibrated. “I think over time, some type of that miscalculation, misperception is pretty likely,” he said.

Read that sentence next to the argument Robert Kaplan made in his New York Times essay four essays and a few days ago: that the First World War was set in motion partly by Germany’s miscalculation about how firm Britain’s ambiguous-sounding commitments actually were, and that the same dynamic — one side reading weakness into what the other intended as careful, deliberate silence — is the live risk sitting underneath the current Taiwan ambiguity. Gertz isn’t talking about Taiwan specifically, and he isn’t invoking 1914. But he is describing the identical mechanism in miniature, playing out continuously, on a shorter clock, across mineral export licences and trade blacklists rather than arms sales and defence commitments. Kaplan’s warning was structural and historical. Gertz’s is operational and current. Put together, they describe the same failure mode at two different altitudes: a relationship that depends on both sides reading each other’s restraint correctly, running on a channel where nobody has agreed on where the actual red lines sit.

David Pierson, reporting for the New York Times from Hong Kong, adds context that makes this risk asymmetric rather than merely mutual: the two leaders are operating, in his phrase, on vastly different clocks. Trump is constrained by a midterm election and a war pulling his attention elsewhere. Xi, having secured a norm-breaking fourth term, answers to no electorate and can afford to let the shin-kicking run indefinitely. Jonathan Czin, a Brookings researcher who previously analysed Chinese politics at the CIA, put it bluntly: each encounter with Trump “would only have reinforced Xi’s prior conviction that Trump is leading an America that is dangerous but declining.” Suisheng Zhao of the University of Denver’s China Center was blunter still: “Xi would love to tell Trump, ‘Go on, stay the course.’” A relationship where one party is patient because it can afford to be, and the other improvisational because it has to be, is not one where both sides read the shin-kicking the same way.

The asymmetry runs deeper than political calendars. Karma’s Radio Atlantic conversation argues Trump’s negotiating position has weakened since his first term, for reasons unrelated to China’s own choices. The Fed, under Kevin Warsh, raised interest rates last week for the first time in three years — a response to inflation the Iran war has driven back up — with consumers angrier and prices higher than in Trump’s first term. “If Donald Trump’s main way to negotiate is by threatening economic coercion,” Karma put it, “China now knows he doesn’t have much to use.” Xi faces his own domestic pressure, by Vivian Salama’s account on the same programme, but answers to a system that won’t punish him for it the way a midterm electorate might punish Trump.

VI. What This Adds to the Scorecard

Part I of this series opened a TACO scorecard and predicted Taiwan would land closest to Threaten, delivered through omission. Part IV updated that to note the omission runs both directions — Beijing threatening by not-saying, Washington accommodating by not-doing. Part V, via Kaplan, argued that Obfuscate carries more embedded risk than the scorecard’s framing initially allowed, because ambiguity only stabilises for as long as both sides keep reading it the same way.

Swanson’s reporting adds a category this series hasn’t had to use yet, because it doesn’t describe a single postured silence but a running, deniable exchange — Accommodate and Threaten happening simultaneously, in the same relationship, calibrated specifically to avoid triggering the Capitulate-or-confrontation binary either government actually fears. Bessent’s water polo metaphor is, in its own way, an admission that Obfuscate isn’t really one move in this administration’s playbook. It’s the entire playing surface. The state dinner, the honour guard rehearsal, the “very good relationship” Bessent credits to the two leaders personally, sit on top of a contest that both governments have agreed, tacitly, to keep running below the waterline precisely because surfacing it would force the confrontation neither wants to have this week.

Xi has a name for this arrangement, and it belongs in the scorecard as its own entry. Pierson reports Beijing has pushed a formula it calls "constructive strategic stability" — language that sounds like exactly the diplomatic infrastructure this series has spent five essays watching both governments avoid building. In practice, the phrase functions as a demand that Washington accept limits on where it's allowed to challenge China, invoked already this year when the FCC proposed restricting Chinese humanoid robots. Czin's assessment is the sharpest line in the piece: "The truce is not constructive because little diplomatic infrastructure is being built. It is tactical rather than strategic. And therefore it is inherently unstable." Obfuscate, with a name and a press release — proof Beijing has already branded the fourth category as something else.

VII. One Day Out

Xi Jinping lands in Washington tomorrow. Whatever gets announced — a truce extension, a mineral-export commitment, another line about “constructive strategic stability” — will be genuine news, and this series will cover it as such once it happens. But Swanson’s reporting is a useful corrective to run the day before the actual event, because it argues, with a specificity the choreography itself can’t supply, that the summit’s real business isn’t really happening in the Cross Hall or the state dining room at all. It’s happening in export-licence approvals, blacklist additions, and sanctions on supply-chain auditors — a shadow negotiation running on its own clock, six months at a time, that the pageantry exists partly to distract from and partly to protect.

Kaplan asked whether America is blundering toward a war with China nobody intends to fight. Gertz’s warning suggests the more immediate risk is smaller and stranger than that: not a war, but an accumulating series of proportionate, deniable kicks under the table, any one of which could eventually land somewhere neither government meant to aim.
