A National Park Service worker drags a skimmer through the algae bloom at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. Fishing for answers, or just for paint? Alex Kent / NYT

There is a famous American proverb, attributed variously to W. C. Fields and to a Scottish schoolteacher named Thomas Palmer, that runs: “If at first you don’t succeed, try, try again.” Palmer added a crucial second line that history forgot: “Then quit. No use being a damn fool about it.”

Donald Trump has memorized the first part.

I have been watching the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool with the rapt attention of a man watching a slow-motion plumbing disaster, which is precisely what it is. Fourteen million dollars. A no-bid contract to a construction outfit with the right friends. A bottom painted a patriotic shade the President himself christened “American flag blue,” because nothing says reverence for the republic like treating one of its most solemn vistas as a craft project. And then — algae. Great blooming clouds of it, the color of pea soup left in the sun, rising up to swallow the patriotic blue like the Potomac reclaiming what was always hers.

The paint began to peel. Tourists came not to reflect, but to gawk. A 67-year-old three-time Olympic canoeist named David Hearn stopped on a bike ride to touch a floating rubbery flap of the stuff and was promptly arrested by the Park Police, detained four hours, threatened with a decade in prison for the crime of being curious near water. And the President’s solution, announced Saturday from the pulpit of social media, was that the pool would likely have to be drained. Repaired. Done over.

The $14 million "American flag blue" coating, peeling off the basin and surfacing like a fin. The two-millimeter flap that got a man arrested.

Try, try again.

Here is what fascinates me, as a Canadian who has had a long time to study this man from the safe distance of the 49th parallel: Trump no longer expects to succeed on the first attempt. He has, I think, made peace with iteration. The first try is no longer an attempt at victory; it is reconnaissance. It is the rough draft he was always going to crumple up.

Consider the pattern, because the pattern is the whole show.

The border wall was going to be built, paid for by Mexico, in one triumphant span. Then by the United States. Then by emergency military funds. Then by GoFundMe, in a stretch so memorable it ended in a fraud indictment for the man who ran it. Each failure was simply the prelude to the next press release announcing the same wall, freshly virginal, as though the previous announcement had never happened.

Consider the tariffs, the purest specimen of all. They go on. The markets convulse. They come off — “paused,” ninety days, a magnanimous reprieve. Then they go back on, at a different number, against a different country, for a different reason. Then a deal is announced, the greatest deal, and the deal turns out to be the thing that already existed, or a handshake that evaporates by the next news cycle, or a number no one on the other side has agreed to. The tariff is never wrong. The tariff is simply between attempts. This is TACO made flesh — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — except the genius is that the cycle never has to resolve. You threaten, you retreat, you reframe the retreat as a victory, and then you threaten again, and a sufficiently tired public stops keeping score.

But the pool, and the wall, and the tariffs are all governance. To understand the man’s relationship with the second attempt, you have to go back further, to the years when the only thing he governed was a brand. Because long before he was trying, trying again at statecraft, he was trying, trying again at commerce — and losing, and relaunching, and losing again, with a consistency that ought to have been a warning label.

Trump Steaks, sold briefly at The Sharper Image — a store for people who buy massage chairs, now also apparently a butcher — before vanishing. Trump Vodka, which was going to dominate a country where a meaningful share of the customers don’t drink. Trump University, which was going to teach you his secrets and instead taught its founder the meaning of a $25 million fraud settlement. Trump Mortgage, launched with great fanfare in 2006, dead by 2007, having chosen the eve of the housing collapse to enter the mortgage business — a feat of timing so perfect it approaches art. Trump Airlines, the Trump Shuttle, repossessed by creditors. Trump: The Game (”It’s not whether you win or lose — it’s whether you win!”), which did not sell. Trump Magazine, folded. Trump Ice, the bottled water, because if there is one product the world was missing, it was water with his name on it.

Each one launched as a sure thing. Each one a triumph foretold. And each one followed by the same maneuver we now watch him perform with national monuments: the failure is never absorbed, never learned from, never quit. It is simply rebranded, repackaged, and relaunched under a fresh coat of gold paint, until the next bottling plant blooms its own quiet algae and the cycle begins again.

The casinos are the masterpiece of the genre — a man who managed to lose money running businesses whose entire mathematical premise is that the house cannot lose. Four bankruptcies in Atlantic City. The house always wins, the saying goes. He found the one way to make it try, try again.

Consider Iran. No — don’t. Some things on this list are funny because no one is dying of them. That one I’ll leave to the masks.

Comedy and tragedy. Some items on the list belong to one; some to the other.

Consider Epstein — and here we arrive at the present obsession, the thing currently being shoveled over with whatever loose dirt is at hand. The files were going to be released. Transparency! Then the files contained nothing. Then the files contained something but it was a hoax. Then it was a Democrat hoax, then a deep-state hoax, then the files were never the point and why are you even asking. Each cloaking story is a fresh coat of “American flag blue” slapped over a basin that keeps leaking the same green truth. Scrub it Monday, and it blooms again by Thursday.

This is the genius and the tragedy of the man. He has discovered that in a sufficiently distracted republic, the attempt is indistinguishable from the achievement. You need never actually drain the pool. You need only announce, with total confidence, that the pool will be drained — for “necessary repairs,” against the work of “vandals,” those shadowy “multiple individuals” who “poured corrosive and destructive chemicals” into the water, evidence pending, evidence eternal, evidence arriving in two weeks.

The vandals, you understand, are essential to the method. If you are constitutionally incapable of succeeding on the first try, you require a villain to explain why the first try failed. The wall failed because of Democrats. The tariffs failed because of globalists. The pool failed not because a hasty no-bid job painted a porous basin during a Washington summer — heaven forbid — but because a 67-year-old Olympian on a bicycle reached into the water to feel the characteristics of a paint flap. Ten years. Up to ten years, for that.

Somewhere in the long history of human governance, a leader stood before a problem he had personally caused and said: I will fix this myself, and I will do it right. Donald Trump stands before that same problem and says: Find me the man who touched the water.

And so I keep returning to the photograph that started me thinking about all this — a figure in chest waders, hooded against the sun, patiently dragging a taped pole across the green murk. Is he fishing for ideas? I’d like to think so. I’d like to think that somewhere in the Interior Department there is one honest soul who looked at fourteen million dollars of blooming algae and thought, let me at least go stand in it and understand the thing. Stand in the problem. Feel its characteristics. The very crime Mr. Hearn was arrested for.

He won’t find anything down there, of course. Not because the answer isn’t in the water, but because the answer was never going to be a solution. It was always going to be another announcement. The pool will be drained. The pool will be refilled. It will be painted again, perhaps a more durable patriotic blue this time, by a contractor with even better friends. And by August it will be green again, and there will be new vandals, and we will all be assured that this time — this time — they have really, truly killed the algae.

Try, try again.

Thomas Palmer, the forgotten schoolteacher, knew when the trying should stop. No use being a damn fool about it. But he was teaching children, who can be taught. He never had to manage a man who had discovered that in America, you can fail at the same thing forever, as long as you fail loudly, blame someone smaller, and promise to try again before the cameras blink.

The pool reflects nothing now. Just clouds, and algae, and a man with a pole, looking for something he was told was there.
