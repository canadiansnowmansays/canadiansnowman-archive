There is a particular kind of American who invokes Winston Churchill the way other people invoke scripture — not to examine the thing, but to borrow its authority. You saw it again on July 12, 2026, when Anthony Scaramucci, a Wall Street financier turned Substack essayist, ran the numbers on the Anglo-American alliance and called Lend-Lease “the greatest pitch letter in history.” Sixty-year loans that performed. A dividend that lands in the accounts of people who never read a word Churchill wrote. It is a good pitch. Scaramucci did not spend thirty-seven years on Wall Street by writing bad pitches, and I do not intend to spend this series pretending otherwise.

But a pitch is not an audit, and I have spent enough of my working life around telecommunications infrastructure — the unglamorous business of maintaining systems built by people who died before you were hired — to know that inheritance and dividend are not the same word. A dividend is a number. An inheritance comes with a family attached, and a family, examined honestly, tells you things a term sheet cannot.

So this series starts somewhere Scaramucci’s essay does not: with Leonard Jerome, Winston’s American grandfather, a Brooklyn stock speculator who made and lost at least three fortunes on Wall Street in the decades before anyone in England had heard the name Churchill. Jerome is usually filed under trivia — the reason Winston could stand before a joint session of Congress on December 26, 1941, and joke that if his father had been American and his mother British, he might have gotten there on his own. The room laughed, and the joke is quoted constantly, always as evidence of Churchill’s charm. It is rarely quoted as evidence of anything else, which is a mistake, because the joke only works if the audience already understands the deal underneath it. Winston Churchill was the product of a transaction between a declining English dukedom and an ascendant American fortune, negotiated by lawyers in 1874 with the specificity of a merger agreement, because that, more or less, is what it was.

This is not a scandalous claim. It is the plainly recorded history of what was, at the time, called the “dollar princess” phenomenon — a wave of American heiresses, daughters of the new industrial and financial fortunes, married into cash-poor British aristocratic families across the final decades of the nineteenth century. Jennie Jerome was among the earliest and most consequential examples. Her father-in-law, the 7th Duke of Marlborough, needed the money that Leonard Jerome could provide, and needed it badly enough to overcome his considerable reservations about an American bride for his second son. Leonard Jerome, for his part, needed the social ascent that only a title could confer on a Brooklyn speculator’s family, however self-made and successful. Both sides got what they came for. Blenheim Palace kept its roof. The Jeromes got a duchess in the family, eventually. And nine months and change after the wedding, on November 30, 1874, the deal produced Winston Leonard Spencer Churchill — named, deliberately, for both grandfathers, English and American, as if the ledger itself required the acknowledgment.

I raise the transactional framing not to be cute about it, but because it is the actual argument underneath everything else in this series, and I want it on the table before we go any further. Scaramucci’s essay treats the special relationship as an investment thesis that Churchill personally authored: he “went long on us before we went long on ourselves,” in Scaramucci’s phrase, and the dividends of that position have been compounding for eighty years. It is a genuinely elegant piece of writing, and I do not think it is wrong so much as incomplete. Because the thesis that Churchill authored the special relationship personally, as an act of statesmanlike foresight, requires you to overlook that the relationship was quite literally in his blood before he ever cast a vote in the House of Commons. He did not invent the bet. He was born as collateral for a bet his grandfather and his father’s family had already made, and he spent the first four decades of his life proving, over and over, in increasingly public fashion, whether that collateral was actually worth anything.

That is the shape this series will take. Sixteen parts, running roughly seventeen decades, from Leonard Jerome’s Wall Street fortunes in the 1850s through to the present-day Spencer-Churchill descendants, whose relationship to the family name is now considerably more decorative than functional. I am not writing a biography — there are shelves of those already, some of them very good, most of them written by men who admired Churchill first and investigated him second. I am writing an inheritance audit. What did this family actually pass down, generation to generation? What got spent, what got squandered, what got quietly written off, and what — if anything — is still generating a return today, eighty-five years after 1941?

Part 2 picks up with Leonard Jerome himself: the man Winston once described, looking at an old photograph, as “very fierce,” and who built and lost enough fortunes on Wall Street that “the King of Wall Street” was less a compliment than an actuarial description. Part 3 turns to Jennie — the marriage, the money, and the particular kind of loneliness that came with being an American woman managing an English ducal family that had accepted her dowry more readily than it accepted her. Part 4 is Randolph Churchill’s ruin: the father whose political career detonated itself in a single tactical miscalculation in December 1886, and whose decline haunted every year of Winston’s early life. And Part 5, closing out this first movement, is the boy himself before any of it made sense — the “backward boy” at Harrow, failing the Sandhurst entrance exam twice, a young man nobody in his own family expected very much from.

It is also worth saying something about why this particular family history keeps getting summoned into present-day political arguments at all, because that pattern is itself part of what this series is examining. Churchill is invoked with unusual frequency by American commentators specifically at moments when the Anglo-American relationship feels strained — Scaramucci says as much himself, noting that the alliance “feels a little stretched on both sides of the Atlantic” right now, in the context of ongoing USMCA review pressure and a general cooling of transatlantic and continental sentiment across 2025 and into 2026. This is not a new rhetorical move. Churchill has been summoned in almost identical language during the Suez crisis of 1956, during the Falklands War of 1982, and during the run-up to the Iraq War in 2003 — each time as a reminder that the alliance has weathered worse and always finds its footing, and each time by commentators who had a specific present-day argument to make and found Churchill’s authority a convenient vehicle for making it. I do not think this makes the invocation illegitimate. I do think it means a series like this one has an obligation to ask, at every turn, whether the version of Churchill being invoked in any given moment actually matches the documented record, or whether it has been quietly smoothed into whatever shape the present argument requires. That is, in the end, the difference between an inheritance and a legend: an inheritance has an itemized list attached to it. A legend does not, and it is considerably easier to argue with a legend’s authority than with its actual contents.

I want to be honest about something before we get further in, because Scaramucci’s essay and pieces like it tend to skip past it: this series is not going to be an uncomplicated celebration. Churchill’s record includes the Bengal famine of 1943, the Amritsar-adjacent politics of empire in India across four decades of his career, and the catastrophic misjudgment of Gallipoli, which we will get to properly in Part 6. A series that only ever tells the comeback story, and never the failure that made the comeback necessary, is not history. It is public relations wearing a good suit. I intend to write the failures with the same care as the triumphs, not because I think it diminishes the man, but because I think it is the only way an “inheritance” framing means anything at all. You cannot audit an estate by only counting the assets.

There is also the matter of my own standing to write any of this, which I should address plainly rather than let a reader wonder about it. I am not English. I am not American. I am a retired Canadian telecommunications man writing from Toronto, admiring a nineteenth-century humorist named Stephen Leacock closely enough that I have built half my satirical instincts on his model, and I am fully aware that Canada does not appear anywhere in Scaramucci’s essay about “the English-speaking peoples.” That is not an oversight on his part so much as a structural feature of how the special relationship gets discussed in both London and Washington: as a bilateral arrangement between two capitals, with everyone else — Canada included, despite having stood in every one of the wars this relationship was forged in — treated as ambient scenery. My great-uncle, the Canadian diplomat Hugh Keenleyside, spent a career managing exactly this problem from the Canadian side of the ledger: how do you maintain a seat at a table that two larger powers have already agreed is set for two? I do not think that makes me a hostile witness to the Churchill family. I think it makes me one of the few people likely to write this series who has no institutional stake in either flattering the family or tearing it down. A Canadian auditing the Churchill estate has no dog in the fight over whether the special relationship was a triumph or a myth. I am just here for the books.

It is worth dwelling a moment longer on the “dollar princess” phenomenon itself, because Scaramucci’s essay treats Jennie Jerome’s marriage as a piece of charming trivia — evidence of Churchill’s American blood, offered and then quickly set aside so the essay can get to Lend-Lease. It deserves more weight than that. Between roughly 1870 and 1914, something on the order of a hundred American heiresses married into the British peerage, in a wave large enough that British newspapers of the period covered it as its own social phenomenon, with its own vocabulary and its own well-understood mechanics. Cash-strapped estates, still enormous in acreage and prestige but increasingly hollow in liquid capital after decades of falling agricultural rents, needed American industrial and financial fortunes to keep the roofs on and the staff paid. American fortunes, however large, still lacked what only a title could confer: standing in a social hierarchy that no amount of railroad or Wall Street money could purchase directly. The dollar princesses were the clearing mechanism between these two forms of capital — social and financial — and Jennie Jerome’s marriage to Randolph Churchill in 1874 was one of the earliest and, in retrospect, most consequential examples of the arrangement working exactly as designed. Consuelo Vanderbilt’s marriage to the 9th Duke of Marlborough in 1895 is the more famous instance today, partly because it was more nakedly transactional and partly because Consuelo herself later wrote about how miserable it made her. But it happened at the same Blenheim Palace that Jennie had married into two decades earlier, to the same family, following essentially the same script. Which is to say: by the time Winston Churchill’s own American blood became a matter of national pride, useful for standing before Congress and invoking, it was not really evidence of some unique personal destiny. It was evidence that he belonged to the single most well-documented category of transatlantic capital arbitrage in the entire history of the British aristocracy.

This is the frame I want to hold onto across all sixteen parts: not that the Churchill family’s story is somehow lesser for having a financial mechanism underneath its more romantic public narrative, but that the mechanism and the narrative operated simultaneously, at every generation, and a series that only tells one or the other is not actually telling the whole story. Scaramucci’s essay is, in its own way, doing to the nation-to-nation relationship exactly what the aristocratic press did to the Jerome-Churchill marriage in 1874: describing a transactional arrangement in the language of destiny and character, because destiny and character make for a better speech than a settlement agreement does. I don’t think this makes his essay dishonest. I think it makes it incomplete in a specific and recognizable way, and this series exists, in large part, to supply the missing half of the ledger.

One last thing, on method. Every essay in this series will use specific dates rather than vague temporal gestures — “in 1886,” not “years later” — because I think imprecision is how myths get built in the first place. Churchill’s own version of his life, told and retold across dozens of his own books, is a masterclass in selective chronology: the wilderness years get their own mythology, the failures get compressed, the successes get expanded. I am not immune to the same temptation, and neither, frankly, was Scaramucci in his essay — Lend-Lease gets a paragraph, the war debt repayment schedule gets a sentence, and the fact that Britain’s final Lend-Lease payment landed only in 2006, sixty-one years after the war ended, gets deployed as a triumphant statistic rather than what it also was: a six-decade-long reminder, delivered annually, of exactly how insolvent Britain had been in 1940-41. Both things can be true. The relationship performed, and it performed because one side needed rescuing badly enough to accept extraordinary terms. That is not a knock against the alliance. It is simply what the ledger says, and this series intends to read the ledger in full, starting with the man who opened the account before Winston Churchill was ever born.
