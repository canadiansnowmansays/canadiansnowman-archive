Attorney General Robert H. Jackson, who warned in 1940: ‘The most dangerous power of the prosecutor [is] that he will pick people that he thinks he should get, rather than pick cases that need to be prosecuted.

Then Attorney General Robert H. Jackson warned his fellow prosecutors long ago of the danger of picking the person first and the crime second. “Therein is the most dangerous power of the prosecutor, that he will pick people that he thinks he should get, rather than pick cases that need to be prosecuted.”

That is how Judge Waverly Crenshaw opened his thirty-two-page memorandum opinion in United States of America v. Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia. “That,” the judge wrote, “is the situation here.” The Robert Jackson quote comes from Jackson’s 1940 address to U.S. Attorneys, “The Federal Prosecutor” — a speech delivered in the shadow of what was happening in Europe, by a man who would go on to serve as chief prosecutor at the Nuremberg Trials. That Crenshaw reached for Jackson eighty-six years later tells you everything about what he believed he was looking at.

The indictment that greeted Abrego Garcia upon his return to American soil on June 6, 2025, charged him with two counts: conspiring to transport undocumented aliens between 2016 and 2025, and transporting undocumented aliens during a traffic stop on November 30, 2022, in Tennessee.

The timing alone should have raised every alarm in the Department of Justice. A man wins a civil lawsuit against the government. The government is ordered by the Supreme Court to bring him back. And the moment he touches American soil, he is placed in handcuffs on criminal charges. In any law school class on prosecutorial ethics, this sequence would be presented as a textbook example of what not to do. In Robert Jackson’s terms, the government had picked the person — Abrego Garcia — and then gone looking for the crime.

The crime they found was a traffic stop.

A Tennessee Highway Patrol trooper had pulled Abrego Garcia over on Interstate 40 for driving 75 in a 65 zone. He was driving an SUV with approximately eight passengers. The officers discussed the possibility of human trafficking among themselves, questioned him, and determined he was driving coworkers to Maryland from a construction job in St. Louis. His wife Jennifer would later explain that Kilmar worked in construction and routinely transported groups of workers between job sites — a practice so common in the industry that anyone who has driven past a construction crew on a highway has seen it. He received a warning citation for an expired driver’s license. He was not arrested. He was not charged. He was not even given a speeding ticket. Released body camera footage from the Tennessee Highway Patrol shows officers openly discussing the possibility that Abrego Garcia was trafficking the passengers — and then letting him go, because there was nothing to charge him with.

Kilmar Abrego Garcia speaks with a Tennessee state trooper on the side of I-40 in 2022. Tennessee Highway Patrol

The investigation into the traffic stop was opened and then closed. It sat dormant for nearly three years. The file gathered dust. No prosecutor in the Middle District of Tennessee looked at it and said, “This is a case that needs to be brought.” No one thought it merited prosecution. No one thought about it at all. Until Abrego Garcia embarrassed the president.

What Judge Crenshaw documented, across those thirty-two pages, was the anatomy of a vindictive prosecution. The timeline is damning in its simplicity:

Abrego Garcia wins in the Maryland trial court. Abrego Garcia wins in the Fourth Circuit. Abrego Garcia wins unanimously at the Supreme Court. Within days of the Supreme Court ruling, the Department of Homeland Security reopens its closed investigation of the 2022 traffic stop. Less than thirty days later, the DOJ charges Abrego Garcia and returns him to the United States to answer a two-count indictment. As CBS News reported on the day the charges were dismissed, the sequence spoke for itself.

Kilmar Abrego Garcia arrives with his wife Jennifer Vasquez Sura and attorney Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg at U.S. District Court in Greenbelt, Md., on Dec. 22, 2025, for a hearing. (Alex Wong/Getty Images, File)

The indictment was driven from the top. As even Fox News acknowledged in its reporting on the dismissal, Judge Crenshaw found that top Justice Department officials, under Attorney General Pam Bondi and Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, pushed for the charges. This was not a case that bubbled up from a field office. It was not a career prosecutor in Nashville who looked at the evidence and said, “We should bring this.” It was leadership in Washington, D.C. — the political appointees, the people who serve at the pleasure of the president — directing a prosecution against a man who had just won a unanimous Supreme Court ruling against the administration that appointed them.

And here is where Todd Blanche made the mistake that sealed the case against his own department. On the day the indictment was unsealed, Blanche appeared on Fox News. He was asked about the prosecution. And he said — on camera, for the record — that the DOJ began investigating Abrego Garcia after a federal judge in Maryland determined he had been improperly deported.

“What should we do as the Department of Justice,” Blanche said, “when a judge is accusing us of doing something wrong?” As both Democracy Docket and MSN reported, those words would become central to the court’s finding of vindictiveness.

Read that quote again. Blanche is not saying: we investigated Abrego Garcia because we had evidence of a crime. He is saying: we investigated him because a judge ruled against us. He is saying the quiet part out loud — that the criminal investigation was a response to the civil litigation, that the prosecution was retaliation for the judicial ruling, that the Department of Justice opened a case against a man not because of what he did but because of what the courts said the government did to him. This is the definition of vindictive prosecution. Todd Blanche defined it on national television and apparently did not realise he was doing so.

Glenn Kirschner, who spent thirty years as a federal prosecutor, has a phrase for what Blanche did. Writing on his Justice Matters Substack, Kirschner said: “Todd Blanch opens his mouth only to change feet.” Because Blanche had just told the country — on national television — that the criminal investigation was retaliation for a judicial ruling. He had described vindictive prosecution while apparently not realizing that was what he was describing.

Blanche did not testify at the February 26, 2026, evidentiary hearing on the vindictive prosecution motion. Abrego Garcia’s lawyers subpoenaed him. He never took the stand. But his public statements, as Judge Crenshaw wrote, “stand unrebutted.”

“Blanch’s words directly confirm that the Executive Branch reopened the criminal investigation because the Judicial Branch required the Executive Branch to facilitate Abrego’s return from El Salvador.” The judge’s language left no room for ambiguity.

There is one more detail from the evidentiary hearing that deserves attention, and it comes not from the court record but from a fact Kirschner surfaced on Justice Matters: the chief of the criminal division in the Middle District of Tennessee, the office prosecuting Abrego Garcia, was a man named Ben Shreder. He had previously served as a prosecutor in Kirschner’s own former office. Shreder resigned the day the indictment was returned. Evidence produced during the hearing indicated he had not supported the decision to indict.

Kirschner notes that the DOJ’s own manual — the U.S. Attorney’s Manual — requires more than probable cause to bring a federal indictment. Prosecutors must have a reasonable likelihood of success on the merits. They must have enough evidence to obtain and sustain a conviction on appeal. The standard is proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The Abrego Garcia indictment, built on a traffic stop that produced no arrest, no ticket, and no charge, met none of those standards.

The government tried to rebut the presumption of vindictiveness. Judge Crenshaw knocked down each argument: the “newly discovered evidence” explanation failed. The “independent prosecutor” explanation failed. “The government’s rebuttal to the presumption of vindictiveness does not engage with the evidence,” Crenshaw wrote. “As a matter of logic, the government cannot rebut a presumption when it does not address the evidence supporting the presumption.”

And then the line that will outlast the case itself:

“The evidence before this court sadly reflects an abuse of prosecuting power.”
