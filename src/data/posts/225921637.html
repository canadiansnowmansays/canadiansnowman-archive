When the Canadian House of Commons elects a new Speaker, the winner does not walk to the chair. The prime minister takes one arm, the leader of the Official Opposition takes the other, and the new Speaker is hauled up the aisle with a show of reluctance, as if being led to the gallows. The custom comes from Westminster, along with a story, probably more legend than history, that a Speaker who carried unwelcome news from the Commons to the king risked losing his head. Nobody in Ottawa has been beheaded for the job, but the play-acting survives, because it says something true. The Speaker serves the House, not a party, and is supposed to look as if he would rather not.

The American speakership works the other way round. The speaker of the House of Representatives is the majority party’s leader, its chief strategist and its loudest voice, and nobody pretends otherwise. When Hakeem Jeffries takes the gavel in January, as he almost certainly will, he will not need dragging. He spent the days before his nomination meeting nearly every member and member-elect of his caucus, and Part XIX described how he moved towards his critics before they could organise. Even though nobody ran against him, he asked for a recorded secret ballot, so that every member could say what they thought without being seen to say it.

Part XX argued that the United States has no leader of the opposition, only a two-year audition that ends with a nominee. That is true of the presidency. It will be less true of Congress from January. With the White House still Republican, the speaker will arguably be the most powerful Democrat in the country, which makes the nearest American equivalent of the Westminster office a man who has never run for president.

So it is worth listening to how he talks. On Friday, 20 November, two days after his caucus nominated him, Jeffries sat down in Washington with Lulu Garcia-Navarro for The Interview, as published in The New York Times on 21 November. It is a revealing conversation, mostly because of what he would not say.

“The American people”

Garcia-Navarro opened by asking him to explain, in his own terms, why he was the man for this moment. He answered with a list of fights House Democrats had waged against the Republicans: on the “One Big Ugly Bill”, Medicaid cuts, the Affordable Care Act tax credits, the Epstein files, the war in Iran, money for Immigration and Customs Enforcement and corruption. She pressed. Was he saying the record was his? No, he said, it was a record he had been proud to be part of. She pressed again, and he gave her Crown Heights: a working-class neighbourhood, a mother who worked for the city for 45 years, a father who was a substance-abuse social worker through the heroin and crack epidemics, and a childhood spent wondering whether he and his friends would survive the violence.

A few minutes later she pointed out that he had already said “the American people” what felt like a hundred times. He did not apologise. “I will consistently use that phrase,” he said, because Democrats work for them. Later, laughing, she said she felt she had to invoke the American people herself.

The sharpest exchange came over whether a lame-duck president who will never face voters again could be pressured by anything, including a long government shutdown. Jeffries said it remained to be seen. She asked again. It remained to be seen. She asked a third time, and told him that was not an answer. “That’s the answer,” he said.

It is easy to mock that kind of discipline, and the interview has fun with it. But it is worth being clear about what it is for. A speaker with 260 Democrats, by his own count, from Blue Dogs to democratic socialists, cannot afford a stray sentence. Every remark about a colleague becomes a headline about a split, and every prediction becomes a promise someone will hold him to. The phrase “the American people” is not a verbal tic. It is a way of refusing to take sides inside his own party while sounding as if he has taken one.

The difficulty is that the voters who just elected his majority may want something else. Part XIII, “The Wrecking Ball,” drew on Sarah Longwell’s hundreds of focus groups to argue that Democratic voters want a fighter more than they want any particular ideology. Jeffries is a fighter, but a disciplined one, and discipline on television can look a lot like caution.

Where he was blunt

He can be blunt. He simply chooses where.

Asked whether he would ever sit down with Stephen Miller, the president’s new chief of staff, he said no, twice. He repeated his description of Miller as a “stone-cold racist” and called him the architect of a “violent mass deportation machine.” Asked about Republican warnings that Democratic investigations would amount to lawfare, he said it was “crazy” for people who had weaponised the federal government against the president’s perceived enemies to lecture anybody about overreach. Pete Hegseth, he said, should be impeached if Trump does not remove him. He boasted that House Democrats, from the minority, had run Pam Bondi and then Kristi Noem “out of town.”

None of that costs him anything with his own caucus. The pattern is plain. On the other party, Jeffries speaks freely. On his own, he speaks in the passive voice.

Where he was careful

Three subjects drew his most careful answers, and each one sits on a fault line inside the Democratic coalition.

The first was Chuck Schumer. Part XIX described Chris Van Hollen’s challenge to Schumer for Senate majority leader, and Garcia-Navarro asked Jeffries four times whether Schumer was up to the job. He praised Schumer’s record for New York. He said he supported him, and had said so repeatedly. Then he said the senators would have to work it out, because House Democrats “don’t like it when the senators start messing around in our stuff,” and he ought to hold himself to the same standard. Only when she warned that listeners would hear his silence as a no did he say, “Of course he’s capable of leading us into the future.” In Westminster terms this is ordinary courtesy between the two houses. In Washington, ten days before the Senate’s secret ballot, it is also a way of not being on the losing side if Van Hollen pulls off an upset.

The second was the war. Jeffries wants the first big act of the new Congress, in its first week or so, to be a vote to end what he calls, every time, the “reckless and costly war of choice” in Iran. But federal funding runs out on 11 December, three weeks before he takes the gavel, and Part XIX argued that the public vote on that bill matters more than any leadership ballot. Asked whether he would risk a shutdown to limit the war money, he would not say “one way or the other.” He did point to the 43-day shutdown, which he called the longest complete shutdown in American history, and said that at the end of it Democrats had forced a vote on extending the Affordable Care Act tax credits. That is a hint, not a commitment.

The third was Israel. Two bills are waiting in the House: the Block the Bombs bill, with 82 co-sponsors when the interview was recorded, and the Stop the Settlements bill, with 76. Asked whether he would put them to a vote, he said the status quo in Gaza and the West Bank was “unacceptable, unsustainable and un-American,” but that he had not taken a position on either bill. What he offered instead was the West Bank Settler Violence Protection and Prevention Act, which he wants to move in the first 100 days because it would have strong support in the caucus. Asked about money from AIPAC, he answered with his own small-donor average of $29 a contribution, and noted that AIPAC says it no longer gives directly as an organisation. The seven democratic socialists who voted against him, as Part XIX noted, named Gaza as part of their mandate. Jeffries named a smaller bill that could pass.

Seen together, the three answers describe the same strategy. On each question he picked the option that keeps the most Democrats in the room.

The fusion caucus

The Atlantic has spent the past fortnight arguing over who won the Democrats’ landslide, and the answer that keeps coming back is everyone.

Writing for The Atlantic on 16 November, Elaine Godfrey set out each faction’s claim. The Congressional Progressive Caucus grew by about 25 members, to more than 105, and the number of democratic socialists in the House went from two to eight. The Blue Dogs grew too, to at least 22, and Third Way argued that moderates in swing seats were the true “majority makers.” Some newcomers are refusing to choose at all. Godfrey pointed to Sam Forstag of Montana, who joined both the Blue Dogs and the Progressive Caucus. The name for this, borrowed from the conservative movement of the 1950s, is fusionism.

Idrees Kahloon, also in The Atlantic, on 18 November, found the moderates being gracious in victory, declaring the old left-right argument out of date. Elissa Slotkin now sorts Democrats into “team fight” and “team flight.” Abigail Spanberger, Virginia’s governor, had the bluntest advice, delivered in language this publication will not print: pass bills on tariffs, the Iran war and Medicaid, stop talking about it, and govern.

Neera Tanden, who runs the Center for American Progress and says parts of the left treat her as a bigger threat than Trump, told David Frum on his podcast, for The Atlantic on 18 November, that the big tent “includes the left. Let me say that again: includes the left.” She called 2026 “a very normie election.” Frum offered a road safety warning for a party that wants to stay in the middle: the buses come from both directions.

Put Jeffries next to these pieces and his interview reads differently. His first answer to Garcia-Navarro was a list of factions: “We have progressives. We have New Dems. We have Blue Dogs.” He said he had strong support in every group. A fusion caucus needs a leader who will not be seen to belong to any one wing, and that is the role he is auditioning for every time he declines to answer a question about one. Part VII, “The Big Tent,” gave this series its working theory. Jeffries is the first Democrat in a position to run the tent rather than argue about its size.

Boring, on purpose

The Denver conference in Part XX was paid for by the centrist group Majority Democrats. Its chairman, Jake Auchincloss of Massachusetts, does not curse, and in a profile by Russell Berman for The Atlantic on 11 November, his ambition came under the headline “Make Politics Boring Again.” After the election, Auchincloss said the voters had handed Democrats “a permission slip” and listed what to do with it: end the war, repeal the tariffs and prosecute Pete Hegseth.

Compare that with the agenda Jeffries gave Garcia-Navarro: end the war early in the new Congress, restore SNAP benefits, reverse the Medicaid cuts, extend the Affordable Care Act tax credits, end the Trump tariffs and ban members of Congress from trading stock. Add Hegseth’s impeachment and the lists are almost the same. The centrists in Denver and the speaker in Washington want largely the same first year. What they are competing over is credit, and who gets to call the result their wing’s victory.

That is good news for the majority and awkward news for the auditions. If the agenda belongs to the speaker, the 2028 contenders will be running on his record rather than their own ideas. Jeffries seemed comfortable with that. He mentioned a housing bill that passed in this Congress, with Republicans in charge of both chambers, and that became law when Trump neither signed nor vetoed it. He called it “a foundation for the type of things that we should be doing.” It was the most boring sentence in the interview, and very possibly the most important one.

Unbought and unbossed

Near the end, Jeffries talked about growing up in Crown Heights, and about the woman who represented it in Congress for the first 12 years of his life. He never met Shirley Chisholm, but he said it was an honour to stand on her shoulders and continue the fights she started “in such an unbought and unbossed way.” That was her slogan. In 1972 she became the first Black candidate to seek a major party’s presidential nomination, and the first woman to seek the Democrats’, and her campaign posters carried those words under her face.

The comparison flatters him and tests him at once. Chisholm was famous for refusing to do what her party’s leaders wanted. Jeffries is about to become one of those leaders, and his whole method depends on not saying what he thinks until he has counted the votes. Both can be honourable. They are not the same thing. When Garcia-Navarro asked about the failed impeachment vote on Hegseth, for which some members did not turn up, he said it “was whipped” and called it “a collective disappointment.” Chisholm, one suspects, would have named names.

What a loyal opposition needs from a speaker

This series has asked for twenty instalments whether the Democrats could act as a loyal opposition. Part XVII found them in a position Westminster has no name for: a majority in Congress that is still the opposition to the government. From January, that opposition will have a leader with a gavel, a 260-seat caucus held together by careful language, and a president who, by Jeffries’s own account, had not yet called.

The Westminster ritual makes a Speaker look reluctant because the office is meant to stand above the fight. Jeffries’s office will stand in the middle of it, and his task is harder than looking reluctant. He has to keep a fusion caucus fused while delivering things its factions can each call their own, and do it fast enough that the voters who elected him, and who wanted a fight, believe they got one.

The first public test comes before he holds the gavel. Federal funding runs out on 11 December, and before then the House must vote on the money for the war, with every member’s name on the record. The phrase “the American people” will not settle that vote. The count will.

Before he walked into the caucus room on 18 November, Jeffries said, the last person he spoke to was his mother. He told her he loved her and asked her to pray for him, as she always has. Nobody had to drag him to the chair. The question for the next two years is whether, when it matters, he will be the one doing the dragging.
