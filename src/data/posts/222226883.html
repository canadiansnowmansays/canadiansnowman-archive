On October 15, 2026, the lights went out in the maze.

That is not a metaphor, exactly. It is a legal threshold. October 14 closed the books on the last full report voters will see before Election Day. After that, outside groups must still report large spending within 24 hours, and candidates must report large contributions within 48. But the donors behind a super PAC’s money — any dollar channelled through a 501(c)(4) nonprofit, any pop-up committee conjured in the final sprint of a Senate race — stay hidden until the post-election report in December, after the outcomes have been decided. Voters can watch the ghosts move. They cannot see who is feeding them.

The mechanism has been in place for years. The effect is always the same. The final days before a midterm election are among the most expensive in American politics and among the least transparent. Money moves in the dark. The ghosts eat. Nobody watching can see which way they are going.

The Structural Paradox

The 2026 midterm campaign has produced a contradiction that the blackout has now made permanent until the polls close tomorrow.

Democratic Senate candidates have out-raised their Republican opponents in competitive races. The energy, the grassroots donors, the small-dollar fundraising that fills candidate war chests — in the seven races rated as tossups by the nonpartisan Cook Political Report, Democratic candidates have the individual fundraising advantage.

And yet, The New York Times reported on October 26, 2026, that Republican outside groups are outspending their Democratic counterparts on advertising in every single one of those seven races. By comparison, Republicans have only a narrow spending edge in House races — the Senate is where the disparity is concentrated. The overall spending in the most competitive Senate contests favours Republicans even as the candidates themselves are being out-funded on the other side.

This is the structural reality of post-Citizens United campaign finance. Candidate fundraising and outside-group spending are parallel systems, governed by different rules, funded by different sources, and capable of producing outcomes that bear no relationship to what individual voters are sending in twenty-dollar increments from their kitchen tables. A Democratic candidate in Iowa might be out-raising her opponent on every platform metric while being buried under eight figures of outside advertising she cannot respond to in kind. The candidate’s bank account and the spending environment her voters are living in are two entirely separate things.

The blackout does not create this dynamic. It simply means that until the polls close, nobody will know how much worse it got.

The $158 Million Ghost

The single largest disclosed contribution to the closing stretch of the 2026 Senate campaign came not from a political party, not from a super PAC with a flag-and-eagle logo, but from Elon Musk — $158 million donated last month to the main super PAC supporting Republican Senate candidates, The Atlantic reported on October 26, 2026.

To put that number in context: it exceeds what many Senate candidates raise across an entire two-year cycle. It is an amount that, deployed across the right combination of media markets, can saturate the advertising environment of a Senate race — every radio break, every streaming pre-roll, every social media interruption — for weeks at a stretch.

Musk’s donation was made before the October 15 cutoff. It is disclosed. You can look it up. What flows in after — through the 501(c)(4) nonprofits that have no obligation to name their donors, into the super PACs that spent it in the final days of the race — will not be disclosed until after the votes are counted. The $158 million is the visible number. The blackout is what comes after, and there is always something that comes after.

The ghosts in this maze are not slow. They learn the routes.

The Ghost Explains Himself

For most of this cycle, Musk let the money do the talking. By the final week, a New York Times analysis put his total support for Republicans at nearly $475 million, the $158 million cheque included. Yet he had said remarkably little about the elections themselves. On October 30 he broke that silence. In a post to his 241.5 million followers on X, reported by Theodore Schleifer in The New York Times, he argued that a Democratic trifecta after 2028 would make America a permanent one-party state and mean “the end of democracy in any meaningful sense of the word.” The remedy, he said, was 51 Republican senators in 2029, and every other reason to vote was irrelevant beside that one. He added that he had already voted early in Texas, home to one of the year’s tightest Senate races.

The irony of a man spending half a billion dollars to warn about the end of democracy hardly needs underlining. The mirror image appeared the same day. Reid Hoffman, Musk’s former friend and now his rival, has put $16.5 million behind James Talarico in Texas alone, part of the tens of millions he has spent on these midterms. “We should get money out of politics,” he wrote on X, before explaining why, until somebody does, he will keep spending it. Both men say they are defending democracy. Both are doing it the only way the maze now allows: outside the candidates’ own accounts, at a scale no voter can match, in the last days before the lights come back on.

The totals are already historic. In its last broadcast before the vote, a panel on PBS’s Washington Week with The Atlantic noted that 2026 is on course to be the most expensive election in American history, presidential years included. The floodgates opened with Citizens United, a decision that predates Trump, and nothing comparable happens in any other democracy. The billionaires have become campaign issues in their own right, and their money amplifies a candidate’s missteps as loudly as his message. Voters are left knowing the candidates’ names far better than they know why anyone is paying so much to elect them.

The Seven Tossups

The Senate map, The New York Times reported on October 26, 2026, “is still wide and unsettled, making both parties nervous.” The battleground at this point in the cycle includes Maine, Michigan, Alaska, Texas, Iowa, Minnesota, Nebraska, Kansas, Ohio, New Hampshire, Georgia, Mississippi, North Carolina and South Carolina — fourteen races of varying competitiveness, with seven rated as genuine tossups where either party could win.

Republicans have stayed out of battlegrounds where Trump’s deep unpopularity could hurt more than help — he has not held rallies in Maine or New Hampshire, where Republican Senate candidates are running from him rather than with him. In Maine, a new Fox News poll found both Senator Susan Collins and her Democratic challenger Troy Jackson at 49 percent, with 53 percent of Maine voters saying they were concerned Collins had voted with Trump too often.

In every one of the seven tossup races, Republican outside groups are spending more than their Democratic equivalents. These are the races where the blackout matters most and where the money flowing in after October 15 will be least visible. By the time the FEC filings arrive, the election will have been decided.

Senators elected on November 3 will serve through the entirety of the next presidential term, The New York Times noted — an observation with particular bite when you consider that the spending determining who wins those races will not be publicly known until after the results are final.

The forecasters are moving in one direction. On October 30, The New York Times reported that the Cook Political Report had shifted Nebraska from likely to lean Republican, and Florida from solid to likely Republican. Cook now projects a Democratic net gain of two to six Senate seats; they need four. A Senate majority may come down to a margin narrower than the money nobody can yet see.

The 501(c)(4) Mechanism

A brief refresher for readers who have followed this series from Part I, because this is the part that never changes.

The 501(c)(4) designation is a tax status for “social welfare” organisations that permits them to engage in political activity as a secondary purpose without disclosing their donors. These groups can accept unlimited contributions from corporations, wealthy individuals, and other entities, spend significant portions of those funds on political advertising, and funnel the remainder into super PACs that are required to disclose their expenditures — but not the ultimate source of the money. By the time a dollar travels from a donor to a 501(c)(4) to a super PAC to a television advertisement targeting a Senate candidate in Iowa, the donor’s identity has been laundered out of the public record.

This is not an oversight in the law. It is the architecture. The Citizens United decision in 2010 opened the door. Subsequent FEC inaction bolted it open. Congressional indifference cemented the hinges.

The October 15 blackout is not an accident of the calendar. It is the system functioning as designed. “A lot of money from shadowy nonprofit groups, which do not disclose their donors, tends to flow into super PACs at the very end of the midterms,” The New York Times reported. The word “tends” is doing heavy lifting there. The final weeks of any federal election cycle are when the nonprofit money floods in precisely because the window for pre-election disclosure is closed. The system is structured to ensure that the largest, most opaque late-cycle contributions arrive when voters cannot see them.

The Last Scoreboard

On November 1, 2026, Matt Zdun and Theodore Schleifer published the cycle’s final pre-election ranking of the top 20 donors in The New York Times. It is the clearest picture anyone will get before November 3, and it is a picture of the maze as it stood on October 14, the last day for which contribution data exists. Everything since sits behind the curtain.

Even the stale picture is staggering. The top 20 have put roughly $2 billion into federal races, a sum equal to about 15 percent of everything that will be spent on advertising this cycle, according to AdImpact figures cited by the Times. Measured by ad spending, no midterm since at least 2018 has cost more.

Musk sits alone at the top with $445.6 million, nearly three times the next donor, more than $200 million of it aimed at electing Ken Paxton in Texas, including a field operation the Republican Party is relying on to turn out its voters. The figure sits below the near-$475 million cited earlier for a simple reason: the ranking counts only what reached the FEC by October 14. By the Times’ own account, it leaves out tens of millions Musk gave after that date or to groups that do not file with the commission. The richest man in the world has outrun the scoreboard built to keep track of him.

Then the part that matters most to this series. Six of the 20 biggest donors are not people at all. They are nonprofits that do not disclose who funds them: One Nation ($137.9 million), sibling of the Senate Leadership Fund; American Action Network ($64.3 million), sibling of the Congressional Leadership Fund; Majority Forward ($104 million) and House Majority Forward ($97.2 million), which do the same job for the Democrats; the American Prosperity Alliance ($48.1 million), last seen in Part XII bankrolling Republican mischief in Democratic primaries; and a newcomer, the Economic Opportunity Fund ($41.5 million). That last one held less than $1 million as of mid-2025, then handed $35 million to Democratic super PACs in September and October alone. Where that money came from, no public filing yet says. Together the six account for about $493 million.

The maze is bipartisan. Both parties’ flagship super PACs are fed by sibling nonprofits whose donors no voter can name. The Republicans simply have more ghosts near the top of the table, not dirtier ones.

And some of the largest sums have not moved yet. The Soros family’s two super PACs were sitting on about $173 million in mid-October. Mike Bloomberg and Richard and Elizabeth Uihlein did most of their giving in the final two months. Late money is the maze’s favourite kind. Whatever gets spent between now and November 3 will appear on a scoreboard posted in December.

The Night the Lights Actually Went Out

On the evening of October 26, the metaphor became a maintenance report. The Federal Election Commission switched off its electronic filing system because someone was trying to break into it, according to an internal agency email obtained by The New York Times. The attempted breach was first reported by Reuters. The system stayed down for roughly two days and came back late on the afternoon of October 28.

Those two days fell inside the one window when the law still tries to keep a light on. In the final stretch, the 24-hour and 48-hour reports are the last flashlight left in the maze, and for two days nobody could switch it on. Any committee with real volume must file electronically, so the forms simply piled up unsent. Meanwhile, super PACs and other groups were spending about $80 million a day on average over the past two weeks, according to OpenSecrets figures cited by the Times, and more than $260 million on October 21 alone.

The commission that might have responded cannot. Only two of its six seats are filled, leaving it without the four votes it needs to open an investigation or bring a lawsuit. Stuart McPhail of Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington noted that such vacancies have lasted longer under Mr. Trump than under any previous president, and said voters would cast their ballots without “the full picture that the law entitles them to.”

The Maze Resets — Again

After every election, there is a period of transparency. The filings come in. Journalists map the money. Advocacy groups publish the flow charts. Congress holds hearings. Nothing changes.

This is what “the maze resets” has meant, in every cycle since Citizens United. The game ends, the lights come up, the tally is visible, the system is critiqued, and the next cycle begins with the same rules and a larger budget. The ghosts reorganise under new names. The 501(c)(4)s refill. The super PACs redeploy. The pop-up committees dissolve and reconstitute.

What changes is only the scale. Musk’s $158 million to a single super PAC for a single chamber of Congress is a figure that would have seemed fantastical in 2012, when the Citizens United era was just two years old and the mechanism was new enough that its critics could still argue it might be reined in. It was not reined in. It expanded. Each cycle the ceiling gets higher, and each cycle the argument that the ceiling might come down gets harder to make with a straight face.

The 2026 edition of the dark-money machine is larger than 2022, which was larger than 2018, which was larger than 2014. The maze does not reset. It grows.

For one more day, the expansion continues in the dark. The Power Pellet has been eaten. The ghosts are moving in corridors you cannot see. The score will be posted after the game is over.
