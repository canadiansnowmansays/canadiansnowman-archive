Stephen Gordon

In September of 1835, a seasick young gentleman-naturalist, four years into a five-year voyage he had very nearly not been allowed to join — the captain, a believer in physiognomy, had almost rejected him over the shape of his nose — stepped ashore on some black volcanic islands six hundred miles off the coast of Ecuador, and spent five weeks collecting specimens, and made the greatest data-entry error in the history of science. This is essay 1,835, and it is about a blank column. The young man collected the birds. He preserved them, crated them, shipped them home in good order. He simply did not write down, for most of them, which island each one came from — because it did not occur to him, at the time, that it could possibly matter. The answer to the deepest question our species has ever asked about itself was in that column, and the column was blank, and the story of how the books were reconstructed is the whole essay, and I have been looking forward to it since the count entered the century.

The voyage first, briskly. HMS Beagle, a small survey ship on an admiralty commission to chart the coasts of South America — the same imperial charting business that sent Cook to Tahiti at essay 1,769; the empire measured everything, and the measuring kept accidentally producing science. Her captain, Robert FitzRoy, wanted a gentleman companion for the long voyage, someone to talk to across the gulf of rank; the post went, after some negotiation about the nose, to Charles Darwin, twenty-two at departure, an amiable Cambridge graduate of no particular distinction who had been drifting toward a country parsonage and collected beetles the way other young men followed horses. By the time the Beagle reached the Galápagos in September 1835, Darwin was twenty-six and had spent four years reading the South American continent like a ledger — fossil giants in the Argentine cliffs that resembled the living animals of the same ground, a monumental earthquake in Chile that lifted the coastline bodily out of the sea before his eyes, strata and species keeping books on a scale of time that the accepted six thousand years could not begin to contain. He did not yet have the idea. He had what comes before the idea, which this series has learned to respect above almost everything: a growing suspicion that the existing accounts did not reconcile.

The Galápagos were five weeks of that suspicion failing to announce itself. Attend to what actually happened on the islands, because the popular version — young Darwin, struck by the finches, perceiving evolution in a flash of Pacific light — is another Wittenberg door, and the truth is so much better, and so much more useful, that the myth amounts to vandalism. The truth is that Darwin nearly missed it. The vice-governor of the islands, an Englishman named Lawson, remarked to him in passing that the giant tortoises differed from island to island — that he could tell, from the shape of a shell, which island any tortoise had come from. Darwin heard this, recorded it, and by his own later admission did not take it in: the islands sat in sight of one another, formed of the same volcanic rock, under the same climate — why on earth would their populations differ? He collected his birds island by island but pooled them, unlabelled, as samples of a single archipelago. The tortoises fared worse: the expedition took some thirty aboard as provisions, and ate them on the crossing of the Pacific, throwing the shells — the very shells whose island-by-island differences Lawson could read at a glance — over the side. The Beagle sailed home, in the most literal sense available to this series, eating the data. Somewhere on the floor of the Pacific lies the evidence, consumed as soup, of the pattern that would remake every science of life. I have covered ledgers burned, ledgers shut in drawers, ledgers misfiled in libraries for centuries. This is the only entry in eighteen hundred essays where the ledger was eaten, and I record it with the awe it deserves.

What saved the discovery was other people’s bookkeeping. Back in London, in the early months of 1837, Darwin delivered his bird specimens to the ornithologist John Gould — a former taxidermist, the finest bird man in England — expecting to be told he had collected an assortment of finches, wrens, and blackbirds. Gould came back within days with a finding that rearranged the furniture: they were all finches — a dozen distinct species, differing above all in their beaks, graded from massive seed-crackers to fine insect-probes, found nowhere else on Earth. A dozen species of one family, on one small archipelago. The obvious question was the one Darwin’s blank column could not answer: were the species sorted by island? And here the reconstruction began, and I want the reconstruction dwelt on, because it is the actual heroism of the story. Darwin went back through the collections of his shipmates — Captain FitzRoy had collected; so had Darwin’s servant Syms Covington and other members of the ship’s company — and their specimens, labelled by island with the unimaginative diligence of men who wrote down where they were because writing down where you were is what you did, supplied the missing column. It was the mockingbirds, in fact — which Darwin himself had happened to separate by island, three islands, three recognizably different forms — that first cracked his own resistance, in a private notebook entry of famous nervousness in which he confessed that such facts would undermine the stability of species. But the finches’ geography was rebuilt substantially from other men’s tags. The greatest theory in the history of biology stands, in part, on the routine field-labelling of a captain and a manservant whose names appear in no rhyme. Essay 1,687 established that every audit needs a Halley. This one establishes the corollary: every genius needs a colleague who fills in all the columns, every time, out of nothing more exalted than habit — and the habit is the genius’s insurance against his own blind spot. Covington filled in the column. File the name.

Here, then, is the idea at the centre of this essay, and it may be the most practically useful one the series owns. The insight was not in the specimens. Darwin held the specimens; he had held them for eighteen months. The insight was in the relation between two columns — species and island — and a relation cannot be seen in either column alone. Every entry in this series about undercounting, from the Bills of Mortality to the Bastille’s seven, has concerned a number recorded wrongly. This is the deeper failure: the number not recorded at all, because the recorder’s theory of the world told him it could not matter. Darwin did not lose data through carelessness — he was among the most careful observers alive. He lost it through confidence: the islands were alike, therefore their birds would be alike, therefore the label was redundant. His framework decided what counted as information before the information could speak, which is the one error no amount of diligence inside the framework can catch. And this, I would gently submit, is the standing condition of every institution I cover under other datelines: the blank columns are never blank by accident; they are blank because the form was designed by people certain that nothing important lived there. The remedy is not brilliance. Brilliance designed the form. The remedy is the Covington protocol — write it all down, every time, especially the fields you cannot imagine needing — because the whole future of a question may depend on a tag that present theory calls redundant. Ask the tortoises. You cannot; they were soup; that is the point.

I will close the historical account where Darwin closed it, which was slowly, and in the proper register, which is awe. He did not announce a theory in 1837. He opened a notebook — the famous Notebook B, with its spindly sketched tree of branching descent and, written above it, two words that belong on the wall of every laboratory and every newsroom on Earth: I think. Not eureka. Not behold. I think — the most honest posting in the history of science, an entry flagged as provisional at the moment of creation. And then he spent twenty-two years — twenty-two years, essay 1,519’s Leonardo hovering anxiously over every one of them — gathering barnacles and pigeons and correspondence, stress-testing the idea against every objection he could invent, before another naturalist’s letter from the Malay Archipelago finally forced his hand and On the Origin of Species went to press in 1859. The count will reach 1,859 in time, and the drawer, I am relieved to report in advance, was opened before it became a tomb. But the origin of the Origin is 1835: five weeks, black rocks, a passing remark about tortoise shells, and a blank column that other people’s habits filled in. The deepest discovery our species has made about itself was very nearly lost to the assumption that geography was a formality. It is the best cautionary tale in the files, and the caution is for the confident.

Dior’s fieldwork, I should report, has never once suffered from the blank-column error, because Dior labels everything by location as a matter of biological policy — every hydrant, post, and corner on our morning route is a record with a timestamp and a place-tag, maintained with a rigour that would have humbled the Beagle’s whole company, and read each dawn like the overnight wires. I used to find the thoroughness comical. Since researching this essay I have revised my position entirely: she is running the Covington protocol, whole-hog, on the entire lakeshore — record everything, tag everything by island, assume nothing is redundant — and if the squirrels of Toronto ever differentiate by neighbourhood, science will have exactly one complete longitudinal dataset, and it will be hers, and she will not share it, and that too is very much in the tradition of the field.

The count moves on, as it does — and here I must set down the lantern I have been carrying and tell you plainly what the road ahead looks like, because this series does not ambush its readers. The next stretch of numbers is the heaviest and the most personal territory on the entire map. At 1,865 an American war ends and a president is shot in the same fortnight, and I will write it the way I wrote 1,633. And beyond it, close together, wait three numbers I have been protecting since this series began — 1,867, 1,869, and 1,898 — my country, my voice, and my family, and I will write those slowly, and by hand, and not tonight. The essays between here and there will breathe when breathing is possible. The count arrives when it arrives. It is nearly home.

One blank column, one eaten archive, one manservant’s habit, and two words in a notebook: I think. The whole modern understanding of life, hanging by a tag.
