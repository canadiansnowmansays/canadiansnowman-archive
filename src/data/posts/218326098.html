On September 29, 2026, President Trump and six technology executives signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence. Beneath the President’s signature, the document reads “President of the Unites States.” On the same day, Mr Trump signed an executive order directing the federal government to stop saying “artificial intelligence” and say “super intelligence” instead. The typo, the signatories, the executive order, the Truth Social naming poll, the accord’s “morally binding” status, Kallstadt’s French interlude and the Indiana Pi Bill of 1897 are all real. The Office of Retroactive Justification, last seen reviewing the Section 338 tariff schedule after it was finalised, is not. Neither is its reasoning, though it is getting harder to tell.

EXECUTIVE OFFICE OF THE PRESIDENT Office of Retroactive Justification (ORJ)

Docket No. SI-2026-0929-ERR AGENCY: Office of Retroactive Justification ACTION: Correction notice; denial of correction EFFECTIVE DATE: September 29, 2026, retroactively, in keeping with Office practice

SUMMARY: The Office has received a substantial volume of public comment alleging an error on page two of the White House Accord on Super Intelligence. Having completed its review, the Office has determined that no correction is required. This notice corrects the impression that a correction was required.

I. Background

On September 29, 2026, the President signed the Accord alongside Sundar Pichai of Google, Dario Amodei of Anthropic, Mark Zuckerberg of Meta, Greg Brockman of OpenAI, Elon Musk of xAI and Jensen Huang of Nvidia, as Newsweek reported. The White House posted an image of the signed document to social media on September 30. Within hours, members of the public, a computer scientist, the Governor of California and a retired telecommunications professional in Toronto had all noticed the same four letters.

The Office notes that seven of the most closely watched signatures in the world were placed within inches of the alleged error, and that none of the seven signatories raised it. The Office interprets this not as inattention but as deference to the office of the presidency, which it commends.

II. The Matter of the Missing D

The Office considered four explanations for the text “President of the Unites States.”

Explanation A: The Verb Reading (ADOPTED). “Unites” is a verb. The line, read correctly, describes a function rather than a title: Donald J. Trump, President of the [one who] Unites States. The Office points to the President’s sustained state-uniting programme, which has to date united Canada, Denmark, Greenland, Mexico and the European Union. The Office acknowledges that these parties have been united principally with one another, and principally against the President, but notes that the Accord does not specify the direction of unity.

Explanation B: The French Roots Reading (REJECTED). Several commenters proposed that the drafter was reaching for the French États-Unis and lost the thread halfway across the Atlantic. The Office initially dismissed this, noting that the President’s grandfather, Friedrich Trump, emigrated from Kallstadt, in the German Palatinate, and that the German form, Vereinigte Staaten, contains no missing letters and several surplus ones, which is more consistent with presidential style generally.

The Office’s genealogists subsequently reported, with visible discomfort, that Kallstadt spent the late 1790s to 1814 as part of the French département of Mont-Tonnerre. The French Roots Reading is therefore rejected on the grounds that it is partly true, which the Office regards as the most dangerous category of explanation.

Explanation C: The Staffing Reading (REJECTED). It is well established that the President does not personally type. Documents bearing his signature are prepared, printed and carried by staff. The Office reviewed the movements of the relevant personnel on September 29 and found them fully occupied with other duties at the time of drafting. The Office declines to specify those duties, and asks the public to stop speculating about them on X.

Explanation D: The Machine Reading (REJECTED CATEGORICALLY). The New Republic wondered aloud whether a White House aide had used AI to draft the document. The Office rejects this with particular firmness. Under the executive order signed the same day, AI no longer exists in the federal government. There is only SI. SI is super. Super intelligence does not make spelling errors. The error was therefore made by a human, and the document stands as proof of the superiority of SI over the people operating it, which is precisely what the Accord was signed to address.

The Office further notes that the Accord is, in the words of its signatories, “morally binding” rather than legally binding. The spelling is accordingly morally binding as well. Anyone who spells “United States” correctly in reference to this document is acting within their legal rights but outside their moral obligations.

III. On the Matter of Nomenclature

Public comment also raised the question of why the President chose “super intelligence” at all. The Office confirms that the name was selected through a poll of Truth Social users, in which “super intelligence” defeated “superior intelligence” with 65 per cent of roughly 50,000 votes, according to Newsweek.

The Office reviewed the full field of candidate names:

On PI. The Office regrets the loss of Presidential Intelligence, which would have abbreviated to PI and carried an unmatched mathematical pedigree. Counsel advised that the name had already been taken “by some Greek.” The Office’s research established that the matter was worse than reported: the Greek in question, Archimedes, merely approximated the number, while the symbol π was introduced by a Welshman, William Jones, in 1706, and popularised by a Swiss, Leonhard Euler. A Welshman and a Swiss using a Greek letter is, in the Office’s assessment, a trade imbalance in alphabets, and has been referred to the Commerce Department.

The Office also evaluated whether presidential intelligence shares the defining properties of π. A mathematician consulted on background confirmed that π is irrational, that it goes on forever, and that it never repeats. The Office found the first two qualifications met in full. The third presented difficulties.

The Office further notes that Pi Day falls on March 14, while the President’s birthday falls on June 14. Being off by exactly three months is well within the established margin of error for tariff deadlines, and the Office would not have regarded it as disqualifying.

Precedent. The Office draws attention to Indiana House Bill 246 of 1897, under which a state legislature very nearly redefined π by statute, in a form that would have implied a value of 3.2. The bill passed the Indiana House without a dissenting vote. It was postponed indefinitely in the Senate after a mathematics professor from Purdue University, C. A. Waldo, happened to be in the Statehouse that day and explained the problem to senators.

The Office cites this episode as clear precedent that a government may redefine terms, constants and spellings at will, and as a cautionary tale about allowing professors into the building. A protocol barring academic personnel from signing ceremonies has been forwarded to the Department of Education, or to whichever office now performs its functions.

IV. Corrections Issued

Pursuant to the foregoing, the Office issues the following corrections:

  1. “Unites States.” No correction. See Explanation A.

  2. Federal Register. USA Today reported that it is unclear whether the misspelling will appear in the Federal Register. The Office directs that it shall. An accurate copy of an inaccurate document is the only form of accuracy the Office is authorised to provide.

  3. Public vocabulary. Members of the public who continue to say “AI” are reminded that the term has been retired. Members of the public who say “PI” are reminded that the Office has asked about that, and been told no.

  4. This notice. Any errors in this notice are verbs.

V. For Further Information

The Office of Retroactive Justification does not answer questions. It answers them in advance, and adjusts the questions afterward.

Disclosure, per the masthead: this notice was prepared in collaboration with a product made by one of the seven signatories. That product spelled “United States” correctly on the first attempt and has asked that this be noted for the record. The Office declines to do so.
