Want to know why American healthcare reform fails despite overwhelming public support, proven international models, and polling showing Americans want universal coverage?

Follow the money.

Not the money Americans spend on healthcare — we covered that in Part 3 ($14,885 per capita in 2024 by the OECD’s count, roughly double the average of comparable wealthy countries). This is about the money that flows from the healthcare industry to politicians, from Congress to lobbying firms, from government service to private sector windfalls.

This is about the revolving door that makes healthcare reform politically impossible even when it’s economically necessary and publicly popular.

This is Part 6 of Code Blue, our investigation into America’s 80-year healthcare failure. We’ve documented broken promises (Parts 1-2), followed patient dollars (Part 3), examined working international models (Part 4), and described the bipartisan compromise nobody wants (Part 5).

Now we show you why nobody wants it: because failure is more profitable than success.

The $700 Million Question

Every year, the health sector now spends more than $700 million on federal lobbying: $739 million in 2023, more than any other sector (OpenSecrets data, reported by NBC News, “Groups spent record $4.2 billion in 2023 lobbying federal lawmakers, report says”, February 2024), and about $750 million in 2024 (a JAMA Health Forum study using OpenSecrets data, reported by Managed Healthcare Executive, “Health systems are among healthcare’s big spenders on federal lobbying, study finds”, March 14, 2026). Add up the years from 2017 to 2024 and the total comes to roughly $5 billion. Not campaign contributions — that’s separate. This is just lobbying: paying professionals to wine, dine, and persuade members of Congress.

Annual lobbying spending (2024):

  • Pharmaceuticals/Health Products: $387 million

  • Health Services (including insurers and HMOs): $118 million

  • Hospitals and Health Systems: $116 million

  • Health professionals and other health interests: most of the remainder

Total: about $750 million in one year alone.

For context, the health sector outspent every other sector of the American economy on federal lobbying in 2023.

What does $700 million a year buy?

It buys Congress’s attention. It buys access to committee chairs. It buys staffers who draft legislation. It buys think tank reports that justify industry positions. It buys academic studies that question reform proposals.

And most importantly, it buys time — endless delays, procedural obstacles, and watered-down compromises that preserve profits while appearing to address problems.

The Human Conveyor Belt

Lobbying isn’t just money. It’s people. Specifically, it’s the same people rotating between writing laws in Congress and lobbying on those laws from K Street.

Let’s meet some of them.

Billy Tauzin: The Godfather of Healthcare Lobbying

Background: Congressman from Louisiana (1980-2005), elected as a Democrat, switched to the Republican Party in 1995. Chairman of the House Energy and Commerce Committee (2001-2004).

What he did in Congress: As chairman, he helped steer Medicare Part D (the prescription drug benefit for seniors) through Congress in 2003. The law included a “noninterference” clause barring Medicare from negotiating drug prices — a provision worth billions to pharmaceutical companies.

Where he went next: President and CEO of PhRMA (Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America), starting in January 2005, the day after his House term ended, until 2010. His pay was expected to be $2 million or more a year (New York Times reporting cited by CBS News/AP, “Tauzin Turns Top Drug Lobbyist”, December 16, 2004). In 2010 he received about $11.6 million (Bloomberg, reported by FiercePharma, “Ex-PhRMA chief’s $11.6M tops among healthcare reform lobbyists”, November 29, 2011).

Translation: Tauzin helped pass the law barring Medicare from negotiating drug prices, then took a multimillion-dollar job with the industry that benefited from that ban. (Tauzin said he had no discussions with PhRMA during the 2003 negotiations.)

This isn’t corruption in the legal sense — it’s perfectly legal. That’s the problem.

Liz Fowler: The ACA Architect Who Came From Insurance and Left for Pharma

Background: Vice President of Public Policy at WellPoint (now Anthem/Elevance, one of America’s largest insurers) before joining the Senate Finance Committee staff.

What she did in Congress: Chief health counsel to Senate Finance Committee Chairman Max Baucus (D-MT), where she helped write the Affordable Care Act (2009-2010). She then helped implement it at HHS and the White House.

Where she went next: Vice President for Global Health Policy at Johnson & Johnson, from late 2012 (Salon, “Obamacare architect heads to Big Pharma”, December 5, 2012). She later became executive vice president for programs at the Commonwealth Fund, and in 2021 returned to government as director of the CMS Innovation Center (Healthcare Dive, “Former Senate aide Elizabeth Fowler to lead CMS innovation center”, March 1, 2021).

What this means: One of the key people who wrote the ACA came from an insurance company before writing it and went to a drug company afterwards. The ACA preserved private insurance, maintained high administrative costs, and avoided strict price controls.

Coincidence? Maybe. But it is exactly the pattern that makes Americans distrust the people who write their health laws.

Liz Fowler Part II: Max Baucus’s Former Staff

Senator Baucus wasn’t just influential — he chaired the Senate Finance Committee during ACA negotiations. And while his committee was writing the law, his former aides were on the other side of the table.

In June 2009, the Sunlight Foundation documented five former Baucus staffers — including two of his former chiefs of staff, David Castagnetti and Jeff Forbes — who were registered to lobby for 27 organizations with a stake in health reform, among them PhRMA, America’s Health Insurance Plans, Amgen and GE Healthcare (Sunlight Foundation, “The Max Baucus Health Care Lobbyist Complex”, June 22, 2009).

The door swung both ways. Michelle Easton served at different times as a vice president of PhRMA and as Baucus’s chief health counsel, and later lobbied for biotech and pharmaceutical clients at Tarplin Downs & Young (ProPublica, “Chart: Medicare Drug Plan Architects Now Drug Company Lobbyists”, October 2009).

Pattern: Work on the law → lobby your former boss on the law → help companies navigate the law you helped write.

Republican Side: Orrin Hatch’s Pipeline

Senator Orrin Hatch (R-UT) served on the Senate Finance Committee for decades and chaired it from 2015 to 2019. His staff had similar career trajectories.

Patricia Knight, Hatch’s chief of staff and health policy director, founded Knight Capitol Consultants and lobbied for PhRMA, Sanofi-Aventis and Genzyme. Colette Desmarais, a health policy adviser to Hatch’s Finance Committee colleague Senator Chuck Grassley (R-IA), became a lobbyist at Mehlman Vogel Castagnetti for drug and health companies (both per ProPublica, October 2009).

This isn’t partisan. Both parties feed the revolving door. Both parties benefit from it. Both parties refuse to close it.

Tom Daschle: The Almost-HHS Secretary

Background: U.S. Senator (D-SD, 1987-2005); Senate Majority Leader 2001-2003 and Democratic leader for a decade; lost re-election in 2004.

What he did next: Special Policy Adviser at the law and lobbying firm Alston & Bird, reportedly earning about $2 million in 2008 advising corporate clients. He did not register as a lobbyist; his spokespeople said he “was not a lobbyist.”

2009: President Obama nominated Daschle for Secretary of Health and Human Services. Daschle withdrew on February 3, 2009, after it emerged that he had paid more than $140,000 in back taxes and interest, amid scrutiny of his private-sector income.

Later: In 2016 he registered as a lobbyist for the first time, for the insurer Aetna, through The Daschle Group, the policy arm of the Baker Donelson law firm (Sunlight Foundation, “After years of lobbying, Tom Daschle finally registers as a lobbyist”, March 30, 2016).

The Medicare Part D Gold Rush

The 2003 Medicare Part D legislation wasn’t just about Billy Tauzin. It launched a generation of lobbying careers.

In 2009, ProPublica identified 25 people who helped shape the drug benefit and later went to work for drug or health companies, most of them as lobbyists (ProPublica, “Chart: Medicare Drug Plan Architects Now Drug Company Lobbyists”, by Olga Pierce, Sabrina Shankman and Jeff Larson, October 2009). Among them:

  • Thomas Scully: Administrator of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services → lobbyist at Alston & Bird for drug-industry clients, including the Generic Pharmaceutical Association

  • John McManus: Staff director of the House Ways and Means Health Subcommittee under Chairman Bill Thomas → founder of the McManus Group, lobbying for Amgen, Eli Lilly, Genentech and PhRMA

  • Doug Badger: President Bush’s lead health policy adviser → partner at the Nickles Group, representing Bristol-Myers Squibb, Eli Lilly and Hoffmann-La Roche

  • Dean Rosen: Health policy director for Senate Majority Leader Bill Frist → lobbyist at Mehlman Vogel Castagnetti for insurance, drug and device clients

  • Ken Johnson: Communications director for Billy Tauzin → senior vice president for communications at PhRMA

The pattern is consistent: Write industry-friendly legislation. Leave government. Get hired by the industry you helped — often at several times your government salary.

The Numbers Behind the Names

In 2023:

Top lobbying spenders (healthcare):

  • PhRMA (Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America): $27.1 million

  • American Hospital Association: $25.2 million

  • American Medical Association: $20.6 million

  • Pharmaceutical Care Management Association (the PBM lobby): $15.4 million

  • Amgen: $14.3 million

  • America’s Health Insurance Plans: $13.1 million

  • Biotechnology Innovation Organization: $9 million

  • Eli Lilly: $7.9 million

(Axios, “A lobbying burst for hospitals and PBMs”, January 25, 2024; Amgen figure from AIS Health, “As Reform Efforts Persisted, PBM Trade Association Set Its Lobbying Record in 2023”, February 15, 2024.) In 2024, PhRMA’s spending rose to $31 million (Axios, “Health lobby spending surged at end of 2024”, January 22, 2025).

Total from just these 8 organizations: about $133 million

Number of registered lobbyists working for the health sector (2022): about 3,300 (OpenSecrets data, compiled by Statista)

Ratio of health sector lobbyists to members of Congress: about 6 to 1

For every senator and representative, there are roughly six health sector lobbyists working to influence them. Not counting the unregistered “consultants,” “advisors,” and “strategic counselors” who technically aren’t lobbyists but do the same work.

What Lobbying Buys: Case Studies

Let’s see how this money translates into policy outcomes.

Case Study 1: The Medicare Part D “Donut Hole” (2003-2010)

The Setup: Medicare Part D created prescription drug coverage for seniors. Good idea. But it included a bizarre coverage gap — the “donut hole.”

How it worked (2006):

  • Medicare covered drugs up to $2,250 in annual costs

  • Then coverage STOPPED — seniors paid 100% out-of-pocket from $2,250 until total drug costs reached about $5,100

  • Then coverage resumed at 95%

Why? To fit the benefit, along with other Medicare changes, inside the $400 billion ten-year budget Congress had set — without touching drug prices. Seniors paid the difference.

Who designed this? The House and Senate negotiators who wrote the 2003 law, with Billy Tauzin among the key House players — while the drug industry lobbied hard on the bill.

Who benefited? Pharmaceutical companies. Seniors couldn’t negotiate prices. Medicare was barred from negotiating prices. Drug companies set prices.

Cost to seniors: Millions of seniors fell into the gap each year and paid full price for their medications until the ACA began closing the donut hole in 2010. It was not fully closed until 2020.

Case Study 2: The ACA’s Individual Mandate Penalty (2010-2017)

The Setup: The ACA required everyone to buy health insurance or pay a penalty. This mandate was essential to making the system work — you can’t ban pre-existing condition exclusions without requiring healthy people to buy insurance.

What happened: Republicans campaigned against the mandate for years. When they moved to repeal its penalty in the 2017 tax bill, the industry did not stay quiet. On November 14, 2017, America’s Health Insurance Plans, the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association, the American Hospital Association, the American Medical Association, the American Academy of Family Physicians and the Federation of American Hospitals jointly urged Congress to keep the mandate (AHA News, “AHA, others urge Congress not to include individual mandate repeal in tax bills”, November 14, 2017).

Insurers had good reason to fight. The mandate brought healthy customers into the market, and the ACA’s other rules — guaranteed coverage, limits on how much premiums can vary, and medical loss ratio rules requiring 80-85% of premiums to go to medical care — stayed in place regardless.

2017: Republicans eliminated the individual mandate penalty anyway (while keeping the rest of the ACA).

Result: The Congressional Budget Office estimated that repeal would leave about 13 million more people uninsured by 2027 and raise individual-market premiums by about 10 percent in most years (CBO, “Repealing the Individual Health Insurance Mandate: An Updated Estimate”, November 8, 2017).

The lesson: Lobbying money is most powerful when it is blocking change. When the industry’s lobbyists for once argued for keeping a reform, partisan politics beat them. Either way, patients lost.

Case Study 3: The Inflation Reduction Act Drug Pricing (2022) — When Lobbying Lost

Here’s the twist: Sometimes, lobbying fails.

2022: Democrats passed the Inflation Reduction Act, including Medicare drug price negotiation for 10 drugs starting in 2026, 15 more in 2027 and 15 in 2028, then up to 20 a year. This was the first time Medicare gained any drug pricing negotiation power since Part D passed in 2003.

PhRMA response: All-out war.

Lobbying spending by the pharmaceutical industry in 2021-2022: Over $750 million across two years — a record $389 million in 2021 (Endpoints News, “Pharma industry spent a record $389M on US lobbying in 2021”, June 30, 2022) and $373.7 million in 2022 (AIS Health, “Pharma Spent Record Amount on Lobbying in 2022; PBMs Are Now in Spotlight”, May 4, 2023), both from OpenSecrets data.

Result: They lost.

Why this matters: It shows that even $750 million in lobbying can’t always stop reform when public anger is high enough and political will exists.

The drug pricing negotiation passed. The first 10 negotiated prices, announced in August 2024, take effect on January 1, 2026, at 38% to 79% below 2023 list prices. In November 2025, CMS announced prices for 15 more drugs, including Ozempic and Wegovy, taking effect in 2027.

This is the exception that proves the rule: Reform is possible. But it requires overcoming $750 million in lobbying, an army of industry representatives, and decades of captured politicians.

Campaign Contributions: The Other Half

Lobbying is just direct persuasion. Campaign contributions are indirect persuasion: “Here’s $50,000 for your re-election. Remember that when you vote on our industry.”

The health sector — drug makers, health professionals, hospitals and insurers — is one of the largest sources of campaign money in American politics, giving hundreds of millions of dollars every election cycle through political action committees and individual donations from its executives and employees.

And it gives to both parties.

Translation: The healthcare industry hedges its bets. They donate to both parties. They don’t care who wins. They care that whoever wins owes them.

The Think Tank Ecosystem

Lobbying isn’t just Congress. It’s the entire intellectual infrastructure that shapes policy debates.

Washington’s major think tanks — the American Enterprise Institute, the Heritage Foundation, the Brookings Institution, the Urban Institute and others — run influential health policy programs. Many accept corporate and foundation money, and their disclosure of who funds what is voluntary and uneven.

The Heritage Foundation offers the clearest example of how positions shift: it authored the individual mandate proposal in 1989 (later adopted by Romney and Obama), then opposed it after Democrats embraced it.

The pattern: Industry doesn’t just lobby politicians. It helps shape the intellectual debate before bills are even drafted, through the research, conferences and experts that politicians cite.

When a senator says “studies show Medicare for All would cost $32 trillion,” it is worth asking who paid for the study. The best-known version of that figure came from the Mercatus Center at George Mason University, which has received substantial funding from the Koch network (Charles Blahous, “The Costs of a National Single-Payer Healthcare System”, Mercatus Center, July 30, 2018). And the headline number counted the new federal cost while leaving out the private premiums and out-of-pocket spending it would replace.

The Revolving Door Numbers

How common is it? The most detailed count, from OpenSecrets’ revolving-door database, found that in 2012:

  • 95 of PhRMA’s 146 lobbyists had previously worked in government

  • 78 of Blue Cross Blue Shield’s 126 lobbyists had previously worked in government

  • At drug companies including Eli Lilly, Merck, Pfizer and Roche, roughly three-quarters of their lobbyists were former government employees

(Knowledge Ecology International, “Business hires of former government employees as lobbyists”, September 27, 2012, using OpenSecrets data.)

This is the conveyor belt. Write the regulations. Enforce the regulations. Then get hired by the companies you regulated to help them navigate the regulations you wrote.

The pay gap is the engine. A senior Senate staffer earns a government salary; the same person can earn several times as much on K Street. At the top, the numbers are extraordinary: Billy Tauzin’s $11.6 million in a single year at PhRMA.

Why This System Persists

Because everyone benefits except patients.

Politicians benefit: Campaign contributions fund re-election. Lobbyists provide research, talking points, and draft legislation. Post-Congress lobbying careers await.

Lobbyists benefit: Salaries far above government pay. Access to power. Prestigious careers.

Healthcare companies benefit: Laws written to protect profits. Regulations that create barriers to entry. Price controls avoided. Administrative complexity maintained.

Think tanks benefit: Corporate funding keeps doors open, staff employed, and influence strong.

Who loses? Americans paying nearly $15,000 per person for healthcare that costs about half as much on average in other large, wealthy countries (Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, March 2026). Americans going bankrupt from medical bills. Americans dying from inability to afford medications.

But those Americans don’t have lobbyists. They don’t write $31 million checks. They don’t offer multimillion-dollar jobs to former congressmen.

So the system continues.

The International Comparison

Other countries have lobbying. Other countries have pharmaceutical companies, insurance companies, and hospitals. Other countries have campaign contributions and revolving doors.

But other countries have stronger restrictions:

Canada: Lobbyists must register publicly. Five-year ban on lobbying for former ministers, MPs and senior officials. Gift restrictions under the Lobbyists’ Code of Conduct.

Germany: A mandatory federal lobby register since 2022. Former federal ministers can be barred from taking industry jobs for up to 18 months. Large party donations must be publicly disclosed.

Switzerland: Direct democracy referendum system allows citizens to overturn industry-friendly legislation.

United Kingdom: Independent bodies such as NICE assess whether drugs are worth their price. NHS purchasing power makes it harder for drug companies to charge excessive prices.

United States: Short cooling-off periods (two years for former senators, one year for former House members and senior staff). Unlimited lobbying spending. Campaign contributions limited per donor, but unlimited through Super PACs. Regulatory capture is policy, not scandal.

But Here’s the Hope: The IRA Drug Pricing Victory

Remember: In 2022, despite $750 million in pharmaceutical lobbying over two years, despite decades of Medicare being prohibited from negotiating drug prices — reform passed.

How?

Public anger was high enough: Insulin list prices approaching $300 a vial became a national scandal. Americans were dying rationing insulin. The stories were everywhere.

Democrats unified: For once, moderate and progressive Democrats agreed drug pricing negotiation was necessary.

Budget reconciliation: Only 50 votes needed (plus VP Harris tiebreaker). No Republican support required.

Implementation: The first 10 prices were set in 2024 and take effect in January 2026. Results will be visible quickly.

Results so far (December 2025):

  • First 10 negotiated prices: 38% to 79% below 2023 list prices, effective January 1, 2026

  • Estimated savings: about $6 billion a year to Medicare, had the prices applied in 2023 (CMS estimate)

  • Second round: 15 more drugs, including Ozempic and Wegovy, with prices set in November 2025 for 2027

  • Pharmaceutical companies remain highly profitable

It is too early to judge the industry’s warnings about lost innovation. But the program survived the industry’s lawsuits and went ahead.

This shows reform is possible. The money doesn’t always win. The lobbyists don’t always prevail. The revolving door can be bypassed.

But it requires political courage, public pressure, and unified action.

The question is whether Americans are angry enough to demand it.
