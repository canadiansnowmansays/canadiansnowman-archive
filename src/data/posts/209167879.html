Ronald Reagan, George H.W. Bush, Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama, Donald Trump’s first term, Joe Biden. Seven presidencies, four Republican and three Democratic, spanning a run of American history that included the end of the Cold War, two Gulf Wars, September 11, the 2008 financial crisis, and a pandemic — and across every one of them, Anthony Fauci held essentially the same job, advising each administration on the same rough portfolio of infectious disease policy, without any of them, by every available account, ever managing to figure out which party he actually favored. This is the single hardest fact for either side of the current argument about Fauci to fully absorb, because it does not fit neatly into anyone’s preferred story. It is not the record of a partisan operative who happened to be unusually good at hiding his politics. It is the record of a man who built an entire professional identity around the discipline of not having politics, at least not any that showed, for the better part of half a century.

The mechanics of how he pulled this off are worth taking seriously as their own achievement, separate from the science. Fauci’s approach to every administration he served was, by his own description over the years, essentially identical: brief the president and the president’s staff on the science as he understood it, as plainly and as free of political framing as he could manage, and let the political decisions belong entirely to the political appointees whose job that actually was. He did this with George H.W. Bush during the later, more urgent years of the AIDS crisis, when the administration’s posture on the epidemic was shifting under continued activist pressure and Fauci’s own institute was simultaneously fielding demands from Congress to justify its research spending on a disease still stigmatized enough that some members of the same Congress questioned whether it deserved federal money at all. He did it with Bill Clinton, whose administration significantly expanded AIDS research funding and global health engagement, and under whom Fauci helped shape the scientific case for what would eventually become the international push toward universal antiretroviral access.

He did it with George W. Bush during the design and rollout of PEPFAR, discussed in the previous essay in this series, working inside an administration that needed his credibility with the international health community precisely because it trusted that credibility had not been purchased by partisan loyalty.

He did it with Barack Obama during the West African Ebola outbreak of 2014, when NIAID’s research infrastructure became central to the American response and Fauci found himself, not for the first time, publicly defending an administration’s cautious, evidence-based approach against a wave of public panic — in that case, panic that a handful of domestic Ebola cases would somehow spiral into a mass American outbreak, a fear Fauci spent months methodically working to correct using the same calm, data-first register that would later serve him during Covid. That episode is worth a moment’s attention on its own terms, because it previewed almost every dynamic that would define his Covid years at much smaller scale and much lower political stakes. A nurse in Dallas contracted the virus after treating a patient who had traveled from Liberia; two isolated cases in New York and Maine briefly dominated national news coverage; and a handful of politicians, including then-candidate Trump, publicly called for travel bans and quarantine measures well beyond what the epidemiological evidence supported. Fauci spent the fall of 2014 doing exactly what he would spend the spring of 2020 doing again — appearing on television, correcting exaggerated claims about transmission risk, and absorbing a round of public criticism for seeming, to some, too calm about a genuine threat. The outbreak in the United States fizzled out within months, largely vindicating the measured approach Fauci had advocated, and by the time Covid arrived six years later he had every reason to expect the same pattern would hold: a period of public alarm, careful correction from the government’s senior scientists, and an eventual return to calm once the data settled the argument. Covid, and the administration overseeing the American response to it, would not follow that pattern nearly as cooperatively.

And he did it, notably, during Trump’s first term as well — appearing regularly alongside the president at Covid briefings through the early months of 2020, a period during which, whatever tensions were building beneath the surface, Fauci maintained in public a studious deference to the office even as the science he was reporting sometimes contradicted what the president standing next to him had just said.

It is that last stretch — the opening months of 2020, inside Trump’s first term — where the pattern that had held for four decades finally began to buckle, and it is worth being precise about how and why, because the popular account tends to compress a genuinely complicated year into a simpler story than what actually happened. Fauci did not set out in early 2020 to become an adversary of the president he was serving. By most contemporaneous accounts, including his own later statements, he approached the relationship the same way he had approached every administration before it: brief the facts, let the politics happen elsewhere, avoid public disagreement wherever the underlying substance allowed it. As The New York Times has reported, this cautious deference is visible throughout Fauci’s own diary entries from the period, in which he records his interactions with Trump largely in clinical, restrained language — describing the president as “rambling” in at least one entry, rather than in the sharper terms his later public critics might have expected from a man they were already characterizing as an open political adversary.

The daily task force briefings themselves, which ran through most of the spring of 2020, put this tension on display in a format American television had never quite hosted before: a sitting president and his government’s most senior infectious disease scientist standing at adjacent podiums, sometimes minutes apart, offering visibly different accounts of the same unfolding crisis. Fauci, by his own later description, tried at first to manage the disagreements the way he always had — through careful phrasing, through waiting for a private moment rather than a public correction, through emphasizing what he could agree with in a president’s statement before gently noting where the evidence pointed elsewhere. That approach worked, more or less, through February and into early March. It became visibly harder to sustain once Trump began floating the idea that the virus might simply disappear “like a miracle,” and it became close to impossible after the disinfectant remarks in April, when foreign governments and American poison control centers alike had to issue emergency guidance warning people not to ingest or inject cleaning products, a public health emergency inside the public health emergency that no amount of careful phrasing could have finessed away.

What changed the calculation was not any decision by Fauci to become more political. It was that the president himself began treating basic epidemiological fact as a subject open to negotiation in a way none of Fauci’s six previous bosses ever had. When Trump suggested in April 2020 that researchers investigate whether injecting disinfectant into the body might help treat Covid-19 — a suggestion so far outside any defensible reading of the science that it required immediate public correction from health officials around the world, not merely from Fauci — the older Fauci playbook of quiet, private course-correction was no longer available to him. A president floating an idea that could get people killed if believed and acted upon is not a disagreement that can be handled through a memo. Fauci’s public contradictions of Trump throughout 2020 — on masking, on hydroxychloroquine, on the pace of reopening, on the disinfectant suggestion specifically — were not, on the available evidence, an abandonment of his lifelong nonpartisan posture. They were what that posture actually required once the administration in question began contradicting settled science on live television. The rule had always been serve the office, not the man. In 2020, for the first time in Fauci’s career, doing exactly that required openly correcting the man.

This distinction matters enormously for how fairly the rest of Fauci’s record can be read, because it is the hinge on which his reputation with roughly half the country turned. To Trump’s most committed supporters, Fauci’s willingness to publicly contradict the president reads, understandably from where they sit, as confirmation that he had finally revealed a partisan hand he’d been concealing for decades — proof, in other words, that the seven-presidents record was less a genuine record of nonpartisanship than a lucky run that finally met its test and failed it. I don’t think the evidence supports that reading, but I also don’t think it is an unreasonable one to hold, and a series trying to be fair to its subject owes that concession. What the evidence actually supports, in my reading of it, is closer to the opposite: that a man who had spent four decades so scrupulously avoiding political entanglement that six previous presidents of both parties trusted him with sensitive, politically loaded portfolios, found himself in 2020 serving the first president whose own statements were themselves the thing requiring correction — and that Fauci’s failure, if failure is even the right word, was not partisanship but a kind of institutional naivety: an assumption, carried over from fifty years of relatively normal governance, that correcting the record publicly would function the way it always had, as a scientist doing his job, rather than the way it actually functioned in 2020, as kindling for a culture war that had been waiting for exactly this kind of fuel.

There is also a harder question underneath all of this that the series has to sit with honestly, because it does not resolve cleanly in Fauci’s favor either: whether a man that scrupulously nonpartisan for that long had, by 2020, also grown unaccustomed to being disagreed with in the ordinary way that democratic accountability actually requires. Six presidencies’ worth of deference from the men above him, and four decades of institutional authority over the people below him, is not a training environment that reliably produces graceful responses to genuine, good-faith challenge. When Fauci told MSNBC in 2021 that attacks on him amounted to attacks on science itself, he was not entirely wrong about the substance of many of those attacks — plenty of them were bad-faith and scientifically illiterate — but the framing itself, collapsing the distinction between himself and the institution of science he represented, was exactly the kind of overreach that fifty years without a serious partisan check might reasonably be expected to produce in almost anyone. The seven-presidents record is real, and it is genuinely rare, and it deserves the credit this essay has tried to give it. It is also, I think, part of what made the eventual reckoning so much more combustible than it might otherwise have been.

It is worth noting, too, that the seven-presidents record is not simply a matter of Fauci’s own restraint — it required each of those administrations, in turn, to decide that keeping him was worth more than replacing him with someone more pliable. Incoming administrations of both parties routinely replace senior agency officials as a matter of course, and NIAID directors, like most institute heads across the federal government, generally serve at something close to the pleasure of whoever is appointing the broader NIH leadership above them. That Fauci survived transition after transition, through administrations with genuinely opposed views on nearly everything else, without ever becoming a target for replacement until 2025, is itself a data point about how differently his nonpartisan reputation registered with seven different sets of political appointees than it eventually would with an eighth. It took a president willing to treat basic epidemiological fact as negotiable to finally turn that fifty-year asset into a liability.

That reckoning — the specific clashes with Trump over bleach, masking, and the pace of reopening, and what they actually reveal about who was right and who was performing — is where this series turns next.
