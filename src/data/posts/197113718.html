The county sent a letter about the decals. Constable Brash described it as political interference. The coin is in Gerald's drawer. Hansard knocked it there.

The Ontario Provincial Police detachment in Elsinore has, for as long as anyone can remember, consisted of one constable and a cruiser parked behind the municipal building, near the loading dock where Gerald receives the recycling bins every second Tuesday. The detachment is technically part of the Perth County command, which is headquartered in Stratford, but Elsinore’s constable has always operated with the independence that comes from distance, modest crime rates, and the understanding — shared by the county and the town — that a community whose most pressing law enforcement challenge involves the seasonal management of tourists who attempt to feed the swans does not require close supervision.

For thirty-one years, the position was held by Constable Dave Chicken, whose name the children found hilarious and the adults had stopped noticing, in the way that you stop noticing anything about someone who has been doing the same job competently for longer than most marriages. Dave knew the town. He knew which families had teenagers who might be found with beer in the park after dark. He knew which intersections needed attention during Festival weekends and which could be left to common sense. He knew that the quickest way to resolve most disputes in Elsinore was to stand near them for a few minutes with his hands on his belt and say nothing, which produced, in the vast majority of cases, a resolution that was both peaceful and permanent.

Dave retired in February. He moved to Prince Edward County with his wife, who had been saying for some years that they should go, and who had finally reached the point in the conversation where Dave understood that “should” had become “will.” A small gathering was held at the Legion. Constance Hay attended and said three words to Dave, which he later told his wife were the finest compliment he had received in his career, and which he declined to repeat, because some things said by Constance Hay are for the recipient only.

The county sent his replacement in March.

Constable Kyle Brash arrived from Mississauga, which he described as “down south” in a way that suggested the phrase meant something different to him than it does to most Ontarians, and which caused Norman Stalker, who was covering the introduction for the Beacon, to write a note in his pad and underline it once.

Constable Brash was thirty-four, broad-shouldered in the manner of a man who spent more time in the gym than on patrol, and possessed of a confidence that preceded him into rooms the way a cold front precedes a storm — you felt it before you understood what was happening, and by the time you understood, the furniture had already been rearranged.

The selecting committee — Reg Plimpton, Gerald, and a sergeant from Perth County command — had interviewed three candidates. The sergeant preferred a quiet woman from Listowel with twelve years of rural posting experience. Gerald had no strong opinion, which is Gerald’s way of having a strong opinion he has decided not to share. But Reg was taken with Brash from the moment the candidate walked in. Part of it was the handshake, which was the kind of handshake Reg respects — firm, sustained, eye contact throughout. And part of it, Reg admitted later to Gerald, was the eyes. Constable Brash had large, prominent eyes — slightly bulging, wide-set, the eyes of a man who appeared to be taking in everything at once. Combined with his general intensity, they gave him the look of someone perpetually on the verge of discovery.

Councillor Don Farnsworth, who had stopped by the municipal building during the interviews and caught a glimpse of the candidate through the window, said that if you put a deerstalker cap on him and handed him a magnifying glass, you’d have the town constable and Sherlock Holmes in one. Reg laughed, but the image stayed with him. A man who looked like he was already solving crimes — that was what Elsinore needed.

What Elsinore had needed, of course, was Dave Chicken. But Dave was in Prince Edward County with his wife, and the selecting committee does not always select for what is needed.

He arrived with a challenge coin.

Nobody in Elsinore had seen a challenge coin before. Challenge coins are a military tradition, small medallions exchanged between members of a unit as tokens of shared service, and their appearance at the OPP detachment in Elsinore was, as Gerald Fitch observed while receiving one at the municipal building, “a first for the loading dock.” Gerald examined the coin with the care he applied to all municipal artifacts. One side bore a skull motif with the letters K.B. on the forehead and what appeared to be hockey sticks crossed beneath the chin. The other side said DEFEND THE COMMUNITY above an image of a cruiser that was significantly more muscular than the cruiser parked behind the building.

Gerald placed the coin on his desk beside the container where he keeps the binder clips. Hansard, his cat, knocked it off the desk within the hour. Gerald picked it up, returned it to the desk, and Hansard knocked it off again. Gerald placed it in the drawer. It has remained there since. Hansard’s editorial judgment, in this as in most matters, was swift and final.

Constable Brash’s first week in Elsinore was eventful in ways the town was not accustomed to.

On Monday, he informed Reg Plimpton, the Deputy Reeve, that the detachment’s signage was “not intimidating enough.” Reg, who had not previously considered the intimidation value of the small green sign that said ONTARIO PROVINCIAL POLICE — ELSINORE DETACHMENT and which had hung above the side entrance to the municipal building since 1994, agreed that it could perhaps be refreshed. He agreed in the particular way that Reg agrees with things — with warmth, with a hand on the forearm, with the air of a man who has just discovered that he and the person speaking to him have been thinking the same thing all along. This is Reg’s gift. It is also, if you know Reg, the sound of a man buying time.

On Tuesday, Constable Brash posted to the Elsinore Community Facebook page — a forum previously devoted to lost cats, festival ticket availability, and the annual debate about whether the Canada Day fireworks should begin at nine-thirty or ten — announcing that the detachment was “open for business” and that residents should expect a “visible, proactive, and fierce” police presence in the community. He used the word “fierce” three times. Norman read the post, clipped it to a file he had labelled BRASH, K., and placed it in the cabinet beside the file marked SMART COMMUNITY, which contained the 2019 press release about Elsinore’s digital transformation, which had produced a parking app that confused the Legion lot with the library.

On Wednesday, Constable Brash drove to the Avon and parked his cruiser near the pedestrian bridge, where he spent forty-five minutes observing the swans through binoculars. When Darlene Potts, the Swan Warden, arrived at her customary time of five forty-five the following morning, she found that Constable Brash had left his card on the bench where she conducts her dawn observations, with a note suggesting they “coordinate on waterway security.”

Darlene read the note. She placed it in the pocket of her vest. She took out her dark green hardcover notebook and wrote something. She did not share what she wrote. She has not called Constable Brash. She has not returned his card. The swans, who were present throughout and who have been on the Avon since before the detachment existed, continued to proceed with the magnificent indifference that is their primary contribution to public life in Elsinore.

On Thursday, the detachment’s first monthly community liaison meeting was scheduled to begin at nine o’clock. Gerald had booked the small meeting room at the municipal hall and confirmed attendance with Reg, two councillors, and the Festival’s front-of-house manager, who had been invited because the Festival’s security arrangements during peak season had historically been a standing item. The meeting began at nine forty-three.

Constable Brash arrived at nine forty-one, which allowed him two minutes to settle before the official start. He did not explain the delay. Nobody asked. Gerald noted the time of arrival in his personal meeting notes, which is what Gerald does, and filed the note without comment, which is also what Gerald does.

Reg, who had been at the table since eight fifty-eight because Reg is always early to things he considers important, watched Constable Brash settle into his chair. He noted the quality of the settlement. He said nothing.

The meeting proceeded. The minutes were accurate.

By the end of his second week, Constable Brash had accomplished several things.

He had redesigned the cruiser’s decals, adding a thin blue line motif that the county had not authorised and which produced a letter from Perth County command that Constable Brash described to Reg as “political interference.” He had conducted a “security audit” of the Festival Theatre, which consisted primarily of walking through the lobby in his tactical vest and asking the box office staff whether they had an “active threat protocol.” Alistair, the Artistic Director, who was in rehearsal and who was informed of the audit by his stage manager, said nothing, which was his way of saying a great deal, and which the stage manager understood and did not follow up on.

He had requested — in writing, to the county — that the detachment be issued a drone for “enhanced surveillance capability.” The county declined, citing budget constraints, which Constable Brash described to Reg as “further political interference.”

And he had acquired a girlfriend.

Her name was Tamara. She sang at open mic nights in Stratford and described herself on social media as a “country artist and freedom advocate,” though the freedom she advocated for was, as far as Carly Meadows could determine from a review of her posts, primarily the freedom to park in fire lanes and to question the ingredients in municipal tap water.

Tamara visited Elsinore on weekends. When she did, Constable Brash would drive the cruiser to the edge of town to meet her, escort her to the municipal building, and park the cruiser across two spaces in a manner that suggested the vehicle was engaged in operational activity rather than waiting for someone to finish doing her makeup.

On one occasion, Constable Brash asked Gerald to arrange for the town’s volunteer fire department to “provide a presence” at a benefit concert Tamara was singing at — a fundraiser for the Perth County agricultural society, held in the basement of St. Andrew’s-on-the-Avon United Church, at which approximately thirty-five people were expected.

Gerald, who has been handling requests in Elsinore for more than two decades, said he would look into it.

He did not look into it.

The incident that brought Constable Brash to broader community attention occurred on a Thursday afternoon in April, when a report circulated on the Elsinore Community Facebook page that one of the swans had gone missing from the Avon.

Constable Brash, who monitored the Facebook page with the intensity of a man who has mistaken social media for police dispatch, immediately posted that the detachment was “aware of the situation” and had “deployed resources.” He followed this, twenty minutes later, with a second post announcing that a “person of interest” had been identified and that the community should “remain calm.”

Constable Brash spent forty-five minutes observing the swans through binoculars. The following morning the Swan Warden found his card on her bench with a note suggesting they coordinate on waterway security. She has not called. She was already there.

Darlene Potts, who had not reported a missing swan, called Norman Stalker at the Beacon. The swan, she said, was not missing. It was nesting. It was nesting in the same location it had nested in for the previous four years, which was behind the groundskeeper’s shed at the south end of the park, a location that was known to Darlene, to the groundskeeper, to anyone who had read her annual swan report, and — until this moment — to no one in law enforcement, because it had not previously been considered a law enforcement matter.

Norman called Constable Brash for comment. Constable Brash said that he had acted on “credible intelligence” and that the situation had been “resolved through proactive engagement.” Norman asked what the proactive engagement had consisted of. Constable Brash said he had driven the cruiser to the south end of the park and observed the swan from a distance of approximately forty metres.

Norman published a three-paragraph item under the headline SWAN FOUND NESTING; DETACHMENT STOOD DOWN. Constance Hay cut it out and placed it on her refrigerator.

The following week, Norman received a letter from Perth County command. It informed him that a formal complaint had been filed by the Elsinore detachment against the Elsinore Beacon, alleging that the Beacon’s coverage of the detachment had been “selective, misleading, and damaging to community confidence in law enforcement.” The complaint requested that the county review the Beacon’s editorial practices and consider whether its coverage met the standard of responsible local journalism.

Norman read the letter. He removed his reading glasses. He held them in his hand. He pointed them briefly at the letter.

He called Carly into his office.

He said: “Write it up. Eight hundred words. The complaint, the full text, our response.”

Carly said: “What’s our response?”

Norman said: “We publish the complaint.”

Carly said: “That’s it?”

Norman said: “That’s it.”

The piece ran on Thursday. It quoted the complaint in full — the allegation of selective coverage, the suggestion of review, the request directed at the county. It noted that the Beacon’s coverage had consisted of one piece about the Facebook swan alert, one piece about the drone request, and one piece about the unauthorized cruiser decals, all of which had been factually accurate and all of which had been offered to Constable Brash for comment before publication, with comment received in each case.

It noted that the Beacon had been covering Elsinore for sixty-one years without receiving a complaint from law enforcement about its editorial practices.

It noted that this was the first.

Norman did not write an editorial. He did not need to. The complaint, published in full beside the Beacon’s factual record, made its own editorial.

Constable Brash described the piece to Reg as “further escalation.”

Reg said he understood. His arm extended. It found Constable Brash’s forearm and landed there with the practiced ease of twenty years. Reg was with him entirely.

He texted Gerald that evening: The file on the Beacon complaint — can you make sure it’s complete.

Gerald replied: It is. Has been since this morning.

Carly filed her piece. Norman did not change a word.

She had found her angle.

In November, Gerald received, at the municipal building, a letter addressed to the Elsinore Beacon care of the municipal hall — which is not the Beacon’s address, and which suggested that whoever had sent it had not previously had occasion to locate the Beacon, which has been on Ontario Street for sixty-one years and is not difficult to find.

The letter was from a law firm in Mississauga. It alleged that the Beacon’s coverage of the Elsinore detachment had caused reputational harm to Constable Brash in his professional capacity, and that the publication of the formal complaint — which the letter described as “privileged internal correspondence” — constituted an actionable breach of his reasonable expectation of privacy as a public official.

Gerald read the letter. He read it a second time. He placed it in a folder he labelled Brash, K. — Legal — Beacon — Mississauga Firm — November 2026. He walked it to the Beacon office. He handed it to Norman without comment.

Norman read it. He removed his reading glasses. He held them in his hand. He pointed them at the letter for slightly longer than usual.

He called the Beacon’s lawyer, who has handled the publication’s legal matters since 2009 and whose calls Norman returns before any other. The call lasted eleven minutes. At the end of it Norman thanked the lawyer, hung up, and sent Carly a text:

Write the lawsuit story. Front page.

Carly filed it by Thursday. The piece noted that the formal complaint — described in the lawsuit as privileged internal correspondence — had been sent to Norman by Perth County command, had been offered to Constable Brash for comment before publication, and had been printed in full without editorial addition or omission. It noted that the Beacon had covered Elsinore for sixty-one years without being sued by a law enforcement officer for publishing a document the law enforcement officer had himself initiated.

It noted that this was the first.

Norman ran it above the fold.

Constance Hay read it at her kitchen table. She cut it out. She placed it on the refrigerator, beside the SWAN FOUND NESTING; DETACHMENT STOOD DOWN clipping from October.

She stepped back. She looked at both clippings.

She said nothing. She went about her day.

I mention Constable Brash not because his tenure in Elsinore has been particularly consequential — it has not, so far, produced anything worse than a misidentified swan, a set of unauthorized decals, a formal complaint against the local paper, and a lawsuit from a Mississauga law firm — but because he represents a type that arrives periodically in small Ontario towns: the man from somewhere larger who believes that what the town needs is not competence, not consistency, not the patient accumulation of trust over decades, but energy. Volume. Presence. The word “fierce.”

Dave Chicken was not fierce. Dave Chicken was present, which is a different thing and a more useful one. He did not audit the Festival Theatre. He did not coordinate on waterway security. He did not post to Facebook about proactive engagement. He stood near problems until they resolved themselves, and in thirty-one years of this approach, Elsinore’s crime rate remained approximately what it had always been, which is to say: low, manageable, and largely related to parking.

Constance Hay, when I asked her what she thought of the new constable, considered the question for four seconds — which is a long time for Constance, who normally requires fewer than two — and said: “The previous constable managed to serve this town for thirty-one years without once requesting a helicopter.”

I had not mentioned the helicopter. I had not known about the helicopter. Constance, it emerged, had received this piece of information through channels she did not disclose, and she offered it with the precision of someone who understood that the detail required no additional commentary.

Reverend Faye Drummond, who I consulted next, said only that she had observed, in her years of ministry, that the people who talked most about protecting communities tended to spend the least time in them. She said this in her way — gently, without naming anyone, looking out the window of the parish office toward the Avon — and then offered me a biscuit, which I accepted, because a biscuit offered by Reverend Faye is not a biscuit. It is a benediction.

Carly Meadows, who had been following the Constable Brash situation for a piece she was drafting — Norman had told her to write it and she was still deciding on the angle — stopped me outside the Beacon office the following morning. She had her phone out, showing me a photograph of a man in Washington, D.C., in what appeared to be a tactical jacket several sizes too authoritative for the occasion.

“Have you seen this?” she said. “The FBI director. He gave himself a challenge coin with a skull on it. He took a government jet to a wrestling match. He’s got a SWAT team protecting his girlfriend, who sings the national anthem at things.” She paused. “He also couldn’t log into his computer and thought he’d been fired.”

I looked at the photograph. The man had large, prominent eyes.

“There’s more,” Carly said, scrolling. “Meetings rescheduled because he’d been drinking the night before. Staff describing him as a national security vulnerability. Unexplained absences. Erratic. Paranoid.” She paused again, making sure I was following. “And at one point they requested breaching equipment. The kind SWAT teams use to gain entry into buildings. Because he was unreachable behind locked doors.”

She looked up.

“They needed a battering ram to get to their own director.”

I said nothing.

“Constable Brash,” she said, “left his card on the Swan Warden’s bench and asked her to coordinate on waterway security.” She put her phone away. “She hasn’t called him. She’s been on the river every morning at five forty-five for twenty-two years. She is reachable.”

“He reminds me of someone,” Carly said.

I told her I couldn’t imagine who.

She looked at me for a moment in the way that young reporters look at older columnists when they suspect they are being managed, which they are, and which is part of the education.

“Maybe it’s the eyes,” she said, and went inside.

The Perth County farmer, who had seen the Facebook posts because his daughter follows the page and reads him things she considers amusing, said nothing about Constable Brash when I passed him at the feed store.

He said nothing for long enough that I understood he had finished saying it.

Then he said: “Dave was fine.”

Then he got in his truck and drove home.

This is, in Elsinore, what a performance review looks like.

The Moonbeam Chronicle: Sketches from Elsinore publishes every Sunday morning. Constable Dave Chicken served Elsinore for thirty-one years. The swan was nesting. It has always been nesting.

One further note, for the record. In Stratford, Ontario — forty-five minutes west of Elsinore on the 401 — the Stratford Police Service recently escorted a family of spring ducklings across a road. On foot. No drone was requested. No Facebook post announced proactive engagement. No complaint was filed against any local newspaper.

Seventeen thousand likes.

The Stratford Police Service has not, to this publication’s knowledge, sued anyone.

This is, in southwestern Ontario, what appropriate waterway security looks like.

Stratford Police Service, spring 2026. Waterway security situation identified. Resources deployed.
