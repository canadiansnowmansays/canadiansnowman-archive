A tanker ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz — where vague ceasefire language (“best efforts,” “arrangements”) reignited conflict

A little over a week ago I wrote in this diary that the United States and Iran had reached an agreement so successful that the two parties could not agree on what it said. I meant it as satire. I did not expect it to become a casualty report.

But here we are. Since I last wrote, the war that was supposed to be over has come roaring back — not in a grand reopening of hostilities, not over the nuclear question that started it, but over a waterway, a toll, and five words of ceasefire language that each side reads in its own favour. The Americans have bombed Iran. The Iranians have bombed American bases in two Gulf countries. Tankers are burning in the Strait of Hormuz, and a Qatari fisherman is dead, killed by shrapnel on a boat that simply failed to come home. And the cause of all of it is a sentence in the memorandum that everyone signed and no one agreed to.

Let me give the genuine good news first, because there was a stretch of it and it deserves acknowledgment before the rest. For the better part of a week, the thing held. The blockade was gone, oil was moving, and traffic through the strait had climbed to its highest levels since the war began. A harder second round of talks over the nuclear program had opened in Switzerland. There was, for the first time since the Lebanon front ignited in March, a single day on which United Nations monitors recorded no attacks at all between Israel and Hezbollah. After four months of catastrophe, the guns went quiet enough that you could hear yourself hope. That happened. It should be said plainly before I explain how it came apart.

It came apart over five words.

I. The Sentence

The memorandum of understanding signed this month says that Iran will “make arrangements using its best efforts for the safe passage of commercial vessels” through the Strait of Hormuz for sixty days. Read it again. “Make arrangements.” “Best efforts.” There is not, in that sentence, a single word that means anything specific. It does not say who controls the route. It does not say what an arrangement is, or whose best efforts count, or what happens if the two sides’ best efforts point in opposite directions. It is the diplomatic equivalent of two people agreeing to “be reasonable” and then discovering, at knifepoint, that they had very different reasonable in mind.

As the New York Times reported this week, that vagueness was not an accident. Nicole Grajewski, of Sciences Po in Paris, put it precisely: the deal “leaned on deliberately flexible language because that was probably the only way to get it over the line. But flexibility only holds while both sides attach similar meanings to the same vague provisions.” That is the whole tragedy in two sentences. The ambiguity was the achievement. It was the thing that let everyone sign. And it was a loaded gun left on the table, because the moment the two sides looked at the same words and saw different things, there was nothing underneath the words to appeal to.

So here is what each side saw. Washington read “safe passage” to mean the strait is an open international waterway, toll-free, forever, full stop. Tehran read “make arrangements” to mean that Iran makes the arrangements — that it decides the route, licenses the passage, and, after the sixty-day grace period expires, charges for the privilege. As Grajewski summed it up: “Washington treats ‘toll-free transit’ as a permanent principle, while Tehran reads the sixty-day waiver as a tactical pause it accepted only to reach a deal.” Same sentence. Two countries. Two irreconcilable readings, each entirely defensible from the text, because the text was built to be defensible from both sides. That was the point of it.

Lest this seem like a subtlety lost on the principals, consider what Iran’s foreign minister said out loud on Sunday, standing in Baghdad as the fourth day of strikes unfolded. Abbas Araghchi declared, as the New York Times reported, that Iran was “solely responsible” for managing the Strait of Hormuz under the agreement, and warned the world against “interference” in the routes. “Under the memorandum of understanding,” he said, “no other entity or country has any responsibility in this regard.” He was not freelancing. He was reading the document — the same document Washington reads as a guarantee of open, neutral, toll-free passage — and finding in it Iran’s exclusive dominion over the water. Both men are holding the same page. Both are telling the truth about what it says to them. That is not a misunderstanding that diplomacy can clear up with a phone call. It is two truths bolted to one sentence, and the sentence cannot hold them both.

And this week, the gun went off.

II. The Route Is the Toll Booth

To understand how a toll dispute became a shooting war, you have to understand a thing I flagged in the last installment and now have to report in full: in the Strait of Hormuz, the route is the toll booth.

Iran cannot legally charge ships for merely passing through an international strait. Even Iran’s own negotiator concedes this; Hossein Ghorbanzadeh said this week that a toll for passage “has no meaning under international law,” which is why Iran insists its planned charges are not tolls at all but “fees for services.” But you cannot collect a fee for a service no one needs to buy. So first you must make the service necessary. You do this by mining the open central channel — which Iran did — and then announcing that the only safe passage is the route through your own territorial waters, on your own terms, with your own mandatory insurance, sold by an authority you created in May for exactly this purpose. You do not charge for the water. You charge for not being shot in it.

This is not my characterization. It is the mechanism, demonstrated. When a United Nations agency set up its own evacuation route to shepherd the eleven thousand sailors stranded in the Gulf out through the strait — a route that did not require Iran’s permission — Iran’s Revolutionary Guard declared, as the New York Times reported, that the only authorized route was the one Iran had declared, and that “violators will be dealt with.” Hours later, a container ship called the Ever Lovely, which had used the United Nations route instead of Iran’s, was struck by an Iranian drone. And then Iran’s Persian Gulf Strait Authority — the toll-collecting body itself — posted that ships transiting outside its designated routes “will not be covered by the guarantee of safe passage.”

Read that last sentence the way an insurance adjuster would, or a man selling protection from behind a fruit stand. You took the free road instead of ours. You were hit. That is what happens to people without our guarantee. The maritime historian Salvatore Mercogliano compared it to selling flood insurance while controlling the gates above the dam, and this week Iran opened the gates to make the sale.

The United States, having spent a war and tens of billions of dollars insisting the strait must be free, responded to the attack on the Ever Lovely by bombing four Iranian sites. Iran responded to that by droning a second tanker, the Kiku, and then by striking American bases. And so on, and so on, each step a “response” to the last, the original disagreement — who collects at the booth — long since buried under the rubble it caused.

III. Violence Will Be Met With Violence

The exchange itself, as of this writing, has the grim choreography of two men slapping each other and each insisting the other started it.

The United States struck Iranian missile and drone storage, coastal radar, air defenses, and — tellingly — minelayer capability, the very equipment that makes the toll booth possible. Iran struck the Fifth Fleet’s naval base in Bahrain and an air base in Kuwait, with drones and ballistic missiles, and warned that American bases in the region “will be experiencing hell during these days.” The President, for his part, posted that “there may come a point when we are no longer able to be reasonable, and will be forced to militarily complete the job that we very successfully started,” concluding with the cheerful coda that “the Islamic Republic of Iran will no longer exist.”

I want to be careful here, because the temptation in a war diary is always to reach for the apocalypse, and the honest reading does not support it — yet. The analysts who watch this closely are nearly unanimous that both sides are testing red lines rather than crossing the final one, and that neither actually wants the full war back. Gregory Brew of the Eurasia Group described Iran’s logic with cold clarity: “If some minimal military action is enough to close the Omani route and move shipping into routes Iran can control, why not give it a shot? The risks are low so long as Iran can be confident that the U.S. won’t return to more aggressive action.” That is not the reasoning of a regime seeking annihilation. It is the reasoning of a regime that has correctly calculated how much it can get away with, and is pricing the toll booth accordingly.

Which is, if anything, the more damning reading. A war that resumes by accident is a tragedy. A war that resumes because one side ran the numbers and decided a few drones were a reasonable cost of capturing a waterway is something else — it is the predictable yield of a peace that left the most valuable thing in the region governed by the phrase “best efforts.”

The Vice President’s contribution was to explain the new rules. “If they have disagreements about how the M.O.U. is being applied,” he said, “they can pick up the phone. But violence will be met with violence.” It is a fine slogan. There is only the small difficulty that, according to a spokesman for Iran’s Revolutionary Guard, the phone does not exist — Iran denies that the de-confliction hotline the Vice President has repeatedly described was ever established. So we have arrived at the logical terminus of a memorandum nobody reads the same way: an administration urging Iran to pick up a phone that Iran says was never installed, to resolve a disagreement over words that were chosen precisely so they could mean two things, while both sides bomb each other to clarify the point.

IV. Why Iran Wanted This

Burning tanker in the Strait of Hormuz — the flashpoint where the memorandum’s ambiguity turned into active strikes.

It would be a mistake, though, to read the week as a simple failure of communication — a tragic misunderstanding that better wording might have prevented. Because for Iran, the misunderstanding was not a bug. It was the plan.

Here is the part the headlines tend to bury. In the days before the shooting resumed, Oman and the United Nations maritime organization had quietly designated a new route through the strait — one that ran entirely through Omani waters and required no permission from Tehran. Read that again with the toll booth in mind. A clean road had just been built that bypassed Iran’s booth entirely. If ships could simply sail the Omani corridor, safely and freely, then Iran’s whole strategy — its one piece of leverage, the only card the war left it holding — would quietly evaporate. As Ali Vaez of the International Crisis Group put it: “Best-case or worst-case scenario, they need this leverage. It doesn’t make any sense for them to allow it to erode before they have a final deal.”

So Iran did not stumble into this fight. It started it, deliberately, the moment its leverage was threatened. The drone that hit the Ever Lovely was not a misunderstanding about routes; it was a message about routes — a demonstration that the Omani bypass was not safe, could not be made safe, and would not be allowed to become the road everyone used. Iran never claimed the strike, and it never needed to. The point was made by the wreckage. As the analyst Farzan Sabet observed, Iran “understood they’re losing control,” and grasped that their power over the strait works only “during wartime and during a hostile cease-fire, with regular hostilities.” Which is to say: the leverage is only real if Iran keeps the water dangerous. A peaceful strait is a worthless strait, to Iran. So the strait cannot be allowed to become peaceful.

This is the genuinely chilling logic underneath the week, and it is worse than incompetence. The vague language did not merely permit the violence; it rewarded it. Iran has every incentive to keep the waterway exactly as unsettled as it is now — open enough to collect on, dangerous enough to matter, never so calm that the world forgets who controls the gates. The ambiguity in the memorandum is not a problem Iran wants solved. It is an asset Iran intends to keep, by force if the alternative is clarity.

And yet — because nothing in this war resolves cleanly — by Sunday night a U.S. official said the two sides had agreed, again, to halt the attacks and let the ships move freely, and that technical talks would go on. (Iran, naturally, announced it had cancelled the very talks the Americans said were proceeding; the two governments cannot agree on the state of their own negotiations any more than on the meaning of their own document.) Both sides, the analysts agree, have powerful reasons to keep the wreck of a peace limping forward: Washington has no stomach for a second war before the midterms, and Tehran wants its sanctions relief and its frozen billions. So the likeliest future is not a return to open war, nor a real peace, but exactly this — a “cease-fire” punctuated by periodic killing, dragging on for months, each flare-up eating the time that was supposed to be spent settling the actual questions. As Sabet put it, the more the negotiators have to spend putting out fires, “that doesn’t bode well for progress on the substantive issues.” The fire is not a malfunction of the process. It is the process now.

V. The Other Deal Nobody Signed

If all this feels familiar, it should, because the same week produced a second agreement with the same fatal architecture — and it fell apart even faster.

On Friday, with the strikes already underway, the United States, Israel, and Lebanon signed a “trilateral framework” meant to wind down the war’s Lebanese front. On paper it is a real thing: a staged Israeli withdrawal from a small fraction of occupied southern Lebanon, in exchange for the Lebanese army gradually disarming Hezbollah, with pilot zones and a coordination group and a hundred million American dollars in humanitarian aid. Secretary Rubio called it a “major milestone,” then, in the same breath, “the beginning of the beginning,” which is the kind of phrase you use when you know the thing you just signed has not actually settled anything.

It had not. The agreement’s entire premise is that Hezbollah will disarm — and Hezbollah was not a party to it. Within a day, Hezbollah’s leader Naim Qassem pronounced the deal “humiliating, shameful and a surrender of sovereignty,” declared it “null and void,” and called the linkage of Israeli withdrawal to his group’s disarmament a proposal that “crosses all red lines.” Netanyahu, for his part, emphasized not the sliver of territory Israel would give up but the far larger swath it would keep, “as long as Hezbollah has not been disarmed” — which Hezbollah has just finished promising never to do. The protesters were burning tires in Beirut by Friday night. An Israeli drone struck a southern Lebanese intersection within twenty-four hours of the signing.

It is the strait all over again, in a different theatre. A document that resolves a conflict on the condition that the people doing the fighting agree to stop — signed by everyone except the people doing the fighting. Washington keeps producing these. They photograph beautifully. They hold for about a day.

VI. What “Best Efforts” Was Always Going to Cost

There is a version of this story in which the vague language was a clever bridge over an unbridgeable gap — a way to stop the shooting now and sort the details later, when tempers had cooled and trust had grown. Diplomats do this all the time, and sometimes it works: the ambiguity buys time, the time builds confidence, and the confidence lets the parties fill in the blanks without reaching for their weapons.

But that only works when both sides actually want to fill in the blanks the same way, and are simply not ready to say so yet. It does not work when the two sides want opposite things and the ambiguity is the only reason they could both sign. In that case the vagueness does not buy time. It buys a delay before the inevitable, and it guarantees that when the reckoning comes, each side will believe — correctly, by the text — that it is the one being cheated. That is the most dangerous kind of grievance, the kind where both parties are right. Iran is not wrong that the memorandum lets it “make arrangements.” The United States is not wrong that the memorandum promises “safe passage.” They are both reading the same sentence. That is the catastrophe.

I keep returning, across these installments, to the senior American official who told CNN in the deal’s first hours not to “read too much into the language,” because “it’s the understandings we have with them which is so much more important.” There were, it turns out, no understandings. There was only language — language built to be misread, signed by men who needed a deal more than they needed an agreement, and presented to the world as peace. The understandings were a fiction. The language was a fuse. And this week, in the narrowest stretch of water in the world, it reached the powder.

The guns may be quiet again by the time you read this. By Monday morning an American official was saying the two sides had agreed to stop firing and let the ships move — though Iran, characteristically, had not confirmed it. Even the agreement to stop shooting exists, like everything else in this deal, in only one country’s telling. The President then announced fresh talks in Qatar on Tuesday, to be led on the American side by Jared Kushner — the same Kushner whose clever scheme to have Iran spend its unfrozen billions on American soybeans Tehran has now denied twice — and Iran, asked to confirm the meeting, would say only that “consultations with Qatar were continuing.” So the halt is announced by one side, the talks are announced by one side, and the meeting to settle whether the two sides understand each other is itself a thing the two sides cannot quite agree is happening. But quiet is not the same as settled, and this particular quiet will last exactly until the next time the two readings of the sentence collide. The war did not return over the bomb, or the regime, or the grand questions that the bombing was supposed to settle. It returned over a toll booth, and a sentence, and the oldest mistake in diplomacy: the belief that two people who want different things have made peace simply because they have both signed the same vague and beautiful words.
