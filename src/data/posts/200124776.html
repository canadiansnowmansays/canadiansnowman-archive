UNITED STATES SECRET SERVICE

ADVANCED THREAT INTERDICTION UNIT

PROTECTIVE OPERATIONS MEMORANDUM — JUNE 2026

CLASSIFICATION: Unclassified / For Public Reassurance SUBJECT: UFC Freedom 250 Infrastructure — Transition from Celebratory to Permanent Protective Status DISTRIBUTION: White House Staff, UFC Management, Dana White, The American Public, Ilia Topuria (personal copy, translated into Georgian)

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This memorandum confirms that the octagon cage erected on the South Lawn of the White House for UFC Freedom 250 will not be dismantled following the June 14 event.

Following a comprehensive threat assessment conducted over the past thirty days, the Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit has determined that the cage — initially constructed for entertainment purposes at a lawn replacement cost of $700,000 — represents a significant and cost-effective upgrade to the existing White House protective architecture.

The octagon stays.

The Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit acknowledges that some members of the public have raised the question of what mixed martial arts combat has to do with the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. The Unit has reviewed this question and determined it to be outside the scope of this memorandum.

This memorandum explains why, answers frequently asked questions from the public and the press, and introduces the newest members of the Presidential Protective Detail.

SECTION I: THE THREAT ENVIRONMENT

The Secret Service wishes to be transparent with the American public about the current security situation, insofar as transparency is consistent with not telling you anything that would actually be useful.

The facts are these.

There have been three shooting incidents in the vicinity of the White House in the past four weeks. Secret Service cases are up 40 percent compared to the same period last year. Cases involving individuals with mental health issues are up seven hundred percent. The Deputy Director of the Secret Service has described the current threat environment as no longer a “linear increase” but rather “exponential.”

For context: exponential is the word mathematicians use when linear stops being scary enough.

The U.S. Capitol Police investigated nearly 15,000 threats and actions against members of Congress in 2025 — an increase of 60 percent over the previous year. The Pennsylvania governor’s mansion was set on fire while the governor’s family slept inside. A state representative was shot and killed in her home. At least half a dozen members of the Cabinet have moved into military housing.

The Secret Service does not wish to speculate about what is driving this threat environment. However, it notes that social media, political polarization, and global unrest have all been identified as contributing factors. It declines to comment on whether launching a regime-change war in Iran while simultaneously threatening Canada, Greenland, Panama, and Oman has contributed to the ambient tension. That is above this memorandum’s pay grade.

What the Secret Service can confirm is this: the White House is now, functionally, the new Green Zone.

The 18-acre complex is laced with fencing, sensors, jammers, cameras, armed guards, bunkers, drone interceptors, and surface-to-air missiles. Tourists can no longer approach the 13-foot perimeter fence. Lafayette Square is closed. The Ellipse area was closed last month. The East Wing no longer exists, having been replaced by a construction site protected by titanium fencing that goes deep into the ground and cannot, the President has assured us, be toppled by a tractor or a bulldozer.

The snipers on the roof can see 1,000 yards in every direction.

In this environment, the Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit has asked a simple question: Is there anything else we can do?

The answer, it turns out, is yes.

The answer is the octagon.

SECTION II: THE STRATEGIC CASE FOR PERMANENT OCTAGON RETENTION

The cage erected on the South Lawn for UFC Freedom 250 is, upon reflection, the single most cost-effective security upgrade the White House has received since the installation of bulletproof glass in the Oval Office during World War II.

Consider the threat profile the octagon addresses that conventional security infrastructure cannot.

The Fence Problem. The existing titanium perimeter fence, while formidable, has twice been breached by toddlers. The Secret Service does not wish to dwell on this. The octagon’s chain-link fencing, by contrast, is specifically designed to contain individuals who have been professionally trained to escape from enclosed spaces under duress. If it can hold Khabib Nurmagomedov, it can hold a toddler from Bethesda.

The Lone Wolf Problem. The Secret Service’s greatest challenge is the unpredictable lone actor who arrives without warning at an event or White House gate. The octagon addresses this through deterrence. A would-be attacker approaching the South Lawn and observing a regulation UFC cage staffed by professional fighters weighing between 145 and 265 pounds must now factor into their operational planning the possibility of being taken down by a rear naked choke before reaching the residence. Threat modeling suggests this is a meaningful deterrent.

The Drone Problem. The President has described his forthcoming ballroom as containing a “drone empire,” a “drone gallery,” and a “drone port” that will house “unlimited drones” to protect all of Washington. The octagon’s overhead netting, standard in indoor UFC venues, provides an additional layer of low-altitude drone interdiction at a fraction of the cost of the ballroom, which is currently the subject of multiple lawsuits and has not yet been approved for funding by Congress.

The Visibility Problem. The Secret Service’s protective methodology relies on “overlapping rings of protection.” The octagon, positioned on the South Lawn with clear sightlines in all directions, provides an additional ring staffed by individuals whose professional training specifically involves maintaining situational awareness of threats approaching from 360 degrees while simultaneously neutralizing the threat closest to them. This is, the Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit notes, precisely the skill set the current moment requires.

The $700,000 lawn replacement cost has been absorbed. The infrastructure is in place. The security calculus is clear.

The octagon stays.

Construction of “The Claw” — Dana White’s name for the UFC octagon arena — on the White House South Lawn, May 2026. The finished arena will seat 5,000 ringside, with 85,000 free tickets for Ellipse viewing. Weigh-ins at the Lincoln Memorial. The Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit has completed its assessment. The octagon stays.

SECTION III: THE UFC PRESIDENTIAL PROTECTIVE DETAIL

Effective June 15, 2026, the Secret Service will integrate a rotating cohort of UFC fighters into the Presidential Protective Detail on a permanent basis.

The following personnel have been briefed, credentialed, and assigned:

ILIA TOPURIA Featherweight Champion | Assignment: East Wing Perimeter

Georgian-born, Spanish-raised, undefeated. Topuria’s lateral movement and finishing instincts make him ideally suited for the East Wing construction zone, where irregular terrain and limited visibility create security challenges that conventional agents find taxing. He has been issued a standard-issue earpiece. He has declined to wear the standard-issue suit, which the Secret Service has accepted as a reasonable accommodation.

Special note: Topuria has requested that any threat neutralization occurring within the octagon perimeter be considered a home game. The Secret Service has agreed in principle.

JUSTIN GAETHJE Former Interim Lightweight Champion | Assignment: North Fence, Lafayette Square Interface

Gaethje is what threat analysts call a “pressure fighter” — he advances toward danger rather than retreating from it. This makes him temperamentally well-suited for the Lafayette Square perimeter, where the Secret Service has identified the highest concentration of individuals who may attempt to approach the White House from the north. His assignment to this sector reflects the Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit’s view that sometimes the best interdiction is a 155-pound American from Safford, Arizona, walking directly at you.

Special note: Gaethje has asked whether the anti-war vigil that operated continuously from 1981 until this year could be reinstated as a training exercise. The Secret Service is reviewing this request.

ALEX PEREIRA Light Heavyweight Champion | Assignment: Roving Detail, South Lawn to Ellipse

Pereira is a former kickboxing world champion who speaks limited English but communicates threat assessment with exceptional clarity through other means. His roving assignment across the South Lawn and Ellipse perimeter reflects his operational preference for open space and unobstructed sightlines. He has reviewed the surface-to-air missile installations and described them, through his interpreter, as “adequate.”

Special note: Pereira has requested that the Ellipse closure be extended to include the block containing a Peet’s Coffee. The Secret Service is reviewing this request on its merits.

CIRYL GANE Former Heavyweight Interim Champion | Assignment: West Wing Entrance, Pennsylvania Avenue Corridor

The French heavyweight brings a technical precision to close-quarters protective work that the Secret Service has found complementary to its existing personnel. Gane’s assignment to the Pennsylvania Avenue corridor — closed to vehicles since the Oklahoma City bombing, now closed to pedestrians as well — reflects the unit’s view that this sector requires someone capable of rapid threat assessment and controlled response in a confined space. He has been briefed on the history of the corridor. He described the gradual closure of Pennsylvania Avenue over three decades as “logique.”

Special note: The Secret Service notes that assigning a French national to protect an American president represents a departure from precedent. It notes further that the precedent was established in 1778 and declines to elaborate.

SECTION IV: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Q: Is the White House still the People’s House?

A: The White House Historical Association operates an excellent museum called The People’s House: A White House Experience directly across the street. It features a replica Oval Office and interactive exhibits on State Dinners. Two bullets recently shattered its display cases. The Secret Service recommends visiting during daylight hours on days when there are no scheduled events in the vicinity. The White House itself is accessible to the public in the sense that the public is welcome to observe it from a distance that the Secret Service considers appropriate, which is increasing.

Q: What does UFC have to do with the 250th anniversary of American independence?

A: The President has described the event as “incredible, the best thing I’ve ever done, maybe ever.” The Advanced Threat Interdiction Unit considers the matter settled.

Q: Is this a Green Zone?

A: The Secret Service objects to this characterization on the grounds that the White House complex does not have 30-foot blast walls, a swimming pool, or buffet-style dining. It acknowledges the complex does have a forthcoming ballroom, drone empire, titanium fencing, surface-to-air missiles, and a UFC cage, and declines to comment further on the comparison.

Q: Who is paying for the UFC fighters’ salaries?

A: Dana White has described the arrangement as a “partnership.” The Secret Service has described it as a “public-private security collaboration.” The Congressional Budget Office has described it as “a line item we were not expecting.” The President has described it as “incredible, the best thing I’ve ever done for security, maybe ever.”

Q: What happens to the octagon after the ballroom is built?

A: The ballroom, when completed, will contain impenetrable steel walls, four-inch-thick windows, a drone empire, and a shield so strong that “if a drone hits it, it bounces off,” according to the President. It will also contain a ballroom. The octagon will remain on the South Lawn as an outer security perimeter and, should scheduling permit, an occasional venue for additional UFC events. The Secret Service notes that the $700,000 lawn replacement cost was a one-time expense. The grass is not coming back.

Q: Is the President aware of the irony of celebrating American independence inside a fortress?

A: The President is aware of many things. He has described the fortifications as “beautiful” and “very strong, stronger than anything you’ve ever seen.” He pushes thoughts of the security situation from his mind. “If I thought about it a lot,” he told reporters, “I wouldn’t be a very good president.” The jackhammering outside the Cabinet Room continues.

SECTION V: HISTORICAL CONTEXT

For reassurance purposes only.

Thomas Jefferson built the first fence around the White House in 1801. It was wooden. It was designed to keep animals away from the gardens. The people were largely free to roam.

Franklin Pierce had the first full-time bodyguard. Abraham Lincoln posted guards outside but had them dress in civilian clothes and hide their firearms. Theodore Roosevelt called the Secret Service “a very small but very necessary thorn in the flesh.” Franklin Roosevelt rejected a proposal to repaint the White House in camouflage.

Pennsylvania Avenue, Washington D.C. — open for the entire history of the Republic. Streetcars, flower gardens, and the Capitol dome in the distance. Bill Clinton called its 1995 closure "a significant infringement on traditional American freedoms." The octagon went up in 2026.

Pennsylvania Avenue was open to traffic through four presidential assassinations, a Civil War, two World Wars, and the Gulf War. It was closed in 1995 after the Oklahoma City bombing. At the time, Bill Clinton called it a “significant infringement on traditional American freedoms.”

We have come some distance since 1995.

The White House, which began as a residence with a wooden fence to keep the deer out, is now an 18-acre security complex with titanium fencing, surface-to-air missiles, snipers who can see 1,000 yards in every direction, a drone empire under construction, a UFC cage on the lawn, and four professional fighters on the payroll.

Harry Truman called it the “great white jail.”

He did not know the half of it.

CONCLUSION: A NOTE FROM THE CANADIAN OBSERVER

From the 49th parallel, the Octagon Zone raises a question that no security briefing can answer: At what point does protecting democracy from violence require building something that no longer looks like democracy?

The Secret Service is doing its job. The threat environment is real. The exponential increase in cases is not a public relations problem — it is a genuine crisis, and the agents managing it deserve acknowledgment for operating in conditions that would have been unimaginable to their predecessors.

But the fortress they are building around the White House is also a symptom of something the fortress cannot cure. You do not get to an exponential increase in threats against the president, the vice president, the Cabinet, state governors, state legislators, and municipal judges by accident. You get there through years of rhetoric that treats political opponents as enemies, that frames every election as an existential battle between good and evil, that tells supporters the system is rigged against them and only one man can fix it.

The octagon is a very good cage.

It will not fix that.

Happy 250th, America. The People’s House is across the street. Mind the bullet holes in the display cases.
