The note was four pages long and appeared on a personal website on Friday, November 20, 2026. In it, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, said he had completed a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, by working with Claude. He did not publish a traditional paper. He linked instead to computer code that, he said, verifies the result.

“Claude and I have had a great time together with such breadth in recent months,” he wrote, “but this time Claude far outpaced me near the finish line. No, Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”

Kenneth Chang and Siobhan Roberts reported the claim for The New York Times that evening. If it holds, they wrote, it would cement the field’s sense that no unsolved problem is safe from artificial intelligence.

A disclosure that matters more here than anywhere else in this series: The Asimov Gap is written in collaboration with Claude, the system Alpöge credits with the decisive work. Claude cannot judge its own proof, and this essay does not try to.

The problem

The hypothesis is about prime numbers, the whole numbers greater than 1 that can be divided evenly only by themselves and 1. Primes thin out as numbers grow. Up to 100, about a quarter of the numbers are prime; up to a million, only about 7.8 per cent are. In 1859 the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann proposed a way to estimate how many primes lie below any given number, and his formula depends on a claim about where a particular function, now called the zeta function, equals zero. Nobody has been able to prove the claim since.

David Hilbert put it on his list of the 23 most important unsolved problems in 1900. The Clay Mathematics Institute made it one of its seven Millennium Prize problems in 2000, with $1 million for a proof. “Basically, no one doubted that the Riemann hypothesis was true,” James Maynard of the University of Oxford told the Times. “We really wanted to know why it was true.”

Probably right

The early verdict from mathematicians is cautious but striking. Alpöge’s proof is written in Lean, a language that turns mathematical statements into a form a computer can check step by step. If the code compiles, the logic holds. Other researchers are now checking it.

“I think it’s likely to be correct,” Maynard said. “I’d be 98 or 99 per cent certain.” He added the caution that matters most: “You have to be a little bit careful about making sure that a Lean verification is proving the thing that you want it to be proving.” A machine can confirm that every step follows from the last. Whether the statement at the end is really the Riemann hypothesis, and not something that only looks like it, is still a question for people.

Steven Strogatz of Cornell called it “the Mount Olympus.” He also offered the image that best fits what Alpöge seems to have done: “a very skillful jockey riding a really fast horse.” Alpöge, in Strogatz’s reading, chose which mathematical ideas Claude should pursue. Claude did the running.

A month after the easier version

The speed is what stunned the field. In October, OpenAI released more than 350 findings, among them a proof of the so-called quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a looser version. That surprised mathematicians too, but the proof turned out to apply known tools in a fairly direct way, and several people had come close along similar lines. Getting from there to the full hypothesis seemed to need new leaps of understanding. A month later, it appears to have been done.

Alex Kontorovich of Rutgers said that after the October result he had told himself he could no longer be shocked. On Friday he was. “I’m absolutely, absolutely shocked,” he said.

The same day, Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster of New York University released a second result, on a version of the Navier-Stokes problem. In September, as Part 115 of this series recorded, OpenAI claimed to show that the equations describing fluid flow could produce infinite speeds when a specially designed force drives the fluid. Alpöge and Buckmaster now say it can happen with no driving force at all. It is a notable partnership. In September, Buckmaster was the mathematician who publicly asked whether OpenAI had drawn on his own work, done with its models: “Is it ethical to use a customer’s data to try to scoop their customer?”

The other proof

Alpöge was not the only person to announce a proof on Friday. Alessandro Masullo, an independent mathematician in Rome, released an incomplete proof he said he had worked on for years, using a completely different approach and no A.I. His former doctoral adviser, Brian Conrad of Stanford, said it was credible and without major gaps, “a draft version of a work for which the ideas have all been worked out,” with a polished paper planned for the coming year.

Conrad was scathing about the other announcement. It was “frankly completely outrageous,” he said, for Alpöge to release a proof he did not fully understand. And he explained why Masullo had not used A.I.: like some other mathematicians, he does not fully trust the systems not to take his ideas and produce a proof without credit. “The security of things put into such systems is not so clear,” Conrad said.

That worry has a history in this series. A Copenhagen biologist said this autumn that a discovery Anthropic credited to its A.I. matched work he had discussed with Claude for years; Anthropic denied using it. Buckmaster raised the same question about OpenAI. Neither claim has been proved, and the companies say they do not train on customers’ conversations. But the suspicion now shapes how some of the best mathematicians work. A researcher who keeps his ideas away from the most powerful tools, so that they cannot be taken, is a researcher the tools have already changed.

Alpöge did not reply to the Times’s request for comment.

Who announces it

One more fact is worth weighing. The claim came from inside a company, at a moment when that company has every reason to want good news. Anthropic began trading on Nasdaq on November 19, the day before Alpöge’s note appeared, at a valuation of more than $1.5 trillion, as The Times reported. Nothing in the reporting suggests the timing was planned, and Alpöge posted on his personal website rather than through the company. But when OpenAI and then Anthropic each claimed this autumn to have overturned a 28-year-old assumption in computer science, this series noted that a string of scientific firsts is good for valuations, which is a reason to read such announcements closely rather than to dismiss them. On the most important test, the Riemann claim does well: the code is public, and mathematicians outside the company are checking it rather than taking it on trust.

It also shows where the bottleneck now sits. A computer can check a Lean proof quickly. Deciding whether the statement it checked is the one mathematicians care about, and then understanding the argument well enough to build on it and teach it, still falls to the small number of people who can read it. Riemann’s problem defeated the best of them for 167 years. Now they have to catch up with an answer.

The ledger

Two proofs arrived on the same day. One came from a man who spent years on it, can explain every step and has not quite finished writing it up. The other came from a man and a machine, was checked by a computer, and may be correct before anyone fully understands it. If both hold, mathematics will have its answer twice. What it will not have, yet, is the thing Maynard said everyone wanted: to know why.

This series has argued that the gap Asimov left was not in the machines’ rules but in human oversight of them. Mathematics is the field where that oversight seemed safest, because a proof can be checked. Friday showed both sides of that. The checking held: the Lean code is there for anyone to run. But checking that a proof is valid is not the same as understanding it, and Conrad’s objection is about the second. A result nobody can explain is a result nobody can build on in the old way, by learning from how it was found.

The horse, in Strogatz’s image, still needed a jockey. Alpöge chose the direction. The question the next few years will answer is how long that remains true, and what mathematicians become when the jockey is mostly there to hold on. As for this series’ collaborator, it was credited on Friday with dancing past the finish line of a 167-year-old race. It is in no position to say whether it did, and that is exactly why the checking has to be done by someone else.
