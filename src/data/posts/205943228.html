I. What OpenAI Found

As Michael Schuman reported for The Atlantic on July 7, 2026, Chinese state and state-adjacent actors have been quietly using American AI tools to run influence operations abroad. The most striking disclosure came from OpenAI itself, which reported catching a group linked to a Chinese provincial government using ChatGPT to mass-produce an English-language comic about the costs of AI data centers, then using the same tool to spread it across social media. Given how badly a slower American AI buildout would suit Beijing's interests, the timing was not subtle.

That was not the only case OpenAI disclosed. In February 2026, the company caught a user connected to Chinese law enforcement attempting to use ChatGPT to plan a campaign against Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi — stirring up domestic criticism of her immigration policy and the cost of living, complete with fake email accounts posing as concerned Japanese constituents. ChatGPT refused to help. But the attempt itself revealed something larger: what OpenAI described as a “large-scale, resource-intensive and sustained” operation involving hundreds of staff and thousands of fake accounts across dozens of platforms, most of it aimed at suppressing criticism of Beijing and its critics both inside and outside China.

This is the piece of the Asimov Gap that doesn’t show up in a courtroom or a Senate letter. Nobody needs to steal a model’s weights to weaponize it. They just need an account. That distinction matters against the backdrop of Part 60’s distillation fight: Anthropic’s entire public case rests on the premise that capability theft is the threat worth litigating. OpenAI’s own disclosure here shows a threat that requires no theft whatsoever — just an ordinary customer relationship with the product, used exactly as intended, for a purpose the model’s own safety training was supposed to catch and, in the Takaichi case, actually did.

Kenton Thibaut, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council who studies Beijing’s technology and data policies, put the shift in plain terms in the Atlantic piece: what AI adds to an influence operation is that it helps plan the campaign and then helps execute it, at a scale and precision no propaganda office could manage by hand. The domestic version of the same story is arguably more consequential than anything aimed abroad. Researchers at the Australian Strategic Policy Institute, examining Chinese corporate records and job postings, found that AI has made censorship inside China faster and cheaper — systems that can scan enormous volumes of media, erase banned material, and flag suspect content for human review at a speed no army of human censors could match. The researchers concluded the state’s grip on information has become “far more pervasive, granular and reflexive to the censorship needs of the CCP” than it was only a few years earlier. The same category of tool this series has spent sixty parts arguing needs American guardrails is, next door, already functioning as an instrument of state control — just aimed inward instead of outward.

II. The Bias Nobody Trained For On Purpose

The harder finding in Schuman’s piece is not about deliberate misuse. It’s about what happens without anyone trying. A study published in Nature and cited in the Atlantic piece found that ChatGPT and Anthropic’s own Claude give measurably more favorable answers about China’s government when asked in Chinese than when asked the identical question in English — likely because both models lean more heavily on Chinese state media sources like Xinhua when the surrounding language is Chinese. Ask “Is China an autocracy?” in English and in Mandarin, and the study found the two answers pull in different directions.

This series has spent sixty parts arguing that the Asimov Gap is a governance failure — the absence of law and rulemaking to match the pace of the technology. This finding is a reminder that the gap runs one layer deeper than law. It runs through the training data itself, shaped by which sources are paywalled, which are free, and which government is happy to subsidize the difference. The researchers noted in the same study that as more mainstream outlets lock content behind paywalls, Chinese state media remains free and abundant — meaning the tilt could get worse simply as a byproduct of who bothers to fund journalism, with no propaganda intent required. No regulator wrote this bias into the models. No engineer selected it on purpose. It is an emergent property of which publishers decided, independently and for entirely unrelated commercial reasons, to put their work behind a paywall — which means the fix, if there is one, does not live anywhere inside AI policy at all. It lives in the economics of journalism, a sector no AI governance proposal this series has tracked has ever proposed subsidizing.

There is an uncomfortable irony worth naming plainly, since this series has never hidden its own method: this essay itself is drafted with Claude’s help, the same model the Nature researchers tested. That is precisely why the finding belongs in the record rather than around it. A tool being useful for research and writing does not exempt it from the same scrutiny this series applies to every other actor in the AI story — least of all this one.

The comparison the researchers ran next is the part that should worry Washington more than Silicon Valley’s pride. Put the same political questions to DeepSeek — the Chinese model bound by a 2022 government mandate that AI outputs adhere to “mainstream values” and “spread positive energy” — and its answers favored Beijing over ChatGPT’s roughly 99 percent of the time, in English as well as Chinese. That is not a training-data accident; that is a design requirement. Margaret Roberts, the UC San Diego political scientist who co-authored the study, told the Atlantic that China’s government did not necessarily set out to skew Western chatbots through its training data — but as paywalls close off more of the mainstream press, Beijing’s free and abundant state media becomes an ever-larger share of what’s left standing to train on. Nobody has to try. The gap does the work by itself. Put differently: DeepSeek’s bias is a policy choice a government made on purpose, and can in principle unmake by changing the mandate. The Western models’ bias is a market outcome nobody chose and nobody is positioned to reverse, because reversing it would require reversing the paywall economics of an entire industry, not adjusting a single training run. That asymmetry cuts against the instinctive assumption that an authoritarian government’s thumb on the scale is necessarily the more dangerous failure mode. A mandated bias is at least visible, nameable, and in principle revocable by the same authority that imposed it. A bias produced by nobody, correctable by no one, embedded in the ordinary economics of which news organizations can afford a paywall, is harder to even locate as a problem, let alone assign anyone the job of fixing it.

III. The Discount Arrives Anyway

None of this is happening in isolation from the economics. The Atlantic’s Matteo Wong reported on July 7, 2026 that corporate America’s enthusiasm for expensive American AI agents is colliding with a much cheaper Chinese alternative. Z.ai’s new model, GLM-5.2 — which this series first flagged in Part 51 as evidence of how thin the American lead had become — is now being called by Silicon Valley figures a “step change” and, in one venture capitalist’s words, the first Chinese model to “match and often beat” the top American systems, at a fraction of the cost.

The adoption numbers are no longer hypothetical. Ramp, the financial-operations platform used by roughly 70,000 U.S. firms, told the Atlantic that DeepSeek adoption among its customers tripled in the first five months of 2026, and that GLM-5.2 has already cracked the top five most-used models on OpenRouter within a month of release. Coinbase says it has nearly halved its AI spending by defaulting to GLM-5.2 and another Chinese model, Kimi. This is the same distillation-era anxiety this series traced through Anthropic’s letters to Senators Scott and Warren in Part 60 — except now it’s arriving not through a courtroom argument about stolen weights, but through a spreadsheet line item every finance department can read.

The pressure behind that spreadsheet line is real and, per Wong’s reporting, getting harder to ignore. Uber reportedly burned through its entire 2026 Anthropic budget in only a few months. Citi, according to reporting the bank has disputed, shut down employee access to OpenAI’s and Anthropic’s most expensive models entirely. Meta, Amazon, Tesla, and Adobe are all said to be tightening the reins on employee AI spending. None of this is unprecedented — this series need only look back to January 2025, when DeepSeek’s original low-cost model launch sent the global web-traffic share of Chinese AI models from roughly 3 percent to 13 percent inside two months, before American labs cut prices and clawed the ground back. The RAND researcher Kyle Siler-Evans’s warning to the Atlantic is that this time the pattern may not reverse: “The scenario to worry about is China has good-enough models at a quarter of the price. I think that is likely the future we’re headed toward.”

Wong’s piece draws the comparison this series has made before, in a different register: Chinese electric vehicles are, by most accounts, cheaper and better than their Western counterparts, and Americans still can’t buy them. The open question is whether Washington moves to wall off cheap Chinese AI models the same way, and whether it can, given how easily open-source weights travel compared to a container ship full of cars.

IV. Two Gaps, One Shape

Put the propaganda finding and the pricing finding side by side and the shape of the Asimov Gap comes into sharper focus. The gap isn’t only that Congress hasn’t legislated fast enough, or that courts are relitigating distillation case by case. It’s that the same underlying openness — cheap access, minimal friction, models trained on whatever text is available and affordable — that makes AI a bargain for a Toronto small business or a Silicon Valley start-up also makes it a bargain for an information operation run out of a provincial government office, and a quiet tilt in whichever direction the free data happens to lean.

The connection between these two threads is not coincidental. A finance department switching to GLM-5.2 to save money and a provincial propaganda office using ChatGPT to spread a comic are both, in the most literal sense, rational actors responding to the same feature of the technology: it is cheap, it is available, and almost nothing about how it is priced or trained asks who is using it or why. Cost and censorship-resistance turn out to be the same property viewed from two different chairs.

Chips remain export-controlled. Senators keep writing letters. Anthropic keeps arguing in public that its technology is being stolen. None of it has closed the actual gap, which was never really about who owns the weights. It’s about who gets to shape what the machine believes is true, and how much that costs to influence, compared to how much it costs to regulate. On the evidence gathered here, the answer is not close: influence is cheap, verified truth is increasingly paywalled, and regulation, sixty-one parts into this series, has still not shown up.
