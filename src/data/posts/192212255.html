One year ago today, I published my first essay on this platform. It was a careful, earnest piece on presidential impeachment — the kind of thing a retired Canadian businessman writes when he’s decided, somewhat impulsively, that the world needs his analysis. I wasn’t sure anyone would read it. I wasn’t entirely sure I’d write a second one.

I’ve now written one thousand.

That number sits strangely in my hands. One thousand essays, across more than 70 distinct series, in 365 days. Nearly three a day on average — though that number masks the real arc: beginning cautiously at two or three a week, and arriving at this anniversary filing four or five a day. The writing, it turns out, is its own accelerant. So much so that I found myself in the peculiar position of deliberately slowing down — filing three essays a day instead of five — to ensure Essay 1,000 landed on the exact anniversary of Essay 1. Stephen Leacock, who understood irony in all its forms, would have appreciated that.

A note of candor, offered alongside the congratulation: I am aware that four or five essays a day is, by any reasonable standard, a lot. I have pushed content at a pace that occasionally made me wince at my own notifications. Volume and quality are not the same thing, and I know it. What I hope — what I genuinely believe, though I acknowledge my bias — is that most of what landed in your inbox met a standard worth your time. You are the better judge of that than I am.

It didn’t begin here. It began on X, where I genuinely believed that tweeting was the end all and be all of commentary. I had a Ghibli-style avatar, a husky named Dior at my side, and a Stephen Leacock meme as my banner — because sharing a first name with Canada’s patron saint of gentle satirical observation apparently gave me the license to attempt satire. Whether Leacock is turning over in his grave or nodding approvingly from wherever distinguished Canadian humorists reside, I cannot say. I prefer to think the latter. He spent his career insisting that satire and serious analysis weren’t opposites. I’ve spent this year trying to prove the same point.

The satirical impulse never entirely left. Stephen’s Sunday Satire became a weekly exercise in holding absurdity up to the light. The Great Gayermandering Series gave that impulse a specific and richly deserving target. And The Enforcers: Inside Trump’s Government Mob proved that sometimes the most satirical thing you can do is simply describe what is actually happening. All three reminded me — and hopefully readers — that accountability journalism doesn’t require solemnity to land its punches.

But long-form writing turned out to be my actual forte, which neither I nor anyone who knew me from 280 characters would have predicted. And nowhere did that feel more natural than in The Moonbeam Chronicle: Sketches of Elsinore — my most deliberately Leacock-flavored work, an Ontario satire series published under its own separate Substack, yet part of the same creative umbrella that houses everything I do. A companion to CanadianSnowmanSays rather than a subset of it. That it helped push me to this very milestone feels fitting — Leacock’s ghost, doing the accounting.

This journey has been AI-assisted — I won't pretend otherwise, and I've never tried to. I updated my About page with a note on process for those who feel entitled to one. The short version: Claude writes the way a very fast typist writes. The ideas, the arguments, the research, the structure, the series architecture, the editorial judgment, and the final word on everything published here are mine. At four or five essays a day across seventy five series, I could not bring you this volume any other way. That is not an apology. Those who consider AI-assisted writing lazy are welcome to their view — I'd ask them to consider whether the alternative, silence or a fraction of the output, serves the reader better. I don't think it does. The ideas matter more than the instrument that carries them onto the page, which is also what every writer who ever dictated to a secretary, worked with an editor, or let a subeditor fix their grammar believed. The Asimov Gap series — now past 30 essays and still running — has been my most sustained attempt to think through what that collaboration means for all of us. I'm still thinking.

History, it turns out, is where I lean hardest. Canada's own complicated story, Cuba's defiant arc, Afghanistan's long tragedy, China's strategic ambitions explored through The Middle Kingdom and Formosa: The Beautiful Problem, the Israel-Palestine conflict that refuses every simple frame. Then the American historical canvas: The $37 Trillion Question staring into the fiscal abyss, a deep dive into PAC money and the architecture of political influence, and How America Built the World Order and Chose to Destroy It — a series whose title, I confess, wrote itself.

Not every series was a diagnosis. Code Blue was also a prescription — my genuine attempt to propose a solution to the American healthcare catastrophe, not merely document it. The Great Energy Challenge tried to map a path through the energy transition. America’s Next Industrial Revolution and my various Tesla series tracked the EV revolution as it unfolded in real time, including my own small stake in its outcome.

Some series followed the news so closely they became something closer to a war diary. The Iran Crisis series has now reached Part LXX, publishing twice a week as events demand — a reminder that some stories don’t wait for your filing schedule. The Trump Accountability Project grew to 47 documents, a number whose irony was not lost on me. The Baseline Problem documented, with clinical seriousness, the evidence around a sitting president’s cognitive decline. When Democracy Fights Back tracked the institutional resistance that refuses to quit.

Seventy plus series. One thousand essays. One year.

I began as an outsider — a Canadian looking south with the particular clarity that distance sometimes provides. I remain an outsider. But I am no longer a newcomer.

My first essay asked whether a president could be impeached for what he was visibly doing. The question hasn’t aged out of relevance.

To everyone who read, replied, argued, or simply showed up in the inbox — thank you for making the conversation real.

One thousand essays later, neither has my reason for writing them.

Thank you Stephen Leacock. And thank you, Dad.
