On Monday, October 5, 2026, the National Institutes of Health told much of its Bethesda campus to work from home. An internal email warned of extra traffic and security and did not say why. The reason was a visit by the Secretary of Health and Human Services, his first to the NIH since taking office. Robert F. Kennedy Jr. brought most of the federal health leadership with him: Dr. Mehmet Oz of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, Dr. Erica Schwartz of the CDC, and Kyle Diamantas, acting commissioner of the FDA. They came to meet patients who believe vaccines injured them.

The visit was not announced. HHS still has not described it publicly. What we know comes from Sheryl Gay Stolberg’s reporting in the New York Times on October 7, 2026, based on several people who spoke anonymously because they were not authorized to discuss it. Bloomberg reported some of the details first.

According to that reporting, Kennedy is planning a broad federal vaccine-injury initiative. It has two concrete parts. The first is a new research clinic at the NIH Clinical Center. The second is a new billing code that would reimburse doctors for filing reports of serious vaccine side effects.

The clinic is not a surprise. The billing code is where this essay starts, because it raises the first question anyone who has worked inside a large billing operation would ask. How easy will it be to abuse?

What is actually being built

The plan has been visible for months, though few people noticed. In June 2026, the NIH posted two “highlighted topics” notices. These are announcements that tell researchers what the agency wants to fund. One said HHS and the NIH would study vaccine injuries through better data collection, including a new research programme at the Clinical Center that could expand to other sites. The other asked for large epidemiological studies of how vaccines relate to long-term health, both positive and negative, across a person’s life.

That second notice matches one of Kennedy’s oldest complaints: that nobody has done long-term studies of vaccination. The claim is false, but he repeats it.

In September 2026, the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences proposed a broad study of environmental chemical exposures. The proposal never mentions vaccines. One of its stated goals, however, is to better understand aluminum, including aluminum from “medically relevant exposures.” Aluminum salts have been used in vaccines for decades to strengthen the immune response. Kennedy has called them dangerous for years. The Times reported that one person familiar with the work said vaccine research could be part of it. Experts say there is no evidence that the aluminum adjuvants in vaccines cause harm.

Taken together, the pieces fit. There is a clinic to study the injured, a research agenda aimed at long-term harms and at aluminum specifically, and a payment mechanism to increase the number of reported cases.

The system that is already there

Before judging the billing code, it helps to know what it would attach to.

The United States has had a national vaccine-safety reporting system since 1990. The Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System, or VAERS, is run jointly by the CDC and the FDA. Anyone can file a report: a doctor, a nurse, a parent, a patient, a lawyer, or a stranger. That openness is intentional. VAERS is meant to be an early-warning system. It is supposed to catch rare problems, so it accepts everything and screens later. The government’s own guidance says plainly that a VAERS report does not show the vaccine caused the event.

Doctors are also already required to report. The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986, the law that created the federal vaccine-injury compensation system, requires healthcare providers to report certain adverse events after certain vaccines. That requirement has been in place for forty years, and providers are not paid for it.

It is also true that the system under-reports. Every passive surveillance system does. Filing a report takes time, and doctors are busy and unpaid for it. Many mild reactions are never recorded. Epidemiologists know this, which is why VAERS is paired with active surveillance systems that monitor large patient populations directly. Paying doctors for their time is not a crazy idea in itself, and the case for it should be heard. I will come back to it.

The problem lies in what exactly the money pays for.

You get what you meter

As reported, the billing code pays for the act of filing a report. It does not pay for a confirmed diagnosis or a clinical finding. It pays for the paperwork.

In any billing system, you get what you meter. If you pay per call, you get more calls. If you pay per ticket, you get more tickets. If you pay a doctor every time they report a suspected serious vaccine injury into a database that accepts any report and screens nothing at intake, you will get more reports of suspected serious vaccine injuries. Most of the extra reports will come from honest people responding to a new incentive. Some will not. The system will have no way of telling them apart.

There is nothing hypothetical about this. It is a familiar pattern in American health care. Low-friction billing codes with little documentation, paid by public insurers, are exactly where fraud tends to concentrate. In April 2019, the Justice Department’s Operation Brace Yourself broke up a scheme involving telemarketers, telemedicine doctors and equipment suppliers who billed Medicare for orthopaedic braces patients often never needed. The alleged losses exceeded $1.2 billion. In June 2025, the department announced what it called the largest health-care fraud takedown in its history: 324 defendants and roughly $14.6 billion in intended losses. A large share of that involved paying for services that were never really delivered.

We do not need to go back as far as orthopaedic braces, though. The closest parallel is the pandemic itself. In 2020, HHS set up the COVID-19 Uninsured Program to pay providers for testing and treating uninsured patients. It paid first and checked later. As Dave Muoio reported for Fierce Healthcare on July 21, 2023, the HHS inspector general estimated that about $784 million of the nearly $4.2 billion the programme paid out in 2020 was improper. The money went for patients who actually had insurance, for services unrelated to COVID-19, and for services patients never received. On April 20, 2022, the Justice Department charged 21 people with COVID-related health-care fraud of about $150 million, as Rebecca Pifer Parduhn reported for Healthcare Dive. Some had offered free COVID tests at drive-through sites, then used the patients’ information to bill Medicare for office visits and tests nobody ordered. Others sold fake vaccination cards. In June 2023, the Small Business Administration’s inspector general estimated that more than $200 billion in pandemic relief loans and grants had gone to potentially fraudulent applicants, as NBC News reported. The lesson of 2020 was that when Washington pays on the basis of a claim and plans to audit later, organized fraud arrives before the auditors do. Kennedy’s billing code would pay on the basis of a report, to a database that says in its own fine print that a report proves nothing.

A vaccine-injury reporting code has the features fraudsters look for. The service is mostly paperwork. Proof that the service happened is the report itself. The underlying claim is a suspicion that is not checked when it is filed. The patient pool is large and anxious, and is being told by the country’s top health official that injuries are common and hidden. No one has to fake a brace. They only have to fill in a form.

The Times story does not say how much the code would pay. It does not say what documentation a doctor would need, whether a report has to meet any clinical standard, or how claims would be audited. Dr. Oz’s presence suggests CMS will handle it. Those are exactly the details that decide whether this is a modest administrative improvement or an invitation to abuse. HHS did not respond to the Times’ requests for comment.

The loop

Even if no one commits fraud, the design creates a quieter problem.

Paid reports will produce more reports. More reports will look, in the raw VAERS numbers, like more injuries. Those numbers are routinely misread, and Kennedy has been one of the people misreading them for decades. The apparent rise then becomes the justification for the clinic, the long-term studies and the aluminum research. In effect, the incentive produces the evidence used to justify the incentive.

A serious safety programme would protect against this. It would treat paid reports as a separate data stream, flag them, and test them against active surveillance before reaching any conclusion. Nothing reported so far suggests anyone has planned for that. It is not clear anyone at the top wants to. This department fired the entire CDC vaccine advisory committee in 2025. It narrowed the childhood vaccine schedule by executive order on August 10, 2026, and this series has documented both.

Kennedy’s attention to VAERS is not new. When Dr. Peter Marks, the FDA’s top vaccine regulator, resigned in March 2025, he said part of the conflict was his resistance to giving Kennedy unrestricted access to the VAERS database. The department now plans to pay people to put more data into it.

Where the reports lead

The billing code would not stand alone. It is one stage in a sequence.

On July 8, 2026, Chelsea Cirruzzo reported for STAT that HHS was preparing a proposed rule to create a table of injuries presumed to be caused by COVID-19 vaccines. People with conditions on that table could seek government compensation through the Countermeasures Injury Compensation Program without proving causation case by case. Fierce Pharma noted a warning from health lawyer Richard Hughes IV: read broadly, the proposed standards could let the secretary make a scientifically unsound decision about the table and then argue that no court can review it.

Alongside that, Kennedy has spent more than a year promising to “fix” the separate National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program, the no-fault system Congress created in 1986. It is funded by an excise tax on each vaccine dose. Kennedy said in July 2025 that it had paid out about $5.4 billion to roughly 12,000 petitioners. He has called the programme corrupt. He has also suggested adding autism to its list of compensable injuries. Yale’s Jason Schwartz told the Associated Press in July 2025 that adding autism would sharply increase compensable cases and could bankrupt the fund.

Put the stages in order. A doctor is paid to file a report. The report adds to a database that is read as a signal. The signal supports a presumed-injury table. The table opens the way to compensation. Each stage looks defensible by itself. Together, they mean money flows toward whoever can produce the most paperwork, and the clinical evidence comes last.

That brings us to the conflict of interest the department has never resolved. Sarah Owermohle reported for CNN on July 28, 2025, that before he became secretary, Kennedy worked as a consultant to the law firm Wisner Baum. He referred what he called many hundreds of cases to the firm, including suits over Merck’s HPV vaccine Gardasil, and was entitled to 10% of any payout. In his ethics filings, Kennedy said he had passed his litigation interests to a “non-dependent, adult son.” His son Conor works at Wisner Baum. On January 27, 2026, four Senate Democrats wrote to him asking how he, his family and his associates would benefit from his changes to vaccine-injury compensation.

I do not know of any evidence that Kennedy designed the billing code to benefit his son’s firm, and I make no such claim. The point is narrower and harder to dismiss. A man with a documented financial history in vaccine-injury litigation is building, in secret, a publicly funded system that increases the number of documented vaccine injuries. He has not explained the design, the safeguards or the reasoning. Under any ordinary standard of public administration, that alone would justify an inspector general’s review before the first claim is paid.

The case for the defence

This is not the whole story, and leaving out the rest would be dishonest.

Vaccine injuries are real. They are rare, but they happen. The World Health Organization estimated in 2024 that immunization had saved 154 million lives over fifty years. That figure does not change the fact that some people are seriously harmed. Mainstream vaccine-safety researchers agree. Dr. Helen Chu, who sat on the CDC’s vaccine advisory committee until Kennedy fired all of its members, told the Times that adverse events deserve more serious attention and more research funding, so future vaccines can be made safer. Dr. Daniel Salmon, who directs the Institute for Vaccine Safety at Johns Hopkins, agreed that more safety research would help. “It needs to be rigorous science,” he added.

Patients have also been badly served. Brianne Dressen, a former preschool teacher from Utah, joined a clinical trial of AstraZeneca’s COVID-19 vaccine in November 2020. She developed neurological symptoms within an hour, spent time in a wheelchair, and was eventually diagnosed by NIH clinicians with post-vaccine neuropathy. AstraZeneca withdrew the vaccine in 2024. Her lawsuit against the company is still pending. In March 2026, her nonprofit, React19, asked the CDC for a dedicated diagnostic code for COVID-vaccine injuries. Axios reported on March 26, 2026, that if approved, the code would take effect in fall 2027. She was one of the patients Kennedy met in Bethesda. She told the Times that this was the first time anything official had happened for people like her. It is hard to read that without sympathy.

Note the difference between what React19 asked for and what Kennedy is reportedly building. A diagnostic code records a condition a clinician has identified. A billing code for filing reports pays for the suspicion. The first helps researchers find patients. The second, unless carefully designed, mainly helps produce reports.

So the honest version of the defence is not “this is fine.” It is “this could be fine.” A paid reporting code could fix a real under-reporting problem if it came with safeguards. Those would include a clinical standard for what counts as a reportable serious event, documentation that can be audited, a cap on the volume any single provider can bill, separate analysis of paid reports, independent review of the research the clinic produces, and a public explanation of all of it before launch.

None of those safeguards has been announced. What has been announced, in effect, is a closed campus and an unanswered request for comment.

Why the secrecy

The Times could not say why the visit was kept secret. The context offers a likely explanation.

At one point the White House told Kennedy to stop talking about vaccines because it feared the issue would hurt Republicans in the midterms. In February he duly shifted to his “Eat Real Food” message. The midterms are on November 3, 2026, less than four weeks after the Bethesda visit. Vaccine-preventable diseases, measles above all, are rising across the United States as vaccination rates fall. A public announcement of a federal programme paying doctors to report vaccine injuries is the last thing a nervous campaign wants on the evening news.

In the TACO framework this series uses, this is Obfuscation, the fourth step. Kennedy did not abandon the vaccine agenda. He took it out of public view. It went into a highlighted-topics notice in June, an aluminum study whose proposal never says “vaccine” in September, and a campus visit in October that NIH staff were told to stay home for. The policy moved forward. Only the public announcement was dropped.

The question to ask

I come back to the question that prompted this essay, because it is the one the department needs to answer and has not.

How open to fraud is a federal payment for filing unverified reports into a system designed to accept anything? On the details available, very open. That is not because doctors are dishonest. It is because the design pays for volume, cannot verify what it pays for, and feeds a compensation system whose rules are being loosened by an official with a financial history in exactly this kind of litigation.

The fix is not complicated. Publish the code, the payment rate, the documentation standard and the audit plan. Put the clinic’s research protocol in front of independent reviewers. Let the HHS inspector general look at the whole pipeline before money moves. If the initiative is sound science, as Dr. Salmon insists it must be, none of that should be a burden.

If it cannot survive being described in public, the reason for the secrecy is clear.
