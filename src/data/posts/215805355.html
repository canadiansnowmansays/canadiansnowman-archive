Stephen Gordon Sep 19, 2026

There is a sentence buried in the middle of a sixty-eight-page ruling, issued in May by a federal judge appointed by Donald Trump, that deserves to be read on its own before anything else in this story is explained. Ruling that the Justice Department could keep hundreds of boxes of 2020 election ballots it had seized from Fulton County, Georgia, U.S. District Judge J.P. Boulee wrote: “The seizure in this case was certainly not perfect.” He went on to call parts of the government’s case “misleading” and “troubling.” And then he ruled for the government anyway.

That sentence is the whole story of this series in miniature, and it is worth sitting with before moving on to the details, because the details will make it tempting to treat this as a Georgia story, a 2020 story, a story about one county’s grievance with one federal raid. It is not. It is the same architecture this series has spent the past week describing in the mail-ballot fight, wearing a different costume: a federal action that does not need to be right, does not need to produce evidence, and does not need to end, in order to accomplish exactly what it was built to accomplish.

What Actually Happened

On January 28, 2026, FBI agents executed a search warrant at Fulton County’s Election Hub and Operation Center in Union City, just outside Atlanta. They left with somewhere between 650 and 700 boxes of material: physical ballots from the 2020 presidential election, tabulator tapes, ballot images, signature envelopes, and voter rolls. Three trucks were required to carry it away. Robb Pitts, the chair of the Fulton County Board of Commissioners, said at the time that he could no longer assure the public the county’s ballots were secure, because he did not know where they had been taken.

The warrant traced back to a referral from Kurt Olsen, a 2020 election denier serving in the White House, who made it formally on the morning of January 5th. By the Justice Department’s own later-disclosed timeline, the pace after that was unusually fast: an “assessment” opened the next day, a request to escalate to a full investigation six days after that, approval from the special agent in charge of the FBI’s Atlanta field office on January 14th. Three weeks later, agents were loading boxes onto trucks. Fulton County’s lawyers would later describe this sequence in court as evidence that the criminal warrant was a workaround — a faster route to records the administration had already been trying, and failing, to obtain for over a year through a slower civil lawsuit.

What the affidavit actually alleged, once a judge unsealed it in February, turned out to be considerably thinner than the raid itself suggested. NPR’s review of the document found it focused on five claimed “deficiencies” in Fulton’s 2020 count: missing ballot images, ballots scanned more than once, inconsistent totals during a hand-count audit, ballots that may have been improperly added to the tally, and shifting numbers during a machine recount. Of those five, state investigators — the people whose findings the FBI’s own affidavit relied on, without ever contacting them for the fuller record — had already found only one “partially substantiated”: that some ballots were scanned twice during a recount Trump’s own campaign had requested. Even that finding came with a conclusion the affidavit omitted: it did not affect the accuracy of the final count, which had by then been confirmed three separate times, including by a risk-limiting audit and a full recount.

This is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to lose in the procedural back-and-forth that followed: the FBI seized the physical evidence of an election that had already been checked, by hand, three times, on the strength of an affidavit that omitted the state’s own finding that the one substantiated irregularity didn’t change anything.

A Ruling That Convicts the Case While Denying the Remedy

Fulton County sued for the ballots’ return, arguing the seizure was improper and unconstitutional. At a March hearing, election security expert Ryan Macias testified that the affidavit was “riddled with inaccuracies” and showed no evidence of intentional wrongdoing — testimony the judge allowed only after what the county’s commissioners called a fight to get it on the record at all. Two months later, Boulee ruled against Fulton anyway, finding there had been at least some basis for the investigation independent of the earlier civil case, even as he catalogued the problems with how it had been carried out. He cited the Eleventh Circuit’s ruling on the Mar-a-Lago search — the one that reversed the appointment of a special master reviewing documents seized from Trump’s own residence — for the proposition that courts must apply the same legal standard “without regard to numbers, wealth, or rank.” It is a notable irony that the precedent protecting a president’s seized documents from special scrutiny was cited to justify withholding the same courtesy from a county’s seized ballots.

Fulton County Commissioner Dana Barrett put the stakes plainly afterward: “We have to stake our claim about the 2020 ballots because we want to be able to stake our claim about the 2026 ballots and the 2028 ballots.” The county said it would appeal to the Eleventh Circuit. As of this writing, that appeal remains unresolved, and in July — two months after the ruling, and with no charges filed and no findings announced — the FBI reportedly redirected hundreds of additional analysts to keep examining the records it already had permission to keep.

Nine months. Three confirmed counts before the raid ever happened. One partially substantiated claim that didn’t change the outcome. Zero charges. And an investigation that, as far as the public record shows, has no scheduled end.

The Part That Isn’t About Georgia

Read in isolation, Fulton County is a story about one raid and one ruling. Read alongside the reporting on the broader federal effort to reach into state election administration — the reporting that arrived, fittingly, in the same news cycle as Monday’s mail-ballot ruling — it is something else: one node in a strategy running simultaneously in dozens of states.

The Justice Department has sued thirty states for unredacted voter lists, including driver’s license numbers and partial Social Security numbers. It has lost twenty-three of those suits outright, with decisions still pending in the rest — a loss rate that would, in almost any other context, be read as a signal to stop. Instead, in July, the homeland security secretary threatened election officials with prison time if they did not comply with the administration’s demands. The Justice Department has explored bringing criminal charges against state and local officials over computer-system security. FEMA, which sits under the Homeland Security Department, has told states they risk losing tens of millions of dollars in terrorism-prevention funding if they don’t change how they run elections. And a survey from the Associated Press, NORC, and USAFacts found that public trust in government certification of election results has fallen from 40 percent in 2024 to 34 percent now — a six-point drop in two years, tracking almost exactly the period this campaign has been running.

Benjamin Hovland, who was removed from the independent Election Assistance Commission by this administration, put it without much room for ambiguity: “What you’re seeing is using the apparatus of government, using federal agencies, using taxpayer dollars, to push that narrative that only undermines confidence in our democracy.” Kimball Brace, who runs Election Data Services and works with local governments on election preparedness, was blunter still about what Monday’s Supreme Court loss on mail ballots would or wouldn’t change: “I don’t anticipate people on the Republican side not wanting to continue and push their side of the story as much as possible.”

The Friction Without a Clock

Part XLIX of this series described what happened when the Supreme Court finally ruled on the mail-ballot plan: a clean 7-2 loss, an administration appointee in the majority, a concurrence built explicitly on the calendar. That fight had to end, because ballots had to go out and an election had a fixed date. The friction it generated was real, and it did its damage before the ruling arrived — but the ruling arrived, and it was decisive, and it gave officials in Washington State and Nevada and Arizona something to point to when they told voters the rules hadn’t changed after all.

Fulton County is the same strategy with the clock removed. There is no ballot deadline forcing an FBI investigation to conclude. There is no Election Day requiring the Justice Department to file charges or drop the case. An investigation, unlike a regulation, does not need a court to bless its implementation before it can start working on the public’s confidence — it only needs to exist, publicly, indefinitely, attached to the word “fraud” and a pile of ballots nobody can see. Boulee’s ruling gave Fulton County a moral victory in the text and a practical defeat in the outcome: the government’s conduct was “not perfect,” parts of its case were “misleading,” and it gets to keep the ballots regardless. There is no equivalent, in this story, of Monday night’s unsigned order. There may never be one. An appeal can be filed and take years. Analysts can be reassigned in the summer and reassigned again next spring. The investigation can simply continue, at zero political cost to the people running it and mounting cost to the county trying to end it, for as long as it is useful to have Fulton County’s name attached to the word “investigation” rather than the word “confirmed.”

That is the asymmetry worth naming plainly. A rule needs to survive a court to take effect. An investigation needs only to survive scrutiny long enough to avoid being closed, and closing it carries a political cost — an admission that the referral was baseless — that keeping it open does not. So it stays open. Nine months in, that is precisely what it has done: no evidence sufficient to charge anyone, no finding sufficient to overturn anything, and no requirement whatsoever that it produce either before the next election cycle it is quietly shading.

The mail-ballot fight taught election officials how to answer a voter who asks whether their ballot will count: point to the ruling, point to the date, point to the fact that the fight is over. Fulton County does not offer anyone that sentence. Nine months after 650 boxes of already-triple-counted ballots left a warehouse near Atlanta in the back of three trucks, the honest answer to “did they find anything” is still: they haven’t said, and they don’t have to.
