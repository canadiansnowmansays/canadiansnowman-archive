Stephen Gordon September 1, 2026

Part XLIII closed on a prediction. The next entry in this series, it said, would likely concern a second Massachusetts injunction and whatever the Postal Service did, or claimed it could not yet do, in the weeks before ballots went out. That entry arrived faster than the sentence anticipated. On Thursday, August 27 — three days after the Supreme Court let the mail-voting order proceed and one day before this series went to print — a federal judge temporarily blocked the Postal Service’s newly finalized rule implementing it. The order that Solicitor General D. John Sauer told the Court “does not require the states to do (or refrain from doing) anything at all” has, in the span of a single week, become a document specific enough to draw its own injunction.

That specificity is the story. Everything this series has tracked since Part XLII depended on an asymmetry between an order vague enough to survive judicial review and urgent enough to demand emergency relief. As Reid J. Epstein reported for The New York Times, the Supreme Court’s August 24 ruling rested on the finding that the underlying lawsuit was premature because the Postal Service had not yet finalized the rule the order called for. That is a narrower and, in its way, more damning description than the one this series offered a week ago. It is not merely that the states could not yet show concrete harm from an abstract policy. It is that the policy, as of August 24, did not yet formally exist. The government’s inert-order argument was not cynical framing layered over a real rule — for one week, it was structurally true. The rule genuinely was not finished. The Court ruled on a document that had not yet been written.

It has been written now, and it is not abstract. The finalized rule requires states to upload voter lists to a federal mail-ballot portal that is not yet online — an infrastructure dependency built into a policy taking effect in weeks. It mandates new bar codes on ballot envelopes, which in practice forces every piece of election mail through major sorting hubs rather than the direct local routing many small counties currently use, since only the larger facilities can read the new codes. And its most severe provision has no ambiguity left in it at all: any state that declines to hand over voter data to the federal government loses postal service for its election mail entirely. Not degraded service. Not additional scrutiny. None. The order’s authors spent July insisting to the Supreme Court that their creation compelled nothing. The creation, finished, compels a state to choose between surrendering voter rolls to Washington and losing the ability to mail ballots at all.

This is the obfuscation phase of the TACO pattern arriving at its logical endpoint. Threaten the states with sweeping federal control over their elections; accommodate nothing when courts intervene; capitulate on no substantive point; and when the argument on the merits looks weak, retreat into procedural fog. What Part XLIII called fog was, this week, revealed to have a closing date. Fog dissipates the moment the thing it was concealing gets built. The administration’s shield throughout this litigation has been novelty itself — an order too new to have caused documented harm is an order too new to enjoin. That shield has an expiration built into it by definition: novelty is not a condition a policy can maintain once it starts operating in the world. The Postal Service’s own decision to finalize the rule surrendered the argument that won the case five days earlier.

The numbers behind this fight are no longer speculative either. Mail voting splits along partisan lines more sharply than this series has previously had cause to quantify. Nationwide estimates place the gap at roughly one in three Democratic voters casting ballots by mail in 2024 against about one in four Republicans. Michigan data from MIT’s Election Data and Science Lab shows that gap widening under pressure: in the 2022 midterms, 57 percent of Democratic ballots arrived by mail against 37 percent of Republican ballots: by 2024, with Mr. Trump on the ticket and campaigning against mail voting, the split had grown to 60 percent versus 34 percent. A rule that disables mail voting in noncompliant states, or slows it everywhere through new bar-code bottlenecks, is not a neutral administrative burden distributed evenly across the electorate. It is a policy whose costs a documented partisan gap assigns disproportionately before a single ballot is rejected.

Michigan is the state where this series’ abstractions become a Senate race. Nearly two million Michiganders sit on the state’s permanent mail-voting list, and the state is hosting one of the most competitive Senate contests in the country alongside open-seat statewide races and four battleground House seats. The same asymmetry that shows up in the national numbers shows up sharper here: a former state Democratic Party chairman argued that anything obstructing mail access compounds Michigan Republicans’ argument that elections themselves are rigged, while the state Republican Party’s chairman, when asked about adjusting turnout strategy around a possible rule change, said he was aware of no such discussions and volunteered his own preference for in-person Election Day voting. Neither man was describing a hypothetical. North Carolina’s ballots begin mailing next week, ahead of a Senate race that could itself decide chamber control, under a rule whose final legal status nobody — including, on the Supreme Court’s own telling one week ago, the Supreme Court — can yet certify.

The disparity is not only partisan; it is geographic in ways that compound the partisan effect rather than offsetting it. A member of the Wisconsin Elections Commission argued that disrupting mail voting would strike the state’s rural communities far harder than its cities, since urban election offices can extend in-person early-voting hours to absorb a drop in mail ballots while townships with a handful of staff, some running elections out of clerks’ own homes, cannot. That distinction matters for a rule whose central enforcement mechanism is an all-or-nothing cutoff of postal election-mail service to noncompliant states: a state that refuses to comply does not lose mail voting evenly across its map. It loses it first and worst in the places least equipped to build an alternative on short notice, which in most of the plaintiff states are also the places furthest from the county-level election infrastructure of a Detroit, a Phoenix, or a Madison. The rule’s finalized text treats state compliance as a single binary switch. Its effects on the ground would not be binary at all.

That geographic vulnerability is no longer theoretical. As Adam Sella and Nick Corasaniti reported for The New York Times on August 31, mail-ballot rejection rates for late arrival have already climbed across a dozen states studied for this year’s primaries and last year’s general elections — independent of any rule still working its way through the courts. Wisconsin’s spring primary saw roughly 5,000 ballots tossed for lateness, 78 percent of all rejected ballots that cycle, up from about 2,300, or 47 percent, in the 2023 spring election. Michigan’s primary this month produced more than 10,000 late rejections, nearly double the 2024 figure. Washington’s rose from roughly 16,000 to more than 20,000 over the same period. None of these numbers required a voter-data portal or a new bar code to materialize. They are the byproduct of a Postal Service that extended its first-class delivery standard from three days to five and began postmarking mail at regional distribution centers rather than at the point of collection — changes made in the ordinary course of a decade-long cost-cutting plan, years before this administration found a use for the same infrastructure that suits its purposes rather better.

The clerks living with these numbers are not waiting on the Supreme Court to tell them what to do about it. Dane County’s clerk described several hundred ballots arriving a day late after Wisconsin’s April state Supreme Court election as something he had rarely seen before; a survey by the state’s own elections commission found two-thirds of clerks reporting delivery problems in that same contest. Colorado’s Democratic secretary of state has expanded the state’s drop-box network by 78 percent since 2018 explicitly to route voters around the mail rather than depend on it. Utah’s Republican election director, running a state built almost entirely on mail voting, put the shift in official advice plainly: clerks who once told voters simply to beat the deadline now tell them to mail a week early, and increasingly, to skip the mail altogether and use a drop box instead. That is the rule’s core proposition — federal leverage over whose ballots the Postal Service will carry — already functioning as policy in miniature, achieved without anyone having to sign an order at all.

What makes this week’s reporting harder to file under partisan complaint alone is where some of the sharpest skepticism originates. Arizona’s secretary of state, a Democrat administering a state where roughly eighty percent of voters cast mail ballots, predicted that restricting the practice would eliminate his state’s battleground status entirely and provoke a backlash from Republican retirees who have voted by mail for decades — a prediction the state’s own partisan mail-voting data, showing 62 percent of Arizona Republicans and 75 percent of Democrats voting by mail in 2024, does nothing to contradict. But the more useful voice for this series’ purposes belongs to a Republican elections supervisor in a Florida county Mr. Trump carried with 62 percent of the vote, who compared altering election procedure on short notice to changing the course of an aircraft carrier and called imposing that responsibility on the Postal Service, given its record, unwise at best. That is not a partisan objecting to a rule’s effects. That is a Trump-county Republican objecting to its execution — the same cross-partisan crack in the administration’s coalition that Part XLIII found in a single Trump-appointed appellate judge splitting his vote on the eligibility-list provision. It appears again here, in a different office, at a different level of government, making a narrower but structurally identical point: speed and constitutional novelty do not coexist with functioning election administration, regardless of who wins the underlying argument about federal authority.

There is an irony sitting undisturbed at the center of all of it. The president who signed the order, and whose Postal Service finalized the rule now sitting under a fresh injunction, voted by mail himself in Florida’s primary this month. This series has resisted, in earlier entries, the temptation to lean on a single ironic detail as though it settled an argument — it does not, and Mr. Trump’s personal voting method proves nothing about the rule’s constitutionality one way or the other. But it is difficult to read past it without noting what it confirms: the objection being litigated here was never that mail voting is inherently unreliable. Nobody advancing this rule believes that, including its author. The objection is to who uses it, in what numbers, and in which states — a distinction the rule’s central mechanism, cutting off service entirely to noncompliant states rather than reforming mail voting nationally, makes almost too plainly.

None of this resolves the underlying question this series has tracked since July: whether the emergency docket’s tolerance for procedural fog outlasts a fog that has, for one week, definitionally cleared. The finalized rule is currently blocked by a federal court, which means the administration is back where the order itself started five weeks ago — in front of a judge, arguing that an emergency stay is warranted, except this time without the option of calling the underlying policy too abstract to review. The Supreme Court will likely be asked again, and soon, to rule on the plan’s constitutionality rather than its ripeness. That is a different case than the one the justices decided on August 24, whatever its surface resemblance, because it asks the harder question the majority explicitly declined to reach a week ago: not whether the states can show injury from something not yet built, but whether a president may direct federal agencies to compile citizenship lists and hand the Postal Service the power to determine, state by state, whose ballots move through the mail at all.

There is a version of this argument that treats the finalization of the rule as a technicality — the same policy, merely typed up in full rather than left as a presidential directive. That version understates what changed. A standing argument built on speculative future harm and a standing argument built on a document’s literal nonexistence are not the same claim wearing different words; the first survives indefinitely as long as courts remain willing to call harm too distant to act on, while the second cannot survive its own success, because the moment the government finishes the document it was accused of not having finished, the argument that won the case disappears with it. The administration’s lawyers understood this well enough in July, which is presumably why the Postal Service did not rush to finalize anything until after the Supreme Court had already ruled. Whether that timing was coincidence or strategy, the effect is the same either way: the government got its five days of operating room from an emergency stay built on a document it then immediately completed, at which point the states secured, within seventy-two hours, exactly the kind of concrete-harm injunction the Court had just told them they could not yet obtain. The shield worked for one week because the rule did not exist. It has not worked since.

Nearly two dozen Democratic-led states and the District of Columbia are refusing to comply regardless of how that question is answered, which introduces a second calendar problem layered on top of the first: even a rule upheld in full requires the cooperation of state election officials who have already said they will not provide it. An order can survive the Supreme Court and still fail to survive first contact with a county clerk in Wisconsin running an election out of her home. This series has said before that litigation outlasting an election is not litigation that protects it. It is now worth adding the reverse: a rule outlasting the litigation against it is not, by itself, a rule anyone has agreed to follow. The next entry in this series will likely concern which of those two failures arrives first — the courts, or the compliance the rule assumes it will get.
