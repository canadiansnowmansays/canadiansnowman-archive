Canadian artillery at Vimy Ridge, April 1917 — the meticulously planned battle that became a cornerstone of Canadian national identity.

Canada entered the twentieth century as a country that had been assembled. It emerged from the World Wars as a country that had been forged.

There is a difference, and it matters. An assembled country is a political arrangement — provinces stitched together by railway lines and federal bargains, held in place by institutional machinery and the absence of anything better. A forged country is something harder and more durable — a nation whose identity has been tempered by shared sacrifice, whose character has been defined by what its people have done together under conditions that tested everything they thought they knew about themselves.

Canada in 1914 was a dominion of roughly eight million people. It had no independent foreign policy. It had no army in any serious sense — a small permanent force and a larger militia, neither of which had seen significant combat since the Boer War. When Britain declared war on Germany in August 1914, Canada was automatically at war — not by choice, but by the constitutional fact that the British Empire’s foreign policy was made in London and applied to every dominion and colony without consultation.

What happened next was, by any measure, extraordinary.

Over the course of the next four years, more than 620,000 Canadians enlisted. For a country of eight million, this was a rate of mobilization that strained every institution and every family in the country. More than 66,000 of them would not come home. Another 172,000 were wounded. The casualty rate, proportional to population, was among the highest of any allied nation.

These are numbers. Behind the numbers are the places — the names that Canadians carry the way Americans carry Gettysburg and Normandy, the names that transformed a political arrangement into a country with a story it could tell about itself.

Canadians at the Second Battle of Ypres, 1915 — holding the line against the first major poison gas attack of the war.

Ypres. April 1915. The Second Battle of Ypres was the first time poison gas was used on the Western Front, and the Canadian 1st Division was in the line when it happened. Chlorine gas rolled across the trenches. The French colonial troops on the Canadians’ left flank broke and ran — not out of cowardice, but because there was no doctrine for fighting in a cloud of poison and no equipment to survive it. A four-mile gap opened in the allied line.

The Canadians held. Improvising gas masks from urine-soaked cloths — a desperate expedient based on the field knowledge that the ammonia in urine could partially neutralize chlorine — they stood their ground and counterattacked. Over four days, they suffered 6,000 casualties and prevented a German breakthrough that could have altered the course of the war.

Ypres was the baptism. But it was Vimy Ridge that became the defining moment.

April 9 through 12, 1917. The ridge — a heavily fortified German position in northern France, seven kilometres long and rising sixty metres above the surrounding terrain — had been attacked repeatedly by French and British forces at enormous cost. The French alone had suffered over 150,000 casualties in their attempts to take and hold the position. The British had tried and failed.

The four divisions of the Canadian Corps, fighting together for the first time under a unified command structure, were given the assignment.

What followed was one of the most meticulously planned and brilliantly executed operations of the entire war. Lieutenant-General Sir Julian Byng commanded the Corps, with Brigadier-General Arthur Currie — who would succeed Byng and become the greatest Canadian military commander of the war — playing a central role in the planning.

The preparation was exhaustive. Scale models of the ridge were built behind the lines, and every battalion rehearsed the assault on them repeatedly. For the first time in the war, maps were distributed not just to officers but to non-commissioned officers and even to individual soldiers. Every man was briefed on the overall plan and his specific role within it. If officers fell, the men behind them would know what to do and where to go. This was a radical innovation — most armies still operated on the principle that enlisted men should follow orders without understanding the larger picture. The Canadians inverted this, betting that soldiers who understood the objective would fight more effectively than soldiers who were merely obedient.

The artillery preparation was equally meticulous. More than a million shells were fired in the weeks before the assault — a bombardment so sustained and so precisely targeted that it destroyed most of the German wire and many of the forward positions. The infantry attack was preceded by a creeping barrage — a wall of shellfire that moved forward at a predetermined rate, with the infantry advancing immediately behind it, arriving at the German positions moments after the barrage lifted.

On April 9 — Easter Monday — the assault began in a driving sleet storm. By the end of the first day, most of the ridge was in Canadian hands. The final objective, Hill 145, fell on April 12.

The cost was 3,598 dead and 7,004 wounded in four days. These are numbers that can be written in a sentence and that represent a reality that no sentence can convey.

The Vimy Memorial — erected on the ridge where Canada earned its reputation as a fighting nation.

Vimy Ridge entered Canadian mythology as the battle where Canada became a nation. Brigadier General Alexander Ross, who fought there, later wrote that “in those few minutes I witnessed the birth of a nation.” The statement has been repeated so often that it has acquired the quality of cliché, but clichés become clichés because they capture something that people recognize as true.

What was true about Vimy was not that Canada was literally born there — the country was fifty years old, and the national identity had roots going back to 1812 and beyond. What was true was that Vimy was the first time Canadians, from every province and both linguistic communities, fought together in a single operation under Canadian command and accomplished something that larger and more established armies had not.

The psychological impact was profound. A country that had entered the war as an appendage of the British Empire — with no independent voice in the councils that decided when and where its soldiers would fight — emerged from Vimy with the conviction that it had earned the right to speak for itself. That conviction would eventually find institutional expression in the Statute of Westminster in 1931, which granted the dominions full legislative independence. But the conviction itself was forged on the ridge, in the sleet and the mud and the meticulous planning that proved Canada could do what others could not.

The Second World War confirmed and extended what the First had begun.

Canada entered the war in September 1939 — this time by its own parliamentary vote, one week after Britain, a small but symbolically significant assertion of independent sovereignty. A nation of eleven million mobilized over a million men and women for military service. The Royal Canadian Navy grew from a handful of ships to the third-largest navy among the Western allies. The Royal Canadian Air Force provided a quarter of all aircrew in the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan. Canadian industry produced trucks, aircraft, ships, and ammunition in quantities that belied the country’s size.

And then there was Dieppe. August 19, 1942. The raid on the French port — a reconnaissance in force that was supposed to test German coastal defences — was a catastrophe. Of the roughly 5,000 Canadians who participated, over 900 were killed and nearly 2,000 were captured. The operation was poorly planned, inadequately supported, and launched against defences far stronger than intelligence had suggested. The beaches were killing grounds.

Dieppe was the national trauma. It haunted the Canadian military establishment and the country for years. But it also, in the cruel arithmetic of war, provided lessons that were applied two years later when the stakes were infinitely higher.

Canadian troops landing on Juno Beach, D-Day, June 6, 1944 — pushing farther inland than any other Allied force on the first day.

June 6, 1944. D-Day. The Canadian 3rd Infantry Division and the 2nd Armoured Brigade were assigned Juno Beach — one of the five landing beaches on the coast of Normandy. Juno was heavily defended. Offshore reefs delayed the landing craft, extending the time the soldiers were exposed to enemy fire during their approach. The first waves took devastating casualties.

The Canadians took the beach. They pushed farther inland on D-Day than any other Allied force — penetrating deeper into the French countryside than the British on their flanks or the Americans at Omaha and Utah. The cost was 359 killed and 715 wounded on the first day alone. By the end of the Normandy campaign, Canadian casualties would exceed 18,000.

The liberation of the Netherlands that followed — the Canadian campaign through the Scheldt Estuary and across the Dutch lowlands in the autumn and winter of 1944-45 — produced one of the most genuine and enduring international friendships any country enjoys. The Dutch have never forgotten. Every year, Canada and the Netherlands exchange tokens of a gratitude that has now survived for more than eighty years. The tulips that bloom on Parliament Hill each spring are descended from bulbs sent by the Dutch royal family in thanks for the Canadian liberation and for Canada’s sheltering of Princess Juliana and her daughters during the war.

But the wars also exposed the deepest fault line in the Canadian experiment — the line that runs between English and French, the line that the Quebec Act of 1774 was supposed to manage and that every subsequent generation has struggled to maintain.

Conscription nearly broke the country. Twice.

In 1917, Prime Minister Robert Borden imposed conscription to sustain the Canadian Expeditionary Force, which had suffered catastrophic casualties on the Western Front. English Canada, broadly, supported the measure as a patriotic necessity. French Canada exploded. Quebec had never shared English Canada’s emotional attachment to the British Empire, and the idea of compelling French-Canadian men to fight and die in a European war that was perceived — not without reason — as an essentially British enterprise was received as an act of colonial coercion.

Riots erupted in Quebec City on the Easter weekend of 1918. Federal troops were deployed. Five civilians were killed. The country fractured along linguistic lines in a crisis that poisoned English-French relations for a generation and confirmed the pattern that has repeated itself at every moment of national stress: the unity that exists in peacetime dissolves the moment the federal government asks French Canada to sacrifice for a cause that French Canada does not consider its own.

In 1944, the crisis repeated itself — this time managed, with exquisite political skill, by Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King, the grandson of the rebel William Lyon Mackenzie, who navigated the conscription question with the formula “conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription.” King held off as long as humanly possible, sending only volunteers overseas, until the casualty rates in northwestern Europe made reinforcement by conscripts unavoidable. The crisis was contained — barely — but the underlying tension was identical to 1917: English Canada regarded military service as a national obligation; French Canada regarded forced conscription as an act of imperial subjugation dressed in democratic clothing.

Both conscription crises confirmed the same lesson: the Canadian experiment — the coexistence of English and French within a single state — was not self-sustaining. It required constant, active, politically demanding management. The country could not simply assume that a shared flag and a shared parliament would produce a shared sense of obligation. Every generation would have to renegotiate the terms, and some negotiations would come closer to failure than others.

Canada emerged from the Second World War as a middle power with a disproportionate sense of its own responsibility. It had earned that sense — in the mud of Passchendaele, on the ridge at Vimy, on the beaches of Dieppe and Juno, in the flooded fields of the Netherlands. A country of eleven million had produced the fourth-largest air force and the third-largest navy among the Western allies. Its industrial capacity had helped arm Britain during the darkest years. Its forces had fought in Italy, in France, in the Netherlands, in the North Atlantic.

And now, standing in the rubble of the old world order, Canada would help build the new one. Not as a great power — Canada has never been that and has never pretended to be. But as something that the postwar world needed and that Canada was uniquely positioned to provide: an honest broker. A country with enough credibility to mediate, enough capacity to contribute, and enough modesty to build institutions without insisting on dominating them.

That story — the story of the quiet builders — is the next chapter.
