There is a specific kind of political claim that does not ask you to weigh two interpretations of the same evidence. It asks you to distrust your own arithmetic. “The tax cut will pay for itself” is the first kind of claim — arguable, contestable, the ordinary stuff of democratic disagreement. “You are not actually paying more for groceries” is the second kind. It is not a policy argument. It is an instruction to disbelieve a receipt sitting in your own hand.

For the better part of a year, the president of the United States has been making the second kind of claim, with increasing confidence, about the cost of living in America. This instalment of The Household Ledger traces how that claim has evolved, sets it against the numbers his own administration is generating, and asks what it costs a governing party to keep making it six weeks before an election in which, by every major pollster’s account, this is the one issue voters say they care about most.

The arc of the denial

The pattern did not begin with a single memorable line. It built, over roughly a year, through three distinct phases, each slightly bolder than the last.

The first phase was outright dismissal. As early as last December, with public concern about affordability already visible in off-year election results, the White House’s response was to call the entire premise a hoax. Trump told supporters that cost-of-living worries were a “hoax” manufactured by his opponents, even as he simultaneously travelled the country insisting he was working to end an “inflation crisis” he blamed on his predecessor — holding, in effect, two contradictory positions in the same breath: there is no problem, and also, I inherited this problem, and also, I am fixing it.

The second phase sharpened the dismissal into an accusation. Asked directly by a Fox News interviewer earlier this year whether affordability concerns reflected a genuine hardship or merely a “voter perception issue,” Trump did not answer the question as posed. He called it “a con job by the Democrats.” Pressed further on whether Americans were, in fact, anxious about the economy, he went further still: “I don’t know that they are saying that. I think polls are fake. We have the greatest economy we’ve ever had.” This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it is not a claim about the economy at all — it is a claim about the instruments used to measure public opinion. The polling itself became suspect, which conveniently removed the need to engage with what the polling found. At the time, a contemporaneous Washington Post/ABC News/Ipsos survey found that a majority of Americans said they were spending more on groceries and utility bills than the year before — the receipts, in other words, disagreeing with the president in real time.

The third phase, delivered in February at a steel fabricator in Rome, Georgia, was the boldest: not denial that the problem existed, but a claim of victory over it. "And then I have to listen to the fake news, talking about affordability, affordability," Trump told the crowd. "You notice, what word have you not heard over the last two weeks? Affordability. Because I've won. I've won affordability." In the same remarks, he cited nine trillion dollars in retirement-account gains as evidence of broad-based prosperity, and, when reminded that he had once called the inflation he inherited "the worst in history," corrected himself only to note that it was merely the worst in forty-eight years — a correction that does not so much rebut the original claim as restate it.

What the receipts actually say

None of the three phases survives contact with the data his own government continued to publish throughout. Grocery and utility costs rose through the period in question, not fell, according to independent surveys conducted at the same time as the “hoax” and “con job” remarks. Multiple studies of the tariff regime specifically — a subject this series will return to in the next instalment — found that pass-through to consumer prices was close to complete: one Federal Reserve analysis estimated that tariffs implemented through November 2025 had raised core goods prices by roughly 3.1 percent by February 2026, accounting for essentially all of the excess inflation in that category relative to pre-pandemic norms, and boosting overall core inflation by nearly a full percentage point. A separate National Bureau of Economic Research paper examining tariff incidence found pass-through to import prices running close to one hundred percent — meaning, in plain terms, that when a tariff rate goes up, very little of that cost is absorbed by exporters or importers, and almost all of it eventually reaches a shelf price. A Joint Economic Committee estimate put the average household’s cumulative tariff-related cost at roughly $1,745 between February 2025 and January 2026 alone, with a further average increase of about $700 projected for 2026. These are not partisan estimates manufactured to embarrass an administration. They are the ordinary output of economists and government bodies doing the ordinary work of measuring prices.

It is also worth being precise about what the president’s own account leaves out, because the “I’ve won affordability” framing rests heavily on one genuinely true fact wrapped around several others that are not: retirement accounts and household net worth, buoyed by a strong stock market, have indeed reached record levels for those who hold significant investments. But that is a story about asset prices, which disproportionately benefit households with substantial savings and retirement accounts already built up. It says nothing about the cost of filling a grocery cart, financing a car, or renting an apartment — the everyday, non-optional spending that dominates a paycheck-to-paycheck household’s monthly ledger and barely registers for a household living comfortably off appreciating assets. Citing the first to argue away the second is not a factual error so much as a category confusion, deployed, it must be said, with some rhetorical skill.

Set against that backdrop, the president’s own approval rating on economic management has moved in exactly the direction you would expect if the “I’ve won affordability” framing were not landing. By early August, roughly three in ten Americans approved of his handling of the economy — a mark lower than the floor he ever touched during his first term, when the same figure never fell under forty percent. Gallup’s tracking through the summer showed a similar pattern, in the high thirties rather than anywhere close to a majority. A Reuters/Ipsos poll in mid-August found sixty-four percent disapproving of his overall job performance, a number that had been sliding steadily since the spring. None of this is the profile of a president who has, in any measurable sense, “won.”

Why the country is not simply arguing past itself

It would be a mistake to read this purely as a story about one man’s rhetorical habits, because the more interesting fact is what is happening among voters while he talks past them. This is not a country where one side has already made up its mind about who is right on the economy and the argument is purely performative. Pew Research’s polling through the summer found Americans almost evenly split, thirty-seven to thirty-six, over which party has the better approach to economic policy — a dead heat on the exact question the president is trying to close by declaring victory rather than by winning the argument. A Washington Post/Ipsos survey found that a majority of registered voters, fifty-four percent, named the economy and high prices as the single most important factor in how they intend to vote this November, comfortably ahead of every other issue tested, including immigration.

That combination — an issue that dominates voters’ thinking to a degree few other issues approach, and a party split roughly down the middle on who has the answer — is precisely the environment in which a sustained, escalating pattern of denial from the top becomes something more than a rhetorical curiosity. It becomes a test of whether a governing party can talk its way out of an economic argument it appears to be losing on the merits, simply by insisting the argument is already over. The early evidence, in the form of approval numbers that have continued falling through each phase of the denial rather than stabilizing, suggests that test is not going well.

There is also a straightforward political logic that the “I’ve won affordability” strategy seems to misjudge. A politician who argues a genuinely contested point — say, whether a specific policy caused a specific price increase — is engaging voters on terrain where reasonable people can disagree, and where persuasion is at least possible. A politician who tells voters the thing they experience directly, every week, at a specific dollar amount, simply is not happening, is not attempting persuasion at all. It is closer to asking for a suspension of the voter’s own senses. Some fraction of any electorate will go along with that kind of request out of partisan loyalty. But the polling above suggests that fraction is considerably smaller than a majority — and shrinking, not growing, the longer the claim is repeated.

What the promises were, against what arrived

The denial pattern is easier to assess against a fixed baseline, and the president supplied one himself, repeatedly, during the campaign that returned him to office. He promised to cut energy prices in half, a specific and checkable claim rather than a vague aspiration. He promised to bring grocery prices down starting on his first day back in the White House. Neither promise resembles the economy this series has documented: energy costs have instead been pushed upward by the unresolved Iran conflict traced in earlier instalments, and grocery prices, examined in detail in Part IV, have continued climbing rather than reversing. A politician is not obligated to keep every campaign promise, and voters generally extend some grace for circumstances beyond anyone’s control. But the “I’ve won affordability” framing does not ask for that grace. It asks voters to believe the promise was already delivered — a considerably harder sell when the specific, numeric version of that promise is sitting right there, unfulfilled, for anyone to check.

It is also worth setting the current period against the one immediately preceding it, since the president’s own “I inherited a disaster” framing depends on a version of the prior economy that the historical record does not fully support. The Biden-era inflation surge of 2021–2022 was real and severe, and this series has never argued otherwise. But it was followed by a period economists have specifically termed “immaculate disinflation” — a sustained fall in inflation back toward target without the recession that classical economic models would have predicted as the necessary cost of taming it. By the final stretch of that administration, multiple international comparisons ranked the American economy as outperforming every other advanced economy on the standard growth and inflation metrics. That is the baseline “worse under Biden” rhetoric is measured against, and it is a considerably stronger baseline than the “shambles” framing typically implies.

What households themselves are reporting

Beyond the approval-rating data already cited, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s own Survey of Consumer Expectations offers a more granular, household-level confirmation of the same gap between rhetoric and lived experience. In recent readings, households reporting that their financial situation has gotten worse over the past year have outnumbered those reporting it has improved by nearly two to one — a finding that comes not from a pollster asking about approval of a politician, but from a survey asking people to describe their own financial circumstances in their own terms. That distinction matters: a household describing its own finances as worse off is not weighing in on a political argument, in the way an approval-rating question implicitly is. It is reporting a fact about its own ledger, the same kind of fact this series has tried to build its case around from the first instalment onward. A gap of nearly two to one, on a question with no obvious partisan framing built into it, is difficult to reconcile with any version of “I’ve won.”

There is a further wrinkle in the current economic data worth naming plainly, because it complicates the picture beyond simple denial versus reality: the gains that have occurred have not been evenly shared. Corporate profits have continued climbing even as household wages have failed to keep pace with prices — the wage story examined in detail in Part VII of this series. Economists increasingly describe the resulting shape as a “K-shaped” economy: one line on the chart, representing asset owners and corporate earnings, continuing upward, while another line, representing wage-dependent households, bends downward or stalls. A K-shaped economy is not simply an economy with winners and losers, which every economy has to some degree. It is one where the aggregate statistics — GDP growth, corporate earnings, stock market performance — can look genuinely healthy even as the specific households this series has spent seven instalments tracking fall further behind, because the aggregate figures are disproportionately weighted toward the line moving upward. It is, in other words, a structural explanation for exactly the disconnect this series has documented from its first instalment: a headline economy that can be defended with real, accurate statistics, existing alongside a household economy that most of the country experiences as declining.

Not entirely unprecedented, but distinct in degree

It would be incomplete, and something less than the evidence-based approach this series aims for, to present presidential insistence that voters are wrong about their own cost of living as an invention unique to this administration. Joe Biden, in the early months of 2024, made a similar trip to a Pennsylvania borough to argue that economic improvements were real and that public perception simply had not caught up to the data — an echo the Associated Press noted explicitly when covering this administration’s own version of the same trip in December 2025. Incumbents of both parties have, at various points, reached for the argument that the public’s lived sense of the economy is somehow miscalibrated relative to the underlying statistics.

What distinguishes the current pattern is less the existence of the claim than its escalation and its specificity. Biden’s version amounted to an argument that things were getting better and people hadn’t noticed yet — a claim about timing and perception, testable and eventually falsifiable by subsequent elections, which is exactly what happened. The pattern traced above moves through something considerably more absolute: from “this is a hoax” to “polls measuring this are fake” to “I have already won this argument, and you no longer hear the word.” Each step forecloses a bit more room for revision. A politician who says “it’s getting better, just wait” has an exit ramp if it doesn’t. A politician who says “I’ve won” and “the word has disappeared” has left himself very little room to later acknowledge that the word, and the problem it names, are both still very much present — which they demonstrably are, according to his own polling.

What comes next in this series

The next instalment turns from the rhetoric of the affordability debate to one of its more concrete and, for readers of this newsletter, geographically close mechanisms: the tariff fight between Canada and the United States, and the very unglamorous way a dispute over steel quotas, softwood lumber classifications, and auto-content rules turns into a more expensive dishwasher, a pricier truck, and a slower home renovation on both sides of the border. The pattern traced here — a real cost, denied at the top, showing up anyway on a receipt — will recur there in a more literal, line-item form.

Because the argument being made in the Rome, Georgia speech, and in the Fox News interview before it, and in the “hoax” framing before that, is ultimately an argument about who gets to define reality: a president declaring an issue closed, or a household holding the receipt that says otherwise. So far, the receipts appear to be winning.
