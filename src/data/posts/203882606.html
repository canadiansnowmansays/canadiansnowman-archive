I should tell you something about myself that I have not previously mentioned in this column.

Every year, in the second week of January, I go south.

I have been doing this for eleven years. I go to a condominium in a community outside Sarasota, Florida, that was purchased in 2014 by myself and my husband Ugyen, who is from Bhutan — which is a small kingdom in the Himalayas about which I will say more shortly — and which we have occupied every January and February with the particular gratitude of Canadians who have survived November and December and require, at that point in the year, two months of weather that does not require a decision about whether to warm the car in advance.

The community is called Pelican Shores. It has a pool, a shuffleboard court, and a residents’ association that meets monthly and whose minutes are, in my assessment, kept with less rigour than Gerald Fitch would consider acceptable but with considerably more warmth. I have friends there. I play cards on Tuesday evenings. I read on the lanai in January light that is exactly what January light should be when it has not come from a sun that is forty-five degrees below the horizon and doing its best.

This is what I do in January. This is what I have done for eleven years.

This year, I am not going.

The decision took longer than it should have.

I am a Canadian citizen. Ugyen became a Canadian citizen this fall, having completed a process that was long and careful and which produced, on the afternoon it concluded, a quiet satisfaction that I have not seen on his face in connection with any other administrative outcome. He is from Bhutan. He holds a Canadian passport. We are, in the relevant legal sense, entirely in order.

And yet.

The United States has, in the past year, implemented a number of policies regarding the entry of foreign nationals that have created, at the Canadian border, a quality of uncertainty that was not previously there. People have been detained. Questions have been asked about social media posts and political views and the nature of a person’s opinions about their host country. The language used at the border has changed. The feeling at the border has changed.

I know people who have gone. I know people who have decided not to go. I know people who have gone and found it exactly as it always was, and I know people who have gone and found it different, and I know people who waited at the crossing for two hours while a guard scrolled through their phones and then waved them through, and who came back and said: it was fine. And I believe them that it was fine. And I also believe that the two hours happened, and that the scrolling happened, and that the question of whether it will continue to be fine is a question I cannot answer in advance.

Ugyen and I talked about it in October. We talked about it in November. We talked about it in December, and by December the talking had reached the point where continuing to talk about it was its own answer, because if we needed this many conversations to decide whether to go somewhere we had gone for eleven years, we had already decided.

We are not going to Pelican Shores this winter.

I told Gerald on a Tuesday morning in the Tim Hortons, at the window table on the right, because Gerald is who I tell things to in Elsinore when I want to tell someone who will note the information and file it appropriately and not offer more response than the situation requires.

He listened. He removed his reading glasses. He put them back on.

He said: “Where are you going instead?”

I said I was considering Bhutan.

Gerald considered this.

He said: “Ugyen’s family is there.”

I said yes.

He said: “And the mountains.”

I said yes.

He nodded. He returned to his coffee. The conversation, in Gerald’s vocabulary, was complete.

Ugyen’s family lives in Thimphu, which is the capital of Bhutan and which is situated in a valley in the eastern Himalayas at an altitude that makes Elsinore’s flat Perth County agricultural landscape feel like a memory from a different geological era. Bhutan has a Festival of its own — the Thimphu Tsechu, held annually, involving masked dances and religious ceremony and the particular combination of colour and seriousness and accumulated tradition that characterises all festivals worth attending. Bhutan measures its national progress partly through Gross National Happiness, which is an economic philosophy that has attracted international attention and which Ugyen’s father, a retired civil servant, describes as “accurate but complicated to explain to economists.”

Bhutan has not, to my knowledge, expressed any interest in annexing Canada.

This is, in January 2027, a more specific distinction than it might previously have seemed.

I mention it because the Moonbeam Chronicle has spent the past year finding, in the small local matters of Elsinore, a mirror of things happening to the south. And I have tried, in those mirrors, to be fair — to note what the mirror shows without distorting it, and to return, always, to Elsinore, which is the ground from which the observation is made.

But the mirror has come closer this year. It is not, in January, a distant reflection. It is the reason I am writing this column from my study on Wellington Street rather than from the lanai at Pelican Shores, and it is the reason Ugyen is on the phone with his mother in Thimphu making arrangements, and it is the reason that a decision which should have been simple has required three months of conversation to make.

I want to be clear about what I am saying and what I am not saying.

I am not saying that Americans are unwelcoming. The people at Pelican Shores are warm and kind and have been my neighbours for eleven years and would, I am confident, be my neighbours again if the circumstances were different. The problem is not the Americans. The problem is the border, and what the border has become, and what it communicates about the relationship between the country on the other side of it and the person standing in front of it.

I am not saying that what is happening in the United States is irreversible. I do not know that. Nobody knows that. The Moonbeam Chronicle has tried, in every Note for the Record, to state what is the case without predicting what will follow, and I am not going to stop doing that now.

What I am saying is that this year, for the first time in eleven years, I looked at the border and the border looked different. And Ugyen and I had three months of conversations we had not had before, and at the end of those conversations we had made a decision, and the decision was Bhutan.

Alistair will have put down the staff. The marquee will have changed. The new artistic director will be in the office on the south side of the theatre, looking at the morning light on the Avon for the first time.

Elsinore will be doing what Elsinore does in January: turning inward, quietly, in the particular Ontario way, conserving and continuing and being exactly what it is while the rest of the world does whatever it is doing.

I will be in Thimphu.

I will read the Beacon on Sunday mornings, because the Beacon is online and Gerald will have filed everything that needs filing and Norman will have published approximately sixty percent of what he knows.

I will write the Moonbeam Chronicle from there, because the Chronicle publishes every Sunday morning, and because the Avon runs through Elsinore in January whether I am watching it or not, and because Gerald has copies of everything, and because something is always, gently, rotten, and the Avon runs through it regardless.

The swans will be wherever swans go in January. They will be back in April. They will use the south bank. The Swan Warden will note it.

I will come back in spring.

Elsinore will be here.

A Note for the Record

The snowbird is a Canadian institution. We have been going south in January for as long as there has been a south to go to and a north cold enough to make it worth the journey. We have rented condominiums in Florida and Arizona and California and we have played shuffleboard and read on lanais and called home to say yes, it’s warm, yes, the orange juice is fresh, yes, we’re fine.

The border has always been there. It has been a border of sufficient familiarity that many Canadians crossed it without thinking about it much, which is a kind of privilege that people who hold other passports have never had and which Canadians have tended to take for granted.

We are not taking it for granted this year.

This is either temporary or it is not. I don’t know which. The Moonbeam Chronicle does not predict. But I will note, for the record, that the decision Ugyen and I made was made by two Canadian citizens with Canadian passports, one of whom was born in Canada and one of whom became a Canadian citizen this fall after a long and careful process, and that the deciding factor was not the distance or the cost or the weather or any of the things that usually go into such a decision. The deciding factor was a quality of uncertainty at the border that was not previously there, and which we were not confident would resolve itself before January.

Bhutan is beautiful. The mountains are extraordinary. The Gross National Happiness index does not capture them, but it is not trying to capture them, and this honesty about what can and cannot be measured is one of the things I have always found admirable about Bhutan.

Ugyen’s mother makes very good dumplings.

The Moonbeam Chronicle will continue from there.

The Avon will still be running in April.
