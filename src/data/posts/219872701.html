By the time this series reaches the present generation, the Churchill name has stopped functioning as a claim to political office almost entirely — Part XIII closed out the last sitting Churchill-adjacent MP’s career in 2019 — and has instead become something closer to a freestanding commercial and cultural asset, detached from any particular office or achievement, available to be drawn on by descendants and strangers alike in whatever form suits them. Anthony Scaramucci’s “The Long Trade”, the essay that prompted this series, priced Churchill’s legacy as a dividend paid out to strangers. This part asks the narrower question of what it still pays the family, and is an attempt to tally what that asset is actually worth today, across four quite different accounts.

Start with Edwina Sandys, Diana Churchill’s elder daughter, born in 1938, who spent her early adulthood as a conventional aristocratic debutante — presented to Queen Elizabeth II, married at twenty-two to Piers Dixon, divorced at thirty-two — before reinventing herself, beginning in 1970, as a visual artist working in sculpture. Her most consequential piece, “Breakthrough,” uses eight actual sections of the Berlin Wall, dismantled after 1989 and installed permanently on the campus of Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri — the exact site where her grandfather delivered the Iron Curtain speech this series covered in Part VIII, the wall’s fall standing as the literal, physical vindication of the argument he’d made there four decades earlier. It is a genuinely well-conceived piece of public art, and it is also, unmistakably, an artist building directly on her grandfather’s specific historical moment for the meaning of her own work — using pieces of the actual curtain he named to complete, in permanent stone and steel, the argument the college is proud to have hosted in the first place. Edwina received an MBE in 1984 for services to British cultural interests, has continued to exhibit internationally, and appeared alongside her cousin Duncan Sandys and the sitting British ambassador at official Churchill commemoration events as recently as the fiftieth anniversary of Winston’s death in 2015 — the family name functioning, in her case, as a durable creative and diplomatic credential rather than any kind of direct inheritance of money or office.

Her younger sister Celia Sandys, born in 1943, has built an even more direct commercial relationship with the family legacy, and it is worth describing plainly rather than delicately, because it is genuinely the clearest example this series has found of the name converted into an occupation in its own right. Celia has authored multiple books specifically about her grandfather — memoirs of her own childhood memories of him interwoven with historical material, with titles built explicitly around the personal-access angle her birth alone provides — and has built a substantial career as a professional keynote speaker, marketed by speaking agencies specifically on the basis of “unparalleled access to intimate knowledge and personal insights into Churchill’s life” that her position as a granddaughter supplies. She contributed to a PBS documentary series, “Chasing Churchill,” built around her own book, and remains active in Churchill-focused historical societies decades into a career that has never required her to hold office, win an election, or produce any achievement independent of the family name itself. This is not offered as criticism — plenty of legitimate historical work has come out of Celia Sandys’s books, and family memoir is a genuine literary form with its own value. But it is worth naming as exactly what this series’ title predicts: an inheritance, converted directly and knowingly into an income stream, by a family member positioned to do so more directly than any other descendant this series has examined.

The name’s value runs in a considerably darker direction on the Marlborough side of the family tree — the line descended not from Winston directly but from the same Churchill-Spencer bloodline via Blenheim Palace, holding the actual dukedom Winston himself was born into but never inherited, since he descended from a younger son. The current holder, Charles James Spencer-Churchill, 12th Duke of Marlborough, born in 1955, inherited the title in 2014 after a family history so troubled that his own father won a court judgment in 1994 specifically to prevent him from ever gaining direct control of the Blenheim estate itself — a legal manoeuvre a father resorts to only when he judges his own son a genuine threat to a three-hundred-year-old inheritance. The Duke served a month in prison in 1995 for forging prescriptions, was sentenced to six months in jail in 2007 following a road-rage attack on another motorist, faced accusations of racist language toward a taxi driver in 2013, and — in reporting current as of this writing — faces criminal charges alleging non-fatal strangulation across incidents between November 2022 and May 2024, charges he has not been convicted of and which remain before the courts. His own father, asked about his son’s record, offered the plainest possible verdict on what four centuries of the same aristocratic inheritance can produce across a large enough sample of descendants: every family has its black sheep, and there is nothing new about that. Blenheim Palace itself, meanwhile, continues operating as a UNESCO World Heritage tourist attraction under a separate heritage foundation rather than the Duke’s personal management — the estate’s actual commercial and cultural value having been institutionally separated, by that 1994 court judgment, from whichever individual happens to hold the hereditary title at any given moment. The palace made international headlines for a rather different reason in 2019, when thieves stole a fully functioning eighteen-karat gold toilet — a satirical artwork titled “America,” valued at roughly £4.75 million, that had been installed at Blenheim as a temporary exhibit — in a brazen five-minute raid before dawn that caused significant flooding damage to the historic building. It took nearly six years for the case to close: members of the gang were convicted and, in June 2025, jailed, but the toilet itself was never recovered, its gold believed to have been broken up and sold off. It is difficult to imagine a more perfectly absurdist coda to this family’s centuries-long relationship with extravagant, precariously managed wealth than a solid-gold commode, on loan as conceptual art, disappearing from the birthplace of Winston Churchill in the middle of the night. It is, in its own way, the starkest illustration this series has found of a family’s public reputation and its living members’ actual conduct pulling in entirely opposite directions.

And then there is the name’s most widely encountered use today, which belongs to no family member at all. Churchill Insurance, a British direct-to-consumer motor and home insurer founded in 1989, adopted a nodding bulldog mascot in 1994 specifically, according to the company’s own account, to distance the brand from any direct association with Winston Churchill even while trading explicitly on his surname — the bulldog serving as a kind of visual firewall, invoking Churchill’s own well-known bulldog imagery and wartime “never surrender” reputation without ever depicting the man himself or requiring the company to secure any family involvement or endorsement. The mascot, voiced across three decades by two different actors and given the catchphrase “Oh, yes!”, has become one of the most recognized advertising figures in British television, now backed by a marketing budget exceeding forty million pounds annually under Direct Line Group’s ownership. It is worth sitting with the peculiar logic of this arrangement for a moment: an insurance company judged Churchill’s surname valuable enough to build an entire consumer brand around, while simultaneously judging the actual man too complicated, too politically contested, or simply too human to put in the advertisements — better represented by a cartoon dog nodding “yes” than by any image of the wartime prime minister whose name the whole enterprise depends on. No Churchill descendant holds any stake in the company or receives any portion of its considerable profits. The name generates value for a business with no bloodline connection to the family whatsoever, while the actual descendants — sculptors, professional speakers, and, in one branch, a man facing criminal charges — manage the name’s more complicated real inheritance largely without it.

There is a broader commercial afterlife worth noting alongside these four individual accounts, because it dwarfs any of them in sheer volume even though no living Churchill descendant holds any direct stake in most of it. Chartwell itself, the Kent estate Winston bought without telling Clementine in 1922 and spent decades struggling to afford, passed to the National Trust in 1966 under arrangements made before his death specifically to keep the house in public hands rather than let it pass down through the family as private property, and it now operates as one of the Trust’s most visited properties, generating revenue for the charitable trust rather than for any Churchill heir. The Churchill War Rooms in London, the underground bunker complex from which he directed much of the war, operates today as a paid museum under the Imperial War Museum’s management. His own wartime speeches — “we shall fight on the beaches,” “their finest hour,” “blood, toil, tears and sweat” — circulate constantly in advertising, political rhetoric, and motivational content entirely detached from any licensing arrangement with his descendants, since the speeches themselves, delivered as parliamentary record, carry none of the copyright protection that might otherwise let the family monetize their reuse. Even his 1953 Nobel Prize in Literature, awarded for his historical and biographical writing rather than any single work, continues to function as a credential invoked constantly in accounts of his genius without generating so much as a footnote of ongoing benefit to anyone bearing his name today. The pattern across all of it is consistent: the institutional and commercial afterlife of “Churchill” as a brand has grown steadily larger and more diffuse with each passing decade, while the family’s own ability to control, direct, or profit from that afterlife has shrunk in almost exact proportion.

There is a specific irony worth closing on here, one that connects directly back to Part I’s opening argument about Leonard Jerome. This series began by establishing that the Churchill fortune’s actual origin was an American Wall Street speculator’s talent for converting personal reputation into recoverable financial value — building and rebuilding fortunes on the strength of his own name and nerve, across a market with essentially no rules governing what that name was worth. A hundred and thirty years later, the family’s most valuable remaining asset appears to be operating on almost exactly the same principle, just distributed across considerably more hands and considerably less risk to any single one of them: a sculptor drawing on the name’s historical resonance, a professional speaker drawing on it for a living wage, a corporate insurer drawing on it for market share while carefully declining to draw on the actual man, and a distant cousin’s criminal record demonstrating, about as starkly as this series’ evidence permits, that whatever the name is worth commercially, it has never once functioned as any kind of moral inheritance at all.

Part XV closes this series with the question it has been building toward since the Introduction: what, when the ledger is finally totalled across sixteen parts and a hundred and seventy years, does this family’s inheritance actually amount to — and was it worth what it cost the people who carried it?
