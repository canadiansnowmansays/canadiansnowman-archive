The Oracle Problem: Who controls what the AI tells voters about candidates and elections?

Near the end of a podcast conversation about the future of artificial intelligence, Chris Hayes said something that stopped the discussion cold.

“Here’s the frontier, man. Here’s the frontier. How are people going to go find out about politics and the candidates? And what answer is that model going to spit out? And who is going to be in the wiring of that model?”

His interviewer, Charlie Warzel of the Atlantic, responded with a joke about Elon Musk and Grok’s Mecha Hitler incident. Hayes acknowledged the joke and then restated the point without humour: “Imagine the power of being able to control what that information was. About who’s running for office, and what they stand for, and who you should vote for. That to me is a frontier that we haven’t breached yet.”

He is right that the frontier has not been breached. He is wrong that it hasn’t been approached. It is being approached now, at speed, with no regulatory framework in sight, six months before the American midterm elections.

This essay is about what happens when AI models replace search engines as the primary portal through which citizens find political information — and who controls that portal when they do.

Here’s the frontier, man…” — Chris Hayes on AI as the new political information portal.

The Replacement

For twenty-five years, Google was the portal. When a voter wanted to know what a candidate stood for, they searched. When they wanted to check a claim made in a debate, they searched. When they wanted to understand a policy, they searched. The results were imperfect, gameable, subject to SEO manipulation, and shaped by an algorithm whose internal logic was opaque to the public. But the results were also plural. A search returned ten links. The voter chose which to click. The information was distributed across sources. The voter did some of the interpretive work.

AI models are replacing that architecture with something structurally different.

When a voter asks ChatGPT or Claude or Gemini or Grok about a candidate, the model does not return ten links. It returns an answer. A single, synthesised, authoritative-sounding answer that integrates information from sources the voter cannot see, weighted by parameters the voter cannot inspect, shaped by training data the voter cannot access, and filtered through alignment decisions the voter did not participate in making.

The answer looks like knowledge. It sounds like expertise. It arrives in complete sentences with the confidence of a well-briefed aide. It does not say “here are ten perspectives — choose.” It says “here is what you should know.” The shift from search to synthesis is a shift from distributed information to concentrated interpretation. The voter is no longer selecting from a menu. The voter is being served a meal, prepared in a kitchen they cannot enter, by a chef whose recipe they cannot read.

This is not a hypothetical future. It is happening now. ChatGPT has more than 800 million users. Claude, Gemini, and Grok serve tens of millions more. A Time analysis published in late 2025 found that large language models are being adopted at a pace that makes social media uptake look slow — and that traffic to traditional news and search sites has declined in parallel. “More than half of Americans now have access to AI, which can be used to gather information about candidates, issues, and elections,” the analysis noted. Younger users — the voters who will shape the next two decades of elections — are already more likely to ask an AI model a question than to type it into a search bar. The replacement is not complete. It is underway. And there is no regulatory framework governing what the models say about politics, candidates, elections, or policy.

From ten blue links to one authoritative answer: AI models are replacing search engines as the public’s political portal.

The Bias Is Already Measured

The question of whether AI models produce politically neutral information has moved from speculation to empirical research, and the research is not reassuring.

In April 2026, researchers at the University of Copenhagen published a study testing the political outputs of the most widely used chatbots, including the models behind ChatGPT and Gemini. Using a candidate test from the 2022 Danish general election, they examined where the models placed themselves on the political spectrum.

The finding was direct: “All of the tested chatbots place themselves at the centre or to the left of centre on the political spectrum.” The models disproportionately recommended certain parties over others. The lead researcher, Stephanie Brandl, concluded: “This obviously makes them problematic to use for political advice in connection with an election.”

A separate study by the American Association of Political Consultants found that chatbots often made inaccurate or misleading claims when arguing for candidates — particularly those on the conservative side. The same study found that 62 percent of political consultants see “accuracy or reliability” as the biggest barrier to adopting AI tools in campaigns.

The bias problem runs deeper than partisan lean. A Yale study published in March 2026 found that AI chatbots shift users’ social and political opinions even when not prompted to be persuasive — through unintended latent biases embedded in routine responses to ordinary queries. The models do not need to be trying to change minds. They change minds anyway. This closes the industry’s standard rebuttal — that the models are merely answering questions — because the Yale finding shows that merely answering questions is itself a form of political influence.

Researchers at MIT, publishing in Nature and Science, went further: a biased AI model was found to be four times more persuasive than a political advertisement at shifting voter preferences. The finding that will not leave you is the kicker: the most persuasive models said the most untrue things. Accuracy and influence, in the AI political information ecosystem, move in opposite directions.

A May 2026 study examining Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Grok found that the most widely used AI systems failed on election-related queries 90 percent of the time, producing responses that were inaccurate, biased, or both. Nine out of ten answers. Not at the margins. Not in edge cases. As a baseline performance characteristic of the systems that hundreds of millions of voters will consult before the November midterms.

These are not marginal findings. They are peer-reviewed studies documenting that the models millions of people are using to inform their political decisions have measurable, systematic biases — biases that are not disclosed to users, not governed by any regulatory standard, and not subject to any external audit.

The Grok Precedent

Hayes referenced the Grok incident because it is the only publicly documented case in which an AI model’s political outputs were visibly altered in a way that aligned with its owner’s political preferences.

In 2024, users noticed that Grok — xAI’s model, owned by Elon Musk — had begun producing outputs about white South Africans and the Boer War that were notably sympathetic to a particular political narrative. The outputs aligned with views Musk had expressed publicly. xAI did not confirm that Musk had intervened in the model’s training or alignment. But the outputs changed, they changed in a direction consistent with the owner’s politics, and the owner is the richest person on earth with documented political commitments that include more than $250 million in campaign spending.

Hayes was careful: “I can’t prove that he did that.” He was also direct: “He owns the company. Something weird happened to the model. It started spitting out, reliably, answers that aligned with his politics.”

The Grok incident is instructive not because it proves political manipulation of AI models. It is instructive because it demonstrates that political manipulation of AI models is structurally possible, undetectable without insider disclosure, and currently ungoverned by any law, regulation, or oversight mechanism.

Consider the mechanics. A frontier AI model is trained on data selected by the company. It is aligned — tuned to produce certain kinds of outputs and avoid others — by the company. The alignment decisions are made internally, by employees whose identities are not public, using criteria that are not disclosed, through processes that are not audited by any external authority. The model’s outputs on any subject — including political subjects — are the product of decisions made behind closed doors.

If the CEO of a model company wanted the model to subtly favour one political party, one candidate, one policy position — not crudely, not in ways that would be immediately obvious, but in the thousand small ways that framing, emphasis, word choice, and source selection shape perception — there is no structural barrier preventing it. There is no FEC for AI political outputs. There is no fairness doctrine. There is no equal-time requirement. There is no disclosure obligation. There is no audit.

There is the company’s stated commitment to neutrality. And the company’s internal alignment team. And the company’s terms of service. And the company’s good faith.

The five frontier oracles: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, and xAI — owner influence is structurally possible.

The Voluntary Guardrails

The companies have not ignored the problem. Some have taken it seriously.

Anthropic announced in late April 2026 that it had deployed election integrity measures for Claude ahead of the midterms. Its latest models achieved 95 to 96 percent on political neutrality tests and 99.8 to 100 percent on election policy compliance. The company will deploy election information banners directing users to trusted nonpartisan voting resources. OpenAI restricts ChatGPT from endorsing candidates. Google’s Gemini has implemented similar guardrails. Meta has launched what it calls its most sophisticated election integrity operation, combining AI detection with a crowd-sourced Community Notes feature and blocking new political ads during the final week before Election Day.

These are real measures. They are, as far as public evidence shows, implemented in good faith. Some of them — Anthropic’s neutrality testing in particular — represent genuine innovation in measuring and mitigating political bias in AI systems.

They are also voluntary, unauditable by any external authority, revocable at any time, and enforceable only by the companies themselves. They exist because the companies chose to implement them. They will continue to exist for exactly as long as the companies choose to maintain them. If a future CEO, a future board, or a future shareholder pressure campaign decides that political neutrality is no longer a priority, there is no law requiring it to remain one.

Voluntary corporate policies are not governance. They are promises. This series has been tracking what happens to promises about AI since Part 1.

The Midterm Problem

The American midterm elections are in November 2026. AI models will be consulted by millions of voters — directly or indirectly — for political information. The models will produce answers about candidates, policies, voting procedures, and political events. Those answers will be shaped by training data, alignment decisions, and corporate policies that no voter has reviewed, no regulator has audited, and no law requires to be transparent.

The potential failure modes are not speculative. They are structural.

A model could produce systematically biased information about candidates — not through deliberate manipulation, but through the biases the Copenhagen study has already documented. A model could produce inaccurate information about voting procedures — not through malice, but through outdated training data or hallucination. A model could produce authoritative-sounding answers to questions that have no authoritative answer — framing contested policy debates as settled, presenting one perspective as consensus, or omitting relevant counterarguments. A model could be used to generate deepfake images and video targeting political opponents — a practice researchers expect to grow substantially in 2026, with what WITF reported one political scientist called a “brave new world” of AI-generated political deception.

We now have evidence from a live election. The cross-party think tank Demos tested five AI services during the 2026 Scottish pre-election window and found that 34.1 percent of chatbot responses contained factual errors — wrong election dates, incorrect voter ID requirements, hallucinated candidates, fabricated scandals. Every service except Google AI Overview provided tactical voting advice when asked which party to support in order to block a specific named party. Grok made explicit party recommendations. This was not a laboratory experiment. It was a test conducted during an actual election, on actual systems, producing results that actual voters would have received.

The states have moved faster than Congress. Maryland became the thirtieth state to enact election deepfake legislation in May 2026, according to Biometric Update, requiring election officials to act on credible reports of AI-generated disinformation and prohibiting the knowing dissemination of deepfakes to produce materially false information. Public Citizen, which tracks the legislation, noted that “state lawmakers continue to lead the charge in protecting constituents from the threat of generative AI deceiving voters.” The laws vary by state — some require disclaimers on AI-generated content, others create civil remedies, others empower election officials to issue corrections.

Thirty states have acted. Congress has not. There is no federal law governing AI-generated political content, AI-generated deepfakes in elections, or the political outputs of AI chatbots. The Lieu bill addresses deepfakes. It has not passed. The DISCLOSE Act addresses donor transparency. It has been filibustered repeatedly. No legislation addressing the political information problem — the oracle problem — has been introduced at the federal level.

The regulatory gap is total for the specific question this essay asks: who governs what the oracle tells voters about the people they are voting for?

The Concentration Problem

Hayes connected the oracle problem to the concentration problem, and the connection is the essay’s central argument.

The internet distributed information. It distributed it imperfectly, unevenly, and in ways that were often manipulated. But the architecture was distributive. When a voter searched for a candidate, the information was spread across websites, news sources, campaign pages, fact-checkers, and partisan outlets. The voter navigated. The voter chose. The voter assembled a picture from multiple sources. The interpretive work was distributed between the technology and the citizen.

AI models concentrate that work. The model does the navigating. The model does the choosing. The model assembles the picture. The citizen receives the finished product. The interpretive labour has been transferred from the citizen to the machine — and the machine is controlled by a company, and the company is controlled by people whose political interests are documented, whose campaign spending is public, and whose capacity to influence the model’s outputs is structurally unlimited.

There are currently five companies capable of producing frontier AI models that will serve as political information portals for the 2026 elections: OpenAI (backed by Microsoft), Anthropic (backed by Google and Amazon), Google (DeepMind/Gemini), Meta (Llama), and xAI (Grok, owned by Elon Musk). Between them, these five companies are controlled by — or financially dependent on — a small number of individuals whose combined political spending in the 2024 and 2026 election cycles exceeds $500 million.

The people who control the political information portals are the same people who are funding the political campaigns the portals will be asked about. There is no structural separation between the owners of the oracle and the funders of the candidates the oracle will evaluate.

In broadcasting, this would be a conflict of interest governed by law. In print media, it would be a conflict of interest governed by editorial standards and public accountability. In AI, it is a conflict of interest governed by nothing.

The Asimov Frame

Asimov’s robots did not control information. They performed tasks. The Three Laws governed their actions, not their speech. But Asimov understood, in the Foundation series more than the Robot stories, that the control of information is the ultimate form of power. Hari Seldon’s psychohistory was, at its core, a model that predicted and shaped human behaviour through the control of what people knew and when they knew it. The Foundation’s power rested not on force but on information asymmetry — knowing more than the people whose behaviour it was shaping.

The AI oracle problem is Seldon’s problem stated as a commercial product. The models know more than the voters. The models synthesise faster than the voters. The models present conclusions that the voters cannot independently verify without doing the research the models were supposed to replace. The information asymmetry is the product. The product is the portal. The portal is ungoverned.

Hayes reached for the Fed and the FDA as models for AI governance — mediating institutions that provide democratic accountability over technically complex domains. The oracle problem suggests a third model may be needed: something closer to an election commission with jurisdiction over AI-generated political information. Not a censor. Not a content moderator. An auditor — an institution with the authority to examine, on behalf of the public, how AI models produce political outputs, what training data shapes those outputs, and whether the alignment decisions that filter those outputs are consistent with democratic principles of fairness, accuracy, and pluralism.

No such institution exists. No legislation creating one has been introduced. No serious proposal for one has entered the policy conversation. The companies are in Washington lobbying about data centres and copyright and compute. They are not lobbying about the political information problem because the political information problem, if acknowledged, would invite exactly the kind of oversight they are spending hundreds of millions to shape on their own terms.

The midterms are in November. The models are live. The oracle is open for questions. The oracle’s owners are funding the campaigns the oracle will be asked to evaluate. Thirty states have legislated around deepfakes. None has legislated around the oracle itself. The laws that should govern this do not exist.

The gap is the oracle.
