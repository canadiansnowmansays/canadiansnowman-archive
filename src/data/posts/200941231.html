The Pentagon, Arlington, Virginia. On February 27, 2026, a private company said no — and the U.S. military’s supply chain risk statute was turned against an American AI lab.

On February 27, 2026, at 5:01 p.m. Eastern Time, a deadline passed.

The Department of Defense had given Anthropic — the San Francisco artificial intelligence company behind the Claude family of models — until that moment to remove two restrictions from its acceptable use policy. The first restriction: Claude would not be used for lethal autonomous weapons systems operating without meaningful human oversight. The second: Claude would not be used for mass domestic surveillance of American citizens. Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, had been clear about both restrictions for months. They were, he said, not negotiating positions. They were the line.

The Pentagon’s position was equally clear. A private company does not get to tell the United States military how to use technology it has integrated into classified systems. The restrictions were unacceptable. Remove them, or face consequences.

Anthropic did not remove them.

What followed was, depending on your perspective, either the most important First Amendment case in the history of artificial intelligence, or a contract dispute dressed up in constitutional clothing. The courts are still deciding which. The answer will determine whether any AI company can maintain safety commitments when the government decides it wants something different — and whether the government can use procurement law as a weapon against a company for the content of its beliefs.

The record will matter.

What The Safety Trial Is

This is a litigation diary covering Anthropic PBC v. Department of War — the case that began when the Pentagon designated Anthropic a “supply chain risk” under a statute previously reserved for foreign adversaries, and that has since produced two simultaneous federal lawsuits, a split between the Ninth Circuit and the D.C. Circuit, and a Supreme Court petition that legal analysts describe as nearly inevitable.

The Safety Trial is not a summary service. What it offers is context — the accumulated analytical framework of The Asimov Gap, applied to the specific filings, rulings, and oral arguments emerging from two federal courthouses simultaneously.

Each dispatch will be organized around what the latest proceedings revealed, what they confirmed, and what they changed — in terms of the litigation’s trajectory, and in terms of our understanding of what it means when a government decides that a company’s safety commitments are a national security threat.

Why This Case Cannot Be Watched Alone

The Asimov Gap series has spent forty-six parts documenting the widening distance between what AI can do and what democratic institutions are empowered to govern. The Anthropic v. DOD case is that gap rendered as a federal lawsuit.

The backstory begins not in February 2026 but in the summer of 2025, when Anthropic signed a $200 million contract with the Pentagon to deploy Claude within classified military systems. The contract included Anthropic’s standard acceptable use policy — the two restrictions that would later become the breaking point. For months, the arrangement held. Then contract negotiations for the next phase began, and the Pentagon demanded what it called an “all lawful use” provision: Anthropic would guarantee that Claude could be used for any purpose the government determined was legal.

Anthropic refused. The Pentagon’s position, made explicit in subsequent filings, was that Anthropic’s restrictions amounted to the company attempting to encode its own policy judgments into systems the military depends on — that a private company was, in effect, claiming veto power over military operations. Anthropic’s position was that it was simply declining to remove safety guardrails it had publicly committed to maintaining, and that the government’s response — designating it a national security threat — was retaliation for a constitutionally protected position.

The gap between those two positions is not technical. It is philosophical. It is the same gap that runs through every serious AI governance debate of the past decade: who decides what the technology can be used for, and by what authority?

On February 27, 2026, President Trump posted on Truth Social directing all federal agencies to immediately halt use of Anthropic technology. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth invoked 10 U.S.C. § 3252 — a supply chain risk statute designed for foreign adversaries — to designate Anthropic a potential risk of “sabotage” and “subversion.” Defense contractors were required to certify they did not use Claude in any work with the military. Several paused or terminated contracts immediately. Anthropic’s 2026 revenue projections dropped by what it described in court filings as “multiple billions of dollars.”

Anthropic sued on March 9. Two lawsuits, filed simultaneously: one in the Northern District of California before Judge Rita Lin, one in the D.C. Circuit.

The results, so far, have been a study in judicial contradiction.

Judge Lin, a Biden appointee in San Francisco, granted a preliminary injunction on March 26 in a 43-page order that called the Pentagon’s action “likely both contrary to law and arbitrary and capricious.” She invoked the word “Orwellian.” She wrote that “nothing in the governing statute supports the Orwellian notion that an American company may be branded a potential adversary and saboteur of the U.S. for expressing disagreement with the government.” A bipartisan group of nearly 150 retired federal and state judges filed in support of her ruling.

The D.C. Circuit reached a different conclusion. On April 8, the court denied Anthropic’s request for a stay, writing that the “equitable balance” favored the government — that a stay would “force the United States military to prolong its dealings with an unwanted vendor of critical AI services in the middle of a significant ongoing military conflict.” The Iran conflict was explicitly cited. The same Claude models the Pentagon was simultaneously using in active military operations in Iran were, in the D.C. Circuit’s framing, a potential security risk too dangerous to be protected from a blacklisting designation.

Then came May 19.

One day after the Oakland jury rejected Musk’s claims in ninety minutes, the D.C. Circuit heard oral arguments on the merits. The panel — Judges Karen Henderson, Gregory Katsas, and Neomi Rao — heard nearly two hours of argument. What emerged from the bench was not the deference to military authority that the April stay denial had suggested. Judge Henderson, a George H.W. Bush appointee, called the Pentagon’s blacklisting “a spectacular overreach by the department.” She noted the statute had been specifically crafted to address risks from hostile nations, and that none of the criteria appeared to apply to an American company that had declined to remove safety guardrails.

The panel took the matter under advisement. A written opinion is pending.

If the D.C. Circuit rules for Anthropic on the merits — as Henderson’s bench comments suggest it may — and the Ninth Circuit’s preliminary injunction holds, the government faces a clean circuit split. A cert petition to the Supreme Court writes itself.

The Safety Trial is watching.

The Analytical Framework

The Asimov Gap series developed several analytical tools that will be applied throughout The Safety Trial.

The Asimov Frame. Isaac Asimov understood that governing transformative technology requires laws that are explicit, public, and prior — built before the capability exists, not improvised after the damage is done. The Anthropic v. DOD case is governance improvised after the damage is done. The question before the courts is not whether AI should be governed, but who gets to do it — a company, a general, a judge, or a statute written for a world that did not yet contain large language models.

The TACO Framework. Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate. The series developed this framework to describe how institutions navigate pressure from more powerful actors. Watch for it as the litigation unfolds: the government threatened, Anthropic declined to accommodate, and what follows is the legal record of what happens when a company in the AI era refuses to capitulate.

The Governance Vacuum. The trial will produce rulings. Rulings are not governance. A circuit court opinion on supply chain risk designation does not answer the question of who should decide what AI weapons systems can do, or what democratic accountability looks like when the technology moves faster than the legislature. The Safety Trial will track, throughout the proceedings, the gap between what the courts can resolve and what only democratic governance can address.

The Canadian Outsider Perspective. Canada has no more democratic control over the outcome of this litigation than any other nation that will live with the technology it governs — and considerable exposure to both Anthropic’s models and the American military’s AI ambitions. That distance is, as always, a feature.

The Cast

Dario Amodei — CEO and co-founder of Anthropic. His late-February statement drew the clearest line: Anthropic understands that the Department of War makes military decisions. It has never raised objections to particular military operations. What it will not do is remove the two restrictions from its acceptable use policy. Amodei founded Anthropic in 2021 specifically because he and his colleagues did not trust OpenAI’s governance of frontier AI. The irony of his company becoming the test case for whether a frontier AI lab can maintain its own governance commitments against government pressure is not lost on anyone watching.

Pete Hegseth — Secretary of Defense, officially Secretary of War since the department’s renaming. His January 9, 2026 memorandum stating that Anthropic posed a risk to military readiness was cited by the D.C. Circuit in its April stay denial as a credible expression of executive authority. His department’s position — that a private company cannot be permitted to encode restrictions into systems the military relies on — is constitutionally straightforward and operationally coherent. It is also, as Judge Henderson noted, a spectacular overreach of the specific statute invoked.

Judge Rita Lin — U.S. District Judge, Northern District of California, Biden appointee. Her 43-page preliminary injunction order is the most significant judicial statement yet on the limits of government authority over AI companies. The word “Orwellian” appears in it. So does a clear-eyed analysis of what happens when procurement law becomes a tool of political retaliation against a company for the content of its publicly stated beliefs.

Judge Karen Henderson — U.S. Circuit Judge, D.C. Circuit, George H.W. Bush appointee. Her bench comments during the May 19 oral arguments — “a spectacular overreach by the department” — signal the panel’s likely direction on the merits. Her analysis of the statute’s original scope, and its inapplicability to a domestic AI company that declined a contract term, is the analytical foundation on which Anthropic’s merits argument rests.

Axel Swingle — DOJ attorney arguing for the government in the D.C. Circuit. His argument that even if Anthropic does not have a “back door” available today, “it doesn’t take away a risk that they could put one in in the future,” was one of the oral argument’s most revealing moments — an admission that the government’s case rests not on demonstrated harm but on speculative future risk. Courts have historically been skeptical of that framing in First Amendment cases.

The Amici — Dozens of scientists and researchers from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and other AI companies filed amicus briefs in their personal capacities supporting Anthropic. A bipartisan group of nearly 150 retired federal and state judges filed in support of Judge Lin’s injunction. The breadth of institutional support for Anthropic’s position is itself part of the record.

A Final Note on Why This Matters

The Oakland Transcript existed because the Musk v. Altman trial was the closest thing to a public accountability moment the AI era had yet produced. It ended in ninety minutes on a statute of limitations, the merits unargued.

The Safety Trial exists because the question the Oakland jury never reached is now being asked in a different form, in two federal courts simultaneously: does the rule of law apply to the AI era, or does emergency — military, economic, technological — always find a way to suspend it?

Anthropic said no to the Pentagon. A company that builds AI said it would not remove the restrictions on autonomous weapons and mass surveillance, even when the government demanded it, even when the cost was measured in billions of dollars and a national security designation.

What the courts do with that no will tell us more about AI governance than the Oakland trial ever could have.

The microphones are working. The briefs are filed. Let’s see what the judges say.
