Every essay in this series so far has been about an erasure — a memorial hollowed, an exhibit pulled, a sign removed, a dataset deleted, a portrait taken down. This one is about the erasure of the eraser-in-reverse: the removal of the single official in the United States government whose entire job is to make sure the record survives. If the Ministry of Memory has an opposite number in the real bureaucracy — an office designed to prevent forgetting — it is the Archivist of the United States. Three weeks into the new term, the president fired her.

On the façade of the National Archives building in Washington, carved into the stone, is a line from Shakespeare’s Tempest: “What is past is prologue.” It is meant as a statement of purpose — the past is the opening act of the present, and this building is where the past is kept so the present can be understood. It is a fine and hopeful sentence to carve over the door of the nation’s memory. It also turns out to be a warning, though not in the way the architects intended. For this administration, the past was indeed prologue: the record of what the president had done was the very thing that had to be brought under control.

The Record-Keeper and the Record

To understand why the firing belongs in this series and not merely in a chronicle of political score-settling, you have to understand what the National Archives and Records Administration actually is. It is not a museum, though it runs museum spaces. It is the custodian of the federal record — the agency that preserves the government’s documents, administers the Presidential Records Act, decides what is retained and released, and stands, in the constitutional design, as an independent and nonpartisan keeper of the account. When Congress created NARA as an independent agency in 1984, it wrote into the statute that the Archivist must be chosen “without regard to political affiliations and solely on the basis of the professional qualifications” — because the person who keeps the record must not answer to whoever the record might incriminate.

Colleen Shogan was the eleventh Archivist of the United States, sworn in in 2023. By every account she was the apolitical professional the statute envisioned; she had even, notably, declined to go along with a Democratic president’s preferred position on the Equal Rights Amendment. She was not a partisan. She was an archivist.

On the evening of Friday, February 7, 2025 — barely three weeks into the term — the director of the Presidential Personnel Office announced on social media that, at the president’s direction, she had been dismissed that night. No cause was given. Shogan confirmed it herself in plain words: the president had fired her, no reason cited. The statute permits the president to remove the Archivist but requires that he communicate his reasons to Congress. The reasons, publicly, were a single line of thanks for her service.

She was not at NARA during the events that made the agency an enemy. But the agency was.

Why This Agency

The National Archives did something, during Trump’s years out of power, that no keeper of the record is thanked for: it insisted on the record. After his first term, when boxes of presidential materials — including classified documents — did not come back as the law requires, it was the National Archives that pressed for their return, and ultimately the Archives that alerted the Justice Department that documents appeared to have been mishandled. That referral was the seed of the classified-documents investigation, the search at Mar-a-Lago, the recovery of further classified materials, the federal charges later dismissed on a technicality by a Trump-appointed judge.

Set the legal outcome aside; it is not the point here. The point is what the agency was, in that story: the institution that remembered what was supposed to be returned and would not pretend otherwise. NARA’s function is to keep the account straight — to know what documents exist, to insist they be preserved, to refuse to let the record be ripped up or, as was alleged of the first term, flushed away. An agency like that is, structurally, a threat to any official whose past contains things he would rather were not kept. It is the institutional version of the eighty-seven-year-old tour guide who remembers what the building used to be, and says so.

So the firing is not, at bottom, about Colleen Shogan, who was a bystander to the events in question. It is about the office. To remove the Archivist without cause, three weeks in, and — as observers immediately noted — to install a loyalist in her place is to convert the keeper of the record from an independent officer into an instrument of the person being recorded. A Trump-aligned archivist, as one watchdog put it, might be less inclined to enforce the Presidential Records Act, less inclined to ask hard questions if documents again go missing. You do not need to burn the archive if you can appoint the archivist.

The Acting Archivist

There is a detail that turns the whole thing from ominous to almost unsubtle. In the wake of Shogan’s dismissal, the role of Acting Archivist was assigned to Marco Rubio — the Secretary of State, one of the busiest cabinet officials in the government, a man with, one assumes, a full schedule.

The Archivist of the United States is a specialist post. The statute demands professional qualifications precisely because keeping a nation’s records is a craft — a matter of preservation science, records management, declassification law, and institutional independence. To place it, even temporarily, in the hands of a sitting cabinet secretary whose day job is foreign policy is to announce that the position is no longer being treated as a professional custodianship at all. It is being treated as a control point — a lever to be held by someone reliable until a permanent, agreeable hand can be found. The message to the career staff of the Archives, and to anyone who files a records request or wonders what will happen to the documents of this administration when it ends, is that the keeper of the record now reports, in effect, to the recorded.

This is the same move this series has watched in five other settings, scaled up to the master institution. At the Smithsonian, the exhibits were brought under review. In the parks, the signs were flagged by the department. In the data, the pages were pulled to comply. At the Pentagon, the portrait came down on order. In each case a body that was supposed to hold the memory independently was made to answer to the subject of the memory. The Archives is simply the purest case, because holding the memory independently is the entire statutory reason the Archives exists. To capture it is to capture the mechanism by which the country would later be able to know what this administration actually did.

The Prologue Being Written

Let me return to the carving over the door, because the more I look at it the more it reads as the thesis of this entire series rather than the motto of one building.

What is past is prologue. The optimistic reading — the architects’ reading — is that history instructs the present, that a people who keep their records can learn from them. But there is a darker and, in 2026, more operative reading, and it is the one this administration appears to have adopted. If the past is prologue — if it sets the terms of what comes next — then whoever controls the past controls the prologue, and therefore the play. Orwell wrote it more baldly, and I have quoted him at the head of every essay in this series: who controls the present controls the past. The Archives is the building where that contest is physically located. It is where the past is either kept honestly, and remains available to indict or instruct, or is quietly brought to heel.

Firing the Archivist without cause, three weeks into the term, and handing the office to a loyalist is not a personnel decision. It is a decision about who will be allowed to write the prologue to everything that follows — a decision to ensure that when the record of these years is eventually assembled, the person assembling it will have been chosen by the people the record concerns.

The keeper of the past has been replaced. The building still says what is past is prologue, in stone, because stone is harder to edit than a webpage or a wall — and because, for now, no one has been able to pull that particular sentence down. It remains carved over the door of an institution whose new leadership answers to the man who most needs the past to stay quiet. Of all the sentences this series has watched an administration try to unsay, that one, at least, is still legible. Whether the record it promises to keep will remain equally legible is the question the next several years will answer.

We still know what the Archives was for. That, again, has had to be enough.
