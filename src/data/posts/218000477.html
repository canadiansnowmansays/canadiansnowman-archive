In the spring of 1839, an imperial commissioner named Lin Zexu arrived in Canton with orders from the Daoguang Emperor to end the opium trade, and he did something no Chinese official had yet managed. He made the foreign merchants hand the drug over.

Lin blockaded the foreign factories on the Canton waterfront, withdrew their Chinese servants, and waited. After six weeks, the British Superintendent of Trade, Captain Charles Elliot, ordered the British merchants to surrender their stock and promised them, on behalf of the British government, that they would be compensated. More than twenty thousand chests of opium were handed over. In June 1839, at Humen, Lin had trenches dug, filled them with water, salt and lime, and dissolved the entire hoard over some three weeks before flushing it out to sea. He offered a prayer of apology to the spirit of the ocean for what he was about to put in it.

Lin also wrote a letter to Queen Victoria, one of the more remarkable documents of the century. He pointed out, politely, that the sale of opium was forbidden in Britain itself, and asked how the British would feel if foreigners brought a poison into England and sold it to her people. He appealed to her conscience. There is no evidence she ever read it.

What she and her government did instead was send the navy.

Lin himself did not survive the consequences in office. When the British fleet appeared off the coast and began winning, the Daoguang Emperor needed someone to blame, and it was not going to be the emperor. In 1841 Lin was dismissed and exiled to Ili, in the far north-west of the empire, for having provoked a war China could not win. The official who had enforced Chinese law against foreign smugglers was punished by his own government for succeeding. That, too, is part of how the pattern works: the target state learns that resistance is so costly it will discipline its own resisters before the bully has to.

The trade

The problem Britain was solving was a trade deficit. Britons had developed an enormous appetite for Chinese tea, and China, under the Canton System that confined foreign trade to a single port and a licensed guild of merchants called the Cohong, wanted very little that Britain made. For decades the difference was paid in silver.

Opium closed the gap. The East India Company held a monopoly on opium production in Bengal, grew it under licence, auctioned it in Calcutta, and left the illegal part of the business, the actual smuggling into China, to private “country traders” such as Jardine, Matheson and Company, who carried it to receiving ships anchored off the coast. The silver the smugglers earned in China bought the Company’s tea. By the late 1830s, the traffic had grown to something like forty thousand chests a year, silver was draining out of China, and addiction had spread through the empire from the coastal provinces to the imperial court.

The Qing had banned opium repeatedly since 1729, and more firmly in 1796 and 1800. The trade was illegal under Chinese law in exactly the way it would have been illegal under British law. Everyone involved knew it.

The war

The rest follows the rubric so closely that it could have been written to illustrate it.

When word of the surrender reached London, the question was not whether the opium trade was legitimate but who would pay the merchants the compensation Elliot had promised. Palmerston, the Foreign Secretary, found the answer: China would. The government sent an expeditionary force, and in April 1840 the House of Commons debated a motion of censure over the war. The young William Gladstone told the House that he did not know of a war more unjust in its origin, or more calculated to cover the country with permanent disgrace. The government survived the vote by nine.

The war that followed, from 1839 to 1842, was one of the most lopsided in the series. The East India Company’s iron steamer Nemesis, shown in the Introduction destroying Chinese war junks in January 1841, could go where no Chinese vessel expected a warship to follow, upriver and into shallow water, and its guns outranged anything on the other side. Chinese coastal forts and war junks were destroyed one after another. British forces took Chusan, Canton’s approaches, Amoy, Ningpo, Shanghai and finally Chinkiang, at the junction of the Grand Canal and the Yangtze, cutting the artery that fed Beijing. By August 1842 they were at the walls of Nanking.

The Treaty of Nanking was signed aboard HMS Cornwallis on 29 August 1842. China ceded Hong Kong Island in perpetuity, opened five ports to British trade and residence (Canton, Amoy, Foochow, Ningpo and Shanghai), abolished the Cohong, and paid an indemnity of twenty-one million silver dollars, part of which covered the value of the opium Lin had destroyed. A supplementary treaty in 1843 added extraterritoriality, meaning British subjects in China would be tried by British consuls rather than Chinese courts, and most-favoured-nation status, meaning any privilege China granted another power would automatically extend to Britain.

The treaty did not mention opium at all. The trade simply continued, still illegal on paper, now through five ports instead of one.

The second war

Fourteen years later, Britain did it again, and the second time the pretext was even thinner.

In October 1856, Chinese officials in Canton boarded a small ship called the Arrow, arrested its Chinese crew on suspicion of piracy, and, according to the British, hauled down the British flag. The Arrow‘s British registration had in fact expired. That did not matter. The British consul in Canton, Harry Parkes, and the governor of Hong Kong, Sir John Bowring, used the incident to demand the right of entry into Canton itself, and the Royal Navy bombarded the city.

This time the House of Commons actually voted against the government. In March 1857 Richard Cobden carried a motion condemning the bombardment, with Gladstone and Disraeli both voting against Palmerston, by then Prime Minister. Palmerston called an election, campaigned on the insult to the flag, and won it handsomely. The voters, given a direct choice, chose the war.

Britain, joined by France, fought on until 1860. The Treaty of Tientsin in 1858 opened more ports, allowed foreign diplomats to reside in Beijing, and opened the Yangtze to foreign shipping. When the Qing resisted ratifying it, the allies marched on Beijing. In September 1860, a British and French negotiating party under a flag of truce, including Parkes and the Times correspondent Thomas Bowlby, was seized by Qing forces. Several of the prisoners were tortured and around twenty died in captivity.

The British commander of the mission was James Bruce, the eighth Earl of Elgin, who only a few years before had been Governor General of the Province of Canada, where he had signed the Rebellion Losses Bill in 1849 and watched a Tory mob burn the Parliament buildings in Montreal in protest. In October 1860, as reprisal for the prisoners, Elgin ordered the destruction of the Yuanmingyuan, the Old Summer Palace outside Beijing, an enormous complex of palaces, gardens and pavilions that held one of the great art collections in the world. After it had been looted by British and French troops, some 3,500 British soldiers burned it over two days. Elgin chose it, he wrote, because it would hurt the emperor personally without harming the ordinary people of Beijing. (His father, the seventh earl, is the Elgin of the Parthenon marbles.)

The Convention of Peking that followed ceded the southern tip of the Kowloon peninsula to Britain and raised the indemnity. The opium question had already been settled: in the tariff negotiations that followed the Treaty of Tientsin in 1858, China, with foreign fleets in its rivers, agreed to legalise the import of opium subject to duty. Britain had fought two wars without ever formally demanding it. It simply arranged for China to be left with no other option.

Where the phrase comes from

The Introduction said this is where “gunboat diplomacy” comes from, and that needs a small correction in fairness to readers. The phrase itself was coined later, in the twentieth century, to describe a practice that was already old by then. But the practice it names found its fullest form here: a trade dispute settled by a warship’s guns, a treaty signed on the deck of a man-of-war, and a standing expectation that any future disagreement would be settled the same way. After 1842, a British gunboat could be summoned up a Chinese river whenever a consul felt a treaty right was being ignored, and for most of the next century, one was.

In China, the period that began at Humen and Nanking is still taught as the start of the “century of humiliation,” which ended, in the official telling, with the founding of the People’s Republic in 1949. Anyone trying to understand why Beijing reacts as it does to foreign pressure over Hong Kong, trade or sovereignty needs to start here.

Scoring the Opium Wars against the rubric

Overwhelming asymmetry. In population, China was many times Britain’s size. In usable military power at the point of contact, the gap was enormous: steam, rifled guns and a professional navy against war junks and coastal forts. The outcome of both wars was never seriously in doubt. Satisfied.

Coercion as first resort. China’s action was the enforcement of its own law on its own territory. Britain did not first seek negotiation over the compensation or the legality of the trade. It sent a fleet. In 1856 the pretext was a lapsed registration. Satisfied.

Extraction is the point. Territory (Hong Kong, then Kowloon), ports, legal privileges, indemnities, and the continuation of a narcotics trade that Chinese law prohibited, all extracted by treaty after military defeat. The stated justifications, free trade, the honour of the flag, compensation for lost property, diverged sharply from the operative goal, which was to keep selling opium to pay for tea. Satisfied.

Self-justifying rhetoric. Free trade and civilisation, with a strong seasoning of national honour. Palmerston’s case to the voters in 1857 was built on an insult to the flag that had flown on a ship not entitled to fly it. Satisfied.

Resistance gets punished. The Old Summer Palace was chosen precisely because its destruction would be seen and felt by the emperor. Satisfied.

Five for five, with the full parliamentary record showing that the people who made the decisions were told, clearly and in public, what they were doing.

What the Opium Wars tell us

Ireland showed the pattern running through sincere economic doctrine. China shows it running through a commercial interest so plainly disreputable that one of the most respected men in Parliament called the war a disgrace on the day it was debated, and the country fought it anyway, then fought a second one after the electorate was asked directly and said yes.

The honest lesson is not that the British public was uniquely callous. It is that bullying abroad is easiest to sustain when the gains are real, the victims are far away, and the language of national honour is available to cover the gap. Every chapter from here to the end of this series will involve some version of that combination.

Part IV turns to the largest prize of all, where the civilising language was not merely implied but written down, in verse and in official minutes, as the stated purpose of British rule.
