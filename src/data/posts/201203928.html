Americans call it the Forgotten War. The label is precise in ways its users rarely intend. The Korean War is forgotten not because it was minor — nearly five million people died, a rate of civilian casualties higher than either World War II or Vietnam — but because it ended without the clarity that Americans require from their wars. There was no V-J Day, no ticker-tape parade, no moment at which one side could claim to have won. There was only an armistice, signed on July 27, 1953, that stopped the shooting without settling anything. The war didn’t end. It froze.

In Pyongyang, they do not call it forgotten. They call it the Great Fatherland Liberation War, and they have spent seventy years ensuring that no North Korean alive is permitted to forget a single detail of it. The carpet bombing. The napalm. The American general who said, before the Chinese intervention, that there were no more targets left in Korea worth hitting. The three years of industrialized destruction that killed an estimated ten percent of the North Korean population and reduced every major city on the peninsula to rubble.

The war is the hinge on which everything in this series turns. Before it, Kim Il-sung was a Soviet-installed leader still consolidating power over a divided, traumatized peninsula. After it, he was the man who had faced the full military force of the United States and survived — the man whose state, however battered, still stood. That survival became the foundational myth of North Korean legitimacy. And the armistice that preserved that survival — a document that ended hostilities but not the war itself, that left 28,000 American troops stationed in South Korea, that created a Demilitarized Zone bristling with weapons along the 38th parallel — became the foundational justification for every weapon Kim’s family has built since.

The war is not history in North Korea. It is the permanent present.

The Decision

Kim Il-sung spent 1949 lobbying Stalin for permission to invade the South. Stalin refused, repeatedly — he judged the North Korean military insufficiently prepared and feared American intervention. But the strategic landscape was shifting. In October 1949, Mao’s communists won the Chinese civil war, establishing the People’s Republic. In August 1949, the Soviet Union tested its first nuclear bomb, ending the American monopoly. The Cold War calculus was changing, and Kim pressed his case.

In January 1950, the US Secretary of State Dean Acheson gave a speech defining the American “defensive perimeter” in Asia that appeared, at least to some interpreters, to leave South Korea outside it. Kim seized on this. In April 1950, he travelled to Moscow and met with Stalin three times, persuading the Soviet leader that a military victory would be quick and easy — a matter of weeks, before the Americans could respond. Kim promised that southern guerrillas would rise up in support and the southern population would welcome liberation. Stalin, still cautious, gave conditional approval: Kim could invade only if Mao Zedong agreed. In May, Kim went to Beijing. Mao, who had his own doubts about American intervention, gave his reluctant consent.

On June 25, 1950, at four in the morning, North Korean artillery opened fire along the 38th parallel, and approximately 75,000 Korean People’s Army troops crossed into the South. Within three days they had taken Seoul. Within two months they had driven UN and South Korean forces into a tiny perimeter around the southern city of Pusan, and it appeared possible that the peninsula would be unified by force under Kim’s control.

It was the high-water mark of Kim’s military ambitions. Everything after it was catastrophe, survival, and myth-making.

The Reversal

The United States intervened with a speed and decisiveness that Kim had predicted it would not. President Truman, drawing the Cold War lesson that appeasement of aggression led to world war, committed American forces within days. The Soviet Union was boycotting the UN Security Council over the question of Chinese representation and was therefore absent when the Council voted to authorize military action — a diplomatic accident that gave the American intervention a multilateral legitimacy it might otherwise have lacked.

General Douglas MacArthur, commanding the UN forces, executed one of the boldest military strokes of the twentieth century. On September 15, 1950, he landed amphibious forces at Inchon, far behind the North Korean lines, severing the supply routes of the Korean People’s Army and cutting off its southern advance. The North Korean army, which had been fighting at the end of a desperately thin supply line, collapsed with remarkable speed. UN forces retook Seoul. Then, in a fateful decision that transformed a war of containment into a war of unification, MacArthur received authorization to cross the 38th parallel and advance into North Korea.

The advance was rapid. By late October, UN forces were approaching the Yalu River — the border with China. MacArthur, meeting with Truman on Wake Island, assured the President there was little chance of large-scale Chinese intervention. It was one of the most consequential intelligence failures in American military history.

On October 19, 1950, 300,000 Chinese People’s Volunteer Army troops crossed the Yalu River and entered the war. They struck the advancing UN forces with devastating effect, driving them back in a catastrophic retreat — one of the longest in American military history — that didn’t stabilize until well south of the 38th parallel. Seoul fell again. The war had transformed from a near-victory into a grinding attritional conflict along a line that, after two more years of fighting, would end almost exactly where it had begun.

The Bombing

While the ground war stalemated, the air war did not. And it is the air war — barely remembered in the West, burned into North Korean consciousness with the permanence of scripture — that explains more about the psychology of the Kim regime than almost anything else in this series.

The United States Air Force dropped more bombs on the Korean Peninsula than were used in the entire Pacific theatre of World War II. The bombing campaign against North Korea was one of the most intensive in the history of aerial warfare. An estimated 430,000 bombs fell on Pyongyang alone. Every major North Korean city was destroyed. By June 1951, General O’Donnell, commander of the Far Eastern Air Force Bomber Command, testified to the US Senate that the entire Korean peninsula was “just a terrible mess” and that there were “no more targets in Korea” worth hitting.

The weapons included napalm — the incendiary gel that burns at over 800 degrees Celsius and cannot be extinguished with water — deployed in massive quantities against North Korean cities, villages, and supply lines. Eighty-five percent of North Korea’s buildings were estimated destroyed. Industrial production in 1953, when the armistice was signed, was running at sixty-four percent of its pre-war level. An estimated two and a half million North Korean civilians died — roughly eighty percent of all North Korean casualties in the conflict.

This was not a secret. It was reported, debated, and in some quarters celebrated in the American press at the time. What it has since become is something the West has systematically declined to reckon with — a campaign of destruction so total that it altered the physical and psychological landscape of an entire country, and that North Korean propagandists have been citing, with complete accuracy, as evidence of American intentions ever since.

When Kim Jong-un stands before his military commanders and talks about the necessity of nuclear weapons to prevent a second Korean War, he is not confabulating a threat. He is pointing to a historical record that is factually correct. The Americans came. They destroyed everything. They can come again. The only language they will understand is one they cannot afford to ignore.

That argument — which is both a manipulation and a truth — is the bedrock of North Korean nuclear doctrine. It was built from the rubble of Pyongyang, and no amount of diplomatic pressure has ever successfully dislodged it.

The Human Cost

The statistics of the Korean War are staggering enough — five million dead, ten percent of the North Korean population, more bombs than the entire Pacific theatre of World War II. But statistics of that magnitude have a numbing effect. They require translation into the scale of individual experience to become fully comprehensible.

Consider the separated families. When the armistice line hardened along the 38th parallel in 1953, it bisected not just a peninsula but a people. Families who had been divided by the chaos of the war — parents in the North, children in the South, siblings on opposite sides of a front line that shifted back and forth across their villages — suddenly found themselves separated by one of the most heavily fortified borders in human history. There was no mechanism for communication. Letters could not cross. Telephone calls were impossible. In the first decades after the war, the separation was total and indefinite.

An estimated ten million Koreans were separated from immediate family members by the division. Most never saw each other again. The first limited reunions of separated families — elderly Koreans meeting brothers and sisters they had not seen in half a century — did not take place until 2000, nearly fifty years after the armistice. These reunions, organized with painstaking diplomatic care and limited to a few hundred families at a time, took place in the mountains of the North, under official supervision, for a few hours. Then the families were separated again. Most of the participants were in their seventies, eighties, and nineties. Many had spent their entire adult lives not knowing whether the people they had been separated from were alive or dead.

As of 2026, over one hundred thousand South Koreans who registered for the reunion programme have died still waiting for a meeting that never came. The North Korean state controls access to these reunions entirely, deploying them as diplomatic currency — offering them when relations warm, suspending them when relations cool — with a casualness about the human cost that tells you everything about how the regime views the people it governs.

The war also produced, in South Korea, a social transformation that makes the divergence between the two halves of the peninsula even more striking in retrospect. The South, devastated by the conflict, rebuilt with American support and its own extraordinary entrepreneurial energy into one of the great economic miracles of the twentieth century — the country that would go from per capita income comparable to Ghana in the 1960s to a top-fifteen global economy within two generations, producing Samsung, Hyundai, BTS, and Parasite. The North, starting from a stronger industrial base and Soviet support, chose a different path — the path of songun and juche and nuclear weapons — and arrived at the satellite image: one half of the peninsula blazing with light, the other sitting in the dark.

The war made that divergence possible by freezing the political conditions that produced it. Without the armistice, without the permanent division, without the American alliance that underwrote South Korean security and trade access, the economic miracle might not have happened. Without the continued threat from the North that the armistice preserved rather than resolved, the political arrangements that produced South Korean democracy might have looked very different. The frozen war shaped both Koreas — the one it imprisoned and the one it accidentally liberated.

The Armistice and Its Absence

On July 27, 1953, after 158 negotiating sessions spread over more than two years — the longest armistice negotiation in history — military commanders from the United States, North Korea, and China signed an agreement ending hostilities. The document was careful and precise. It established a four-kilometre-wide Demilitarized Zone along the front line. It provided for the exchange of prisoners. It called for a political conference to be held within three months to achieve “a peaceful settlement of the Korean question.”

That conference was never held. The peaceful settlement was never achieved. The armistice was not a peace treaty and said explicitly that it was not a peace treaty — it was a ceasefire, intended to hold until a final settlement could be negotiated. Seventy-three years later, that final settlement has still not been negotiated. The Korean War has never legally ended.

The negotiations themselves were a study in the depth of the antagonism they were meant to resolve. They dragged on for over two years — 158 sessions — largely because of a single issue: what to do with prisoners of war who did not want to return home. Tens of thousands of North Korean and Chinese prisoners held in UN camps said they would refuse repatriation. The communist delegations insisted on forced return. The UN refused. The argument — over whether men could be compelled to return to states they were fleeing — was not merely procedural. It was ideological, a proxy war over the legitimacy of the regimes themselves. While diplomats argued about prisoners, soldiers continued dying along a front line that barely moved. By the time the armistice was finally signed, the negotiations had claimed more lives than the fighting they eventually stopped.

This is not merely a diplomatic technicality. It is the legal and psychological architecture within which the entire North Korean nuclear programme exists. The United States maintains over 28,000 troops in South Korea — a presence Pyongyang describes as military occupation and cites as evidence that the war is ongoing and the threat is real. The armistice, intended as a temporary pause, became permanent. The temporary became the world.

South Korea was not even a signatory to the armistice. President Syngman Rhee, furious that the war was ending without unification, refused to sign. The document was signed by the United Nations Command, the Korean People’s Army, and the Chinese People’s Volunteer Army. South Korea formally accepted the ceasefire without signing it. North and South Korea are therefore, in the technical language of international law, still at war — a war that has been frozen for seven decades, that has killed no soldiers since 1953, and that has nevertheless shaped every political decision made on the peninsula in the years since.

What the Frozen War Made

The Korean War made several things that are essential to understanding the present.

It made the Kim dynasty’s survival mythologically significant. A state that had faced the full military force of the United States and the United Nations and still existed had demonstrated something — not military prowess exactly, since survival had required Chinese intervention and Soviet material support, but a kind of indigestibility that became the core of North Korean national identity. You could not destroy us then. You cannot destroy us now.

It made the American military presence in South Korea permanent and its justification circular: the troops remained because the war had not ended, and the war had not ended partly because the troops remained. North Korea pointed to the troops as proof of hostile intent. The troops pointed to North Korean belligerence as justification for their presence. The logic feeds itself across decades.

It made Chinese intervention in any future Korean conflict a near-certainty in North Korean strategic planning — and Chinese caution about North Korean collapse a near-certainty in Beijing’s. China sent 300,000 soldiers to prevent a unified, American-aligned Korea on its border in 1950. It lost an estimated 180,000 of them. One of those soldiers was Mao Zedong’s own son, Mao Anying, killed in a US air strike on November 25, 1950 — a loss that gave the Chinese intervention a personal dimension that echoed through Beijing’s Korea policy for generations. China has never stopped worrying about a repeat scenario. A collapsed North Korea would mean a US-allied state on the Yalu River — the same provocation that sent Chinese armies across that river in 1950. Kim Jong-un has always known this. The Chinese buffer is not an alliance of affection. It is a geopolitical calculation that has held for seventy years and that Kim exploits with the precision of a surgeon — pushing Beijing far enough to extract economic and diplomatic support, but never quite far enough to make collapse seem preferable to accommodation.

And it made nuclear weapons, eventually, inevitable. The Americans had used atomic bombs in Japan. They had carpet-bombed Korea into rubble with conventional weapons. MacArthur had reportedly advocated for nuclear use during the Korean War, and Truman had not entirely ruled it out. The lesson Kim Il-sung drew from this — and that his son and grandson carried forward — was that a state without nuclear weapons was a state that could be bombed into the stone age by anyone with sufficient air power and insufficient restraint. The solution was not diplomatic. The solution was the bomb.

The frozen war never thawed. It simply changed its character — from a shooting war to a nuclear standoff, from armies on a front line to missiles in underground silos, from a conflict over territory to a conflict over existence itself. The soldiers stopped dying in 1953. The war did not stop.

It is still being fought. The weapons have simply changed.
