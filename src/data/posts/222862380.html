On the evening of July 17, 2026, Donald Trump went before the country to prove that the 2020 election had been stolen from him. He spoke for nearly half an hour. He talked about stolen voter records, vulnerable voting machines and fake registration drives. He hinted that a foreign power might have reached into American elections, and might do it again.

What he did not do was say that any foreign government had changed a single vote. As Shane Harris, Michael Scherer, Sarah Fitzpatrick and Yvonne Wingett Sanchez reported for The Atlantic that night, a White House official had told them earlier in the day that investigators had gone through “millions” of documents and found no evidence to support that claim. The president had spent a year and a half sending analysts across the intelligence community to look for it. He came up empty, and then he went on television anyway.

This series has spent sixty instalments watching a pattern repeat: the administration rarely needs to win a fight over elections in order to get what it wants from that fight. It needs the fight to exist. The July speech was not a failed attempt at proof. It was a rehearsal. With the polls opening on Tuesday, November 3, it is worth looking closely at what was rehearsed, because the performance is about to begin.

What the speech actually contained

The centrepiece of Trump’s case was a startling number: China had obtained the voter records of more than 200 million Americans in eighteen states. It sounds like a heist. It is old news.

During Trump’s first term, U.S. intelligence analysts reported that Chinese intelligence had been analysing voter registration data from several states, information that is often publicly available anyway. That finding sat in an assessment of the 2020 election that was presented to Trump and his advisers and later declassified. The same assessment stated plainly that the intelligence community had found no sign that any foreign actor had tried to alter any technical part of the 2020 vote, from registration to casting, counting or reporting. China wanted the data to predict outcomes and to shape its influence over U.S. policy toward Beijing, whoever won. That is espionage, and it is serious. It is not ballot-stuffing.

Some of the speech contradicted the very documents the White House had declassified to support it. Trump claimed the CIA had uncovered a Venezuelan plot to digitally rig elections. The CIA report he cited, dated June 29, 2026, said the Maduro regime had developed techniques for manipulating vote totals but that U.S. intelligence could not definitively confirm such fraud had actually been carried out. One redacted source judged that the regime had not needed gross fraud in the December 2020 National Assembly elections at all, because almost the entire opposition boycotted them.

There was also a revealing piece of history. In February 2020, before he lost, senior intelligence officials briefed Trump on their efforts to secure the election and certify the voting machines. According to a person familiar with the meeting, he was so pleased he wanted to call a press conference to reassure the country. On July 17, 2026, he mocked those same assurances from the White House. The machines had not changed. The result had.

ABC and NBC declined to carry the speech live, citing his history of false election claims. Trump suggested this was fraud deserving the loss of their broadcast licences. Nevada’s secretary of state, Cisco Aguilar, who chairs the Democratic Association of Secretaries of State, offered a four-word review that cannot be printed here but can be summarised as a firm negative. In the room, the senior officials of the administration and its intelligence agencies applauded.

Why the rehearsal matters now

Shane Harris returned to the subject in The Atlantic on October 30, 2026, and found Democrats treating the July speech as exactly what it was: a dress rehearsal for the week of the midterms. Representative Jim Himes of Connecticut, the senior Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, told reporters that his side was planning on the assumption that the president would claim foreign interference if Republicans lost badly. He was equally plain about the intelligence he had seen. Adversaries spread propaganda; nobody has seen an operation to change votes.

The current threat picture is familiar. Russia is still spreading misinformation designed to make Americans distrust their elections and their voting machines, increasingly with AI-generated deepfakes, and a Russian propaganda outfit known as Storm-1516, active in 2024, may surface again. China concentrates on local races and policy influence, often through recruited agents; in May, a former mayor of a Los Angeles suburb pleaded guilty to acting as an illegal foreign agent. Iran, which once dabbled in American elections, is reportedly more interested this year in keeping gas prices high by disrupting traffic through the Strait of Hormuz.

None of that is vote-flipping, and the experts Harris spoke to explained why vote-flipping is so hard. Lawrence Norden of the Brennan Center for Justice pointed out that voting machines are not connected to the internet, and that almost every vote cast in the United States now has a paper backup. A hacker who somehow altered a tally would leave a discrepancy that the paper would expose. Norden’s view was that the new threat this year is not foreign at all. It is the administration.

The administration’s own former intelligence director has, in effect, already made the point for him. Tulsi Gabbard, while still director of national intelligence, obtained voting machines from Puerto Rico and had them tested for tampering. Her investigation found nothing.

The legal walls

There are real limits on what a president can do with a claim of foreign meddling, and it is worth stating them clearly, because they are the reason the experts are not in despair.

The Constitution leaves the running of elections to the states. Richard Pildes of New York University’s law school told Harris that a presidential declaration is not legally enough to justify seizing voting machines or ballots. Federal agents would need a warrant, which means persuading a judge there is probable cause that a crime occurred, and any classified intelligence used to support that claim might have to be shown to the judge. Even then, Pildes said, the government cannot legally remove the ballots from state custody; it can work only from copies or images. There is, in his words, no executive power to take over the election process.

Himes and his Senate counterpart, Mark Warner of Virginia, say they will phone the president’s intelligence chiefs the moment any such claim is made and demand the underlying facts. Himes says he has told CIA Director John Ratcliffe and Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, confirmed this summer, that they had better answer. The career staff who monitor election threats in Clayton’s office are fewer than before the administration’s cuts, but committee members came away confident that if the president ordered them to fabricate something, Congress would hear about it.

Readers of this series will notice the gap between those reassurances and the record. The Fulton County ballots that Part LI followed, and that Part LIX found still haunting every clerk’s contingency binder, were taken under a warrant a judge granted. The walls held in law and leaked in practice. Phone calls from the Intelligence Committees are a fine thing, but they are not a court order, and the commenters beneath Harris’s piece who scoffed at them had a point.

The machinery is already local

The deeper problem was laid out in September by Toluse Olorunnipa, whose cover story for the October issue of The Atlantic argued that the most serious threats to a fair count no longer come from the White House. They come from county offices and state boards that election deniers have spent six years quietly filling.

Georgia is the clearest case. The Republicans who held the line in 2020 are going or gone. Geoff Duncan is out of office. Gabriel Sterling is leaving the secretary of state’s office before the midterms. Brad Raffensperger is a lame duck who was pushed off the state election board in 2024 and won 15 percent in the Republican primary for governor in May. The five-member state election board, which now has the power to take over election administration in counties it judges to be failing, is controlled by three Trump allies.

One of them, Salleigh Grubbs, had no interest in politics before 2020. Then she heard that a shredding truck was parked outside a Cobb County recount centre and sped over in her Toyota to film it and follow it, later comparing the chase to Thelma & Louise. The truck was hauling empty envelopes and rubbish. Grubbs remained convinced she had seen fraud in broad daylight, became chair of the Cobb County Republican Party, and was appointed to the state board in January. When the FBI raided the Fulton warehouse, she was outside on a video call with Steve Bannon’s War Room, calling the moment wonderful. Another board member, Janelle King, has not ruled out a state takeover of elections in Fulton County, the centre of Georgia’s Democratic vote. On the May primary ballot, Republican officials asked Fulton’s GOP voters whether the board should do exactly that. Nearly three-quarters said yes.

Fulton’s own election board includes Julie Adams, a Republican appointee who refused to certify the results of Georgia’s 2024 primary, despite no evidence of irregularity and despite Trump having won it. Adams is also a regional coordinator for the Election Integrity Network, founded in 2021 by Cleta Mitchell, the lawyer who helped Trump fight the 2020 result. The network now has affiliates in at least thirty states, including every battleground. Its volunteers pack once-sleepy board meetings, train poll observers and challengers, and push for laws giving local officials more discretion to delay or refuse certification. A 2024 study by the Center for Media and Democracy found more than 100 election deniers holding election posts in eight swing states. There are probably more now.

The board’s chair, John Fervier, a lifelong Republican who has sometimes sided with the board’s lone Democrat, put the only question that matters. These people keep pointing back to 2020, he told Olorunnipa. To what end?

The end

Here is the answer to Fervier’s question, assembled from the pieces.

The goal is not to change the count. As Olorunnipa noted, it is very difficult for any single official to alter a tally without leaving an obvious trail; the system is too well built for that. The goal is to make the count unknowable. On election night, the local activists are primed to treat any delay, any rumour, any stray photograph as evidence. Sympathetic officials can press to extend certification deadlines or order audits. A president standing at a podium can then say that foreign powers have meddled, citing intelligence he will not show. Seizing machines, the step Part LIX noted he regrets not taking in 2020, would break the chain of custody and bring the count, as a practical matter, under federal control. Once enough doubt exists, the dispute moves to the place where the law gives the final word: the House and Senate themselves, which judge the elections of their own members.

And the calendar already contains a document built for that moment. On August 31, 2026, the President renewed the national emergency declared under Executive Order 13848, whose text sits in the Federal Register, as every administration has done each year since he first signed it in 2018. The renewal is routine. The timetable is not. The order requires the Director of National Intelligence to deliver an assessment of any foreign interference within 45 days of an election, which for November 3 means by December 18, 2026, with an Attorney General and Homeland Security report due within a further 45 days, as late as early February 2027. Its definition of interference is broad enough to include efforts to undermine public confidence in an election. Part LVII set those dates beside the certification calendar. Read alongside the July speech, they describe a pretext with a filing deadline: an official federal finding on foreign meddling, landing in the middle of certification season, with its sequel arriving after the new Congress has been sworn in.

That is the playbook Part LVII called the weeks nobody is war-gaming. The foreign-interference claim is the key that opens the door. The local machinery is what is waiting on the other side.

Georgia gave us a preview on primary night in May. Two Atlanta-area precincts closed for about four hours after 911 reports of a gunman near a school, and a judge kept them open late, which delayed Fulton’s results. Within the hour an election-denial author was asking his followers to guess which county had not reported. A Republican state senator running for lieutenant governor, Greg Dolezal, posted that it was always Fulton County and that the state should take over the process. Weeks later an audit confirmed the night’s totals were accurate. By then the skeptics had moved on, which is the point. The rumour does its work and leaves before the correction arrives.

This year the rumour comes with a bounty. In the campaign’s final week, Trump offered a $1 million reward for information leading to an election-fraud conviction, and said it applies to past elections as well as this one. The White House would not say where the money would come from or how Americans should report what they see, The New York Times reported. Local officials saw the danger at once. Pima County’s sheriff, Chris Nanos, warned against a vigilante system in which anyone can earn a million dollars by accusing a neighbour of fraud. Green Bay’s police chief, Chris Davis, said a single voter challenging another could quickly turn into a disturbance. A pretext needs witnesses. A reward recruits them.

What still stands

None of this is inevitable, and it would be poor service to readers to suggest it is.

The courts have largely rejected Trump’s attempts to change voting rules, as this series has recorded from Part XLIII onward, most recently in the Ninth Circuit’s refusal on October 30 to make California set aside 20,000 mail ballots the administration claims went to noncitizens. On Monday, the day before the vote, the administration asked the Supreme Court to order it anyway, as The New York Times reported. Legal experts were sceptical, the UCLA election-law scholar Richard L. Hasen called it an emergency Hail Mary, and filing so late may simply push any ruling past Election Day. Senate Republicans have not abolished the filibuster to pass the SAVE America Act. Raffensperger will still be in office on November 3. David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation & Research says more than 10,000 lawyers have volunteered to help local administrators withstand pressure, and the ACLU has been training its members for dozens of scenarios. Part LVIII followed the Democratic coalition’s nationwide suit before Judge Boasberg. Above all, paper ballots exist, and paper does not care what the president says.

The best defence, as several election officials told Olorunnipa, is a result too large to argue with. Landslides are hard to challenge. Close races are where the machinery earns its keep.

Mark Earley, the election supervisor in Leon County, Florida, described what his job now involves. He used to plan for hurricanes and power cuts. Now he plans for online mobs, real ones incited by rumours, and the possibility of unreliable voices from inside his own system. Some states have gamed out who steps in if election officials are arrested while trying to stop federal agents from seizing ballots. Several officials told Olorunnipa they had exhausted the limits of their imagination.

A Canadian watching all this is struck by how much of it depends on a single sentence that has not yet been spoken: foreign powers interfered. In July the president came to the edge of saying it and stopped, because the evidence was not there. On Tuesday night, the evidence will still not be there. The question is whether, this time, he decides he no longer needs it.
