The White House had five days to decide what its legal defence would actually be, and on Tuesday night, hours before Wednesday’s emergency hearing, it filed one. The answer it settled on is not the accountability-for-bias argument Steven Cheung and Brendan Carr have supplied throughout this series. It is narrower, more specific, and considerably more revealing about what this ban was actually punishing — though, as it turns out, the administration’s own social media account gave away the game before the lawyers ever got involved. Trump’s original Friday post calling this “the ban on the free press” was a Kinsley gaffe in the purest sense: a politician accidentally telling the truth. By Monday he had cleaned up the language into the “assault on Fake News” framing this series quoted in Part 16 — but in that same clean-up post, before Tuesday’s filing existed, he had already supplied the argument the lawyers would formalise two days later, writing that the outlets’ coverage “is a threat to our National Security, and must be stopped, NOW!”

The privilege argument

The government’s filing does not dispute that CNN, MS NOW, and Politico were barred without warning. It argues instead that none was owed, because White House access is, in its words, a privilege rather than a right, consistent with what the filing calls a long history of presidents favouring some journalists over others. That framing does real work: it recasts three newsrooms losing their ability to cover the executive branch as no different from a seating-chart preference, rather than as the removal of previously granted access over the content of specific reporting.

Attached to the filing were letters sent to each outlet Tuesday, identifying the reporting the administration says justified the ban. CNN’s letter cited a January story describing construction details of the East Wing bunker as disclosing “top-secret” information. MS NOW’s letter pointed to a June story in which a source said Trump had underestimated Iran’s willingness to resume conflict. Politico’s letter cited a story detailing Secret Service security investments tied to the White House ballroom project. The government’s language throughout is “trafficking in verifiable falsehoods about national security” and publishing “sensitive or classified information” — not bias, not tone, not the general “fake news” framing that opened this story on Truth Social nine days ago, but a specific claim that each outlet handled national-security material improperly.

That is a meaningfully different argument than the one this series has been tracking since Friday, and it matters for two reasons. First, it is the first time the administration has offered anything resembling a factual basis for the ban rather than a mood. Second, it converts a First Amendment fight over viewpoint discrimination into something closer to the Pentagon access disputes this series covered in its earlier parts — the Arithmetic Room’s casualty-count control, the Classified Room’s credential gating — where the government’s tool of choice is not a stated objection to coverage but a claim about what information should never have been public in the first place. The mechanism is one this series has named before: control access to information by controlling what counts as classified, then punish whoever published it anyway.

The due-process answer, and its problem

On the specific due-process question this series flagged after Monday’s lawsuit — that CNN, MS NOW, and Politico received no notice or chance to respond before their credentials were pulled — the government’s filing claims it has now provided “sufficient post-deprivation process,” pointing to Tuesday’s letters and giving the outlets until Friday to contest the decision. Gabe Rottman of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press identified the problem with that sequence bluntly: an agency cannot suspend someone first and supply the justification afterward. The letters arrived four days after the badges stopped working, addressed to outlets already locked out, laying out grounds for a decision that had already been made and enforced. Whether that satisfies Judge Timothy J. Kelly — the same judge who found in the Acosta case that Trump had not given the reporter enough notice to challenge his removal — is now the question Wednesday’s hearing exists to answer. Trump himself all but conceded the outcome in a Truth Social post last week, writing that given Kelly’s history on this exact question, an appeal would be needed “almost without question,” before any ruling had been filed.

A rationale in wider use

The specific claim that CNN, MS NOW, and Politico threatened national security did not arrive in isolation, and it did not originate with the lawyers — Trump had already written the sentence himself, two days before the filing, as this piece’s opening notes. The same week, he has invoked the same justification to defend a White House ballroom project, to pause offshore wind construction, to exempt Gulf of Mexico drilling from endangered-species protections, to strip union rights from federal workers, and — in the most striking instance — to reframe his planned 250-foot triumphal arch near Arlington National Cemetery as a military installation, complete with drones, snipers, and an ammunition store, after months of describing it publicly as a tourist attraction with an 80-person-an-hour observation deck. Legal scholars quoted on the arch project make an argument that applies with equal force to the press ban: courts have historically deferred to the executive on national-security claims precisely because that deference is meant to be reserved for matters within the president’s actual constitutional role as commander in chief, and invoking it for a triumphal arch or a cable news credential risks diluting the doctrine for the cases where a future president might need it legitimately. One law professor’s description of the underlying theory — that the presidency is meant to function as what she called a “de facto sovereign” — is as good a one-line description of this series’ entire subject as any single quote this year has supplied.

Applied to the press ban specifically, the national-security framing does something the “fake news” framing never could: it gives the administration a doctrinal home for an argument that would otherwise have no home at all. A president is entitled to almost no deference on whether coverage he dislikes is biased. He is entitled, under decades of precedent, to considerably more deference on what counts as a legitimate national-security concern — which is exactly why, nine days into this fight and one day before a hearing in front of the one judge who has already ruled against this White House on this precise question, the government’s chosen defence pivoted from a complaint about tone to a claim about classified information. It may not survive contact with Judge Kelly on Wednesday. But it is the more durable argument of the two the administration has now tried, and the one this series will need to track most closely if this ban, or its next iteration, outlasts this particular lawsuit.
