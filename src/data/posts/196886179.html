Day 70. Friday morning, Middle East time. The ceasefire is simultaneously: intact (Trump), violated by the United States (Iran), violated by Iran (CENTCOM), a trifle (Trump), and the subject of active one-page memo negotiations (three senior Iranian officials speaking anonymously). Three US destroyers were fired on in the strait Thursday night. The US struck Qeshm Island and Bandar Abbas in response. The UAE is being attacked for the third consecutive day. Gas is $4.55 nationally — up 53% since February 28. Trump is renovating the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool. The child in Hormozgan is no longer on the swing.

I. The Week That Was

Let us account for the week in sequence, because the sequence is the story.

Sunday: Trump announced Project Freedom — the US would guide stranded ships through the Strait of Hormuz beginning Monday morning Middle East time. Called it “a direct gift from the United States to the world.” Said interference would be dealt with “forcefully.”

Monday: Iran fired cruise missiles and drones at US ships. Six Iranian speedboats destroyed by Apache helicopters. UAE attacked — Fujairah Oil Industry Zone on fire, largest oil storage area in the Emirates. South Korean ship HMM Namu on fire in the strait. Four ships transited. Pre-war: 130 per day. Araghchi posted: “Project Freedom is Project Deadlock.”

Tuesday morning: Rubio at the White House briefing room, calling on reporters by the color of their blazers, invoking Ice Cube’s 1992 “Check Yo Self” to describe Iran’s situation. “The operation is over. Epic Fury — as the president notified Congress — we’re done with that stage of it.” Tuesday evening: Trump paused Project Freedom. “For a short period of time.” Project Freedom had lasted 36 hours.

Wednesday: Trump Truth Social — the war would end if Iran “agrees to give what has been agreed,” without specifying what that was. “If they don’t agree, the bombing starts, and it will be, sadly, at a much higher level and intensity than it was before.” This came hours after Rubio had declared the war over. Rubio was not informed before Trump posted. Trump Wednesday afternoon: “We had very good talks in the last 24 hours.” On deadlines: “Never a deadline.” On the conflict itself: “I call it a skirmish.”

Thursday: Fassihi confirmed via three senior Iranian officials that the two sides are discussing a one-page plan — lift the blockade, open the strait, end hostilities for 30 days, negotiate the rest. Thursday evening: Iran fired missiles, drones, and small boats at three US destroyers transiting the strait. The USS Truxtun, the USS Rafael Peralta, the USS Mason. None hit. US struck Qeshm Island and Bandar Abbas — missile and drone launch sites, command and control, ISR nodes. Trump, at the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool renovation, surrounded by construction workers in hard hats and MAGA hats, with Interior Secretary Burgum, DHS Secretary Mullin, and border czar Homan: “They trifled with us today. We blew them away. They trifled — I call that a trifle.” Then: “If there’s no cease-fire, you’re not going to have to look very hard. You’re just going to have to look at one big glow coming out of Iran. And they better sign their agreement fast.”

Friday: UAE air defenses intercepting Iranian missiles and drones for the third consecutive day. Brent at $101. Gas at $4.55 — five dollars per gallon in seven states. Three US destroyers back in the Arabian Sea. The one-page memo under review. The ceasefire intact, or not, depending on who is speaking and when.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio struggles to hold a briefing at the White House on Tuesday.Credit...Tierney L. Cross/The New York Times

The Green analysis — “Trump’s Gyrations on the War Leave Even Rubio Out of Sync” — provides the definitive taxonomy. Throughout this war Trump has described the conflict as a “war,” an “excursion,” a “skirmish,” and now a “trifle.” He has said Iran has no leaders left but that he is in communication with some who are “desperate” for a deal. He has threatened to wipe out Iranian civilization and extended the ceasefire at the last minute. White House spokeswoman Kelly: “This president is the most transparent and accessible in history.”

II. The Gift That Lasted 36 Hours

The Cooper/Schmitt analysis — “U.S. Troops in Middle East Wait for the Next Big Moment” — documents what Project Freedom actually was in military terms: 50,000 US troops, two carrier strike groups, the 82nd Airborne in an undisclosed location, 2,500 Marines of the 31st Marine Expeditionary Unit, several hundred Special Operations forces, all on standby while the White House gave contradictory signals about whether the war was over, paused, or a trifle.

Ashley Cai/The New York Times

The 82nd Airborne could be used to take Kharg Island, Iran’s main oil export hub. Or to seize an airfield. Officials note that holding territory in a country a quarter the size of the continental United States with 90 million people “would be challenging.” The Special Operations forces could be used in a mission aimed at Iran’s highly enriched uranium at Isfahan. Nobody has been given orders to do any of these things. They are waiting.

What Project Freedom produced in 36 hours: four ships transited Monday, zero Tuesday. The Maersk Alliance Fairfax — a vehicle carrier flying the US flag — made it through. Crowley’s CS Anthem made it through. Two ships. Three ships total for the operation’s duration. Before the war: 130 per day.

The shipping industry’s verdict, per the Eavis/Schmidt reporting — “Shipping Firms Are Left Guessing by Confusion in the Strait of Hormuz” — was delivered in the clearest possible terms. Hapag-Lloyd: “Our risk assessment remains unchanged, and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed for Hapag-Lloyd transits until further notice.” BIMCO’s Larsen: “It is unclear whether Project Freedom is sustainable in the longer run.” Kpler analyst Subasic: “a low chance of fully reopening Hormuz quickly.” The International Maritime Organization: 22,500 mariners on 1,550 vessels remain trapped in the Persian Gulf.

Ships and boats in the Strait of Hormuz near Oman on Monday.Credit...Reuters

Hegseth, Tuesday morning, declared that the Iranians “don’t control the strait.” Cooper’s annotation, same briefing: “It is clear the U.S. military is not in control of the Strait of Hormuz either.” Four ships transited. Pre-war: 130. The Fujairah Oil Industry Zone burned. The South Korean ship burned. The ceasefire held, technically.

III. The Persian Gulf Strait Authority

A billboard in Tehran last week, depicting a closed Strait of Hormuz over the mouth of President Trump.Credit...Arash Khamooshi for The New York Times

While Project Freedom launched and paused, Iran was building the administrative architecture of permanent control.

The Patil reporting — “Iran Announces a New System for Traffic in the Strait of Hormuz” — documents it. Ships seeking to cross must now receive an email from the “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” laying out transit regulations, obtain a transit permit, and adhere to the Iran-designated corridor. The IRGC Navy issued a separate warning: only the Iran-designated corridor is safe. Deviation will be met with “action.”

Iran’s judiciary head Mohseni-Ejei: the era of an “artificial order” in the strait is over. Iran will pursue “legal support for the sovereignty of the ‘new order’ in this region.”

The Eyre doomsday weapon thesis — filed in this series from the Atlantic piece in Week Three — has now reached its fullest expression. The toll road is now a bureaucracy with an email address. The “new order” now has a name. The Persian Gulf Strait Authority is the institutional form of what Haass described as the permanent implicit control Iran has established. Even when the strait reopens — and it will — the email address will remain. The permit system will remain. The knowledge that it can be closed again will remain.

The Gottsegen Atlantic analysis — “Iran’s Unexpected Resilience” — places this in strategic context. Iran’s Deputy Speaker Nikzad, Sunday: the country “will not back down” from its position on the strait “and it will not return to its prewar conditions.” Tolls are reportedly being collected in cryptocurrency and Chinese yuan. The mosquito fleet that Project Freedom was designed to overcome typically deploys “between 20 and 40 small boats” per CENTCOM’s own Cooper — the six destroyed Monday was a reduced deployment. The Shahed-136 drone costs tens of thousands. The anti-air munition to shoot it down costs more. The war’s asymmetric mathematics, still running.

IV. The One-Page Memo

The Fassihi reporting — “Iran and the U.S. Consider a One-Page Plan to End Hostilities” — is the most important diplomatic development since Islamabad I. Three senior Iranian officials confirmed it. Three immediately actionable points: lift the US blockade on Iranian ships and ports, open the strait to commercial traffic, end the fighting — for 30 days while both sides negotiate a comprehensive deal.

The nuclear gap that has blocked every previous round remains precisely where it was. The US demands: Iran agree in principle to hand over its entire enriched uranium stockpile, close three nuclear facilities, suspend enrichment for 20 years. Iran proposes: dilute some uranium, transfer the rest to Russia, suspend enrichment for 10 to 15 years, no mention of the three facilities.

Iran would agree to never seek a nuclear weapon. The duration of the suspension is unresolved. The major issues — nuclear program future, sanctions relief, frozen Iranian funds — are left for the 30-day window.

Trump, asked Thursday whether Iran had agreed to hand over the nuclear dust: “Yes.” Then: “When they agree, it doesn’t mean much, because the next day they forget.”

This is the negotiation. Both sides are debating a one-page framework while simultaneously firing at each other’s ships. The Sanger framing — filed in this series from Week Four — is the operating analytical reality: “Trump is impulsive and temperamental; Iran’s leadership is stubborn and tenacious. Trump demands immediate results; Iran’s leadership plays the long game.”

The Fassihi IRGC power structure piece remains the essential context: the generals are making the decisions. Araghchi has been marginalized. The pragmatists want a deal. The generals want to preserve their leverage. The one-page memo is the pragmatists’ instrument. Whether the generals authorize it is the question on which everything else depends.

V. The Xi Summit — Six Days Away

The summit is May 14. Six days from Friday. Trump postponed it once because of the war. He cannot postpone it again without signaling weakness to the one adversary he most needs to appear strong against.

The Buckley reporting — “China’s Top Diplomat Meets Iran’s Foreign Minister in Beijing” — documents what happened Wednesday in Beijing. Wang Yi and Araghchi met. Wang Yi: “A comprehensive cessation of hostilities should not be delayed, any reigniting of hostilities is even less desirable, and sticking to negotiations is particularly important.” Wang Yi also: China supports Iran’s right to peaceful nuclear energy. “China supports Iran’s efforts to safeguard its national sovereignty and security.”

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi of Iran meeting with Foreign Minister Wang Yi of China in Beijing on Wednesday.Credit...Seyed Abbas Araqchi, via Telegram, via Reuters

China is walking both sides of the line simultaneously — pressing Iran to deal while validating Iran’s core position. That is the same dual track the IRGC is running militarily: firing at US ships while Araghchi signals diplomatic flexibility. The Chinese and Iranian dual tracks are operating in parallel, probably not by accident.

Bessent on Fox News Monday: “Let’s see if China step up with some diplomacy and get the Iranians to open the strait. Iran is the largest state sponsor of terrorism, and China has been buying 90 percent of their energy, so they are funding the largest state sponsor of terrorism.” China responded by invoking its 2021 blocking measure ordering Chinese companies not to comply with US sanctions on Iranian oil purchases.

The summit agenda has shifted entirely. What was supposed to be about tariffs and trade is now about Hormuz and nuclear dust. Trump goes to Beijing needing to be seen as a man who ended a war. Xi goes to Beijing having already positioned China as the indispensable diplomatic actor. Both men want this done. The one-page memo is the instrument they both need signed before May 14.

VI. The $25.7 Billion That Wasn’t Announced

The Wong reporting — “As Stockpiles Fall, U.S. Sells More Missiles Worth $17 Billion to Gulf Nations” — is the week’s most quietly devastating accountability document. The State Department authorized $25.7 billion in total arms sales last Friday. Only $8.6 billion was announced publicly. The hidden $17.1 billion: Kuwait $9.3 billion, UAE $6.25 billion, Bahrain $1.625 billion — all Patriot interceptor missiles, expansions of previous congressional sales requiring no new authorization, therefore requiring no announcement.

A Patriot missile launcher in Kuwait City in 2019. The United States is selling Kuwait another $9.3 billion worth of interceptor missiles.Credit...Noufal Ibrahim/EPA, via Shutterstock

Total Patriot missiles ordered across all Friday sales: approximately 5,250. Annual US production: approximately 600. The United States and its Gulf partners have burned through three years of Patriot production in this war. The new orders would take years to fill. When Raytheon and Lockheed Martin send missiles to foreign customers, that means fewer for the United States.

Representative Meeks: “Again invoking emergency authorities, this time for over $25 billion in major weapons transfers, only highlights the administration’s failure to manage this war of choice. It did not do the necessary homework before going to war, and now it is rushing to resupply Gulf partners while unnecessarily bypassing Congress.”

The hidden $17.1 billion is the Accounting’s companion piece — what the announced numbers conceal is often more significant than what they reveal.

VII. The Pope, the Ice Cube, and the Reflecting Pool

Three scenes from the week that belong in the record.

Scene one: Rubio at the White House briefing room Tuesday, smiling, calling on reporters by blazer color, invoking Ice Cube’s 1992 “Check Yo Self” to describe Iran’s diplomatic options. “They better check themselves before they wreck themselves in the direction that they’re going.” Hours later Trump paused Project Freedom. Rubio had not been informed.

Pope Leo XIV meeting with Secretary of State Marco Rubio at the Vatican in a photograph released Thursday by Vatican Media.Credit...Simone Risoluti/Vatican Media, via Reuters

Scene two: Rubio at the Vatican Thursday, sitting in the pope’s private library, listening “in concentration” while Pope Leo XIV spoke. The State Department reported they discussed “the situation in the Middle East and topics of mutual interest.” Trump had accused Leo of “endangering a lot of Catholics” and claiming the pope thought “it’s OK for Iran to have a nuclear weapon.” The pope has never said this. Leo to reporters: “If someone wants to criticize me for proclaiming the Gospel, let him do so truthfully. The church for years has spoken against all nuclear weapons.” The same secretary of state who invoked Ice Cube on Tuesday sat and listened to the pope on Thursday. The Rich/Motoko reporting — “Rubio Meets Pope Leo Amid Tensions Between the Trump Administration and the Vatican” — captures the scene in full.

Scene three: Trump at the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool renovation Thursday night, shortly after CENTCOM confirmed US strikes on Iranian military sites in retaliation for Iranian attacks on three US destroyers. Construction workers in hard hats. A MAGA hat. Trump providing a lengthy update on the renovation — it would look “like a swimming pool, but industrial strength — much stronger.” A reporter asked why he was focusing on renovation projects while the country was at war. Trump: “A question like that is a disgrace to our country.”

He then warned that Iran would become “one big glow.”

The three scenes are the week in a frame: the secretary of state contradicted by his president, then listening to the pope; the president discussing industrial-strength swimming pools while three destroyers were fired on in the Strait of Hormuz.

VIII. Lebanon: The Ceasefire Within the Ceasefire

The Ward reporting — “Israel Says It Killed a Hezbollah Commander Outside Beirut” — documents the week’s most significant Lebanon development. Ahmed Ali Balout — commander of Hezbollah’s Radwan force, the group’s elite commando unit — killed in Wednesday’s Israeli strike in Beirut’s southern suburbs. First attack near the Lebanese capital since the Lebanon ceasefire took effect. Israel and Hezbollah have continued daily attacks in southern Lebanon throughout the ceasefire, but Beirut had been spared until Wednesday.

Israeli broadcasters reported the strike was coordinated with the United States. Netanyahu and Defense Minister Katz joint statement: “This is how we act, and this is how we will continue to act.” Lebanon death toll: more than 2,700. One million displaced at peak. Lebanon-Israel talks still scheduled for Washington next Thursday and Friday.

The Goldbaum finding from southern Lebanon remains the essential ground truth: gravediggers in Majdal Zoun preparing 20 graves — nine filled, eleven waiting. Hezbollah’s fiber-optic cable drones, immune to electronic jamming, continue to operate across the six-mile occupation zone and beyond. Israel occupying a swathe of southern Lebanese territory it says it will hold indefinitely.

The Trump administration pushed for the Lebanon ceasefire to preserve the diplomatic track with Iran. The Beirut strike — reportedly coordinated with the US — threatens the very diplomatic track it was designed to protect. Iran has demanded an end to Israeli strikes in Lebanon as a condition of comprehensive negotiations. Israel has just struck Beirut.

IX. The Oil Companies Are Fine

The Schmidt/Elliott reporting — “Shell Reports Nearly $7 Billion Profit Amid ‘Unprecedented Disruption’” — completes the week’s financial picture. Shell Q1 adjusted profit: $6.92 billion — up 24% year on year, more than twice the previous quarter. BP: more than doubled to $3.2 billion. TotalEnergies: $5.4 billion. The European oil majors, reporting in the same week. Shell CEO Sawan: “unprecedented disruption in global energy markets.” He did not mean that as a criticism.

The IEA, in its latest report: “Energy markets and economies around the world need to brace for significant disruptions in the months to come” — even if Hormuz reopens soon. The damage to Qatar’s Ras Laffan LNG facility alone could delay expected global LNG supply growth by two years. The disruption, in other words, is now partly structural and not fully reversible by a one-page memo.

The war’s financial beneficiaries are reporting quarterly earnings. The war’s financial victims are in the record assembled across 72 essays: the taxi driver in Malawi who postponed his wedding, the nurse in Zimbabwe considering sending her children away, the glass cutter in Firozabad who found four days of work in a month, the Jordanian camel keeper whose entire tourist season disappeared. The oil companies are fine. The record shows the difference.

X. The Child on the Swing

On Thursday evening, as the UAE conducted retaliatory strikes on Iranian shipping infrastructure at Qeshm Island and Bandar Abbas — strikes that two Iranian officials said targeted the Bahman pier, a ship-making facility, and an air force base — someone photographed the Hormozgan province beachfront.

Take a look at the lead in visual again. The HORMOZGAN sign standing in shallow water, the letters yellow against the cloudy sky. Behind it, a massive column of black smoke rising from the strikes. On the right side of the frame, a child on a swing, suspended mid-arc, back to the camera, facing the water.

The war’s newest front, photographed from a beach. A child on a swing, oblivious, or perhaps not oblivious — perhaps simply having no alternative to being on the swing, the smoke rising, the water flat, the sign spelling out the name of the province that contains the world’s most important waterway.

There is nothing else to say about the photograph. It says everything.
