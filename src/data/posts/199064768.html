Both parties hold the scissors. Both parties wear the chains. The redistricting spiral constrains the people cutting the map as surely as it constrains the voters living on it.

Recently, inside the Capitol, the bipartisan Problem Solvers Caucus held a meeting about the redistricting crisis that has consumed this series for thirty instalments. The members discussed what they called the “death spiral” — the escalating, retaliatory gerrymandering war between Republicans and Democrats that is steadily draining competitive elections from American democracy.

The meeting took place a short walk from where House Democrats were beginning to plot their next round of revenge on gerrymandering Republicans.

Russell Berman reported the scene for The Atlantic on May 23, and what he found was a portrait of institutional paralysis so complete it would be comic if the stakes were not so high. The Problem Solvers have identified the problem. They have not identified a solution. They have not endorsed a proposal. They have not drafted a bill. They have agreed, in the words of co-chair Tom Suozzi, a Democrat from New York, that gerrymandering is “a problem.”

That is the state of the institutional resistance to the redistricting death spiral: a bipartisan caucus, meeting inside the building where the laws are made, has achieved consensus that a problem exists.

The Problem Solvers Caucus on the steps of the Capitol. They agree gerrymandering is a problem. They have not solved it.

The Speed Gap

To appreciate what that consensus is worth, consider what has happened in the three weeks since the Supreme Court decided Louisiana v. Callais.

Tennessee convened a special session on May 5 and eliminated its lone majority-minority congressional district. Governor Bill Lee signed the new map into law the same week. The state’s delegation is projected to go from 8R-1D to 9R-0D. Steve Cohen, the only Democrat in the delegation, had his Memphis district carved into pieces.

Alabama petitioned the Supreme Court to lift injunctions against its previously blocked congressional map. The Court obliged. The state will now hold special primary elections under a map that federal courts had twice ruled violated the Voting Rights Act.

Louisiana suspended its own House primaries — discarding 45,000 absentee ballots in the process, as Part XXIX of this series documented — so that the legislature could redraw its congressional maps under the new rules.

Florida passed a new map on April 29, the same day as the Callais ruling. Governor DeSantis signed it on May 4. The map shifts four Democratic-held districts toward Republicans.

Georgia’s Governor Kemp called a special redistricting session for June 17 — the day after the state’s primary runoffs, so that the current Republican majority can lock in new maps before November’s elections potentially change the legislature’s composition.

South Carolina Republicans are debating whether to carve up the district long held by Representative James Clyburn.

That is six states in three weeks. Maps redrawn, districts eliminated, primaries suspended, ballots discarded, special sessions convened. The machinery of redistricting moves with extraordinary speed when the people operating it want it to move.

The Problem Solvers Caucus, in the same three weeks, agreed that a problem exists.

Classic gerrymandering: Districts drawn to maximize one party’s advantage regardless of voter will

Three Tiers of Reform

Berman’s reporting captures something this series has circled for months: the reform landscape is not a movement. It is three separate conversations happening at three different altitudes, none of them connected to the levers of power.

The first tier is the Problem Solvers themselves. Co-chair Brian Fitzpatrick, a Republican from Pennsylvania, represents one of just three GOP-held districts that voted for Kamala Harris in 2024. He supports independent redistricting commissions but told Berman that Congress cannot require their use — only incentivise them through federal funding. He joined every other Republican in opposing a 2022 Democratic bill that would have banned partisan gerrymandering nationwide. His co-chair Suozzi represents a Long Island district that Trump narrowly carried. Both have personal incentives to oppose gerrymandering: their purple seats are exactly the kind both parties target in redistricting. But neither has a vehicle. The caucus has not endorsed even the funding-incentive approach Fitzpatrick described. They are, by their own admission, at the stage of agreeing that there is a problem.

The second tier is the structural reformers. Lee Drutman, a political scientist who published a book in early 2020 warning of the “doom loop” created by America’s two-party system, told Berman the system is now “doomier and loopier.” His prescription is proportional representation — multimember House districts where seats are allocated by vote share, producing a Congress with several parties rather than two. Harris, who is considering another presidential bid, mentioned multimember districts in an interview last week as part of what she called a “no-bad-ideas brainstorm” to “strengthen democracy.” Drutman called the Callais ruling a potential “hinge point” — a moment when the rules changed so dramatically that systemic reform might finally gain traction.

It might. Proportional representation would solve the gerrymandering problem by making single-member districts irrelevant. It would also require rebuilding the entire electoral architecture of the House of Representatives. This is the correct diagnosis attached to a prescription that requires a patient who does not yet exist.

The third tier is the primary reformers. Nick Troiano, the executive director of Unite America, argues that opening primaries to independent voters is a more achievable path to reducing polarisation than restructuring the House. His organisation invested heavily in statewide ballot initiatives to replicate Alaska’s system — a nonpartisan primary advancing four candidates to a ranked-choice general election. The campaigns lost nearly everywhere they appeared on the 2024 ballot. Troiano told Berman that the strategy was not a failure; the timing was off. He believes that the current redistricting brawl will generate exactly the “populist backlash” needed to make primary reform viable.

He may be right. He may also be describing the reform equivalent of the Alexander memoir from Part XXX of this series: the correct argument, arriving in a form that cannot yet change anything.

State legislatures in special session — redrawing the maps at extraordinary speed after the Supreme Court ruling

The Forty-Five Percent

There is a number in Berman’s reporting that deserves more attention than it received: forty-five percent.

In a Gallup poll released earlier this year, 45 percent of respondents identified themselves as independents — the highest percentage Gallup has ever recorded. The two major parties are shrinking. The fastest-growing share of the American electorate belongs to neither of them.

And both parties are working to shut that 45 percent out.

Closed primaries exclude independent voters from the elections that actually determine who holds office in gerrymandered districts. In a district drawn to guarantee one party’s victory, the primary is the election — and in states with closed primaries, a growing plurality of voters cannot participate in it. They are, as Fitzpatrick told Berman, effectively disenfranchised. “As a matter of justice, it’s wrong,” he said. “And it has a corrosive effect on the House floor. You can tell the people who live in closed-primary states. They conduct themselves very differently.”

This series documented exactly that mechanism in Part XXIX, where Louisiana’s new closed primary filtered out the independent voters who might have softened Cassidy’s defeat. Fitzpatrick is describing the same dynamic from the inside.

Garret Graves, a Republican who represented Louisiana in the House for a decade before redistricting eliminated his seat, was even more direct. Closed primaries, he told Berman, “distort democracy. They distort free markets.” He said that “hundreds of times” he had colleagues — Democrats and Republicans — tell him they knew the right vote but could not cast it because they would be primaried. The closed primary does not just exclude voters. It constrains the people who are supposed to represent them.

Graves suggested that a lawsuit challenging closed primaries as unconstitutional disenfranchisement might succeed. “I would really welcome something like that,” he said.

He was not looking to Congress for the answer.

The Trap

There is a structural reason why none of these tiers can gain traction, and Berman identifies it precisely: as soon as one party adopts a reform proposal, the other party opposes it.

Ranked-choice voting enjoyed bipartisan support until Maine adopted it and Democrats started winning. Now Republicans oppose it reflexively. Open primaries face resistance from leaders of both parties because the model explicitly challenges their dominance. In California, top Democrats have never loved the state’s voter-approved nonpartisan primary, and the risk of being shut out of the governor’s runoff this November has prompted a new effort to scrap it. Louisiana Republicans ditched the state’s open primary — the jungle primary system — in 2024, and this series documented the consequences in Part XXIX.

The trap is this: any reform that would reduce the power of the two parties requires the two parties to enact it. Any reform that one party embraces becomes, by that act, a partisan proposal that the other party must oppose. The system that needs fixing is the same system that prevents the fix.

Harris’s mention of multimember districts will now make proportional representation a “Democratic idea” in Republican circles. Fitzpatrick’s support for open primaries already marks him as suspect in his own party. Trump said it himself, on Fox News, speaking of Fitzpatrick: “He likes voting against Trump. You know what happens with that? It doesn’t work out well.”

That is not a prediction. After Cassidy, after Massie, after Raffensperger, it is a description.

Where Democracy Fights Back

Every instalment of this series asks the same question. In this one, the answer is harder to find than usual.

45% of Americans now identify as independents — the largest group, and the one both parties work hardest to exclude

The Problem Solvers cannot solve the problem because they occupy a caucus, not a majority, and the majority is busy drawing maps. The structural reformers have the right diagnosis but a prescription that requires rebuilding institutions from the ground up. The primary reformers have a more achievable goal but lost every campaign they ran two years ago and are betting on a backlash that has not yet arrived.

And yet.

Forty-five percent of Americans now identify with neither party. That is not a caucus. It is not a movement. It is not organised, funded, or represented. But it is the largest share of the electorate, and it is growing, and both parties are working to exclude it from the elections that matter most. The parties are consolidating power inside a structure that represents fewer and fewer of the people it governs.

Troiano may be right that the backlash is coming. Drutman may be right that Callais is the hinge point. Fitzpatrick may be right that open primaries would transform the House floor. None of them can make it happen from where they stand.

Graves, who lost his own seat to redistricting, was asked about Congress acting on any of this. His answer was the most honest sentence in Berman’s reporting.

“I have zero hope,” he said.

This series has spent thirty instalments documenting why. It will spend the next ones asking whether Graves is right — starting on June 17, when Georgia’s legislature convenes to redraw the maps that will determine who represents four million Black Georgians in 2028, the day after the primary runoffs that will decide whether the legislators doing the drawing still hold power in November.

The machinery does not stop. The question, as always, is whether anyone is building a machine that runs the other way.
