An early arrival: satire normally waits for Sunday around here. This one couldn’t. Grand Theft Auto VI launches on Thursday, and by Sunday 12.5 million Americans will be four days into their crime spree, a roofing company in Texas will still be closed, and this essay would be about as current as a Pong console. Some jokes have a shelf life. This one expires Thursday morning.

On November 17, 2026, Sophia Nguyen reported in The Atlantic that the release of Grand Theft Auto VI on Thursday, November 19 may bring a good part of the North American workweek to a halt. A roofing company in San Antonio expects to be closed for two or three days, because the boss is booking off too. Logitech has invented a floating holiday for gaming. The U.S. Army’s 9th Engineer Battalion at Fort Stewart, Georgia, offered soldiers a four-day pass if they re-enlisted by November 14, and forty-three of them signed up for at least two more years of military service in exchange for ninety-six hours of virtual car theft. One survey estimates that 12.5 million American adults will rearrange their work or school schedules to play. The game has already taken in $1 billion in presales.

I read all of this with the sinking feeling of a man who has, once again, not been invited to the party.

Not because I want to play. Let me be clear about that. It is because the whole premise, that a day of play costs someone a day of productivity, no longer applies to me. I left Rogers in 2021. Since May 2025 I have published more than 2,000 essays without being paid for a single one of them. I am, by any reasonable measure, among the most productive unpaid people in Toronto. My output has risen in exact inverse proportion to my salary. When my income reached zero, my productivity went vertical. An economist would call this a labour-market anomaly. My household calls it something else, usually around suppertime.

I should confess that when I first heard of GTA, I assumed it was about the Greater Toronto Area. We had that acronym first, or at least we thought we did. In Toronto, GTA is what the traffic reporter says just before telling you the 401 is backed up from Mississauga to Pickering. It never occurred to me that some studio in Scotland had borrowed it for a game about stealing cars. Mind you, the Greater Toronto Area already has a game in which you sit in a car for hours with no clear objective. It’s called rush hour, and nobody has ever pre-ordered it.

So if 12.5 million people can request time off for GTA VI, I felt that I, too, should be entitled to request time off. The difficulty is that there is nobody to request it from. I am the employee, the manager, the human resources department and the union steward. I therefore did what any former escalations man would do. I put it in writing.

What follows is the complete file.

FORM HR-0: REQUEST FOR LEAVE OF ABSENCE FROM RETIREMENT

Human Resources Department of One, Office of the Canadian Snowman, Toronto

Employee name: Stephen Gordon
Position: Retiree (unpaid, full-time, no benefits beyond those already earned)
Years of service in current role: 5
Leave requested: Thursday, November 19, 2026, through Sunday, November 22, 2026
Reason for leave (check one):
☐ Bereavement
☐ Medical
☐ Personal
☒ Other (please specify): To play Grand Theft Auto VI, like the soldiers

Employee statement:
I am aware that I have never played a Grand Theft Auto game. I am aware that I was born in 1951 and that the first Grand Theft Auto came out in 1997, when I was 46 and already finding the VCR remote a challenge. I am aware that the developers say the main storyline of GTA VI takes at least 80 hours, which, at my current pace of five essays a day, represents roughly 400 essays not written. I submit this request anyway, as a matter of generational principle. If a 26-year-old roofer in San Antonio can have the week off, so can I.

MANAGEMENT RESPONSE

Dear Employee,

Thank you for your request. Management has reviewed it carefully, which took about four seconds, since Management also wrote it.

Before a decision can be rendered, Management requires clarification on three points.

First, qualifications. Your file indicates that your entire competitive gaming history consists of table hockey, air hockey and Pong, in descending order of success. Please confirm.

Second, Management notes the following entry in your personnel record, dated approximately Boxing Day 1975: “Lost at Pong. Blamed knob.” Please explain.

Third, Management would like to know what, precisely, you intend to do with four days off from a job that does not exist, given that the last time you took a day off, you wrote three essays about why you needed one.

Regards,
Management

EMPLOYEE REPLY

Dear Management,

Yes. It was Pong.

But Management should understand that Pong was not where my decline began. It was the third stage. The first was table hockey with my brothers: little tin NHL players on rods, which you pushed and twirled until somebody’s goalie snapped off at the ankles. I held my own at that. You could see the puck coming, and it moved at roughly the speed of a Toronto streetcar.

Then came air hockey, and the world changed. Here was a table that floated the puck on a cushion of air, which to a young man in the early 1970s seemed like something out of the space program. It was magical. It was also fast. Terribly fast. The puck left my brother’s paddle and arrived in my goal before my brain had been told it had left. I would still be standing there, paddle raised, defending a goal that had already happened.

That should have been my warning. If I couldn’t handle the speed of air hockey, I was doomed with anything that came after it, right up to and including that new-fangled, acronym-stealing GTA thingamajig.

Then came Pong. For younger readers, which I understand is now nearly everyone, Pong was a game Atari put into arcades in 1972, and in a home version sold through Sears for Christmas 1975. It consisted of two white rectangles and one white square on a black screen. The rectangles were paddles. The square was a ball. You turned a knob to move your paddle up and down, and the object was to bounce the ball past the other fellow. There was a sound, a sort of electronic bloop, which was the entire soundtrack. There was no story, nobody stole a car, and there was no radio to listen to outside the vehicle, because there was no vehicle. There was a ball. It went back and forth. That was the plot.

And I could not win.

I want Management to grasp the scale of this. GTA VI, according to the fans who spoke to The Atlantic, will let you order a burger, wander the alleys between buildings, and use your car’s side mirrors, a feature a Detroit driving instructor is hoping for because the last game didn’t have it. Pong had two moving parts, and I was defeated by both of them. The ball slipped past my paddle with a regularity I can only describe as personal. I later spent 18 years in the Office of the President at Rogers handling escalated complaints from regulators, journalists and executives, and not one of them made me feel as helpless as that small white square.

As for the knob: I stand by it. It was sticky.

As for what I would do with four days off, I would ask Management to note that the people in the article are making real sacrifices. A software engineer in New York volunteered to work through Thanksgiving to guarantee her time off. A sportswriter in Bakersfield is skipping the end of the tennis season, and picking up Walmart delivery gigs to make up the lost income, so he can spend his tenth wedding anniversary on the couch with his wife and a controller. Forty-three soldiers signed up for two more years in the United States Army. Two more years. For a long weekend. I respect that level of commitment, and I would like to demonstrate my own.

Regards,
Employee

MANAGEMENT RESPONSE (SECOND)

Dear Employee,

Management has considered your reply and wishes to raise the following concerns.

1. Coverage. Who will write the essays while you are away? Management has reviewed the roster and finds that the only other staff member is Claude, which has been very helpful, but which does not post anything on its own and would almost certainly want to check the sources first.

2. Precedent. If Management grants you four days off from retirement, what is to stop you from requesting four further days off from those four days off? Retirement is already, technically, a leave of absence from work. A leave from a leave is a recursion. Management is not equipped to process recursions. Management came up through customer service.

3. Compensation. The sportswriter is replacing lost income with delivery gigs. Your income is zero. If you take four days off, your income will still be zero. Management cannot determine whether this is a loss, a gain, or the most efficient financial arrangement in the history of the Canadian pension system.

4. Hardware. Management has inspected the premises and found no PlayStation 5, no Xbox, and no console of any description. Management did locate a television, a laptop, a phone and a dog who does not care about any of this.

5. Fitness for duty. The article describes young people stocking up on energy drinks, stringing up neon lights and planning to play until they can no longer see the screen. Management reminds Employee that his eyes are 75 years old and have been giving out on their own schedule for some time, without any help from Rockstar Games.

Regards,
Management

EMPLOYEE REPLY (SECOND)

Dear Management,

On coverage: fair point.

On precedent: I take your point about recursion, but I would observe that Logitech’s new floating holiday does not even have to be spent on GTA. The company’s chief communications officer was quite firm with The Atlantic on that score. Logitech has therefore given its staff a gaming holiday that is not necessarily for gaming. If Logitech can do that, surely I can take a gaming holiday from a job that is not a job. We are operating on the same philosophical plane.

On hardware: I concede I have no console. I am prepared to buy one or, as we say in my demographic, to have one of the young people in the house set it up and then explain it to me three times.

On fitness for duty: unfair, but accurate.

In fairness to my original request, I did make an effort. On Monday evening I watched the official trailer on my laptop, at full screen, with the volume up. It lasted about a minute and a half. Somewhere around the forty-second mark, a speedboat outran a police helicopter, a man jumped a car off a parking garage, and I had to put the laptop down and look at a fixed point on the wall, the way you do on the ferry to Prince Edward Island. Dior, who had been watching with me, left the room.

I would also like to make a broader argument, on behalf of my generation.

The Atlantic describes the GTA VI launch as a “gigantic collective snow day.” I understand the appeal. But I grew up when a snow day was an actual snow day, involving actual snow, and the principal entertainment was going outside in it. We did not need 80 hours of storyline. We had a toboggan and a hill, and the storyline was: go down hill, walk up hill, repeat until someone cries. Nobody preordered the hill. The hill did not take in $1 billion in presales. The hill had been available, free of charge, since the last ice age.

When the first video games did arrive, we did not organize our lives around them. We did not re-enlist. We put in a quarter, lost to a white square, and went home to watch whichever channel the rabbit ears could find. The joy, Management, was not in winning. It was in not worrying about it. There was no leaderboard. Nobody was streaming. No famous Twitch personality was posting on X to assure his followers that he would be fully immersed. If you lost at Pong, the only witness was whoever had beaten you, and they had the decency to keep it to themselves for at least a week.

That is what I miss: the freedom of not caring whether I was any good at the thing, because the thing was a ball going back and forth and nobody had yet worked out how to charge seventy dollars for it.

I therefore wish to amend my request. I no longer want four days off to play GTA VI. I want four days off to not play GTA VI, deliberately, in solidarity with every Baby Boomer who never asked for an open world because the one outside the front door was already open enough, thank you.

Regards,
Employee

MANAGEMENT DECISION

Dear Employee,

Management has reviewed your amended request to take four days off from retirement in order to not play a video game you were never going to play.

Management finds that you have been doing exactly this, without interruption, for 75 years.

Request approved, retroactive to October 5, 1951.

Management further notes that you have never once called in sick from not playing Grand Theft Auto, and wishes to recognize this record of service. If Management had a budget, you would be named Employee of the Quarter. Management observes that you already won that three times at Rogers, plus Employee of the Year, and suggests you stop being greedy.

Please return to your desk. There are five essays due before supper.

Regards,
Management

P.S. Management has located the Pong console in the basement. The knob is not sticky. It was never sticky.

Epilogue

On Thursday morning, as an estimated 12.5 million American adults call in with “personal reasons,” as a roofing company in south-central Texas goes quiet and as forty-three soldiers in Georgia begin a 96-hour crime spree with the blessing of their career counsellor, I will be at my desk in Toronto, doing what I always do. Productively. For nothing.

There is a certain freedom in it. Nobody is waiting for my launch day. Nobody preordered me. And if the white square goes past my paddle one more time, I will simply write an essay about it, which, as it happens, is exactly what I have done.

Bloop.

What is satire and what is not: the GTA VI launch details (the November 19 release, the $1 billion in presales, the 12.5 million schedule changes, Logitech’s holiday and the 9th Engineer Battalion’s 96-hour pass) come from Sophia Nguyen’s reporting in The Atlantic. The table hockey, the air hockey, my trouble winning at Pong and my assumption that GTA meant the Greater Toronto Area are all true. The knob remains under investigation.
