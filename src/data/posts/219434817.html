Stephen Gordon October 10, 2026

“Who can stop him?”

That question was put to Sarah Fitzpatrick of The Atlantic by a former FBI official, and it appears near the end of her October 9, 2026 report on Kash Patel’s planned trip to Russia. It is the most honest sentence in the piece, and possibly the most honest sentence anyone inside the Federal Bureau of Investigation has spoken on the record this year. It is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a structural observation. The officials who spent their careers defending the United States against Russian intelligence services can tighten the security protocols, sweep the hotel rooms, brief the travelling party, and pray. What they cannot do is tell the director no.

So, by every available account, he is going.

What Is Actually Known

The trip is not a rumour that surfaced this week. It was first reported on July 20, 2026, by Nahal Toosi, Kyle Cheney and John Sakellariadis of Politico, citing a U.S. official familiar with the plans: Patel would visit Moscow first, then St. Petersburg, in mid-October, with the host likely to be the FSB, the Federal Security Service that succeeded the KGB. The Washington Examiner, following the Politico report the same day, put the dates at October 14 and 15. Reuters reported the planned trip that summer as well.

Three weeks before the American midterm elections, in other words, the director of the agency responsible for defending those elections against foreign interference intends to be a guest of the security service most consistently identified by American intelligence as the source of that interference.

What has happened since July is, officially, nothing. The FBI has not confirmed the trip. The White House has not confirmed the trip. In July, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov told Russian reporters he had no information on the matter, adding helpfully that such visits are not usually made public, according to the Russian state news agency TASS. Neither the Bureau, the White House, Peskov, nor the Russian embassy in Washington answered Fitzpatrick’s repeated requests for comment.

What has happened unofficially is considerably more interesting.

The Campaign Nobody Answered

According to Fitzpatrick, current and former FBI officials described a quiet internal effort to persuade senior leadership to cancel the trip. When that effort, and other attempts to send concerns up the chain, went unanswered, some of those officials took their worries to Congress. Seventeen current and former FBI, Justice Department and intelligence officials, along with others familiar with the situation, told her they were deeply troubled — most on condition of anonymity, because they feared retaliation.

That fear is not hypothetical, and readers of this series will recognise its source. Earlier this year Fitzpatrick reported that Patel had alarmed colleagues with episodes of excessive drinking and unexplained absences, which some regarded as potential national-security vulnerabilities. Patel disputes that reporting and responded with a $250 million defamation lawsuit, filed April 21, 2026, against The Atlantic and Fitzpatrick personally. When the reporter covering your agency is being sued for a quarter of a billion dollars by the man who runs it, the incentive to speak on the record is, shall we say, attenuated.

The result was a letter. Representative Jamie Raskin, the ranking Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, and Representative Jim Himes, the ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, wrote to Patel this week asking him, in the interest of national security, to call off the trip. The letter, which Fitzpatrick obtained, asks why the FBI director would visit a hostile power that is committing war crimes, running influence operations against U.S. elections, attacking American critical infrastructure, and helping American adversaries target and kill American soldiers.

It also says something that members of Congress do not usually put in writing to a sitting FBI director: that Patel’s “affinity for the Russian government” does not inspire confidence in his ability to safeguard the secrets he carries.

The two ranking members requested a closed-door briefing by 5 p.m. on October 12, 2026. They want to know what prompted the trip, the itinerary, and the planned meetings. And then the letter asks for three things that tell you exactly what its authors are worried about: details of any social activities, “a full accounting” of Patel’s whereabouts during downtime, and the names of everyone travelling with him — specifically including any private citizens or members of President Trump’s family.

You do not ask for an accounting of a man’s downtime unless you have reason to wonder what he does with it.

The Downtime Question

Here the series’ earlier instalments stop being background and become evidence.

Part VII documented the May 2026 Senate Appropriations hearing at which Patel, pressed on the drinking allegations, agreed to alcohol testing. Part IX documented the September 15, 2026 Senate Judiciary hearing at which it became clear that the pledge had never been followed up on. Nobody, so far as the public record shows, ever administered the test, and nobody in the administration appears to have asked.

That is the same unanswered question now travelling to Moscow. A counterintelligence officer does not need to believe any particular allegation about Patel to see the problem. The question is not whether the director drinks. The question is whether a hostile service believes it has something to work with — and the Russian services, whose methods are documented in decades of declassified case files, have never needed a confirmed vulnerability to begin probing for one. As one person familiar with the situation told Fitzpatrick, there is not a single American official who visits Russia without being targeted.

Raskin put it more bluntly. Patel, he told The Atlantic, would be “a target and sitting duck” for every kind of Russian espionage and influence operation.

The Same Man, Saying Both Things

The difficulty with writing about Patel and Russia is that he has made both cases, often within the same month.

In August 2026, speaking to Reuters, Patel described the FBI’s new approach to what he called its “non-traditional partners,” Russia and China, and boasted of extensive behind-the-scenes cooperation with Russian authorities against violent criminal networks. That work, he said, had been going on for fifteen months. He called the relationships “new and dynamic,” while adding that the Bureau remained extremely mindful of Moscow’s adversarial approach.

In congressional testimony in September, he identified Russia as among the FBI’s biggest nation-state threats and said counter-espionage arrests were up 53 percent, including arrests involving Russia. And roughly three weeks before Fitzpatrick’s story, Patel stood at the announcement of charges against five people in what he described as an alleged plot by Russian intelligence services to murder a dissident on American soil. The plot, he said, showed the lengths to which adversaries would go to silence critics.

Hold those two positions side by side. In one, Russia is a partner with whom the FBI has quietly built fifteen months of trust. In the other, Russia’s intelligence services are plotting assassinations inside the United States. Patel is now planning to be hosted by the service most directly associated with the second activity, on the strength of the first.

There is also the older record, which Patel has never repudiated. He spent the years between Trump’s terms calling the 2016 interference findings the “Russiagate hoax” — a characterisation Moscow shares and the American intelligence community, including the bureau Patel now leads, does not. This summer the president gave a prime-time address on foreign threats to American elections that, by Fitzpatrick’s account, focused almost entirely on China and barely mentioned Russia.

The Precedent That Isn’t

Defenders of the trip have one genuine argument, and it deserves a fair hearing: FBI directors have gone to Russia before. Robert Mueller, the last to do so, travelled to Moscow in 2013 to discuss the Boston Marathon bombings and the related investigations. Russian authorities had warned the FBI about one of the bombers before the attack. There was a specific case, a specific shared interest, and a specific purpose.

The difference, according to four current and former FBI officials who spoke to Fitzpatrick, is that previous visits came with considerably more internal transparency about why they were happening and how they were planned. Two former senior officials who oversaw Russian counterintelligence, and who were familiar with earlier directors’ contact with Russian counterparts, told her they could not think of a compelling reason for Patel to go now that outweighed the risks.

Julia Curlee, a former CIA analyst and National Security Council director in the current administration, offered the cleanest test. A legitimate reason for the trip is imaginable, she said, but unless Patel is going to warn Moscow to stay out of American elections and get out of Ukraine, he has no business there.

The Mueller trip had a stated reason. This one has a host.

The Room Around the Trip

A trip like this does not happen in isolation, and Fitzpatrick’s reporting places it in a pattern worth listing plainly.

Special Envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner have each spent considerable time with Vladimir Putin in pursuit of a negotiated end to the Ukraine war. The New York Times has reported that two of the president’s senior political advisers, James Blair and Chris LaCivita, found time in the middle of a midterm campaign to advise the pro-Russian government of Republika Srpska on voting issues. A senior administration diplomat has floated trading sanctions relief for the release of political prisoners held by Russia, a deal that would bypass Ukraine entirely. A gold-framed photograph of Trump with Putin now hangs in the White House Palm Room.

And ProPublica has reported that Donald Trump Jr. accepted a lavish wedding gift from Russian oligarch Umar Kremlev, who covered hundreds of thousands of dollars for a private island rental and a fireworks display. Kremlev said this week that Trump Jr. has since repaid him. Readers of Part IX will remember that Senator Richard Blumenthal raised that wedding at the September 15 hearing. The question then was whether the FBI would look into it. The question now is whether any member of the president’s family will be in the room in Moscow — which is precisely why Raskin and Himes asked for the names.

Meanwhile, the tools that would ordinarily catch what happens in those rooms have been dismantled. The FBI’s Foreign Influence Task Force was disbanded almost immediately after the administration took office in 2025, with dozens of specialists reassigned or removed, according to Curlee. NPR reported this week that the counterintelligence squads that track foreign spies on American soil have only about half their positions filled. And Russia, as the Atlantic piece notes, is supporting Iran in its war with the United States — the same war this Substack has been documenting in real time.

Christopher O’Leary, a former senior executive in the FBI’s counterterrorism division, made the point about timing most directly. If there were genuine concern about election interference, he told Fitzpatrick, the director would be in the United States leading that effort around the clock, not going to Moscow. He noted the irony that FBI leadership is pressing agents to substantiate election conspiracy theories at home — theories that Russian services continuously amplify.

The View From North of the Border

A Canadian reader cannot look at this without remembering last month.

Part X of this series covered the New York Times reporting that Patel had abruptly halted some FBI cooperation with the Royal Canadian Mounted Police — Canada’s national police force, a Five Eyes partner, the agency the FBI relies on along nearly nine thousand kilometres of shared border. The stated grievance was a CBC newsroom memo about attributing the word “terrorism” when describing the September 11 attacks. CBC reversed the guidance within a day. The FBI cut cooperation with the RCMP anyway.

Set that beside this. An allied police force, with no connection to the broadcaster’s style guide, is treated as an adversary and punished after the offence has been corrected. An adversary intelligence service, currently accused by the director’s own Bureau of plotting a murder on American soil, is treated as a “non-traditional partner” and is about to host him.

There is an old adage about keeping your friends close and your enemies closer. It is usually credited to Sun Tzu or Machiavelli, and neither of them said it. The line belongs to Michael Corleone in The Godfather Part II (1974), which may be the more fitting source for an administration this Substack has more than once described in the vocabulary of organised crime. Even the Corleone version, though, assumed you kept your friends close. Patel has kept the second half of the advice and thrown away the first: the RCMP pushed away over a style memo, the FSB holding the door open in Moscow. And the adage was always about watching your enemy, never about putting yourself where your enemy can watch you.

Ukraine, too, appears to have been left out. A senior Ukrainian official told The Atlantic that Kyiv had not been briefed or consulted and had “no idea” why Patel was going. A senior European official said American diplomatic and intelligence counterparts had privately expressed their own concern. The allies are in the dark. The adversary is setting the table.

Measured Against TACO

This series has used the TACO framework — Threaten, Accommodate, Capitulate, Obfuscate — to read the director’s behaviour, and Part X added a fifth mode, Retaliate After Compliance, to describe what was done to the RCMP.

The Moscow trip sits almost entirely in the fourth category. There has been no threat and no capitulation, because there has been no engagement. There is only obfuscation, executed by silence: no confirmation, no itinerary, no purpose, no answer to Congress, no explanation to the agents who will have to secure the trip and debrief it afterward. Even Peskov’s non-answer in July was more forthcoming than anything the FBI has said publicly.

What the two modes share, Canada and Moscow alike, is the absence of an explanation the public is entitled to. In September an ally was punished and nobody would say why. In October an adversary is courted and nobody will say why. The pattern across eleven instalments is not that Patel makes unpopular decisions. Every FBI director does. It is that he makes consequential national-security decisions as though accountability were a courtesy rather than an obligation.

Who Can Stop Him?

The honest answer is the president, and the president is not going to.

Congress can ask, and it has. The October 12 deadline will tell us whether the director regards a written request from the ranking members of the Judiciary and Intelligence Committees as something to answer or something to outlast. Based on the alcohol-test pledge, the polling-place reversal and the RCMP episode, the safer prediction is the latter.

The professionals will do what professionals do. Fitzpatrick’s sources said they were confident FBI staff would do everything possible to reduce the counterintelligence risk. The phones will be left at home. The rooms will be assumed compromised. Somebody will be assigned to keep track of the director’s evenings, whether or not anyone ever puts that in writing.

And then, sometime around October 14 or 15, the man who called Russian interference a hoax, who told Reuters that Russia was a partner, and who told Congress that Russia was among the gravest threats the Bureau faces, will land in Moscow as a guest of the FSB. Three weeks later Americans will vote.

He should be worried. The people charged with protecting the country from what might happen in those rooms already are.
