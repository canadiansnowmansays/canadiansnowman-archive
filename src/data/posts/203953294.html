The Louisiana Delta — soil worked for generations. Here, on land once farmed by sharecroppers, democracy’s quiet reply takes root

By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

Two instalments ago, this newsletter watched the most powerful endorsement in the democratic world discover the one move that guarantees it can never again be wrong. In South Carolina the President endorsed both candidates for governor; in New York he endorsed neither; and the lesson, drawn across two states in a single Tuesday, was that the kingmaker had grown unwilling to make kings — because the thing more dangerous to a myth of infallibility than losing is being seen to choose, and then to lose.

That was the comedy. This is the part that isn’t funny.

Because a finger that has lost its certainty about whom to crown has lost none of its precision about whom to bury. On Saturday night in Baton Rouge, the second half of a five-year-old sentence was carried out. Representative Julia Letlow won the Republican runoff for the United States Senate, and in winning she completed the political execution of the man whose seat she will take — Senator Bill Cassidy, who committed the one offence in this party for which there is no statute of limitations and no path to parole.

He voted to convict.

I. The Crime and the Patience of the Court

The facts are old enough now to have acquired a kind of patina, and it is worth wiping it off to see them clearly. In February of 2021, in the impeachment trial that followed the sacking of the Capitol, as the public record shows, seven Republican senators voted to convict Donald Trump. Cassidy — a mild-mannered Baton Rouge gastroenterologist who had spent the first Trump term mostly declining to make trouble — wrestled with it for days and then cast the surprise vote. He said afterward he was at peace with it.

The party was not. And the party, in the person of its proprietor, has a long memory and a longer reach.

What makes the Cassidy case instructive is not that the punishment came — punishment always comes — but how patiently it was administered. This was not a hot-blooded purge. It was a cold one, executed across five years and two election cycles with the unhurried confidence of a man who knows the defendant cannot leave the building. Of the seven who voted to convict, as multiple outlets have reported, four read the room and retired rather than face the voters. Two remain insulated by the accidents of geography — Lisa Murkowski by Alaska’s freewheeling nonpartisan primary, Susan Collins by a Maine moderation so essential to the math that even the President’s allies have left her alone, calculating she is their only hope of holding the seat.

Senator Bill Cassidy, a Republican from Louisiana, voted to convict Mr. Trump in his second impeachment trial.Credit...Eric Lee for The New York Times

That left Cassidy, alone, in deep-red Louisiana, with no unusual primary system to hide behind — and, by the time the reckoning arrived, not even his own state’s traditional rules. Governor Jeff Landry, a top Trump ally, had moved to close Louisiana’s famously open primary, shutting out the Democrats and independents whose votes had been the very thing keeping moderate Republicans alive in that state for a generation. The trap was not merely set. It was redesigned for the occasion.

In January, the President told Letlow to run. As he wrote on social media, “RUN, JULIA, RUN!!!” She ran. And on May 16, Cassidy — a two-term incumbent who could point to billions in federal money brought home, including his hand in the bipartisan infrastructure law — finished behind two challengers and failed even to make the runoff. He was gone before the final round began.

He did not pretend otherwise about why. Returning to the Capitol the following Monday, as AP News reported, he told the assembled press that he had no regrets about his “momentous” vote. “I voted to uphold the Constitution,” he said. “It may have cost me my seat, but who cares? I had the privilege of voting to uphold the Constitution, isn’t that a great thing?” And in his concession he aimed a quiet, surgical dart at the man who had ended him — the kind of line that lands harder for its calm: when you take part in democracy, he said, sometimes things don’t go your way, but you don’t sulk, you don’t complain, and you don’t say the election was stolen.

It was the most eloquent thing a defeated man could say, and it changed nothing, because eloquence was never the currency in this transaction.

II. The Executioner Says the Quiet Part

If you want the thesis of this instalment, you do not have to infer it. One of the President’s closest allies said it aloud, on national television, the morning after Cassidy fell — and it deserves to be quoted at length because it is, in its way, a complete theory of the modern Republican Party compressed into four sentences.

Senator Lindsey Graham, as reported on Meet the Press, was asked about Cassidy’s defeat. “I like Bill,” he said. “I thought he’s a great senator, but he made a political decision — he voted to impeach President Trump.” And then the doctrine, stated with the flat certainty of a man reading a verdict: those who try to destroy Trump politically, who stand in the way of his agenda, are going to lose. You can disagree with the President, Graham allowed — magnanimous, this — but if you try to destroy him, you will lose. Because this is the party of Donald Trump.

Read that again, because the architecture is precise. Disagreement is survivable. Destruction — and a vote of conscience in an impeachment trial is here filed under destruction — is not. The distinction is the whole machine. It is not an ideological test; Cassidy voted with Trump’s agenda the overwhelming majority of the time. It is a loyalty test, and loyalty tests do not care about your voting record. They care about the one moment you were asked to choose between the Constitution and the man, and chose wrong.

The President supplied the coda himself, with his customary economy of grace. Cassidy’s career, he posted, was OVER — capital letters his — and it was, he said, nice to see.

This is the finger that, two instalments ago, could not decide which South Carolinian to anoint. Here it has no trouble at all. The asymmetry is the point of this entire essay: the endorsement has grown unreliable as a tool of creation and remains lethallyreliable as a tool of destruction. Trump’s pick lost the Iowa governorship; as Politico reported, his choice fell short in the Georgia governor’s runoff when a self-funder spent better than a hundred million dollars of his own money to beat him; in South Carolina he had to back both horses to keep from backing a loser. The kingmaker’s batting average on kings is slipping. But ask him to end a heretic, and the aim is as true as it ever was.

A man may lose the knack for blessing and keep the knack for cursing. They are different muscles. Louisiana is the proof that the second one has not atrophied.

III. What the Purge Cost, and What It Bought

Representative Julia Letlow, Trump-endorsed winner of the Republican runoff. The seat changes hands; the test of loyalty is passed.

It is worth being clear-eyed about what actually changed hands here, because the temptation is to file this as one more red seat staying red and move on. That misses it.

Letlow is, by every account, a formidable and genuinely sympathetic figure — a former college administrator who won her House seat in a 2021 special election after her husband, Luke Letlow, died of COVID six days after being elected to it, before he could be sworn in, while waiting for the vaccine. There is real human gravity in that story, and the runoff did her the indignity of dragging it through the mud: her opponent, Treasurer John Fleming, running to her right, amplified an AI-generated video that invoked her late husband, which she rightly called disgraceful. She won anyway, by something close to fourteen points, with the President’s endorsement and a Landry-fueled super PAC behind her. She will almost certainly be Louisiana’s next senator, and the first Republican woman the state has sent to that chamber.

But strip away the biography and look at the transaction. A senator who brought home infrastructure billions and voted with the President most of the time was removed and replaced with a candidate whose central promise — stated plainly, as Democracy Docket has documented — is fealty. “He endorsed me because he knows I will stand with him,” Letlow said in an online rally with the President days before the vote. Among her priorities: passing the SAVE Act, the voter-suppression vehicle this series has tracked under other names in other states. The seat did not move ideologically. It moved from a man who would, once, on one night, put the Constitution above the President, to a candidate whose qualification is that she never will.

That is what the purge bought. Not a redder seat — Louisiana was already red. A more obedient one. The transaction was not about policy. It was about removing from the chamber the last Louisianan who had demonstrated, with a recorded vote, that there existed a line he would not cross. The line itself was the target. And the line is now gone.

This is what democratic backsliding looks like when it wins cleanly, without drama, inside the rules — rules a friendly governor helpfully rewrote for the occasion. No martial imagery, no contested count. Just a primary, conducted in a closed system, producing a more pliant result. The machinery worked exactly as designed.

IV. Where Democracy Fights Back

So where, in a sentence carried out this efficiently, does democracy fight back at all?

It fights back, as it so often does in this series, in the place no one was watching — in the other runoff, the one that drew a fraction of the coverage, on the Democratic side of a ballot that nobody expects to matter in November.

Louisiana has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1996, nor a Democratic governor since the last century. The party’s nominee in November is, by any sober accounting, a longshot wrapped in a near-impossibility. And on the same Saturday night that completed the Cassidy purge, Louisiana Democrats chose, as that longshot, a man named Jamie Davis.

Davis is a third-generation crop farmer from Waterproof, in Tensas Parish, up in the Delta — sorghum, corn, soybeans, cotton, as Ballotpedia’s candidate file records, on more than two thousand acres. He studied electrical engineering at LSU and came home to work the family farm his father founded in 1977. He won his primary with 47 percent and carried all sixty-four parishes. He talks less about party than about rural hospitals closing and the price of home insurance and a state that ranks first in the nation in poverty. He wants, of all the heresies, an independent federal ban on gerrymandering — the very mechanism this series has spent a dozen instalments tracking.

And here is the detail that makes him the right close for this essay, the image I would ask you to sit with after the caps-lock and the verdicts have faded. Some of the two thousand acres Jamie Davis farms today were worked by his own grandfather — as the record of his life shows — as a sharecropper. Land his grandfather labored on without owning, his grandson now owns and works and hopes to hand down. That is not a campaign slogan. It is a fact of soil and lineage, and it contains, in a single span of three generations on one piece of Louisiana ground, the entire argument for why the franchise is worth fighting over even when the fight looks lost before it starts.

The sharecropper’s grandson on the sharecropper’s land. Jamie Davis carries more than a campaign; he carries three generations of Louisiana soil and franchise

He will probably not win. The map says so, the money says so, a century of history says so. But the machine that took down Cassidy is built precisely to make contests like his feel pointless in advance — to make the outcome so foreordained that participation seems like sentiment. And democracy fights back, here as everywhere in this series, not by promising Jamie Davis the seat, but by putting his name on the ballot beneath it: the sharecropper’s grandson, on the sharecropper’s land, asking the people of Louisiana to do the one thing no endorsement and no closed primary and no rewritten rule can finally prevent them from doing.

Choose.

The President carried out his sentence on Bill Cassidy with the patience of a man who knew the defendant could not flee. But there is one court whose docket he cannot clear and whose verdict he cannot pre-write, and it convenes on the third of November. He has spent five years and a great deal of borrowed power teaching his party that crossing him is fatal. Jamie Davis, on two thousand acres his grandfather did not own, is the quiet Louisiana reminder that the last word in a democracy was never the President’s to give. It belongs to whoever shows up to cast it.
