FROM: The Office of Reciprocal Nomenclature, Global Affairs Canada (Interim, Unstaffed, Self-Appointed) TO: All Cartographers, Atlas Publishers, and GPS Voice Assistants of Goodwill RE: Adjustment of Certain Geographic and Commercial Designations, Effective Upon Someone Noticing

Pursuant to the principle, recently and repeatedly articulated by the Government of the United States, that a body of water, strait, or mountain may be renamed by whichever party (a) does the most work maintaining it, or (b) has simply decided it no longer wishes to do business with the other party — and further pursuant to the demonstrated compliance rate achieved under this principle to date, being one (1) geofenced mapping application and zero (0) foreign governments — the undersigned Office wishes to formally propose the following adjustments, in the spirit of fairness, precedent, and getting there first before somebody else does.

Section 1 — Niagara Falls The Office has reviewed the relevant measurements and finds them dispositive. Horseshoe Falls — the one on the postcards, the one carrying roughly ninety percent of the Niagara River’s flow, curved into the shape cartographers politely call horseshoe and this Office calls decisive — runs about 670 metres wide along its crest, on the Canadian shelf. American Falls, by contrast, spans roughly 260 metres, carries the remaining ten percent, and has spent the better part of a century accumulating a talus pile — a rock heap, in plain English, built from repeated rockfalls in 1931, 1954, and 1959 — at its base, such that its true drop is frequently measured not to the river but to the top of its own rubble. One side offers a horseshoe. The other offers a scree slope with a mist machine.

Under the “does the most work” standard already invoked elsewhere in this memo, the matter resolves itself: the Falls shall henceforth be known as Ontario Falls, full stop, with the American fragment permitted to retain a modest secondary designation — this Office proposes Rockpile Vista — provided it is signed appropriately small.

Section 2 — The Great Lakes, Canadian Portions Thereof Four of the five Great Lakes are boundary waters, administered jointly under a 1909 treaty neither government appears to have reread lately. In the interest of symmetry, Canada shall rename its half of Lake Erie, Lake Huron, Lake Superior, and, as of this week, Lake Ontario. Suggested working title for the latter: Lake Not America. A shortlist of alternates remains under review, pending focus-group results in Kingston.

This Office also wishes to note, for planning purposes, an asset the renaming schedule does not require but escalation might. Since 1955, Canada and the United States have jointly funded and operated the Great Lakes Fishery Commission’s sea lamprey control program — the reason the lakes are not currently overrun by a parasitic, jawless predator with no natural enemies on this continent and a documented talent for killing lake trout by attaching to them and not letting go. Suppression of the lamprey population, presently running at about ninety percent below its historic peak, is not automatic. It is annual, binational, and ongoing, and it depends on both governments continuing to show up and pay their share.

Premier Ford has this week already flagged electricity and critical minerals as available levers should the trade dispute escalate further. This Office notes only that the sea lamprey file sits on the same shelf, is considerably cheaper to action than a power grid, and does not require Ontario to do anything so dramatic as flip a switch — merely to stop doing the extra thing it has been doing for seventy years to keep somebody else’s fish alive. The lamprey does not read border treaties. It has never needed to. We would simply, for once, let it not need to on both sides at once.

Section 3 — The St. Lawrence Seaway Canada holds the majority of the navigable length, the majority of the locks, and, not incidentally, the better winter coats. Effective immediately, the Seaway’s American-adjacent stretch shall be marked on all Canadian charts as the St. Lawrence (Continued, No Charge).

Section 4 — The Detroit River and Ambassador Bridge Same logic, smaller river. The Ambassador Bridge, being a private American toll asset carrying the bulk of Canada-U.S. freight, shall be informally referred to at this Office as the Bridge We Are Currently Being Charged To Use, pending further study.

Section 5 — The Strait of Juan de Fuca If the strait separating Iran from Oman may be considered “American territory” on the strength of a Truth Social post, this Office notes that the Strait of Juan de Fuca is at least half Canadian by treaty, coastline, and every nautical chart ever printed. Accordingly, it shall be renamed the Strait of Frankly We Have More of a Claim Than He Does, pending a shorter alternative from the Translation Bureau.

Section 6 — Trump Turnberry and Trump International, Doonbeg These two items are not proposed by this Office; they are already in motion, and rather better sourced than anything above. Local campaigns in Ayrshire and County Clare have pushed for years to strip the Trump branding from both courses and restore their original names. This Office simply notes, for the record, that reverting a golf course to what the land was called before an American businessman bought it is precisely the “restoring names that honor greatness” logic Washington has cited for every rename in the other direction. We look forward to the President’s public support for this initiative, on principle.

Section 7 — Denali No change proposed. It is already being renamed back and forth by the country that owns it, which — this Office concedes — is at least a coherent use of sovereign authority, however whiplash-inducing for the cartographers.

Section 8 — North America A minority position within this Office holds that, since Canada, the United States, and Mexico jointly occupy the continent, and since Canada is by land area the larger of the three signatories to that arrangement, the continent itself might reasonably be renamed. This Office declines to formally propose it, not out of restraint, but because the paperwork involved in renaming an entire continent — even hypothetically — sounds exhausting, and this Office is unstaffed.

Anticipated Compliance

Based on the adoption curve observed for “Gulf of America” — one platform, one country, zero foreign recognition, eighteen months and counting — this Office projects that the above renamings will achieve an identical level of global uptake: total, within Canada, on maps nobody outside Canada will ever open, and entirely beside the point to everyone who actually has to sail, ship, or fly through any of the water in question.

We consider this an acceptable outcome. It is, after all, the standard that was set for us.

Filed without irony, which was the hardest part.
