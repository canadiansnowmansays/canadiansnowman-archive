This series began with a Federal Reserve chairman’s silence about interest rates and has spent seven instalments tracing that silence, and the broader affordability picture surrounding it, down to a mortgage statement, a grocery receipt, a credit card bill, and a paycheck that no longer stretches quite as far as it did a year ago. This instalment turns to the place all of that arithmetic is ultimately headed: a ballot box, on November 3, where voters will render a verdict on everything this series has documented, whether or not any single voter has read a word of it.

The number that keeps moving in one direction

As recently as November 2024, registered voters were evenly divided on the generic congressional ballot, 48 to 48. That is worth holding onto as a baseline, because nearly every measure since has moved in the same direction, and by a widening margin. A Marist poll last November found Democrats ahead by 14 points among registered voters nationally — the first notable Democratic advantage on this specific question in more than three years, and a poll conducted, notably, at the tail end of the nation’s longest government shutdown, with a plurality of Americans placing most of the blame on congressional Democrats for that specific episode even as the broader ballot number moved the other way entirely. By late July, a Reuters/Ipsos poll had Democrats up 5 points, CNN/SSRS had them up 8 (rising from 5 points in January), and Emerson College’s tracking, which had shown a 7-point Democratic lead in March, had widened to 11 points by late July, with Emerson’s pollster attributing much of the gap to a 27-point Democratic advantage among female voters specifically, against a far narrower 5-point edge among men. As of late September, Nate Silver’s polling average — which applies a likely-voter adjustment that in this environment favours Democrats further — puts the generic ballot at roughly D+8, while a separate tracker averaging ABC/Washington Post, CNN/SSRS, Fox News, and Quinnipiac polling has it closer to D+6.6, alongside a presidential approval rating of 38 percent — among the lowest second-term readings for a Republican president at this point in a term since George W. Bush in 2005.

The historical context for what a gap of this size typically produces is worth stating plainly, while also stating its limits honestly. A generic ballot in the D+6 to D+8 range roughly matches the environment that preceded the 2018 midterms, when Democrats gained 41 House seats and flipped the chamber. Historical models built on generic-ballot and approval-rating relationships project Democratic gains in the 25-to-40-seat range in the House this cycle if the current environment holds through November — comfortably enough to flip control — with Senate models showing smaller but still meaningful gains against a map where Democrats are defending more seats than Republicans. The honest caveat, one this series has tried to apply consistently to every figure it has cited, is that these are historical relationships, not certainties: 2022’s midterms defied similar projections in the other direction, when cross-cutting issues and candidate-quality differences scrambled what the generic ballot alone would have predicted. A polling average three weeks out is a strong signal, not a verdict.

Why this movement tracks the ledger, not just the man

It would be simplistic, and inconsistent with the evenhanded approach this series has tried to maintain throughout, to attribute this shift entirely to the affordability story traced in the previous seven instalments. Immigration enforcement methods, federal spending cuts affecting popular programs, and a range of other issues are cited alongside economic concerns in the polling driving this movement. But the economic thread running through the shift is difficult to dismiss as incidental, because of one specific data point buried in Pew’s July survey: for the first time in nearly a decade of that polling series, voters now say they trust the Democratic Party’s economic plan over the Republican Party’s — a genuine reversal on the single issue this series has argued, from Part I onward, sits at the centre of this election. Reuters/Ipsos found the same reversal independently. This is not a party winning on an issue it has traditionally owned. It is a party gaining ground on the issue it has traditionally lost, at the exact moment this series has documented real wages turning negative, tariff costs compounding, and mortgage rates refusing to fall.

It is worth being precise about what is, and is not, driving this shift among the electorate itself. Pew’s data shows Democratic voters overwhelmingly frame their vote this fall as a vote against the president — 81 percent describe it that way — while Republican voters are more evenly split between viewing their vote as an endorsement of the president specifically and viewing it as a more conventional partisan choice. That asymmetry matters for turnout dynamics, but it does not explain the movement among independents, where Marist found a genuinely enormous 33-point Democratic advantage last November — a group with no particular loyalty to either party’s brand, moving on the merits of an argument rather than on tribal loyalty. That is the group Part II’s account of the “I’ve won affordability” framing was always going to be tested against most severely, since independents have no partisan reason to accept a claim that contradicts their own grocery receipt.

There is also an engagement asymmetry worth flagging, because it complicates any simple reading of the topline numbers into a guaranteed outcome. Pew found 70 percent of Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents say it “really matters” which party controls Congress this fall, compared with 60 percent of Republicans and Republican leaners saying the same — a real but modest gap, and one considerably narrower than the polling advantage itself, suggesting Republican voters have not disengaged from this election even as their party’s standing on the economy has slipped. Meanwhile, both parties remain broadly unpopular in absolute terms: one national poll found only 30 percent of Americans view the Democratic Party favourably, against 51 percent unfavourable, while the Republican Party fares almost identically, 31 percent favourable against 52 percent unfavourable. This is not, in other words, a race in which one party has won public affection. It is a race in which one party — the incumbent one — is losing an argument about a specific, measurable problem, and voters are responding to that argument rather than embracing the alternative on its own merits. That distinction matters for how durable this environment is likely to prove: an electorate voting against a failure to address affordability is a different, more volatile coalition than one voting for a positive alternative vision, and it could move again, in either direction, depending on what either party actually does about the ledger this series has documented.

The grievance beneath the receipt

Tim Wu, the Columbia law professor who worked on competition and technology policy in the Biden White House, made the case in a New York Times opinion essay on September 28, 2026 (”Affordability Is a Winning Message for Democrats. But There’s an Even Better One.”) that the price complaint is only the surface of what these polls are measuring. The deeper division, he argues, runs between Americans who believe the economic system is fair and working for most people and those who believe it is not — and the second group is the majority. He cites a CBS News poll in which roughly 60 percent of Americans called the economy unfair, with most of those under 45 saying they have fewer opportunities than their parents had; an Economist/YouGov poll in which two-thirds of Republicans called the gap between rich and poor a big problem; and Gallup’s Economic Confidence Index stuck in negative territory month after month despite a rising stock market and no recession. That last detail is the K-shaped economy this series has traced since Part IV, seen from the voter’s side of the table: the aggregate figures look healthy, and the household does not believe them, because its own ledger says otherwise.

Wu’s argument helps explain the independent number above. The affordability candidates who won in 2025 — Zohran Mamdani in New York City, Mikie Sherrill in New Jersey, Abigail Spanberger in Virginia — and those running now, such as James Talarico in Texas, Rob Sand in Iowa and Roy Cooper in North Carolina, have increasingly attached their price promises to a claim about who holds economic power. Mamdani’s city-run grocery stores are meant to lower the price of milk, but also to undercut the food conglomerates. Sherrill’s separate utility rate for data centres is meant to ease household power bills, but also to make Big Tech pay for the grid it is straining. Sand’s housing plan includes barring private equity from buying up single-family homes as investments. Talarico has compressed the whole argument into a line: “the biggest divide in this country is not left versus right.” In his telling, it is top against bottom — a framing that travels well with voters who distrust both parties’ brands, which, as the favourability numbers above show, is most of them.

Wu also attaches a warning, and it bears directly on the volatility point. He holds up Kyrsten Sinema, who campaigned as a fighter for the forgotten middle class and finished her Senate career as a reliable ally of the pharmaceutical industry against drug-price reform, along with the cautious, donor-friendly economic programme of the 2024 Harris campaign, as examples of what happens when fundraising arithmetic overrides voter arithmetic. The administration, in Wu’s reading, has vacated this ground entirely — the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, has joined the president in denying there is an affordability problem at all, and more than 70 percent of Americans now disapprove of the president’s economic management. But an electorate voting against a failure on affordability will apply the same test to whoever replaces that failure. A party that wins on the receipt and leaves the rulebook untouched should expect the coalition in these polls to move again, and no generic-ballot average can measure in advance whether the people claiming the ground mean it.

What the ledger actually shows so far

It is worth pausing here, three weeks before the vote, to restate, plainly and without editorializing further, what the seven prior instalments actually established, because the case for or against any political outcome should rest on that record rather than on the polling numbers alone. A Federal Reserve chairman inherited an economy with inflation drifting further from target, and has spent months saying too little about how he intends to address it, leaving markets — and by extension mortgage rates — to price in uncertainty rather than a clear plan. A president spent a year moving from calling affordability concerns a hoax, to calling the polls measuring them fake, to declaring outright victory over a problem his own administration’s data shows getting worse rather than better. A trade war with a neighbouring country turned a dispute over steel quotas into a documented, dollar-figure increase in the cost of appliances, vehicles, and home construction on both sides of the border. Grocery prices, even by the government’s own more conservative measure, have compounded to the point that a cart costing $150 in 2019 now costs roughly $190, with the burden falling hardest on exactly the households with the least room to absorb it. Mortgage rates have sat near 6.65 percent for the better part of two years with no meaningful relief in sight, pricing a majority of American households out of the median home. Credit card and auto loan delinquencies have ticked upward even as the aggregate economy holds steady, a leading indicator of strain building beneath a reassuring headline. And, as of this summer, real wages — the final backstop against everything listed above — turned negative for the first time since the depths of the 2022 inflation shock.

None of that is a partisan argument. It is a ledger, built line by line across seven instalments, from sources spanning the Federal Reserve, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the USDA, Experian, the New York Fed, and half a dozen independent pollsters — none of them chosen for their politics, all of them chosen because they measure something real. Whatever verdict voters render in November, they will render it with a fuller accounting of that ledger than the “I’ve won affordability” framing this series began by examining was ever built to survive.

Where this series goes from here

The ledger is not closed, and neither is this series. Three more instalments will arrive before the polls open. Next week, Part IX follows a quieter line item: federal child-care subsidies being steered toward married, single-earner households. Part X steps back to measure what years of compounding prices have done to the American shopping list, one remembered steak at a time. And Part XI, landing the day before the vote, turns to the Federal Reserve decision that will not wait for the election to be over. After November 3, the series will follow the results, and what they mean for the affordability argument, as events warrant.
