August 14, 2026

For the better part of ten years, Ken Paxton has operated on a working theory of Texas politics: that character doesn’t count. He was charged with felony securities fraud within months of taking statewide office in 2015. He weathered a bribery investigation over a $100,000 gift from a donor with business before his own agency. He was impeached by his own party’s legislature in 2023 on corruption charges, tried by the Texas Senate, and acquitted. Through all of it, he kept winning. Three statewide elections, three victories, and a state Republican electorate that seemed to regard the accumulating scandals the way it regards Gulf Coast humidity — an unpleasant fact of the climate, not a reason to leave.

That theory is now being tested by two things happening at once, and neither is going away before November 3rd. The first is a polling environment that has moved, steadily and without much precedent, against him. The second is a story published on August 10th by the New York Times that gives the “corrupt Paxton” narrative something it has rarely had before: a single, concrete, easily-told example of him turning public position into private income.

Put those two things together and you get something Texas Republicans haven’t had to reckon with in a long time — a Paxton candidacy where the scandal isn’t background noise to be managed, but the actual subject of the race.

Six Polls, One Direction

Since Paxton secured the Republican nomination in late May, the shape of this Senate race has been consistent even when the margins weren’t. Every public poll released since mid-July has shown Democrat James Talarico, the 37-year-old Austin state representative and former seminary student, ahead of the sitting attorney general. Not one has shown Paxton back in front.

The sequence is worth laying out because the trend line matters more than any single number. A Texas Public Opinion Research survey in late July had Talarico up five points, 45 to 40, his largest lead of the general election period to that point. Days later, Texas Southern University’s Barbara Jordan Center found a tighter two-point Talarico edge. Axios then reported a third consecutive poll putting Talarico ahead, 47 to 45. The Texas Pulse survey, run in part out of the Texas A&M Bush School, moved from a dead-even tie in June to a four-point Talarico lead in its August release. And a poll from the Democratic-aligned research group 254 Labs, conducted July 15th through the 31st, put the margin at 48 to 45.

Prediction markets have followed the polling rather than led it, which is usually the more honest tell. Talarico overtook Paxton in Kalshi’s Senate odds this past weekend, 51 percent to 49, after trailing for most of the summer following Donald Trump’s endorsement of Paxton in the primary.

None of these individual results is dispositive on its own — most sit inside or near the margin of error, and Paxton’s campaign is not, by its own account, panicking yet. But six consecutive surveys pointing the same direction is not noise. It’s a trend, and trends in Texas statewide races involving a Democrat are rare enough that they deserve to be named as what they are: a genuine opening, in a state that hasn’t elected a Democrat to statewide office since 1994.

What’s Actually Moving the Number

The polls themselves offer a fairly consistent explanation, and it isn’t really about Talarico. It’s about Paxton.

The Texas Pulse survey found that by a 55-to-35 margin, Texas voters now say Paxton is corrupt. That is not a marginal or ambiguous finding — it’s a majority of the state’s likely electorate applying a specific, damning word to their own attorney general, a man they have already elected three times. The Texas Public Opinion Research poll found Paxton posting the worst net favourability rating of any figure tested in the race, at minus 13, against Talarico’s plus 12 — the best of anyone surveyed.

Affordability, not corruption, remains the top-ranked issue for voters — 44 percent name cost of living as their top priority, well ahead of healthcare and Social Security. But the corruption finding matters precisely because of how it interacts with that affordability anxiety. A politician who is merely unlikeable is a manageable problem for a well-funded incumbent party. A politician who voters believe has been enriching himself while they struggle with grocery and housing costs is a much harder needle to thread — and it is exactly the needle Paxton now has to thread, because on August 10th, the Times gave that abstract “corrupt” label a name, an address, and a dollar figure.

The Tower at the Hospital

As Lauren McGaughy reported for the New York Times, federal financial disclosures and property records show that Paxton has drawn between $100,000 and $1 million roughly every eighteen months from a cellular tower company called Premier Vertical Properties — a firm he co-founded in 2005 while sitting on the board of Centennial Medical Center, a hospital in Frisco, north of Dallas. Less than a year after Paxton joined that board, Premier signed a ten-year lease with T-Mobile for tower space on the hospital’s own grounds. The site’s internal name on the paperwork: “Paxton MP.”

The mechanics are almost absurdly simple for a story about influence. Cell tower economics reward whoever controls the land a tower sits on, and prime real estate near a highway and a hospital in a growing suburb would ordinarily attract established telecom infrastructure firms, not a one-year-old company formed by a sitting hospital board member. One industry consultant described landing that site as akin to hitting a home run on the very first swing. It is not established that Paxton personally voted to approve the lease, and the hospital’s current operators say they cannot speak to arrangements made two decades ago under a previous parent company. But Paxton never disclosed the tower’s location on either his state or federal ethics filings — the Times had to find it in Collin County deed records — and the asset now appears to be one of his largest sources of income, worth more, by some industry estimates, than the roughly half-million-dollar valuation he has reported.

Paxton’s campaign response, when asked about the hospital dealings specifically, did not engage with the substance at all. It pivoted instead to complaining about press attention paid to a two-decade-old lease while ignoring “overflow crowds” at campaign events — a rhetorical move Texans have watched Republican officials make for a decade now whenever the underlying question gets uncomfortable: change the subject, question the questioner’s motives, wait for the news cycle to move. It is the same Obfuscate move this newsletter has tracked across a dozen other accountability stories, applied here to a story that isn’t really about journalism at all. It’s about whether the state’s chief law enforcement officer spent twenty years quietly monetizing a hospital board seat he was trusted with in the public interest.

The Pen Problem

The tower story is not, it turns out, the only piece of Paxton biography currently being repurposed for the 2026 race. A decade-old episode — Paxton pocketing a lawyer’s $1,000 Montblanc pen at a Collin County courthouse security checkpoint in 2012, caught on camera, returned only after a sheriff’s deputy called to ask for it back — resurfaced last week in a Talarico ad featuring an endorsement from the pen’s original owner, Joe Joplin. The Talarico campaign is now selling its own pens for a $6 donation, stamped “Not Ken Paxton’s Pen.” It is, on its face, the smallest scandal on Paxton’s résumé — smaller than the securities fraud indictment, smaller than the impeachment, smaller even than the tower lease. Nobody is alleging a crime. Paxton’s campaign calls it, not unreasonably, a “childish attack” over a decade-old “mix-up.”

But the pen and the tower are doing the same work, and Talarico’s campaign clearly knows it. Both are stories a voter can hold in their head without a law degree: a man in a position of public trust took something that wasn’t his, on camera, and had to be asked to give it back. The tower story supplies the dollar figure and the decades of quiet income; the pen supplies the character sketch — petty, self-serving, caught in the act — in five seconds of security footage. Pair a felony indictment with an impeachment trial and most voters’ eyes glaze over. Pair a stolen pen with a hospital-adjacent income stream nobody disclosed, and you have the same accusation told twice, once as farce and once as finance.

Why This Time Might Be Different

Every Texas political reporter covering this race is required, apparently by statute, to include some version of a “reality check”: Paxton has survived worse, voters already knew about the securities fraud charge and the impeachment and the divorce, and they elected him anyway. It’s a fair caution. Prediction is a losing game in Texas politics, and Beto O’Rourke’s near-miss against Ted Cruz in 2018 is the ghost every Democrat in this race is trying not to become — a campaign that generated enormous energy and money and lost anyway, followed by a Senate challenge two years later that barely registered.

But the comparison to Beto actually cuts the other way on close inspection. Beto led in two polls across an entire cycle; Talarico has now led in six straight. Cruz’s vulnerabilities in 2018 were built on hardline policy positions that his own voters largely agreed with — he wasn’t accused of a felony. Paxton is a nominee who was formally impeached by his own party’s state legislature and required a Senate acquittal to stay in office, running against a well-funded opponent for the first time in a race where Trump’s coattails are not, this cycle, doing all the work by themselves.

What the tower story adds isn’t new information about Paxton’s character — Texans have had a full decade to form a view on that. What it adds is specificity. “Corrupt” is a poll number. A hospital board member steering a lucrative lease to his own newly formed company, on the grounds of the hospital he was supposed to be stewarding, is a story — the kind that survives past the news cycle because it’s simple enough to repeat at a kitchen table. Combined with an electorate that has already decided, by a 20-point margin, that the word “corrupt” fits, it gives that impression a very specific shape to attach itself to.

A Decade of Practice Managing Scandal

It’s worth remembering how much practice Paxton has had at exactly this kind of story, because it’s the reason his campaign’s dismissive response felt so rehearsed. He was indicted on felony securities fraud charges in 2015, months into his first term as attorney general, accused of steering investors toward a friend’s business without disclosing he was being paid a commission to do so. That case dragged across nine years and four judges before Paxton, facing trial in 2024, agreed to a pretrial diversion deal requiring restitution of roughly $300,000, 100 hours of community service, and legal ethics training — an outcome that let him avoid a conviction without ever being cleared. A separate bribery allegation, tied to Austin developer Nate Paul and reported to the FBI in 2020 by eight of Paxton’s own senior deputies, fed directly into the 2023 impeachment: the Texas House voted 121-23 to impeach him on twenty articles covering bribery, abuse of office, and obstruction, and the Texas Senate — where his own wife sat as a juror, barred from voting — acquitted him on the sixteen articles it actually tried, by a narrow 16-14 margin. The Justice Department quietly declined to pursue a parallel federal investigation into the same conduct in the final weeks of the Biden administration.

Each of those episodes followed the same rhythm: allegation, investigation, prolonged uncertainty, resolution without a conviction, and Paxton emerging politically intact. Texas voters, by the time of his last re-election as attorney general, had essentially priced all of it in. What makes the tower story different isn’t its severity — a hospital board member routing a lease to his own company is, on paper, a smaller allegation than felony securities fraud or a bribery-driven impeachment. What’s different is the timing and the format. It arrives ninety days before an election he’s already losing in the polls, it requires no jury, no legislative vote, and no years-long process to resolve — just deed records anyone can read — and it lands in a race where, for the first time in a decade, enough voters are already primed to believe the worst that the story doesn’t have to argue its own premise. It just has to be legible.

The Rest of the Ticket Is Watching

Paxton is not running alone, and neither is his corruption problem. Democrat Gina Hinojosa’s campaign for governor has stayed close enough to Republican incumbent Greg Abbott that Texas commentators have started asking, cautiously, whether 2026 might be the first cycle in over thirty years where Texas Republicans have to actually defend the top of their ticket rather than run up the score. None of the gubernatorial or down-ballot polling approaches the consistency of the Senate numbers, and Abbott remains the favourite. But a Republican Party used to treating Texas as a formality is now running defence in two statewide races at once, in a midterm year with a president whose approval ratings are themselves a drag on the ballot. Paxton’s collapse, if it continues, will not stay contained to his own race — every down-ballot Republican in a competitive district will be asked, at some point between now and November 3rd, whether they still stand by him.

The Coalition Talarico Still Has to Hold

None of this means the race is decided, and the polling itself contains a warning for Democrats that has nothing to do with Paxton. The Texas Southern University survey that found Talarico’s two-point statewide lead also surveyed 1,200 Black voters specifically, and found overwhelming majorities who believe Talarico defeated Congresswoman Jasmine Crockett in the Democratic primary largely because Crockett is Black — and who believe the state party takes Black voters for granted more broadly. That is a live grievance inside Talarico’s own coalition, not a hypothetical one, and it explains why his campaign has already committed $25 million specifically to outreach and mobilization among Black voters ahead of November. A four-or-five-point statewide lead in the polling averages is not a governing majority if turnout among a core constituency softens because of exactly this kind of unresolved resentment.

Paxton’s path back into this race almost certainly runs through consolidating the Republican base he’s held for a decade regardless of scandal, betting that character simply doesn’t move enough votes in a state Trump carried by nearly 14 points two years ago. Talarico’s path runs through keeping affordability the top issue, giving the corruption finding a face voters can picture, and making sure the coalition that currently has him ahead in six consecutive polls doesn’t fracture over the very real question of whether Texas Democrats have taken Black voters seriously enough. Both campaigns know the stakes. Neither has ninety days to waste.
