The Fathers of Confederation — the pragmatic deal-makers who forged the Dominion of Canada in 1867 through negotiation rather than revolution.

On July 1, 1867, the Dominion of Canada came into existence. Not with a revolution. Not with a declaration of self-evident truths. Not with cannon fire or barricades or the soaring rhetoric of liberation. With a statute.

The British North America Act was passed by the Parliament at Westminster, received Royal Assent from Queen Victoria, and took effect at midnight. The new country was proclaimed. Its first prime minister, John A. Macdonald, was knighted for the occasion. Church bells rang. Bonfires were lit. And ordinary Canadians largely went about their business — some with enthusiasm, some with indifference, and some, particularly in Nova Scotia, which had been dragged into Confederation against the wishes of a substantial portion of its population, with active resentment.

It was the most Canadian of beginnings: a compromise, negotiated by committee, ratified by a foreign parliament, and greeted by the people it was supposed to unite with a range of reactions spanning from mild celebration to principled objection, with the vast middle ground occupied by people who were mainly concerned about whether it would affect the price of lumber.

The forces that produced Confederation were not idealistic. They were practical, urgent, and in some cases desperate.

The united Province of Canada — the shotgun marriage of Upper and Lower Canada forced by the Durham Report in 1841 — was functionally ungovernable. The double-majority system, which required legislation to command support from both the English and French sections of the legislature, had produced chronic deadlock. Between 1854 and 1864, the Province went through a succession of governments so unstable that the average lifespan of a ministry was measured in months. Legislative paralysis on basic questions of governance — railway policy, tariffs, western expansion — had become the norm.

Meanwhile, the external threats were real and growing.

The American Civil War, which raged from 1861 to 1865, concentrated the minds of British North Americans in ways that no amount of constitutional theory ever could. The war demonstrated several alarming things simultaneously. First, that the American republic was capable of extraordinary violence against itself, which did not bode well for neighbours who had fought the Americans within living memory. Second, that the Union victory had produced the largest, most experienced, and best-equipped army in the world — and that army had nowhere in particular to go once the Confederacy surrendered. Third, that American territorial ambition had not diminished; the doctrine of Manifest Destiny remained a live political force, and there were voices in Washington suggesting that British North America would make a suitable next acquisition.

The Fenian raids drove the point home. Between 1866 and 1871, armed bands of Irish-American nationalists launched a series of cross-border incursions into British North America, ostensibly to strike at Britain through its colonies. The raids were militarily insignificant — they were repelled each time — but their psychological impact was considerable. They demonstrated that the border was porous, that the American government was unwilling or unable to prevent its citizens from launching attacks on Canadian soil, and that the individual colonies of British North America were too small and too dispersed to defend themselves alone.

Something had to give. And what gave was the colonial structure itself.

The architects of Confederation were not philosophers. They were politicians, lawyers, and railway promoters, and they operated with the pragmatism that has always characterized Canadian governance at its most functional.

John A. Macdonald — political genius, railway promoter, and the dominant figure in the forging of the Dominion.

The principal figures were John A. Macdonald, the Conservative leader from Upper Canada, whose political genius, personal charm, and prodigious alcohol consumption were equally legendary. George-Étienne Cartier, Macdonald’s French-Canadian counterpart, a former rebel in 1837 who had evolved into a Conservative ally and who understood, better than anyone, that Confederation would only work if it gave Quebec enough autonomy to protect the French fact. George Brown, the Reform newspaper editor and Macdonald’s bitter political rival, who swallowed his antagonism long enough to support the project because the alternative — continued deadlock or American absorption — was worse. And Thomas D’Arcy McGee, the orator, the visionary, the man who spoke most passionately about the idea of a new northern nation stretching from sea to sea.

Delegates at the Charlottetown Conference, 1864 — where talks on Maritime union became the foundation for a transcontinental Dominion.

They gathered first in Charlottetown in September 1864, where a conference originally convened to discuss Maritime union was hijacked by the Canadians into a discussion of something much larger. Then in Quebec City in October, where the Seventy-Two Resolutions — the framework of Confederation — were hammered out over two weeks of debate, negotiation, and what contemporary accounts suggest was a considerable quantity of champagne.

The debates were practical, not philosophical. The allocation of powers between federal and provincial governments. The structure of the Senate. The distribution of revenue. The terms under which existing provincial debts would be assumed by the new federal government. Railway subsidies. Tariffs. The mechanics of governance in a country that would stretch four thousand miles from Atlantic to Pacific.

Macdonald, who had watched the American Civil War with horror, believed passionately in a strong central government. He regarded the American federal system — with its robust state sovereignty and its doctrine of states’ rights — as the design flaw that had made civil war possible. The Canadian constitution, he argued, should vest residual powers in the federal government rather than the provinces, ensuring that the centre would hold even when the parts pulled away.

Cartier agreed to this arrangement — against the instincts of many French Canadians who wanted maximum provincial autonomy — because Macdonald guaranteed in return that Quebec would control education, civil law, and the institutions that protected the French language and Catholic religion. The deal was pragmatic, asymmetric, and has been renegotiated in every generation since. But it held. It held through the conscription crises, through the Quiet Revolution, through the near-death experience of the 1995 referendum. It is still holding, however creakily, today.

McGee — the poet of Confederation, the most eloquent voice for the vision of a transcontinental northern nation — was shot dead on April 7, 1868, less than a year after the Dominion came into being.

He was returning to his boarding house on Sparks Street in Ottawa after a late-night parliamentary debate. A bullet struck him in the back of the head. He died on the doorstep. Patrick James Whelan, a Fenian sympathizer, was arrested, tried, and hanged in what would be the last public execution in Canada.

Thomas D’Arcy McGee in formal portrait — the Irish-Canadian statesman assassinated in 1868, less than a year after Confederation.

It remains the only assassination of a federal politician in Canadian history. One hundred and fifty-eight years. One assassination. The contrast with the country to the south — where the Bullet and the Body Politic series I have just completed documents a president surviving three violent incidents in twenty-two months — is the contrast between two fundamentally different political cultures.

The Dominion lost its poet before its first birthday. And it has proceeded, ever since, without one — building its national project not through soaring rhetoric but through the prosaic machinery of parliamentary democracy, federal-provincial negotiation, and the perpetual management of a country that was assembled by deal-makers and sustained by the willingness of its people to keep making deals.

Driving the Last Spike, Craigellachie, British Columbia, 1885 — the Canadian Pacific Railway physically united the Dominion from sea to sea.

The deal that bound Confederation together was, in its most literal form, a railway.

British Columbia agreed to join in 1871 on the explicit promise that a transcontinental railroad would be built within ten years. Without the railway, British Columbia was an isolated colony on the Pacific coast, accessible from the rest of Canada only by ship or by an overland journey of months through uncharted wilderness. The railway was not a metaphor. It was the physical sinew that would make a continent-wide country possible.

Macdonald built the railway. The Canadian Pacific Railway was completed in 1885, driven by the last spike at Craigellachie, British Columbia. It was an extraordinary achievement of engineering, finance, and political will. It was also built on the backs of thousands of Chinese labourers — men who were imported specifically for the most dangerous work, who were paid a fraction of what white labourers received, and who died in uncounted numbers in the tunnels and gorges of the Rockies. Their contribution was not officially acknowledged by the Canadian government for over a century.

The railway also facilitated the dispossession of Indigenous peoples across the prairies. The numbered treaties — negotiated under conditions that ranged from unequal to coercive — cleared the way for the railway and for the wave of settlement that followed. Indigenous nations that had occupied the plains for millennia were confined to reserves, their children taken to residential schools where the explicit policy was, in the words of one official, to “kill the Indian in the child.” The full reckoning with the residential school system — which operated from the 1880s to the 1990s and produced generations of trauma, cultural destruction, and death — is beyond the scope of a single essay, but it must be named here as part of the cost of the Confederation project.

And then there was Louis Riel.

Riel is the most tragic figure in Canadian history, and the most contested. He was Métis — a member of the distinct people born of the intermarriage between French-Canadian fur traders and Indigenous women, who had developed their own language (Michif), their own culture, and their own communities across the Red River Valley and the western plains. The Métis were neither fully Indigenous nor fully European. They were something new — a people of the in-between, and the Confederation project had no space for the in-between.

Louis Riel — hero to many in Quebec and the Métis, traitor to others in Ontario, and a symbol of the costs of nation-building.

In 1869-70, when the new Dominion attempted to assert control over the Red River Settlement — present-day Winnipeg — without consulting the Métis who lived there, Riel led the Red River Resistance. He established a provisional government, negotiated the Manitoba Act that brought the province into Confederation with guarantees of Métis land rights and bilingual governance, and was broadly recognized as the founder of Manitoba. He also authorized the execution of Thomas Scott, an Ontario Orangeman, a decision that made Riel a hero in Quebec and a murderer in Ontario — a split that would never heal.

Riel fled to the United States. He spent years in exile, suffering from what appears to have been serious mental illness, before returning to the Canadian West in 1885 at the request of Métis communities in Saskatchewan whose land rights were being ignored by Ottawa. The North-West Rebellion that followed was brief and hopeless — Métis and Indigenous fighters against the Canadian militia and the newly completed railway that carried federal troops west with unprecedented speed.

Riel surrendered. He was tried for high treason in Regina. His lawyers wanted to plead insanity — there was substantial evidence to support it. Riel refused. He wanted to be judged as a political leader, not as a madman. The jury, composed entirely of English-speaking Protestants, convicted him and recommended mercy. The judge sentenced him to death.

Macdonald could have commuted the sentence. Quebec begged him to. The French-Canadian press, the Catholic Church, and French-Canadian members of his own caucus urged clemency. Macdonald refused. “He shall hang,” Macdonald reportedly said, “though every dog in Quebec bark in his favour.”

On November 16, 1885 — just eight days after the last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway was driven at Craigellachie — Louis Riel was hanged in Regina. The timing was coincidental. The symbolism was not. The railway that bound the country together and the scaffold that executed the man who had tried to protect the people the railway displaced — these were twin expressions of the same Confederation project, and they revealed, with brutal clarity, whose interests that project served.

Riel’s execution split the country along the same English-French fault line that every crisis in Canadian history has exposed. In Quebec, Honoré Mercier rallied French Canadians with the cry that Riel had been killed because he was French. In Ontario, the Orange Order celebrated the execution as justice. The wound never fully healed. Riel was eventually recognized, more than a century later, as a Father of Confederation — but the recognition came too late for the Métis communities whose land rights he had fought to protect and whose dispossession proceeded without interruption after his death.

The Dominion was built on a railway that was built on exploitation, dispossession, and broken promises. Chinese labourers who died in the mountain passes. Indigenous nations confined to reserves. Métis communities scattered. A leader hanged. Any honest account of how Canada was forged must include this, because the Dominion’s founding bargain — like most founding bargains — served some communities at the expense of others, and the debts incurred in 1867 and 1885 remain, in many respects, unpaid.

And yet. The Dominion endured. Not because it was just — it was not. Not because it was inspired — it was not. But because it was, in its pragmatic, unglamorous, deal-making way, workable. A country built by negotiation, sustained by compromise, and held together by the perpetual willingness to sit in a room with people you disagree with and keep talking until something functional emerges.

That is the Dominion. Not born. Forged. In the heat of deadlock and the pressure of external threat, by men who were more interested in railway subsidies than in self-evident truths, and who produced — almost accidentally, almost reluctantly — one of the most enduring democracies on earth.
