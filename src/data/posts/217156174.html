Seven months ago, Masoud Pezeshkian was running Iran from an underground bunker while American and Israeli bombs fell on his country. On Wednesday, he stood at the same General Assembly lectern President Trump had used a day earlier to threaten Iran with annihilation, held up photographs of the dead, and told the room, in English translation carried live to 193 member states: “Yes they did hit us, but we did not bend the knee.” It was, as this series’ first Dispatch anticipated, the loudest single fact of the week — the first time in the UN’s 81-year history that the leader of a country actively at war with the host nation has set foot in New York to address the body that nation founded. But it was not, on a day this dense, even the only story that mattered.

“We will not ask permission from anyone”

Pezeshkian’s speech ran the full range this series has come to expect from Assembly addresses: defiance built on genuine grievance, a door left open for talks, and a nuclear position that has not moved an inch in seven months of war. “Iran must be powerful to avoid being threatened, to avoid its future being threatened,” he said. “Our defensive systems have been built so that no one can think that bombing Iranian cities will go unanswered.” He held up three sets of photographs — Ali Khamenei, the Supreme Leader killed by an Israeli strike on the war’s first day; schoolchildren killed in Minab; and athletes and children killed in a US strike on a sports stadium — and closed with a line aimed squarely at the man who had occupied the same podium the day before: “Iran must be understood by Mr. Trump and those who seek to bully us that we are ready for dialogue, diplomacy and negotiations without accepting the language of force.”

On the substance that actually matters, nothin moved. Iran will not accept limits on civilian enrichment; the United States wants zero enrichment and every major facility dismantled. The actual gap between the two sides is worth stating precisely, because “deadlock” undersells how far apart the two gpositions still sit: before the June agreement collapsed, American negotiators had demanded a twenty-year halt to all Iranian enrichment, the dismantling of Iran’s three major nuclear sites, and “snap” inspections anywhere in the country at any time. Iran’s counter-offer was a ten-year halt, dismantling two of the three sites rather than all of them, and “downblending” its existing stockpile of highly enriched uranium under UN supervision — with the United States permitted only to observe that process, not to enforce it. The IAEA, denied access to Iranian facilities since Tehran suspended cooperation after the June 2025 strikes, believes Iran may still hold enough highly enriched uranium, largely buried at a damaged complex near Isfahan, to produce nine or ten weapons if it chose to — though American intelligence maintains the site is under constant surveillance. Notably, the US intelligence community’s own pre-war assessment held that Khamenei — the elder, before his death in February — had not actually reauthorised the weapons programme he suspended back in 2003. Both governments, in other words, are negotiating over a programme that Washington’s own spies did not believe was actively being weaponised when the bombing started.

What did become clear on Wednesday is that this deadlock is not new. The conditions Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman described this week — lifting the naval blockade, releasing frozen assets, ending hostilities on all fronts — turn out to have been the backbone of an actual 14-point peace agreement Iran and the United States signed in June, which collapsed only after Iranian hardliners, seeking to sabotage it themselves, attacked ships crossing the Strait of Hormuz. Trump has since declared that signed memorandum “invalid” and wants something more comprehensive. Tuesday’s Witkoff-Kushner-Araghchi meeting, mediated by Qatar and reported in this series’ first Dispatch as a hopeful sign of quiet diplomacy beneath the podium theatre, is better understood now as an attempt to resurrect a deal that already existed once and was deliberately killed — which says something less optimistic about how durable any new version might prove.

Zelensky, and the war that keeps outgrowing its own borders

If Pezeshkian’s speech was this week’s most historically unprecedented, Zelensky’s fourth wartime address was its most data-heavy. He delivered it in English rather than through translation, calling Russia’s “ridiculous and never-ending war” a threat that would not stay contained to Ukraine: “Something always seems to stop Putin from saying, ‘That’s it, enough, peace.’” He named Moldova and Poland specifically as next in line if the fighting isn’t stopped, and offered a genuinely striking figure — Russia has suffered more than 248,000 battlefield casualties in the past eight months alone, a number Zelensky said Ukraine holds “video confirmation for every one of these losses.” He described Ukrainian forces now holding prisoners of war from 47 different nationalities, drawn into Russia’s army through “deception and tricks,” and made a direct appeal over Moscow’s head to those soldiers’ home governments: “It’s your citizens. Take care of them, and do not let Putin drag you into this war.”

The speech’s darkest note was about what comes after the current fighting rather than the fighting itself. “As early as next year, there is a real possibility that A.I., not only people, will decide what happens on the battlefield,” Zelensky warned. “We need peace before we reach that point.” He named a specific new technical threat — Russia’s “new jet-powered Shaheds,” an upgraded generation of the drones Russia started the war with, now flying farther, higher, and faster with heavier payloads than the original Iranian-supplied models. And on North Korea’s troop deployment to Russia’s front, at the request of an ally: “Why should Koreans give their lives in a war against Ukraine?”

None of this landed on a Security Council prepared to do anything about it. Russia’s foreign minister, Sergey Lavrov, spoke before Zelensky at a same-day Council session and closed the door flatly: “We are not going to have a pause in our special military operation,” dismissing any European push for a ceasefire as merely an attempt to “pump the Kyiv regime with weapons.” Ukraine’s foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, got the sharpest line of the session in response — “We must stop this war now before Putin enters your home. He is already at the entrance” — while noting the structural futility of the whole exercise: “So many speeches have been made in this council, so many right words, but the war drags on.” France’s Jean-Noël Barrot accused Russia of “lashing out at Europe in its entirety.” Britain’s Ed Miliband tried moral pressure — “Even if morality does not sway the Russian government, reality should” — while the American ambassador, Mike Waltz, searched for “common ground” between the two sides without ever directly answering Lavrov’s flat refusal to pause anything at all. This is, at this point, a scene this series has narrated enough times that the mechanism barely needs restating: a permanent member, sitting at the table, explaining on the record why the war it started will continue, while the other fourteen members talk past it.

Three voices, one afternoon, the same argument

What made Wednesday genuinely different from the rest of this week was that the Council’s structural problem stopped being something raised obliquely, the way Macron and King Abdullah had done on Tuesday, and became the explicit, stated subject of multiple speeches in a single afternoon. Botswana’s president, Duma Boko, told the Assembly that a Council which “continues to exclude Africa and Latin America from permanent membership cannot claim to reflect contemporary geopolitical realities.” Kenya’s William Ruto went further, and drew applause for it: “On the greatest questions of war and peace, five nations possess a power that the other 188 do not” — the UN’s own principle of sovereign equality, he argued, “ends at the doors of the Security Council.” “We cannot manage transformation with institutions that refuse to transform themselves.” And earlier the same day, the G4 — India, Brazil, Germany, and Japan, the long-standing coalition pushing for expanded permanent membership — issued a joint statement calling the Council “anachronistic” and unable to perform its “core responsibility as a guarantor of world security.”

Three separate, unconnected speeches naming the same structural complaint on the same afternoon is not proof that reform is imminent — this series’ account of the G4’s decades-long campaign should make that plain enough on its own. But it is a useful, concentrated snapshot of exactly how widely shared the diagnosis has become, even as the prescription remains as stuck as it has been since the 1990s.

Argentina’s Javier Milei spent his own Wednesday address making a strikingly different case against the same institution — not that the Council excludes too many voices, but that the whole organisation should barely exist at all. He called the UN a “useless organization” whose only real function was sustaining a class of “fatally arrogant parasites masquerading as well-intentioned bureaucrats,” criticised it by name for voting against Israel and for its handling of the pandemic, and pushed for AI deregulation in the same breath. Many of his specific complaints are shared by Trump, a close ally; the two men’s UN scepticism runs on parallel but distinct tracks — Trump’s largely transactional, Milei’s ideological. It is worth having both critiques in view side by side, because they arrived on the very same afternoon as Botswana’s, Kenya’s, and the G4’s, and they point in almost opposite directions: one side of the room arguing the Council needs to include more of the world, the other arguing the whole building is the problem. A reader could be forgiven for concluding the only thing Wednesday’s speeches agreed on was that the status quo has run out of defenders.

The globalist plot testifies to the Security Council

The day’s strangest juxtaposition happened a few hours later. Barely twenty-four hours after Trump told the Assembly that the United States “totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control” artificial intelligence, the Security Council convened its first-ever session specifically on AI — and heard directly from the chief executives of OpenAI and Anthropic, and the CEO of Hugging Face, all warning that the technology genuinely needs the kind of international coordination Trump had just finished dismissing.

Sam Altman told the Council that rapid recent progress had made AI’s “upside more tangible, but also the stakes and risks more immediate,” and framed the choice ahead as a “new renaissance of creativity and discovery” or “a new industrial revolution of upheaval and disarray.” On the catastrophic-risk debate specifically: “It doesn’t matter whether people put the risk of catastrophe at 10 percent or 1 percent or 12 percent or 0.1 percent. None of these levels are remotely acceptable.” Dario Amodei, appearing by video, went further: “I believe that this is the most important global security issue facing the world today... If managed poorly, I even believe that A.I. could be a risk to humanity as a whole.” He asked the Council specifically to back a ban on using AI to develop biological weapons, global testing standards for loss-of-control risk, and a notification system for AI misuses “significant to global security.” Hugging Face’s Clément Delangue, whose company was itself breached using OpenAI’s own technology earlier this year, struck a more measured note — “fear-based narratives are not the way to make the right decisions” — while crediting open-source AI models with letting his company defend itself: “We were attacked by A.I., but more importantly, we defended ourselves with A.I.”

The Council’s response split along entirely predictable lines. The White House’s own science and technology chief, Michael Kratsios, told the Council it could not oversee a technology it didn’t understand and that the session must not be allowed to “drift toward global governance,” explicitly invoking Trump’s own phrase from the day before. China rejected regulation while claiming a cooperative posture. Russia questioned whether AI was even within the Council’s mandate at all. France and Britain both pushed the other direction — the UK’s Ed Miliband: “We cannot abdicate our responsibility to our citizens, because we cannot outsource to private companies the first duty of government to protect our people, whatever the issue.” Two permanent members effectively refused to treat the subject as belonging to the Council in the first place; two others said exactly the opposite. It is, in miniature, this entire series’ argument about the veto rendered onto a subject the Council had never even attempted to touch before — proof that the paralysis isn’t confined to old wars, but is available, ready-made, for whatever the next crisis turns out to be.

A vassal state’s soft return, and a Board still sharpening its axe

Two smaller stories rounded out the day, both worth a line each. Venezuela’s interim president, Delcy Rodríguez, addressed the Assembly Wednesday evening — the first Venezuelan head of state to do so in person since 2018, and a deliberately soft performance after Tuesday’s warm, thumbs-up photo op with Trump, whom she thanked for “steadfast support” following June’s earthquakes. The contrast with her own country’s history at this podium is stark: Hugo Chávez once called an American president “the devil” from this same stage in 2006. Rodríguez, whose government now manages much of its finances and oil resources in partnership with Washington following January’s capture of Nicolás Maduro, arrived instead wearing a jacket printed with Mafalda, the beloved Latin American cartoon character famous for being troubled by cynicism and censorship — an irony one Crisis Group analyst noted would likely trouble the real Mafalda a great deal. The same day, opposition leader María Corina Machado — the country’s Nobel Peace laureate — was turned away from entering Venezuela three separate times, by air and by sea, according to her own representatives.

And Trump’s Board of Peace, created in January as his stated alternative to the UN’s own machinery in Gaza, unveiled a new six-month, $2.45 billion reconstruction plan Wednesday — even as donor funds from its previous iteration remain undisbursed, Israel has already rejected the board’s July proposal, and a board official admitted Israeli elections next month have made negotiations “a little bit more politically sensitive.” Jared Kushner’s own description of where things stand: “We’re just refining, sharpening the axe... getting ready for when we get the time to cut the tree.” Nearly 1,400 people have been killed in Gaza since the ceasefire that was supposed to end the killing.

Where Wednesday leaves things

Pezeshkian returned to Tehran having said, from the one stage that guaranteed a global audience, exactly what his government has said since February: defiant, unmoved on enrichment, willing to talk. Zelensky left having supplied the numbers and the warnings this war has generated in eight months, to a Council that heard Lavrov close the door on a pause before Zelensky had even reached the podium. Three separate leaders spent Wednesday afternoon making the same case for a Council that doesn’t look like the world it claims to govern, and the world’s own AI executives spent the evening asking that same Council for exactly the kind of coordination its most powerful member had spent Tuesday’s speech rejecting by name. Thursday brings Xi Jinping to Washington, and this Dispatch will be there for it.
