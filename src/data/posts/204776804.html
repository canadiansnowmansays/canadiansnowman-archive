We have reached, in this series, the question that every climate argument eventually arrives at, no matter where it starts. It can begin with a heat wave or a melting glacier or a burning forest or a carbon tax, but it ends, always, at the same hard door, and on that door is written a single word: who pays?

I want to spend this essay on the backlash, because I think it is the most underestimated force in the entire climate story, and because the temptation among people who share my general alarm about the climate is to dismiss the backlash as ignorance, as manipulation, as the gullible led astray by the wicked. That dismissal is a serious mistake, both morally and strategically, and I have come close to making it myself in earlier essays of this very series. So let me try to do better here, and take the backlash seriously as a thing with reasons.

The Europeans have coined a word for it: greenlash. The pushback against the European Green Deal — the EU’s sweeping plan to decarbonize the continent — has gathered into one of the most consequential political forces of the decade. Its most vivid image is the farmer: the tractors rolling into Paris and Brussels and Prague and Berlin, the manure dumped on government steps, the highways blockaded by men and women who grow the continent’s food and have decided that the climate agenda is being written on their backs. The protests began in earnest in the Netherlands over rules to cut nitrogen pollution, spread to France over fuel taxes and regulation, and rolled east into Poland and the Czech Republic, and they worked. Faced with the tractors, the European Commission caved: it watered down the climate targets for agriculture, shelved the pesticide-reduction scheme, suspended the fallow-land requirements, and removed a proposed thirty-per-cent emissions cut for the farm sector from its 2040 targets. The backlash did not merely complain. It changed the policy.

Now, why did the farmers revolt? Here is where the honest analysis has to begin, because the reasons are not stupid. A farmer makes capital investments — a tractor, a barn, a herd — on a horizon of ten or fifteen years, and the climate rules change every five, so that the ground beneath a working life keeps shifting. The farmer is told what to plant and when, asked to leave land fallow, required to navigate reporting requirements that turn a vocation into paperwork, and then watches food imports arrive from countries that face none of these rules, undercutting prices in the farmer’s own market. The farmer is, in other words, being asked to bear concentrated, immediate, personal costs for a diffuse, distant, collective benefit — and to do it while being lectured by urban populations who will feel none of the same pain. Whatever you think of the politics, the grievance is real, and it has a structure, and the structure is the same one I have been circling since the essay on the carbon tax that died: the costs of climate action are visible, local, and felt by specific people, while the benefits are invisible, global, and shared by everyone, including the unborn. That asymmetry is not a detail. It may be the central political fact of the entire crisis.

And it generalizes far beyond the farm. The original modern instance was the gilets jaunes — the yellow vests — who erupted across France in 2018 not as climate deniers but as ordinary people, many of them rural and exurban, who could not absorb a rise in the fuel tax meant to fight climate change. They were not wrong that the tax fell hardest on people who had no alternative to driving, who lived where the train does not go, whose lives had been quietly arranged around cheap petrol by the same society now declaring petrol a sin. “The elites talk about the end of the world,” went the slogan that defined the movement, “while we are talking about the end of the month.” I have rarely seen the politics of climate compressed more honestly into a single sentence. The end of the world versus the end of the month: that is the contest, and the month tends to win, because the month is now and the world is later, and the people worried about the month are not fools for prioritizing the thing that will hurt them first.

The right has understood this with a clarity that the centre and the left have not. Across Europe, the populist right has made opposition to climate policy a central plank — Le Pen in France, Wilders in the Netherlands, the AfD in Germany campaigning against what it calls an “eco-dictatorship,” the winning Polish presidential candidate promising a referendum on the Green Deal. They have fused the climate backlash with their broader politics of grievance and nation and resentment of distant elites, and it is a potent fusion, because it takes a genuine material complaint — this transition is being done to me, not with me, and I cannot afford it — and weds it to an identity and an enemy. The genius and the danger of it is that the material complaint underneath is legitimate. You cannot defeat a movement by telling the people in it that their lived experience of the cost is imaginary. It is not imaginary. The fuel does cost more. The farm is harder to run. The month is genuinely hard to get to the end of.

So what is the lesson? It is not, I want to be clear, that climate action is therefore impossible, or that the backlash is correct that we should simply stop. That conclusion is the counsel of despair I warned against at the very beginning, and it is also suicidal, because the heat and the floods and the fires do not care about the price of diesel. The lesson is harder and more constructive than either “the backlash is right” or “the backlash is stupid.” It is this: a climate policy that ignores the question of who pays will be defeated by the people who are made to pay, and they will be right to defeat it, and they will hand their victory to politicians who will then do nothing about the climate at all. The backlash is not an argument against climate action. It is an argument against unjust climate action — against a transition whose costs are loaded onto farmers and commuters and the rural poor while its benefits and its moral satisfactions accrue to a comfortable urban class that can afford the heat pump and the electric car and the conscience.

This is where my own country’s experience, which I described in the essay on the carbon tax, rejoins the European story. The Canadian consumer carbon price died for exactly this reason: it was, or was successfully portrayed as, a cost loaded onto ordinary people, and the rebate that was meant to make it fair was invisible and therefore politically worthless. The British Columbia version survived as long as it did partly because it returned its revenue as visible income-tax cuts. The design lesson is not subtle: if you want people to accept a cost, you must make the compensation at least as visible as the cost, and you must make sure it actually reaches the people who bear the burden, and you must not allow the whole thing to become a symbol that a clever opposition can hang around your neck. We failed that test in Canada. France failed it with the fuel tax. The EU is failing it with the farmers. The pattern is consistent, and it is not about the science at all. It is about justice, and visibility, and trust.

There is a phrase for the thing we keep failing to deliver, and it is worth naming because naming it is the beginning of doing better: a just transition. The idea is old and simple — that the move away from fossil fuels and toward a sustainable economy must be done in a way that does not sacrifice the workers and communities who depend on the old order, that brings them along rather than leaving them as roadkill on the highway to net zero. It is the principle that the coal miner and the farmer and the oil-rig worker are not obstacles to be overcome but citizens to be included, owed retraining, investment, and a genuine place in what comes next. And it is, almost everywhere, the part we have done worst. We have been good at announcing targets and bad at building the bridges that carry real people across to them. The backlash is, in large part, the revenge of the people who were promised a transition and handed only a disruption.

When it has been done well, it has worked, which is the hopeful half of the lesson. There are regions — parts of Denmark, pockets of Germany’s old industrial heartland — where the wind-turbine factory was deliberately planted in the town the coal mine was leaving, where the new green jobs were sited where the old dirty ones died, where the community could see the future arriving in a form it recognized as its own rather than as a lecture from the capital. Where people can see the transition working for them, in a paycheque and a purpose and not merely a slogan, the backlash loses its fuel. The lesson is not that climate action is doomed by human selfishness; it is that climate action divorced from economic justice is doomed, and climate action wedded to it has a fighting chance. We have simply, mostly, chosen the divorce.

There is a darker corner of this subject that I will name but not fully enter, because it deserves more care than a few lines and may earn its own essay someday. It is the question that hangs over the whole backlash: if democracies keep rejecting the cost of climate action, will some people begin to argue that democracy itself is the obstacle — that the crisis is too urgent to be left to electorates who will always choose the end of the month? I have seen the argument made, usually in a whisper, sometimes out loud. I think it is both wrong and dangerous, and I think the backlash, for all its frustrations, is actually the proof that the democratic path is the only durable one: a transition imposed over the population’s objection would be torn up the moment the population got the chance, exactly as the carbon taxes were. The only climate policy that lasts is one the people will keep voting for. Which means the work — the real work, harder than any engineering — is the work of building a transition that ordinary people experience as fair. We have not done it yet. The tractors are the receipt for our failure to do it.

Next: the hardest mirror of all, the one I have been putting off since the introduction. Not the populists, not the Americans, not the Chinese — but my own country, and the oil sands, and the comfortable Canadian habit of believing we are the good guys.

Degrees

2018 — the year the gilets jaunes movement erupted in France over a climate fuel tax, giving the world the defining slogan of the climate backlash: the elites worry about the end of the world, while ordinary people worry about the end of the month. Seven years later, no government has yet solved the problem that sentence describes, which is why the tractors keep coming.
