Stephen Gordon July 8, 2026

In March, I wrote a postscript to The American Contradiction documenting Pete Hegseth’s purge of decorated officers from Army promotion lists. In June, I wrote a second, after The New York Times revealed that Hegseth had been consulting a crowdsourced watchlist of “woke” officers to override the judgments of Navy promotion boards — and had told Congress, under questioning, that he was unaware of interventions he had personally made.

Both times I closed with some version of the same sentence: the argument had not been complicated by events. It had been confirmed.

I did not expect a third occasion so soon. But Clint Smith’s July 2026 cover story for The Atlantic — and the Radio Atlantic conversation built around it — does something the first two postscripts could not. It moves the story from mechanism to testimony. Smith spoke with more than two dozen current and former Black service members, and what he heard was not primarily about watchlists or board procedure. It was about what it feels like to serve a country that is, in his words, engaged in “delegitimizing the accomplishments—and the very presence—of Black people in the military.”

The Cognitive Dissonance

The officers Smith interviewed described something close to a double consciousness, though he doesn’t use that word. On one hand, they are hearing — from the Secretary of Defense on down — that their ranks were handed to them rather than earned, that thirty years of deployments and boards and character reviews amount to a “so-called first” rather than a career. On the other hand, many of them are second- or third-generation service members, acutely aware that their grandfathers fought in segregated units, that some served in a South still lynching Black men while they wore the uniform, that the arc bending toward justice has always bent slowly and never on its own.

That tension produces an odd kind of endurance. Several officers described the current moment to Smith using weather metaphors — a storm that will pass, a season that will change — and a felt obligation to stay through it, specifically so that someone is still standing on the other side to rebuild what’s being dismantled. One officer’s formulation, relayed on Radio Atlantic, was that you have to treat the moment like a retirement portfolio: if you check it every day you will panic and pull your money out, but the only sane approach is the long view, measured across an entire career, not a news cycle.

That is not optimism. It is closer to triage.

The Meritocracy Argument, Examined

Hegseth’s stated rationale — a move toward a “purely meritocratic,” “color-blind” military — has been the load-bearing plank of every purge I have documented in this series. The June postscript showed what that rationale looks like in practice: a one-star Navy list with no women on it, in a service where women are twenty-one percent of the force; a watchlist consulted in place of a board’s judgment; a Secretary who told a House committee he didn’t know about an intervention he’d made himself.

Smith’s reporting adds the structural detail that makes the meritocracy claim harder to sustain, not easier. Promotion to flag rank does not happen by accident or by quota. It runs through boards that review a candidate’s entire service record — deployments, combat history, character references — over weeks, with a selection rate in the low single digits. These are not, as Smith puts it, decisions made on a whim or to fill an identity marker. They are decisions built on paper trails thick enough to survive scrutiny — until the watchlist and the Secretary’s office decided the paper trail didn’t matter.

What the Military Was For

The part of Smith’s reporting that belongs permanently in this series is his argument about mobility. The military has functioned, across generations, as one of the only reliable on-ramps to the American middle class for Black families — often making a single officer the financial centerpiece not just of a spouse and children, but of an entire extended family network leaning on that stability. Push that person out early, and the damage doesn’t stop at one household. It ripples through however many relatives depended on it.

This is the piece the “meritocracy” framing conveniently omits: the military was never only a career. For a great many Black American families, it was the mechanism — sometimes the only one — for building the kind of generational stability other Americans took for granted. Dismantling the officer corps that made that possible doesn’t just cost the individuals removed. It removes a rung of the ladder for everyone standing behind them.

The Classroom Test, Again

In April I proposed the Classroom Test: if your politics require you to walk into a room of three-year-olds and explain why some of their friends are problems, your politics have failed. I applied it to gerrymandering, then to the Navy’s watchlist purge. It applies once more here, with a harder edge.

Those children will grow up. Some of them, per the actuarial reality of who serves, will be Black, and some of those will want to fly, or command, or make flag rank the way the officers Smith interviewed did. What Smith’s reporting confirms is that the country is currently constructing, in real time, the explanation those future officers will eventually be handed: that the room they walked into was never really built to hold them, that their presence at the top of it will always be treated as a claim requiring proof rather than a fact requiring none.

Confirmed, Again

I am a Canadian writing about an American institution I have never served in, watching from a country with its own unfinished reckonings. I don’t pretend that gives me the standing to tell American service members what their oath should mean to them. But I can observe, as I have three times now, that the pattern doesn’t change: exclusion dressed as merit, tested against the actual record, and found — every time — to be exclusion.

The officers Smith spoke to are, by his account, mostly staying. Not because the storm has passed. Because someone has to be there when it does.

It has been confirmed again.
