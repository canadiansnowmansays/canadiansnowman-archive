Anthony Stephen Fauci was born on Christmas Eve, 1940, in Brooklyn, above his father’s pharmacy, into the kind of Italian-American Catholic household where a boy’s report card mattered roughly as much as his soul did. His father, Stephen, filled prescriptions behind the counter; his mother, Eugenia, and his older sister, Denise, worked the register and the deliveries. Young Anthony delivered too, pedaling a bicycle around the neighborhood with medicine in the basket, which is as tidy an origin story for a public health career as biography ever hands a writer for free. I am not going to pretend it doesn’t help the narrative. It also happens to be true.

The formation that mattered more, though, came a few years later, at Regis High School in Manhattan — a Jesuit school, tuition-free, admission by exam only, the kind of institution that took working-class Catholic boys and taught them Latin, rhetoric, and a specific version of discipline that assumed excellence was a debt owed rather than a gift received. Fauci has said in interviews across the decades that the Jesuits gave him three things: a habit of rigorous, structured thought; a belief that intelligence obligated you to public service rather than private comfort; and a tolerance for argument that bordered on relish. He was not, by his own account, the top student in his class. He was, by every account since, the classmate who wanted the debate to keep going after the bell rang. From Regis he went on to the College of the Holy Cross, another Jesuit institution, this one in Worcester, Massachusetts, where he captained the basketball team as its point guard — a detail Fauci has returned to often enough over the years, including decades later while trying to explain to television interviewers why he thought he understood pressure, that it has become something close to a personal parable. A short point guard, he liked to say, has to be the smartest player on the floor, because he cannot be the strongest one.

There is something worth noticing in how consistently Fauci has reached for that same image across fifty years of interviews, long before anyone outside his own field had reason to ask him about his childhood. It is a story about compensating for a physical limitation through sheer preparation and superior judgment — a self-portrait, essentially, that he had already settled on decades before a pandemic gave the rest of the country a reason to either believe it or doubt it. Holy Cross itself, like Regis, ran on a curriculum still heavy with the classical Jesuit emphasis on rhetoric and moral philosophy alongside the pre-medical sciences, and Fauci has said the combination left him more comfortable arguing a position in public than most of the physicians he later supervised — a comfort that served him well in front of Senate committees for most of his career, and rather less well once the committees in question decided that comfort itself was suspicious.

He arrived at Cornell University Medical College in 1962 and graduated first in his class in 1966, which by itself would have been enough to end most stories of ambition satisfied. Instead he went to New York Hospital–Cornell Medical Center for his residency, and in 1968 arrived at the National Institutes of Health, in Bethesda, Maryland, as a clinical associate — a young doctor in an institution that, at the time, had nothing like the cultural weight it would eventually carry. This is the part of Fauci’s career that almost never makes it into a headline, because nothing about it is dramatic in the way a pandemic is dramatic. For most of the 1970s, Fauci worked in relative obscurity inside the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, doing bench and clinical research on immune-mediated diseases — vasculitis syndromes in particular, a family of conditions in which the body’s own immune system attacks its blood vessels, often fatally. Before Fauci’s work, a diagnosis of Wegener’s granulomatosis was close to a death sentence: most patients died within a year of onset, generally from kidney failure. Fauci and his NIH colleagues developed a combination therapy, adapting cyclophosphamide protocols originally built for cancer treatment, and administered it in carefully staged clinical trials through the mid-1970s. By the end of the decade, remission rates for patients on the regimen had climbed into the range of ninety percent — a reversal so complete that it changed how physicians thought about autoimmune disease generally, not merely about the specific conditions Fauci had studied. The New England Journal of Medicine would later describe the shift as one of the more significant achievements in the treatment of autoimmune vasculitis in the twentieth century. Almost nobody outside the field noticed at the time, which is more or less the point: this was not a man building himself a public profile. This was a man building an institution, quietly, one grant cycle and one clinical trial at a time.

He became director of NIAID in 1984, at age forty-three, inheriting an institute that was, relative to its NIH siblings, something of an afterthought — respected within infectious disease circles, invisible to nearly everyone else. Over the subsequent decades he grew it into a research operation with a budget north of $6 billion a year, one of the largest and most consequential branches of the NIH, its portfolio eventually stretching from HIV/AIDS to Ebola to anthrax to, ultimately, coronaviruses. Colleagues who worked under him across that period describe a leader who ran his institute with an intensity that bordered on the theatrical in its own right, long before television ever asked him to be theatrical for a national audience. Being early to a meeting with Fauci, in the accounting several former NIAID officials have given over the years, meant you were on time; being on time meant you were late; being late meant something considerably worse than either. Judith Feinberg, an infectious-disease researcher who worked at the institute early in her career, has described him as a man with what she called a “very Italian temper,” prone to eruptions behind closed doors aimed at anyone who pretended to understand something they did not. This was not a flaw invented by the pandemic or manufactured by his enemies. It was there from the beginning, inseparable from the same perfectionism that built the institute in the first place. A boss who tolerates sloppy reasoning does not build a $6 billion research empire out of an also-ran division of the NIH. A boss who does not tolerate it is not necessarily pleasant to work for. Both things can be true of the same man, and were.

What Fauci did not do, across those same decades, was pick a side. He served under Ronald Reagan, George H.W. Bush, Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama, Donald Trump in his first term, and Joe Biden — six presidents before the current one made seven — and by every account from colleagues who worked closest to him, none of them, nor his own staff at NIAID, ever quite knew how he voted. He has described himself for decades as a registered independent, and whatever private opinions he held about the men he served, he kept them out of the institute entirely. This was not merely a personality trait; it was, by his own account and by the account of nearly everyone who has studied his career, a deliberate professional strategy. Fauci understood, earlier and more completely than most senior scientists in government, that his usefulness to the causes he cared about — AIDS research funding, pandemic preparedness, the institute’s basic survival across changes of administration — depended entirely on his being perceived as untouchable by the ordinary mechanics of partisan suspicion. A Republican president could trust him because he had never said an unkind word about a Republican president. A Democratic Congress could fund him because he had never said an unkind word about anyone’s opposition either. As Katherine J. Wu reported for The Atlantic, this apolitical posture is precisely what allowed him to receive the Presidential Medal of Freedom from George W. Bush for his work building PEPFAR, and to be praised within the same decade by Barack Obama as a once-in-a-century public health leader. It is difficult to think of another unelected American official who managed that particular trick across five decades and that many administrations, and it is worth sitting with the fact that it was a genuine, cultivated skill, not an accident of good luck or a media halo he backed into.

It is also worth sitting with what that skill cost, because nothing in this series is going to argue that Fauci’s decades of nonpartisan positioning were without their own kind of vanity. To be the person everyone trusts, across every administration, for fifty years, is not merely a service rendered to the country. It is also an extraordinarily comfortable position to occupy — a seat from which you are rarely challenged, in which your judgment is rarely second-guessed by anyone with the standing to make the second-guessing stick, and in which your own sense of your indispensability has every incentive to grow undisturbed. The habits that would later curdle into something his critics could fairly call self-regard — the diary entries marveling over his own fame, the seven separate mentions of standing ovations, the visible discomfort with being challenged that colleagues have described in private meetings for decades — did not appear in March 2020. They were present in embryo in the young point guard who believed he had to be the smartest man on the floor, and they had forty years to mature, entirely out of public view, before a pandemic finally handed them a stage large enough to matter.

None of which changes the underlying fact this essay is chiefly interested in establishing: that by the time most Americans had ever heard his name, Anthony Fauci had already spent more than three decades building one of the more genuinely consequential scientific careers in the postwar United States, almost entirely without an audience. The vasculitis work saved lives that will never be counted in any pandemic ledger. The institute he built from scratch became the vehicle through which the American government would eventually fund and coordinate the AIDS research that forms the subject of the next essay in this series — a fight that, unlike the quiet vasculitis years, would force Fauci out of the laboratory and into the one confrontation of his career that genuinely changed him. Before he was a symbol, in other words, he was simply very good at his job, for a very long time, in a way that almost nobody outside a fairly narrow circle of specialists had any reason to notice. That the reward for three uneventful decades of competence would eventually be three hours of hostile questions about the color of his tie is not a story about who Anthony Fauci is. It is a story about what happens to expertise once it becomes famous enough to be feared.
