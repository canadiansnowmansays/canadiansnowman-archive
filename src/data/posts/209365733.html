There is a particular kind of erasure that a free country is supposed to find unthinkable, and it has a plain old-fashioned name that no euphemism has ever quite managed to cover: the book ban. The removal of titles from a library because the state has decided its readers should not read them. This series has watched memory pulled off walls and out of servers; this essay is about memory pulled off shelves — and about the moment, in the spring of 2025, when a United States service academy became, by one careful accounting, the first American college to ban books since the current wave of censorship began.

I want to start with the single title that makes the whole episode legible, because occasionally the material arranges itself into a metaphor so exact that a writer’s only job is to get out of its way.

Among the first books removed from the Nimitz Library at the United States Naval Academy was Maya Angelou’s I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings — her 1969 memoir of growing up Black in the segregated American South, a book about racism, trauma, and the recovery of a voice. It was pulled, along with hundreds of others, for the offense of touching the subjects the administration had decided its future officers should not encounter. And when a Naval Academy alumnus organized a campaign to get the books back, he named it, inevitably, Operation Caged Bird. He said he could feel the bars closing in — on what he was allowed to read, and what he was not.

A country that pulls I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings from the shelf of its naval officers, and thereby teaches an alumnus exactly why the caged bird sings, has arrived at a level of self-parody that would be funny if it were not an actual act of state censorship at an actual military academy. Let me lay out how it happened.

Four Hundred Books

At the end of March 2025, the leadership of the Navy, acting on directives from the office of the Secretary of Defense, ordered the Nimitz Library at Annapolis to review its collection and remove books touching diversity, gender, race, and related themes — in compliance with a pair of executive orders the president had signed in January, one titled “Ending Radical and Wasteful Government DEI Programs and Preferencing,” the other “Ending Radical Indoctrination in K-12 Schooling,” the latter extended by directive to reach the military academies.

The method is worth pausing on, because it is the same automated crudeness we saw in the deletion of the federal datasets. Officials ran keyword searches against the library catalogue. Roughly nine hundred books surfaced. Department reviewers narrowed the list, and on March 31 and April 1 they pulled the shelved and cataloged copies of 381 titles. The Naval Academy then posted, on its own website, an extraordinarily terse press release with a link to the spreadsheet — a public list of the banned books, published by the institution that banned them.

Look at what was on it. I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, by Maya Angelou. How to Be an Antiracist, by Ibram X. Kendi. White Rage, by Carol Anderson. Bodies in Doubt, Elizabeth Reis’s history of intersex people in America. Harper Lee’s To Kill a Mockingbird was reported among the flagged. These are not, whatever the executive orders imagine, works of indoctrination. Many are simply history — academic accounts of the movements and injuries and arguments that made the country what it is. Their common feature, as a local librarian observed, was less “DEI” than authorship: they were disproportionately by writers who were Black, female, or LGBTQ, or anyone who was not a white, heterosexual man. The keyword search, in the end, was a search for a certain kind of author as much as a certain kind of subject.

A retired Air Force chief historian called the effort what it was: a “cleansing.” He added that it revealed “a certain kind of weakness” — a lack of confidence. That is exactly right, and it is the thread this series keeps pulling. A country secure in its account of itself does not need to pull To Kill a Mockingbird from the shelf where its naval officers might find it. The removal is not strength. It is fear of what the midshipmen might read and conclude.

“Not Immediately Available”

Now the euphemism, because there is always a euphemism, and this series is in some ways a catalogue of them.

When the Naval Academy was asked whether it had banned these books, its spokesman demurred. The books had not been banned, he said. They had been rendered “not immediately available.” They had been moved to a room where library patrons could no longer reach them.

Consider the phrase against the fact. A book relocated to a room the reader may not enter is, for that reader, gone — as gone as if it had been pulped. “Not immediately available” performs the same softening we have seen at every stop in this series: the Smithsonian did not delete the impeachments, it “restored the exhibit to 2008”; the parks did not censor the signs, they conducted a review; HHS did not suppress the data, it noted the page “does not reflect reality.” Here, the academy did not ban the book; it made the book not immediately available, by putting it in a locked room. Each phrase is chosen so that the person repeating it can feel reasonable, and the person describing what actually happened — a book removed so students cannot read it — can be made to sound as though they are overstating things. The locked room is the memory hole with a nicer sign on the door.

The List a Judge Had to Pry Loose

There is a second act to this, and it is the one that shows the machine did not stop when the cameras arrived.

After the public outcry — after Operation Caged Bird, after the alumnus’s fundraiser stocked a nearby bookshop with free copies of every contested title, after historians and members of Congress objected — most of the 381 books were quietly returned to the Nimitz shelves in May, with a small number sequestered pending “review.” A partial retreat. A win, of the kind this series occasionally gets to record.

But the Naval Academy was the visible tip. Beneath it, the Department of Defense Education Activity — DoDEA, which runs some hundred and sixty schools for military families across eleven countries, two territories, and seven states — went on removing books from the libraries that serve the children of American service members. And here is the detail that belongs in the same glass case as everything else in this series: the full list of nearly six hundred books DoDEA removed became public only because a federal judge ordered it released, as part of a lawsuit brought by the ACLU on behalf of military families and their children.

Sit with that. The government removed hundreds of books from the libraries of the schools attended by the children of the very soldiers it commands, and it took a court order to make it even disclose which books. The Naval Academy at least posted its own list; DoDEA had to be compelled. An erasure you must sue to see the shape of is the most complete kind there is, because you cannot object to the removal of a book whose removal you cannot confirm. The judge, in forcing the list into daylight, did for those six hundred books what the data-rescue archivists did for the deleted datasets and what Judge Kelley did for the park signs: made the erasure visible, which is the necessary first step before it can be resisted.

The Reader They Are Afraid Of

Let me say who is actually at the center of this, because it is easy, amid the executive orders and the keyword searches, to lose the human being the whole apparatus is pointed at. It is a reader. Specifically, it is a young reader in uniform, or the child of someone in uniform — a midshipman at Annapolis, a soldier’s kid at a DoDEA school in Germany — walking into a library and reaching for a shelf.

The entire machinery of this episode exists to control what that reader finds when they reach. Not to punish an author, though it does that; not even, in the end, to score a point in the culture war, though it does that too. Its deepest function is to shape the mind of the person who will lead, or live in, the country next — to ensure that a future naval officer does not encounter White Rage or How to Be an Antiracist or Maya Angelou’s account of a caged childhood, and so does not carry those particular pieces of the American past forward into command. This is the version of “who controls the past controls the future” that operates not on the record but on the reader of the record. You do not have to burn every copy of a book if you can simply ensure the next generation of officers never meets it on a shelf.

That is why the library is not a soft or secondary target in this series but a central one. A memorial holds the memory of a person; an archive holds the memory of a record; a library holds the memory of everything a society has thought worth writing down, and hands it, open-handedly, to whoever walks in and reaches. To close a shelf is to reach into the future reader’s mind and remove, before they ever meet it, a thought the state would prefer they never have.

They pulled I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings from a naval library, and an alumnus named his resistance after it, and a bookshop filled with free copies, and most of the books came back, and six hundred more had to be pried into public view by a judge. The caged bird, it turns out, still sings — a little louder, now, for having briefly been locked in a room where the midshipmen could not hear it. But the hand that reached for the shelf has not been withdrawn. It has only, for the moment, been slapped.

This is Part 8 of The Ministry of Memory, a series on the erasure of shared public memory. This essay follows the erasing onto the library shelf — the roughly 400 books pulled from the U.S. Naval Academy’s Nimitz Library in the first documented American college book ban of this censorship wave, and the nearly 600 removed from the schools of military families, a list a federal judge had to order released. The target here is not a record but a reader: the future officer, and the soldier’s child, reaching for a shelf.
