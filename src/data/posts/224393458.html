On October 13, 2026, The Atlantic published Josh Tyrangiel’s cover story for its November issue under a headline that was half joke and half dare: “Choose Your Overlord.” The piece is long, deeply reported and very good. It follows Jeff Bezos from a foam party on a yacht off the Croatian island of Cres to the launch pads of Cape Canaveral, and Elon Musk from a Moscow meeting where a Russian engineer is said to have spat on him to a $1.8 trillion stock-market listing. Its argument is that two men are now racing to build the infrastructure of an off-world economy, that governments have largely stopped asking whether this is wise, and that the only check on either billionaire may turn out to be the other one.

Jake Sullivan, who was Joe Biden’s national security adviser, gave Tyrangiel the line that sums it up. America needs a second source of rockets, he said, and the likeliest route to one runs through another mercurial billionaire. Senator Mark Kelly, asked whether he trusted either man completely, called it a loaded question.

I read the piece twice and came away with one objection, which is to the headline rather than the reporting. Why are we choosing between two?

Musk and Bezos are the most vivid cases of a private fortune doing what states used to do, because rockets are vivid. But they are not the only cases, and they may not be the most consequential. One man decides what more than three and a half billion people see in their news feeds each day. Another has placed his former employee one heartbeat from the presidency. A family that already owned a cloud-computing giant now owns CBS and CNN. An Australian-born press baron spent half a century helping to pick prime ministers and presidents on three continents, then spent his nineties in a Nevada probate fight over who would inherit the habit. A network of foundations, law firms and model bills has spent fifty years rewriting American regulatory law without ever putting a face on a poster.

Tyrangiel framed a duel. This series sets out to audit a field.

A word with a long pedigree

“Oligarch” is a word that does a great deal of work in 2026 and is rarely defined. In ordinary use it means something close to “very rich person I distrust,” which is why it gets thrown at Russian aluminium magnates, Silicon Valley founders and Hungarian-American philanthropists with roughly equal enthusiasm, and why the people it is thrown at tend to dismiss it as an insult rather than a description.

It is a description, and an old one. Aristotle sorted constitutions by who rules and in whose interest. Rule by the few for the common good he called aristocracy. Rule by the few for their own benefit he called oligarchy, and he added the detail that matters most: in practice the few who rule an oligarchy are the rich, and they rule because they are rich. The distinction was never between wealth and poverty as such. It was between power that answers to the governed and power that answers to its owners.

The modern sense of the word was minted in Moscow in the middle of the 1990s, when a handful of bankers acquired Russia’s oil, nickel and television channels at fire-sale prices, financed and broadcast a president’s re-election, and briefly treated the Kremlin as a client. That episode gave us the template for the word as we now hear it: men who own the networks the way other people own lawnmowers, and who expect the state to return their calls. It also gave us the template for how such men can be stopped, which is not a comforting one. That is where Part I begins.

Why a rubric

If “oligarch” is going to be more than an insult, it needs a test that can be applied the same way to everyone, including people the reader admires and people the reader can’t stand.

I have used this approach before. In Nation Bullies, I scored great powers from Rome to the present against a fixed checklist rather than relying on the reader’s sense of who the villains were, and the results were more interesting for it. Britain did worse than its admirers expected, and some of the usual suspects did better. A rubric doesn’t remove judgment, but it forces the judgment into the open, where readers can argue with it item by item.

So every subject in this series is scored against the same five tests, each marked from zero to five, for a total out of twenty-five. I call the total the Overlord Index, because a little mockery helps the medicine go down.

Chokepoints. Does society depend on infrastructure they control? The question isn’t how much money someone has but what stops working if they decide it should. Launch capacity, satellite internet, cloud computing, payment rails, refineries, pipelines and the platforms that half the world uses to message its relatives all qualify. A fortune held in index funds does not. When Musk declined in 2022 to extend Starlink coverage over Crimea for a Ukrainian operation, he demonstrated what a five on this test looks like.

Megaphone. Do they own or steer the channels through which the public learns things? This covers newspapers and networks, but also the algorithms that decide which newspaper stories anyone sees. Owning a megaphone is not the same as shouting through it, and the score reflects both reach and willingness to use it.

Purchase. Have they bought political access? That includes campaign donations and super PACs, inaugural-fund cheques and dinners at Mar-a-Lago, but also the subtler forms: placing protégés in office, sitting on advisory councils, or simply being the person the president phones. Tyrangiel reports that Musk spent $288 million to help elect Donald Trump and other Republicans in 2024. That is one way to score a five. There are cheaper ones.

Defiance. Can they bargain with the state as an equal, or refuse it outright? An ordinary citizen who ignores a law is fined. A company that can switch off a country’s news, or threaten to ground the only American spacecraft able to reach the International Space Station, is doing something else. Canadians have a home-grown example: in August 2023 Meta stopped carrying news on Facebook and Instagram for everyone in Canada rather than comply with the Online News Act, and the block has outlasted a wildfire season and a federal election.

Impunity. Who can actually check them, and has anyone managed it? Here a five means effectively unaccountable: voting control that no shareholder can touch, a private company with no public filings, a legal team larger than the regulator pursuing it. A low score here is the best news an oligarch’s critics can hope for.

To these five I add one thing that is not scored: civic return. What do they give back, and on whose terms? It sits beside the total in each assessment rather than inside it, for a reason the series will test more than once. Philanthropy can be enormous, sincere and life-saving, and it can still be a form of power that answers to nobody. Good intentions should be recorded. They shouldn’t be allowed to launder the arithmetic.

Each assessment also records political contributions: the headline figure for the money a subject has put into campaigns, PACs and inaugural funds, with a note on whether it was personal, corporate or network-wide. Money already counts towards the Purchase score, so this line isn't scored separately. It is there so readers can compare the cheques directly, which the Concluding Essay does in a single table. Every score and figure in the series is also collected on a companion page, The Overlord Ledger, which lets readers compare any two subjects head to head.

The house rules

A few conventions, so that readers can hold me to them.

Each subject is scored at the height of their power unless the essay says otherwise. The Russian bankers are scored as they stood in 1996, not in exile. Rockefeller is scored as Standard Oil stood before the Supreme Court broke it up in 1911.

Scores are argued, not asserted. Every essay ends with a short assessment box, written in the dry language of a corporate performance review, because anyone who has worked in a large company knows that the annual review is where power is measured in its most honest and least flattering form. The box gives the five scores with a one-line note on each, a sentence on civic return and a manager’s comment. The essay above it explains the reasoning, and where I have marked someone up or down from where I expected to, I say so.

The test is applied evenly, including to people I have praised. Readers of this Substack know that I once argued at length that George Soros deserved a Nobel Peace Prize more than Donald Trump did. He is in this series too, scored against exactly the same five questions, and if the rubric is honest it should be able to survive that. Part X is where you find out.

And Musk gets less space here than his score would justify, for the simple reason that I have already written a great deal about him. The Musk Stack covers his portfolio in far more detail than one essay could. In this series he appears where he belongs, as a point of comparison, scored alongside Bezos in Part III. (Readers who prefer their overlords in costume can find a certain electric-ark captain named Ollie fleeing the planet in my Sunday satire, Ollie's Ark. Any resemblance remains the reader's responsibility.)

The field

The series runs to fourteen numbered essays and a conclusion.

It opens with two historical benchmarks. Part I goes to Russia in the 1990s, where the word was born and where a strong state eventually crushed its oligarchs, only to replace them with a court of loyal ones. Part II goes to the Gilded Age, when Rockefeller controlled most of America’s oil refining and J.P. Morgan twice did a central bank’s job because the country did not have one, and asks how the republic eventually answered them. It took a generation, a muckraking press, new antitrust law and two constitutional amendments, but it worked, and it is the yardstick for everyone who follows.

Then come the living. Part III takes up Tyrangiel’s duel, Musk against Bezos, and scores both men. Part IV is Mark Zuckerberg, the attention merchant. Part V is Peter Thiel, the kingmaker, who owns no newspaper and runs no platform but has done more than any other billionaire to choose who governs. Part VI is Larry Ellison, the quiet consolidator, whose family now holds a remarkable share of what Americans watch and the code behind what their teenagers scroll. Part VII is Rupert Murdoch, and the question of what happens to a megaphone when it is left to the children. Part VIII is the Koch network, the case for oligarchy without a personality cult.

The back half tests the rubric’s edges. Part IX is Bill Gates, and the problem of the benevolent oligarch whose foundation steers world health without facing a single voter. Part X is Soros, the man much of the right treats as the archetype, measured rather than mythologized. Part XI is the new donor class of 2024 to 2026, the venture and crypto money that skipped the business of buying politicians and moved into the government itself. Part XII comes home, to Canada’s quiet dynasties: the Irvings, the Westons, the Thomsons and, with a declared interest, the Rogers family. Part XIII breaks the pattern of portraits to follow a live experiment, California’s Proposition 40, which asked voters on November 3, 2026 to tax the state’s billionaires while those billionaires paid for the campaign against it. They said no. Part XIV, written after the midterms, asks what Silicon Valley’s turn to Trump actually bought.

The Concluding Essay borrows Tyrangiel’s headline, Choose Your Overlord, and puts every score in one table. It also looks at what has worked before and what might work now, from antitrust to campaign-finance reform, along with one idea from Tyrangiel’s reporting that is so simple it made me laugh: if two rival billionaires are both indispensable, the state’s job may be to make them work together.

The question underneath

Late in his piece, Tyrangiel makes the point that the moon is leverage, and that a government bargaining with men who want something badly enough still holds a card. That’s true as far as it goes. But it assumes the state is still the party doing the bargaining.

The running question of this series is the one his essay leaves open. When private power grows large enough to rival the power of a state, who checks it? History offers three answers. A stronger democracy can do it, with courts, a free press, competitive elections and the patience to pass laws that outlive a news cycle; America managed that once, between about 1890 and 1913. A stronger tsar can do it, which is what Russia got after 2000. Or nobody does it, and the overlords sort things out among themselves, which is roughly where Tyrangiel leaves the American space programme.

I would like to know which of those three we are heading towards, and the only honest way I know to find out is to measure. So let’s start where the word started, in Moscow, in the winter of 1995, at an auction where the bank running the sale also happened to be the bank that won it.
