By Stephen Gordon | CanadianSnowmanSays

I want to tell you what the polling numbers look like, because they tell the story of this series better than I can.

In February, when Jack Schlossberg entered the race for New York’s Twelfth Congressional District, a poll commissioned by the George Conway campaign found him leading the field at 25 percent. Conway sat second at 16. Micah Lasher and Alex Bores were tied for last at 11 percent each. The poll measured name recognition more than anything else, and Schlossberg had more of it than anyone.

By mid-May — after the New York Times published its detailed examination of a campaign plagued by staff turnover, strategic chaos, and a candidate who took a nap on his launch day — a new Emerson College poll found Lasher leading at 22 percent, Bores at 20, Schlossberg collapsed to 11, and Conway at 10.

From 25 percent to 11 percent in three months.

And then, on May 28th, the New York Times Opinion section published a column by Michelle Cottle under the headline: He’s Entitled and Nepotistic. This Is Not What Democrats Need.

The newspaper had spoken twice. Once in news. Once in opinion. The message, stripped of editorial decorum, was the same both times: look more carefully at what you are being asked to vote for.

I have been watching this race from Toronto since November. I started this series because a Kennedy was running for Congress and the dynasty question seemed worth examining. Five essays later, the dynasty question has answered itself.

What Cottle Said

Michelle Cottle’s column is worth reading in full. It is not a hit piece. It is a reckoning — with Schlossberg, yes, but more precisely with the Democratic Party’s decades-long habit of treating the Kennedy name as a substitute for vetting.

She traces the arc with unflinching economy: Teddy Kennedy, whose four decades in the Senate made him one of the most effective legislators of the modern era, alongside the “unbridled appetites” that foreclosed higher office. Joe Kennedy II and his annulment scandal. Michael Kennedy, dead on a ski slope in 1997. And at the terminus of the bloodline, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — a public-health catastrophe now running the Department of Health and Human Services, steering the country toward more measles and less cancer research.

“Every royal family has its scoundrels and scandals,” Cottle writes. Her point is not that the Kennedys are uniquely fallen. Her point is that the golden aura of the brand has repeatedly taken the place of scrutiny — and that the Democratic Party, which lectures endlessly about equity and dismantling systems of privilege, keeps coronating candidates because their last name opens doors that talent alone cannot.

Jack Schlossberg, she argues, is not the real problem. The enduring allure of Camelot is.

This is a harder argument than the Fandos news piece made two weeks earlier, and a more important one. Campaign chaos is correctable. A candidate can hire a better manager, tighten a schedule, stop copying other congressmen’s Instagram posts. But the structural failure Cottle identifies — a party that mistakes dynastic glamour for democratic fitness — is not correctable by a better campaign manager. It is correctable only by voters, on June 23rd, pulling a lever for someone else.

The Numbers Do Their Work

Let me be precise about what the polling collapse does and does not tell us.

It does not tell us that Schlossberg is finished. Three weeks before a primary in which 32 percent of likely voters remain undecided — a number larger than any candidate’s current vote share — finished is not a word that applies to anyone. The undecided pool, as Tavern Research’s crosstab analysis noted, skews younger and less white: 38 percent of 18-to-34-year-olds are undecided, as are 42 percent of Black voters. Whoever moves that bloc will probably win. Right now, nobody has.

What the polling collapse does tell us is something about the nature of Schlossberg’s support. A lead built entirely on name recognition is not a lead — it is a placeholder. It holds until voters start paying attention, and then it either converts into genuine preference or it evaporates. In the Twelfth District, one of the most politically engaged electorates in the country, voters pay attention early and thoroughly. They attend forums. They read endorsements closely. They consume the New York Times the way other cities consume box scores.

When the Times told them to look more carefully, they looked. The numbers moved.

The race that political insiders had described from the beginning — a West Side versus East Side contest between two Assembly members, with the celebrity candidates providing colour commentary — has now materially arrived. Lasher and Bores are the story. Schlossberg and Conway are the context.

Polymarket, which aggregates prediction market odds, now describes the race as having narrowed to a contest between Lasher and Bores. Schlossberg is drawing, in their framing, “limited traction despite name recognition.” That is a diplomatic way of saying: the trick didn’t hold.

The Sentimental Canadian

John and Robert Kennedy, outside the Capitol. The image the dynasty has been living off ever since. Corbis, via Getty Images

I was thirteen years old when a president was shot in Dallas.

I watched it from Toronto, which meant I watched it from a particular vantage point — close enough to feel the shock, far enough to observe rather than participate in what came after. What came after, among other things, was Camelot: the word, the mythology, the decades-long American project of treating a truncated presidency as an eternal promise.

I understand that project. I have never been immune to it. There is something genuinely moving in the image of a three-year-old boy saluting his father’s coffin — an image that still carries weight sixty years on, not because of what it means politically, but because of what it means humanly. A child. A loss. A country watching.

That image, and the weight it carries, is not a reason to send Jack Schlossberg to Congress.

I started this series because I wanted to examine the dynasty question seriously — not to dismiss it, but to trace what it actually means when a Kennedy runs for office in 2026, fifty years after the era that made the name luminous. What I found, across five essays, is that the dynasty question is less interesting than the questions it obscures.

Micah Lasher, a child magician who fired Joel Stein at twelve and spent thirty years learning how to operate the machinery of New York government, is not a glamorous candidate. He is something rarer: a prepared one.

Alex Bores, a former Palantir data scientist who has become the candidate that the OpenAI-funded super PAC considers threatening enough to spend $2.4 million defeating, is running in the district’s East Side institutional lane with sufficient conviction to have attracted both the most hostile outside money and the most interesting policy scrutiny in the race.

George Conway, who gave this series its sharpest early material and has now faded to single digits in some polls, was never really running for Congress. He was running for something harder to define — absolution, perhaps, or relevance, or simply a platform from which to be George Conway at a moment when being George Conway felt like a political act.

And Jack Schlossberg, the grandson of a president, is discovering that a famous name is not the same as a mandate.

The Pelosi Problem

Nancy Pelosi endorsed Jack Schlossberg in February. She called him a leader who gives people something to believe in again. She noted, in the same breath, that she had served alongside his uncle Ted, his cousins Patrick and Joe, and Joseph P. Kennedy III.

That is not an endorsement of qualifications. That is an endorsement of continuity — a signal from the party’s most formidable institutional operator that the old guard intends to manage its own succession on its own terms.

The Twelfth District has responded, if the polls are any guide, with polite indifference. Local analysts had noted from the beginning that endorsements in this district carry weight primarily when they bring organizing infrastructure — volunteers, club relationships, neighbourhood networks. What Pelosi brought was a television ad and a famous face. In a district full of people who already have strong feelings about Nancy Pelosi and equally strong feelings about Kennedy nostalgia, the multiplication of those two brands produced less than the sum of its parts.

Schlossberg’s campaign manager, pushing back on the Times piece, cited Pelosi’s endorsement as evidence of seriousness. But seriousness, in the Twelfth District, is demonstrated at candidate forums, in policy depth, and in the unsexy work of building local relationships — not in the acquisition of national names.

Pelosi understood Congress. She may not have understood this district’s immunity to the kind of credential she represents.

What June 23rd Is Actually Deciding

There are three weeks left. The undecided voters are younger, less white, and have not yet been moved by Lasher’s Bloomberg-funded mailer saturation, Bores’s East Side infrastructure, Schlossberg’s social media presence, or Conway’s decades of anti-Trump credibility.

Whoever reaches them first, in the right register, will likely win.

Lasher’s campaign argues, with some evidence, that he is gaining momentum — that the institutional endorsements are starting to translate into name recognition, and that the Bloomberg money flooding the district in mailers and ads is doing the slow work of converting a policy credential into a voting preference. His campaign manager told Newsweek last week that voters want someone “actually prepared to fight to fix our broken government.” That is the closing argument: competence as resistance.

Bores is making a different closing argument — that the East Side candidate with the fundraising lead and the AI-industry-opposition-spending distinction is the one who represents the district’s future rather than its past. In a race where the past is running in the form of a Kennedy grandson and a nostalgia-soaked endorsement structure, that distinction may carry more weight than it seems.

What this race is deciding, underneath the personalities and the polling fluctuations, is something that matters beyond Manhattan: whether the Democratic Party is capable of choosing competence over charisma, preparation over performance, and institutional fitness over the comfortable warm feeling of a familiar name.

The Twelfth District is the most educated congressional district in the country. It is, on paper, the electorate best positioned to make that choice clearly.

Whether it does is what Part VI of this series will cover — after the votes are counted on June 23rd.

A Note From Toronto

I have written five essays about a congressional primary I cannot vote in, in a country I watch from across a border that has never felt simultaneously closer and more consequential than it does in 2026.

I started because of the dynasty question. I stayed because of the deeper question underneath it: what does a healthy democracy ask of the people it sends to do its work?

The Twelfth District’s answer, in three weeks, will be instructive — not just for Manhattan, but for the Democratic Party trying to figure out what it is, and for the broader democratic project of deciding whether the skills that win primaries are the same as the skills that govern.

Let me close with Michelle Cottle’s instruction to her own country, borrowed here with full attribution and no apology:

For the love of God and the sake of our democracy, let us stop treating Mr. Schlossberg and his extended family as inherently worthy of important political jobs that affect the lives of real people.

She is right. The flowers, as the campaign staffer said two weeks ago, need roots.

On June 23rd, the Twelfth District votes.
