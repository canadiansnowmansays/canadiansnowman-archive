You have now read, across three series, the full arc of how China became the country it is. The treasure fleets that demonstrated centrality to a world that already knew it. The unequal treaties that dismantled it. The revolution that ended a century of humiliation and installed, in the process, a system capable of its own catastrophes. The pivot under Deng that built the economic engine. The patient decades of industrial construction. Xi’s consolidation into the man who published 141 pages of strategic intention. The disputed island that sits at the intersection of all of it. That is the architecture — the ambition, the history, the territory. What the architecture does not show is what it costs at the scale of a single person, living alone in a studio apartment in a city of twenty million, pressing a button every forty-eight hours to prove they are still alive. That is what this essay is about.

Earlier this year, one of the most popular paid apps in China was called Are You Dead?

It was not a game. It was not a wellness platform. It was a dead-man’s switch for the lonely — a service that required users to press a green button every forty-eight hours to confirm they were still alive. If they failed to check in, the app automatically alerted a designated contact. In January 2026, it became the top paid iPhone download in China. Millions of young people, living alone in cities where they knew almost no one, found the concept sufficiently useful to pay for it.

Then it vanished. Apple removed it from its Chinese store on orders from the country’s cyberspace watchdog. The authorities gave no detailed explanation, because none was required. The message was self-evident: an app predicated on mass urban isolation is not the image the Communist Party wishes to project. The implicit narrative of the past three decades — that China’s industrialization has delivered its people into a better life — does not accommodate the spectacle of young professionals in Beijing and Shanghai pressing a button every two days to prove they have not died alone in their apartments.

I have been to China several times. The scale impresses every time. The bullet trains, the skylines, the density of ambition — extraordinary. What visitors are less likely to see is the toll the machine extracts from the people inside it. The Are You Dead? story is the toll in its purest form: a green button that told the truth, and a government that needed the truth to disappear.

The bargain has always been explicit, even if unwritten.

You know Deng Xiaoping’s deal from Part VII of The Long Return — prosperity in exchange for politics, the pragmatic cat catching the mouse, the thirty years of industrial construction that followed. What the bargain did not advertise was the social cost embedded in its terms.

Over the past two decades, China’s urban population has swelled by roughly 400 million people. That is not a statistic easily absorbed — it represents the largest internal migration in human history, compressing multiple generations of urbanization into a single lifetime. Farmers’ children became factory workers. Factory workers’ children became the young finance professionals and software engineers and advertising executives now sitting alone in studio apartments in Shenzhen and Hangzhou, scrolling through their phones on a Friday evening, wondering how they got here.

China was never, in its essentials, an individualist society. It was a society built on the compound — the danwei, the village, the extended family network that provided identity, support, and belonging across generations. The danwei, the work unit that organized Chinese urban life under Mao, was many things, most of them repressive — but it was also a community. It assigned housing and rationed food and monitored ideology, yes, but it also knew your name, tracked your children’s schooling, and buried your parents. It was a cage, but it was inhabited. The market reforms that succeeded it offered something the danwei never had — freedom of movement, freedom of aspiration — and stripped away something it quietly provided: the architecture of belonging.

That structure was not gently dissolved by modernization. It was detonated. The migrants who flooded into the cities arrived not as communities but as individuals, stripped of the social scaffolding their parents and grandparents had inhabited. They found apartments, not villages. They found colleagues, not kin. They found cities of extraordinary vitality and scale in which they knew, with some precision, exactly nobody.

The party got the labour force it needed. The workers got the wages. Nobody budgeted for what would be missing.

The human evidence is not subtle

A 29-year-old woman from Shanxi province relocates to Beijing for an advertising job and spends years unable to make genuine friends, her days consumed by ten-hour shifts, her social life reduced to posting on RedNote in search of strangers willing to take a walk in a park. She eventually pays to attend something called a “blind-box dinner” — a fee-based gathering of six strangers who split the bill after a meal and, if luck holds, do not part as strangers. The entrepreneur who organizes these evenings now runs them regularly in Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou. The demand is steady.

A warehouse manager in Shanghai earns roughly a thousand dollars a month, sends a portion home to family, cannot afford to go out, and spends his evenings on his phone — not because he prefers it, but because real relationships, as he puts it, require a level of depth that takes more energy than he has left to spend. He is not without company; he has colleagues, he has acquaintances online. What he does not have is the kind of relationship in which you can say something true about yourself and have it received without judgment. He admits to bouts of loneliness with the slightly embarrassed air of a man confessing to something he considers a personal failure rather than a structural outcome.

A video game developer in Hangzhou — married, not alone — still weeps regularly from loneliness, because his entire social identity rests on his professional status, and with AI eliminating programming jobs at accelerating speed, that identity feels provisional. Being a programmer at a big firm used to confer a certain prestige, a social legibility. Now the ground is shifting. He has retreated from friendships preemptively, cutting ties before the ties can register his diminishment. He has not told his wife. The loneliness is so layered — shame inside fear inside isolation — that he cannot speak it even to the one person in his life whose job description includes receiving it.

A 2023 survey by the Chinese networking app Soul found that nearly sixty percent of young respondents reported having no more than two close friends. In a country of 1.4 billion people, a generation has come of age in which genuine intimacy is a minority experience. This is not a Chinese peculiarity — loneliness has been rising across the developed world for decades, and Western cities have their own versions of the blind-box dinner and the paid companion. But the gap between China’s traditional social architecture and its current urban reality is steeper than almost anywhere else, and the speed of the transition has given people no time to adapt. Anthropologists get to study these transformations in historical retrospect. The people living through them get the rent bill and a ten-hour shift.

The social adaptations are instructive in themselves

The dazi has emerged as a cultural institution — a companion for specific activities, be it the gym or video games or a trip to a museum, who carries no expectation of evolving into an actual friend. The relationship is deliberately kept shallow, because depth costs emotional resources that nobody can spare. It is not a friendship; it is a friendship’s skeleton, preserved for use without the inconvenience of the flesh. Young people post requests for a dazi on social platforms with the same practical tone one might use to find a flatmate: available Saturdays, interested in hiking, no romantic expectations.

Paid cosplay companions, hired for an hour or two of simulated conversation and role-play by women who find real men too disappointing and too complicated, represent a market, not a pathology — which is to say, the market emerged because the demand was real and the alternatives had failed. The women who hire these companions are not delusional; they are pragmatic. They know the companion is performing. They prefer the performance to the negotiation that actual relationships require. This is not a comment on their character. It is a comment on the cost-benefit analysis that urban Chinese life has imposed on intimacy.

The economics of romance are genuinely punishing. The expectation in many families — still operative, still exerting pressure on the marriage market in major cities — is that a man must own a home and a car before he is considered a viable husband. In a market where property prices in Beijing and Shanghai have remained ruinous despite recent softening, this expectation disqualifies enormous numbers of men before the conversation begins. It has also produced a dating app landscape of extraordinary peculiarity, in which platforms like Taqu, with 200 million registered users, must mediate between women who have the leverage to be selective and men who have the numbers but not the assets. The result is a market that functions poorly for almost everyone and enriches the platforms handsomely.

The demographic distortion underneath this runs deeper still. China’s one-child policy, enforced for thirty-five years, skewed the population toward boys — families under pressure to produce a male heir were quicker to abort girls. The resulting gender imbalance has left millions of lower-income or lower-status men effectively priced out of marriage by arithmetic alone. They are not lonely by choice or by temperament. They are lonely by policy.

Marriage has been quietly collapsing under the weight of all this

In 2010, 22 million Chinese citizens got married for the first time. In 2024, that number was 9.2 million — less than half, in fourteen years, with no sign of a floor. Some of this is economic: the home-and-car expectation, the urban cost of living, the starting salaries that look reasonable until they are divided by rent and remittances home. Some of it is the demographic distortion of the one-child era. But beneath the economics and the demographics is something harder to quantify: a widespread, low-grade pessimism about the future that makes long-term commitment feel reckless.

You do not make a thirty-year decision when you cannot see three years out. The economy is deflating. The job market is contracting. AI is arriving faster than anyone anticipated, and it is arriving in the sectors — software, finance, design, translation — where the educated urban young had expected to find their footing. The programmer who used to represent upward mobility is watching his social identity become unstable in real time. The finance analyst who spent four years in university acquiring credentials is reading about the deals her firm is closing with AI tools that don’t need credentials. The future that was supposed to justify the sacrifice of leaving home, working ten-hour days, and pressing a green button every forty-eight hours is no longer obviously there.

When the future looks like this, the marriage rate is a lagging indicator. The loneliness is the leading one.

The Communist Party is aware of all of this

The suppression of Are You Dead? was not an overreaction by a clumsy bureaucracy. It was a precise intervention. The party monitors public mood with sophisticated attention, and what the app’s popularity revealed was not a technical violation of cyberspace regulations — it was an indictment. An app designed around the fear of dying alone does not exist in a thriving society. Its popularity constituted evidence, and the party has always understood that evidence it cannot refute is best deleted.

The party’s response to its demographic and social crisis has been largely exhortatory. Young people are encouraged to marry, to reproduce, to be optimistic about a future the data does not support. Cash incentives for children have been introduced, parental leave extended, pro-natalist campaigns launched with the mild desperation of a government that knows the arithmetic and cannot change it by wishing. The total fertility rate in China was around 1.0 in 2023 — well below the replacement level of 2.1, and among the lowest in the world. A society that has atomized its young adults, loaded them with economic anxiety, and told them to be fruitful is not surprised by a 1.0 fertility rate. It is, in a certain light, the logical outcome of everything that preceded it.

The deeper problem is not addressable by policy, because the deeper problem is the bargain itself. The party industrialized a civilization by atomizing it, and the young professionals sitting alone in their apartments are the residue of that process — not a malfunction of the system, but its product. The loneliness is not a side effect. It is what efficient urbanization looks like at human scale.

What is new is the doubt. Previous generations accepted loneliness as the price of the opportunity the city offered — the apartment was better than the village, the salary was better than the field, the future was worth the isolation because the future was credible. The current generation is beginning to ask whether the opportunity was worth the price when the opportunity itself is starting to look uncertain. That question, once asked openly, has implications the party finds genuinely threatening. A citizenry that has surrendered its rights in exchange for prosperity begins to evaluate the exchange when the prosperity wobbles. The math changes. Hence the green button’s disappearance.

A Canadian note on all of this

I write about China from outside — fascinated, clear-eyed, unwilling to be either a hawk or a naïf. I have watched this country’s rise with the attention it deserves, which is considerable. But the Are You Dead? story illuminates something that strategic analysis tends to pass over: the human interior of a system that runs very well at the aggregate level and produces significant suffering at the individual one.

The 15th Five-Year Plan — the 141 pages I wrote about in The Quiet War — projects a China of technological dominance, AI supremacy, rare earth control, and industrial triumph. It is a document of extraordinary ambition, and the ambition may well be realized. China has earned the right to be taken seriously on that score. The rare earth chokehold is real. The robotics investment is staggering. The quantum networks are being built. I do not dismiss any of it.

But the plan says nothing about the young woman in Beijing who is posting on RedNote looking for someone to walk with her in a park. It says nothing about the developer in Hangzhou who cries alone while his wife sleeps three feet away. It says nothing about the 9.2 million people who decided last year, once again, that the risk of a shared future was greater than they could manage. The plan is silent on what has been lost in the building of everything it proposes to build next.

The 141 pages describe what China intends to construct. The green button described what the construction has cost.

The party deleted one of those documents. The other, it published with pride.

That asymmetry is, I think, worth sitting with. Not as an indictment — all industrializations have extracted their social costs, and the West has its own epidemic of loneliness to account for. But as a question: what is the bargain actually worth, at the level of a single life, in a studio apartment in Beijing, with nobody to call?

The green button knew the answer. That is why it had to go.

We began this section with Zheng He’s treasure fleets sailing out from Nanjing in 1421 — the most powerful demonstration of centrality the world had seen, dispatched by a civilization that already knew it was the centre and wanted everyone else to remember. We passed through the century that dismantled that centrality, the revolution that ended the humiliation, the catastrophes that followed, the pivot that rebuilt the economy, the consolidation that aimed it. We arrived at the 141 pages. And we end here: not with a satellite or a robot army or a quantum network, but with a green button on a phone screen, pressed by someone alone in a city of millions, confirming they are still alive. That is the full arc. The Long Return is real. So is what it costs.
