Every few years someone in Washington declares that a political movement is finished, and every few years the movement takes the declaration as a personal challenge. Donald Trump has been pronounced politically dead after the Access Hollywood tape in October 2016, after the Democratic House wave of November 2018, after his defeat in November 2020, after the Capitol riot of 6 January 2021, and after the Republicans’ underwhelming midterms of November 2022. In January 2025 he was sworn in for a second term. A Canadian learns to be careful with obituaries, much as a farmer in Mariposa learned to be careful with the first warm day in March.

So a word about the title before anything else.

“The Rise and Fall of…” is not a neutral phrase. It belongs to Edward Gibbon and his Roman Empire, and to William Shirer and his Third Reich. Anyone who has read this publication for long will assume the echo is deliberate, and it is, in the narrow sense that both books are about something larger than the man at the top. Gibbon was not writing about any one emperor. Shirer was writing about how a society came to hand itself over. The echo is not a claim that 2026 is 1933. It is a claim that the subject of this series is the movement, not the man.

That distinction is the spine of everything that follows.

The man and the -ism

Donald Trump is a person: eighty years old as this is written, with a temperament, a grievance list, a family business and a mortality like everyone else’s. Trumpism is something different. It is a set of instincts that existed before him: suspicion of trade, hostility to immigration, contempt for the press and the courts, a belief that the country has been stolen by people who despise the ones who built it, and a conviction that politics is war and the only sin is losing. Since 2016 those instincts have been given a theory, a network of think tanks, a governing programme and an heir apparent.

Stephanie Slade, author of Fusionism: Liberty, Virtue and the Future of the American Right, made the distinction sharply in an exchange with John Guida that the New York Times published on 5 October 2026 and the Salt Lake Tribune republished two days later under the headline “Disappointed by Trump? There Is a Better Way to Be a Republican.” Slade describes what she calls “will-to-power politics,” in which politics becomes “purely a war to determine which side will get to wield power to reward its friends and punish its enemies,” and in which “hatred toward the opposition is treated as a virtue.”

She sets it against the older conservative settlement known as fusionism, “the idea that both liberty and virtue are necessary for human flourishing,” which held the American right together from the 1960s until roughly a decade ago. She also notes that the national conservatives “bet all their chips on Trump and Trumpism as the future of the conservative movement,” a bet she says “looks at the very least more questionable now.” And she names Vice-President JD Vance as “an open proponent of anti-fusionist, Dissident Right ideas.”

Put plainly, the man may be losing altitude while the machine he rode keeps its engines running. Whether those engines can fly without him is the question this series is built to answer.

Why now, and why after the vote

This series was drafted in October 2026, in the weeks before the 3 November midterm elections, and is being published after them. That order is deliberate.

The first four parts are history and record: where Trumpism came from, how it captured a party, how it acquired an intellectual wardrobe, and what it did with power. None of that depends on who won any particular House seat. The later parts depend on the result, and the result is not something an essayist in Toronto gets to predict in print and then quietly delete.

The month before the vote carried its own warning. On 15 October 2026 Zolan Kanno-Youngs reported in the New York Times, under the headline “Trump’s Attacks on Voting Raise Fears He Will Try to Challenge Midterm Results,” that election officials worried the administration “could use its own unsubstantiated claims to contest the vote in November.” Federal agents were combing state rolls for noncitizen voters. The Justice Department planned to send 1,000 monitors into all 50 states. The president had recently met at the White House with Tina Peters, the former Colorado county clerk convicted of tampering with voting machines after 2020. When the conservative host Wayne Allyn Root told him in August, “You have the right to declare a national security emergency for elections,” he replied, “Let me just say that stranger things have happened. I’ll leave it at that.” Asked earlier in October whether he would accept the result, he said, “Of course I will. But we don’t want cheating.”

Readers of The Grievance Machine will recognise the pattern. In 2020 the stolen-election story was written after the votes were counted. In 2026 it was being drafted beforehand. A movement that rehearses its excuses before the game is played is telling you something about how it expects the game to go, and about what it intends to do if it goes badly.

What “fall” would mean

A midterm defeat is not a fall. Trump lost the House in 2018, lost the presidency in 2020, and came back with the popular vote in 2024. A movement falls when its ideas stop winning arguments inside its own coalition, when its institutions stop attracting talent and money, and when its would-be successors stop wanting the brand. Those are the tests this series will apply, and they are harder to pass than an election night.

It is also possible that the movement does not fall at all, but sheds its founder and carries on under new management. That outcome would make the title wrong in an interesting way. It would also be the most consequential result of the lot, for Americans and for the neighbours who share a continent and a trade relationship with them.

The shape of the series

The series runs in seven parts after this Introduction:

Part I, The Soil: the quarter-century of grievance politics, from Pat Buchanan’s 1992 “culture war” speech to the Tea Party, that prepared the ground long before Trump came down the escalator.

Part II, The Capture: how a reality-television host with no party loyalty took over the Republican Party between June 2015 and July 2016, and why its establishment folded.

Part III, The Intellectuals Arrive: how National Conservatism, the post-liberal right and the Heritage Foundation turned an instinct into a theory, and a theory into a governing manual.

Part IV, Power in Practice: what Trumpism did with real power in the second term, measured against what it promised.

Part V, The Cracks: the defections, the scandals and the cost-of-living disappointment that set in before the vote.

Part VI, The Verdict: what the 3 November 2026 results actually said.

Part VII, Trumpism Without Trump: whether the movement survives its founder, and who would inherit it.

This series sits alongside others on this site. Why They Stay asked why the base has remained loyal. Loyal Opposition audited whether the Democrats are functioning as an opposition at all. The Pendulum and the Ratchet tracked what can and cannot be undone. The Uncomfortable Truth applied the historical checklist for fascism. This series is meant as the synthesis those pieces feed into: not another indictment, but a history with an open last chapter.

Historians usually get the luxury of knowing how the story ends before they write the first chapter. Essayists rarely do. This time, for once, we get to read the election results before we publish the verdict, and we get to start where any honest history of a movement starts: in the ground it grew from.
