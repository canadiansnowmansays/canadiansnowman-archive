Every argument about Social Security in 2026 is, at bottom, an argument about a decision made in 1935. Before we can talk sensibly about the cliff in 2032, or the rescue of 1983, or the bill this series will end with, we have to understand what Franklin Roosevelt built and why he built it the way he did.

The short version is this. Roosevelt did not set out to create a welfare programme for the old. He set out to create something that would feel, to the people paying into it, like property. He succeeded so completely that ninety years later, the programme is almost impossible to cut and almost impossible to fund. Both of those facts come from the same design.

A Country With No Floor

It is hard now to picture how exposed old age was in the early 1930s. Most Americans had no private pension. Savings had vanished in bank failures. Adult children who might once have supported their parents were themselves out of work. According to the Social Security Administration‘s own history of the programme, by 1934 more than half of the elderly lacked enough income to support themselves. Some states had old-age pension schemes, but they were thin and patchy, and only about 3 per cent of the elderly were receiving benefits under them.

Into that gap stepped two men with very large ideas.

The first was Dr. Francis Townsend, a retired physician in California, who proposed that every citizen aged 60 and over receive a pension of $200 a month, paid for by a 2 per cent national sales tax, on condition that the money be spent within the month. Economists found the arithmetic fantastical. Older Americans found it irresistible. Within about two years, the SSA history records, there were some 7,000 Townsend Clubs with more than 2.2 million members.

The second was Huey Long, the former governor of Louisiana and by then a United States senator, whose “Share Our Wealth” movement promised to guarantee every family an annual income of $5,000 by breaking up great fortunes. By 1935, by the SSA’s count, it claimed 27,000 local clubs and 7.7 million members.

Neither plan became law. Both mattered enormously. They told Roosevelt, in the plainest political language there is, that if he did not offer the old and the anxious something workable, someone else would offer them something unworkable. Social Security was, among other things, a reasonable answer designed to crowd out the unreasonable ones.

Perkins and the Committee

The work fell to Frances Perkins, the Secretary of Labor and the first woman to sit in an American cabinet. In June 1934 Roosevelt created the Committee on Economic Security, made up of five cabinet-level members, and Perkins chaired it. Its executive director was Edwin Witte, an economist from the University of Wisconsin, a state that had been experimenting with social insurance for years.

The committee was asked to report by December 1934 and delivered its recommendations in early January 1935, a few weeks late, which by the standards of any committee I have ever encountered counts as punctual. Roosevelt sent the report to Congress on January 17, 1935. The bill that emerged passed both houses by large margins, and Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act on August 14, 1935.

The Act did several things, including unemployment insurance and aid to the states for needy old people, children and the blind. But the piece that became what Americans now simply call “Social Security” was the federal old-age insurance programme. Workers and employers would each pay a tax of 1 per cent on the first $3,000 of a worker’s annual wages, beginning in 1937. In return, the worker would receive a monthly benefit at 65, related to what he or she had earned and paid in.

That last clause is the heart of the matter.

Why Roosevelt Wanted a Payroll Tax

Roosevelt could have paid for old-age pensions out of general revenue, as many reformers wanted, and as Townsend effectively proposed. Paying benefits from the general treasury to everyone over a certain age would have reached more people faster. It would also have looked, to many Americans of the time, like relief, and relief carried a stigma that is hard to overstate.

Roosevelt insisted on contributions, and he was candid about why. The best evidence comes from a memorandum written in the summer of 1941 by Luther Gulick, a public administration expert, recording a conversation in which Gulick suggested that the payroll taxes had been a mistake in economic terms. The memo was identified as the source of a famous remark by the SSA Historian’s Office in a research note posted on July 21, 2005; Arthur Schlesinger Jr. had first published the line in 1958 without clear sourcing.

According to Gulick, Roosevelt conceded the economics. The taxes, he said, were political, through and through. Because workers paid in, they would have a legal, moral and political right to collect their benefits. And then the line that has been repeated ever since: with those taxes in place, “no damn politician can ever scrap my social security program.”

He was right, though perhaps not in the way he meant. As a strict matter of law, Congress kept the power to change the programme. Section 1104 of the 1935 Act expressly reserved to Congress the right to alter, amend or repeal any of it, and in Flemming v. Nestor in 1960 the Supreme Court confirmed that workers do not hold an accrued property right to their benefits in the way they would hold a private annuity. The legal right Roosevelt spoke of was weaker than he suggested.

The moral and political right, however, turned out to be stronger than anyone could have imagined. Americans do not think of their Social Security cheques as a gift from Congress. They think of them as their own money coming back. Courts may disagree. Voters do not.

Who Was Left Out

A fair account of 1935 has to include who was not part of the bargain.

The original Act excluded agricultural labourers and domestic workers, along with several other groups, including government employees and the self-employed. The scale of this was large. In a 2010 article in the Social Security Bulletin, Larry DeWitt, then a public historian at the SSA, estimated that about half of the American workforce was outside the original programme. The farm and domestic exclusions fell unevenly by race: about 65 per cent of African American workers were excluded through those two categories, compared with about 27 per cent of white workers.

Why this happened is a genuine historical dispute, and I want to set out both sides honestly. A substantial body of scholarship, including Mary Poole’s The Segregated Origins of Social Security (2006) and Robert Lieberman’s Shifting the Color Line (1998), argues that the exclusions reflected the demands of Southern Democrats who controlled key congressional committees and wanted to preserve a low-wage, largely Black agricultural and domestic labour force outside federal reach.

DeWitt’s article argues the opposite case. He points out that the Treasury, through Secretary Henry Morgenthau Jr., told the House Ways and Means Committee on February 5, 1935, that collecting payroll taxes from farmhands and household servants was administratively unworkable, and urged that they be dropped. He notes that most excluded workers, about 74 per cent, were white, and argues the exclusions were meant as a delay in coverage rather than a permanent bar. His conclusion is that the evidence does not support racial motive as the cause.

I am not in a position to settle a debate among professional historians, and I will not pretend to. What can be said without dispute is the consequence. Whatever the intent, the design of 1935 meant that for its first fifteen years, the programme largely passed by the people working on farms and in other people’s homes, and a disproportionate share of those people were Black. Farm and domestic workers who were regularly employed began to be brought in by the 1950 amendments, with broader coverage following in 1954. An earned-insurance programme rewards those whose work is counted, and for a long time some work was not counted.

Ida May Fuller and the Growing Promise

Taxes began in 1937. The first monthly benefits were scheduled for 1942, but the 1939 amendments brought them forward to 1940. On January 31, 1940, cheque number 00-000-001, for $22.54, went to Ida May Fuller, a retired legal secretary from Ludlow, Vermont.

Her story is the programme’s favourite piece of folklore, and the arithmetic explains why. According to the SSA’s own history pages, Fuller had worked under Social Security for a little under three years and paid a total of $24.75 in payroll taxes. She lived to 100, dying in 1975, and collected $22,888.92 in benefits.

Ida May Fuller is a useful reminder that Social Security was never a savings account. Her own contributions covered roughly her first month. Everything after that was paid by the workers coming behind her. That is what “pay-as-you-go” means, and it is why the ratio of workers to retirees matters so much in 2026. But to Fuller and to millions after her, the cheque still felt earned, because in a real sense it was: she had paid what the system asked of her.

The 1939 amendments did something even more consequential than moving up the start date. They added benefits for the spouses and minor children of retired workers, and for the survivors of workers who died. In the SSA’s own words, this turned the programme from a retirement plan for workers into a family-based economic security programme.

From there the promise kept widening. Truman signed the 1950 amendments on August 28, 1950. They brought roughly 10 million more people into coverage, including most of the non-farm self-employed and regularly employed farm and domestic workers, and raised average benefits for current retirees by about 77 per cent. Fuller’s own monthly cheque rose from $22.54 to $41.30.

In 1956, Congress added disability benefits for workers aged 50 to 64, building on a 1954 “disability freeze” that had protected the records of workers who could no longer earn.

Then, on July 1, 1972, Richard Nixon signed Public Law 92-336, a bill whose main purpose was to extend the federal debt limit. Attached to it was a 20 per cent across-the-board increase in benefits and, more lastingly, an automatic annual cost-of-living adjustment tied to consumer prices, with the first automatic increase taking effect at the start of 1975. Since then, benefits have risen with inflation without anyone in Congress having to vote for each raise.

The VanDerhei households in Tara Siegel Bernard’s New York Times piece of October 12, 2026, depend on every one of these layers. The article notes that the programme is more than an inflation-adjusted pension; it also provides disability, survivor and family benefits. Each of those was added after 1935, and each was added on the same earned-insurance logic.

Why the Design Cuts Both Ways

Here is where the 1935 bargain stops being history and becomes the problem this series is about.

The earned-insurance design is the reason Social Security has survived ninety years of political weather. Programmes for the poor are always vulnerable, because the people who pay for them are not the people who receive them, and that gap can be exploited by any politician who wants to. Social Security has no such gap. Nearly everyone pays; nearly everyone expects to collect. Roosevelt’s taxes created a constituency of almost the whole country, and that constituency has defended the programme ever since.

But the same design makes repair extraordinarily difficult, for three reasons.

First, benefits feel owned. If you believe the money is yours, any reduction is not a policy adjustment. It is a confiscation. A politician who proposes trimming the benefit formula is not, in the voter’s mind, adjusting a government programme. He is reaching into the voter’s pocket.

Second, and for the same reason, any cut feels like theft from people who kept their side of the deal. Someone who paid payroll tax on every cheque for forty-five years has done exactly what the system asked. Telling that person the promise will be smaller than advertised feels like a breach of contract, and Flemming v. Nestor notwithstanding, most people will call it one.

Third, any tax increase feels like a premium hike. Workers are not told they are contributing to a national budget; they are told they are paying for their own insurance. Raise the rate and you are charging more for the same policy. Raise the cap on taxable earnings and you are charging higher earners more for a benefit that, under the formula, does not rise in proportion.

I spent enough years on the receiving end of escalated complaints to recognise this shape. People will accept a great deal when they feel they are being treated fairly under the terms they were given. What they will not accept quietly is the sense that the terms changed after they had paid. The design that makes Social Security beloved is also the design that makes every honest fix sound like a broken promise.

There is one more irony. Roosevelt’s taxes were meant to stop politicians from scrapping the programme. They have worked so well that politicians are now afraid to touch it at all, including to save it. The default cut in 2032 is the one change nobody has to vote for, and so it is the one the system drifts towards. The protection Roosevelt built against politicians has become a protection for politicians against making a hard decision.

What 1935 Leaves Us

None of this is an argument against the earned-insurance design. I think it was a brilliant piece of political engineering, and I suspect most Americans of every party would agree. It is an argument for understanding what any fix has to respect. A reform that ignores the feeling of ownership will fail politically, however good its arithmetic. A reform that respects it, by phasing changes in slowly, protecting people already near retirement, and asking each group to contribute something it can see, has a chance.

That is not a theory. It is what happened the last time the money ran short. In 1983 a Republican president and a Democratic Speaker, each of whom understood the 1935 bargain in his bones, found a way to change both the taxes and the benefits without anyone being able to say the promise had been broken. They did it by spreading the pain thinly and slowly, and by making sure both parties’ fingerprints were on it.

When Part VIII of this series sets out a draft bill, it will try to work within the same bargain rather than against it. But first, it is worth looking closely at how the handshake of 1983 was actually made, and what it cost the people who made it.
