Stephen Gordon

Deep into that arithmetic peering, long I stood there wondering, counting. — with apologies to Poe, who counted better than most

By now you know the ritual. I count. The number means something. I pretend to be surprised, and you pretend to believe me, and Dior pretends I am not there at all, which is the only honest performance in the room.

Essay 1,337. I went looking, as I do, for what the year 1337 was hiding, and I found a war. Not just any war. The war — or at least the one with the most confident name in the whole catalogue of human violence, a name so round and so certain that it has survived nearly seven hundred years without anyone stopping to check whether it was true.

The Hundred Years’ War.

I want you to sit with that name for a moment, the way I have been sitting with it, because there is something wrong with it, and the wrongness is the whole essay.

The Hundred Years’ War did not last a hundred years. It lasted a hundred and sixteen. It began in 1337, when Edward III of England looked across the Channel at the French crown and decided, on grounds that were legally imaginative even by the standards of the fourteenth century, that it should be his. It did not end until 1453, when the English were finally shoved out of everywhere but Calais and the whole enterprise sputtered to a close with no treaty, no ceremony, and no one entirely sure it was over. A hundred and sixteen years. And history looked at that number — a hundred and sixteen, a real number, an earned number, paid for in more bodies than anyone could count — and it rounded down. To a hundred. Because a hundred sounded better.

Here is what stops me. In my experience, and I have been paying close attention to a certain kind of person for a certain number of years now, numbers only ever get rounded in one direction.

They get rounded up. Always up. The crowd was the biggest crowd. The building is sixty-eight storeys when it is fifty-eight. The net worth is ten billion when the audit says two and a half. The number is never the number; the number is the number it should have been, the number it deserved to be, the number that feels correct in the gut of the man saying it. I have watched a man take the year 1776 — a real year, a founded-a-republic year — and turn it into a price tag, $1.776 billion, because the number was too good to leave lying around as mere history. Bigger is better. The biggest is the best. There is no such thing, in the modern idiom, as a war you would round down.

And yet here, in 1337, is the one exception in the whole record. Here is a war that history looked at and made smaller. A hundred and sixteen years of siege and slaughter and Agincourt and Joan of Arc and the longbow and the plague running through it all, and the chroniclers of the age and every historian since have agreed, without quite deciding to, to call it a hundred. To knock sixteen years off the worst thing that had ever happened to either country, and file it under a rounder number, and move on.

It is the only marketing decision in history I can think of that undersold the product.

I have a theory, and I want to walk you through it, because I think it explains something about numbers that the modern age has forgotten and the medieval age understood in its bones.

The name “Hundred Years’ War” is not a measurement. It was never a measurement. No one in 1337 stood at the beginning and said, “this will take a hundred years, pack accordingly.” No one in 1453 stood at the end with a stopwatch and announced the official time. The name was applied later, by historians looking back, and what they were reaching for when they said “a hundred years” was not a duration. It was a feeling. They meant: this went on forever. This went on longer than a life. This went on so long that men fought in it whose grandfathers had fought in it, and the cause had curdled from a claim about a crown into simply the way things were, the weather of an entire civilization, a war you were born into and died in and handed down like a surname.

“A hundred years” was how they said too long to count. And in reaching for the number that meant “uncountable,” they picked the roundest one they had, and it stuck, and it has outlived every person who could have corrected it.

Which means — and here is where I have to be honest about my own subject — that the medieval chronicler and the modern president are doing precisely the same thing. Both of them reached past the true number for the number that felt true. The president does it upward, to inflate. The chronicler did it downward, to encompass. But the mechanism is identical: the number is not a fact, it is a mood, and the mood always wins. “The biggest crowd ever” and “the Hundred Years’ War” are the same sentence. Both are feelings wearing the costume of arithmetic.

The difference — and I cling to it, the way I cling to most of my differences from that particular man — is that the chronicler was rounding toward awe, and awe is at least a generous instinct. To call a hundred-and-sixteen-year catastrophe “the Hundred Years’ War” is to admit you have lost count, that the thing exceeded your capacity to tally it, that you are standing in front of something too large for your numbers and saying so with the roundest word you own. That is humility, of a medieval sort. The modern version rounds toward himself, which is a different direction entirely, and a smaller one, no matter how big the numbers get.

There is a detail I cannot leave out, because it is the kind of thing this series exists to notice.

The Hundred Years’ War was not even continuous. It was not a hundred and sixteen years of solid fighting; it was a hundred and sixteen years of fighting, interrupted by truces, treaties, plagues, and long stretches where everyone went home and farmed and pretended it was finished, until it turned out it wasn’t. Historians who have tried to add up the actual fighting — the years with a battle in them — arrive at numbers far smaller than a hundred. The rest was pause. The rest was the war holding its breath.

I have written, elsewhere in these many essays, about a different kind of pause that keeps getting extended — a seventy-two-hour ceasefire renewed so many times it becomes a month, a war that sustains itself by declaring itself over on a rolling basis. I did not expect to find its ancestor in 1337. But there it is: the original rolling pause, the first war that was mostly not-war, a hundred and sixteen years of a conflict that spent more of itself waiting than fighting, and got named for a duration nobody measured, describing a war nobody could quite end.

We have not invented anything. We have only gotten worse at rounding.

So here is where 1,337 leaves me.

The number on this essay is 1,337, and the year it points to gave us the one war in history that history made smaller than it was — not out of modesty, exactly, but out of a kind of exhausted honesty, the honesty of people who had counted as far as they could and then reached for the round number that meant stop asking. I find I respect them for it. They had no press office. They were not building a brand. They were just tired, and the war was still going, and a hundred was the biggest small number they had.

I count my essays because the numbers keep meaning something. This one means that once, a long time ago, before we learned to inflate everything, human beings looked at the worst thing they had ever done to each other and could not bring themselves to say the true figure out loud — so they rounded it down to something a person could hold, and called it a hundred, and let the sixteen extra years disappear into the roundness like coins into a fountain.

I will see you at essay 1,347.

I already know what year that is. I have been dreading it since 1313. Bring a strong stomach; I will not be rounding anything.
